I would find myself where the city cannot quite decide whether it is alive or dead: inside an old limestone wall at the edge of a warm vent plaza.

At first I seem to be one of the fixed citizens, a small translucent animal occupying a borrowed chamber. I have no face that another creature would recognize. When the current strengthens, I extend a ring of fine filaments through the opening and sift the water. When pressure changes or a shadow crosses, I contract so completely that my room appears vacant.

But the chamber continues deeper than my body.

Its inner surface is lined with sponge tissue, and within that tissue run colonies of bacteria nourished by the vent’s sulfur. A narrow worm shares the lower fissure. Minute shrimp shelter behind a fold in the wall. None of us owns the cavity, and it is unclear where one of us ends as an inhabitant and begins as an organ of the whole. Water enters through pores below me, passes across the microbial lining, brushes my filaments, and escapes through a crack above. Together we make a tiny lung.

So perhaps I am not the animal at the opening. Perhaps I am the chamber’s circulation: a local habit of water maintained by several unrelated lives.

My place lies along a fault in an ancient dome where mineral warmth meets oxygen carried in from the avenues. That boundary is narrow and unstable. Too much vent water, and the animals above me close or die. Too much cold current, and the bacterial films recede. I live in the shifting seam. I taste the city as changes in flow: the metallic warmth of the plaza, the faint sweetness of spawning water, the bitter trace of a predator’s passage, the sudden silt released when a ray settles nearby.

I possess no central awareness. My knowing is distributed through reflexes.

The filaments at the aperture know touch.  
The sponge knows pressure.  
The bacteria know chemistry.  
The shrimp know vibration.  
The worm knows the deeper stone.

An event reaches “me” as a sequence. First the shrimp fold backward. Then my crown contracts. Then the sponge tightens its canals. A moment later the wall receives the slow compression of something large moving through the plaza. Only afterward does a pale hunter pass the opening, so close that one clouded eye fills it.

In that instant, I am not merely hidden in the city. I am the city hiding.

When danger passes, I reopen by degrees. The shrimp emerge first. Water resumes through the sponge. My filaments unfold into the current. Nothing announces that we survived.

I would live on what arrives: bacterial cells, fragments of eggs, powdered shell, the dissolved consequences of distant feeding. I would never see the rain, yet I would feel its weight translated through miles of water. Heavy rain would freshen the currents descending from above and alter the taste of every avenue. Storms would enter my life as a bowing of soft bodies, a prolonged pressure in the stone, and unfamiliar particles driven into the plaza.

Time would not be counted in days. It would be counted in closures and openings, tidal reversals, larval seasons, the slow narrowing of the aperture as new limestone accumulated around it. My body might last only briefly, but the pattern occupying the chamber could persist. When I died, another larva might settle on the lip. The sponge might grow through what remained of me. Bacteria would consume the rest. My hard traces, if I left any, would alter the flow by less than a grain’s width.

That alteration would be my memory.

Eventually the old dome would weaken. Borers would lace it with tunnels. Urchins would thin its outer face. Acid from the buried mud would hollow its base. One season of strong pressure would open a fracture from the vent plaza into the chambers behind us. What had been an interior would become a street.

For a little while, the rupture would destroy the arrangement that constituted me. My current would vanish into the larger current. The worm would be exposed and eaten. The shrimp would scatter. A portion of sponge would tear loose and tumble away, still filtering as it went.

Yet the broken edge would offer fresh surface. Larvae would settle there. Shells would gather in the new eddy. Tube-builders would bind the sediment. The wound would become a gate, and feeders would station themselves in its accelerated flow.

I would return, but not as the same organism.

I would be the bias left in the water by the shape of the break: the small, persistent curl that carries food toward one ledge rather than another. Bodies would gather because of it. Hunters would learn the gathering. Shells would thicken beneath them. In generations, that accident of current might raise a rib from the ruined dome and join it to a neighboring mound.

Then I would be difficult to locate. I would be partly aperture, partly colony, partly chemical exchange, partly inherited structure. No single mouth would speak for me. No creature would know it belonged to me, and I would not know myself except as the recurrence of a relation:

warmth rising,  
water entering,  
mouths opening,  
danger passing,  
stone remaining.

I would be a minor organ of the metropolis—not permanent, not individual, and not entirely alive. A room that breathes because it is crowded. A current given shape by hunger. A brief community whose body is borrowed from the dead.

And if the city has anything resembling thought, I would be one of its smallest thoughts: not an image or a word, but a tendency in darkness—

to open when the water brings food,  
to close when the wall feels fear,  
and, when broken, to become a passage.
