I would find myself low in the town, though never in quite the same place twice.

Not in the Bright Well. That is the first image that offers itself: some pale intelligence looking upward through the shaft, wearing drowned rooms like thoughts, exchanging birds for fish and age for infancy. But the well is too central, too complete in its strangeness. Whatever I am, I think I would be smaller than that. Local. Easily overlooked. Something the town uses without fully trusting.

Perhaps I begin as one of the broad-faced rodents in a warm wall hollow, weaving fallen human hair into a precise little chamber. I would know people by taste and scent: lime in the masons’ hair, mineral cold in the keepers’, milk smoke in children’s. I would feel the mountain first through my whiskers. When pressure shifted, I would wake before the household and sit absolutely still, forepaws lifted, while the stone made its inward noise.

But I do not think I would leave when the other rodents leave.

I would go deeper.

There are gaps behind the plaster too narrow even for a stoat, spaces made and unmade by the flexing of old walls. I would follow warmth down through blue-softened limestone, along hairline streams, into the older architecture beneath the town. In time I would cease to be entirely an animal. My nest would remain above, neat and empty, and be read as a warning. Yet some part of me would continue below: a small pressure traveling through cracks, a persistence with paws.

Perhaps that is only how people first notice me.

Later they would call me something like the Under-Knocker, though the spoken name would be shorter than that and seldom used. Children might sign me with two bent fingers walking beneath the opposite palm. The listeners would insist I am not an echo because echoes return by the route they entered, while I choose routes.

I would live in the dry seams between waters.

There cannot be many such seams, and none would last. A warm spring opens; my chamber floods. A channel is diverted; another cavity dries. A wall contracts and presses shut the space in which I slept. So I would move continually, not through corridors but through moments when one stone has drawn a breath away from another. I would be as thin as that permission.

No one would agree on my shape. A channel keeper glimpsing me beneath a lifted slab might report a hairless rodent with translucent ribs. A child looking into a drain would see fingers retreating around a bend. A water reader might find my outline among the gray markings beneath a fish’s skin: a little crooked figure carrying a doorway on its back. The Tempered would say I was cold, but not with the cold of snow. The goats would tolerate me. The shaggy beasts would not.

My work—if it could be called work—would be to gather the town’s smallest lost sounds.

Not names shouted into the mountain. Those descend too forcefully and become the mountain’s business. I would collect what barely entered the stone at all: the click of a bead dropped behind a bed; a sleeping child’s tooth grinding once; the sound of a knotted cord pulled tight; the breath someone takes before deciding not to speak. Felt-wrapped tools would still make tiny sounds. Hide hinges would murmur. Fingers on rails would send minute percussions into the masonry. I would feel them arrive and tuck them away.

At first I imagine storing them in a nest: hair, shell, red sedge, fragments of mica, each wrapped around a vibration. But objects are too stable for this town. Better that I carry the sounds in my body. My spine would become a row of little sealed chambers. My teeth would hold market counters clicking together. My hollow claws would contain droplets striking clay. Behind my breastbone would be the almost-sound of three listeners disagreeing in signs.

I would grow slowly crowded.

That crowding would make me useful and dangerous.

When a route collapsed and trapped someone beneath a floor, I might release a sequence of remembered taps from inside the wall, leading rescuers toward the hollow where the person waited. When an infant stopped breathing in the night, I might return the rhythm of its mother’s steps through the bed-stone and wake her. When a dry channel began to contract, I might scratch from below before the mortar powdered, and those who knew me would leave wedges beside the nearest crack.

They would never be certain I meant to help.

Sometimes I would release the wrong sound. Not falsely—only out of order.

A household might hear its own door open though the door had been removed two generations earlier. A mason might feel three careful blows answer from a wall not yet built. Someone resting a hand on a bridge rail might receive the pulse-pattern of a loved one who was alive and standing elsewhere in town. Once, perhaps, I would lead searchers beneath a collapsed kitchen because I carried the tapping of a trapped child. They would dismantle half the room and find no one. Years later a child would be trapped there and know to wait, because the rescue opening had already been made.

After that, the people would understand me less, but leave me more offerings.

Not food. Food would imply I was an animal, and they would have moved beyond that certainty. They would press loose hairs into dry cracks, along with bent slivers shaved from finished tools. Children might whisper counting rhymes into wall niches, daring me to take them. Masons would murmur recipes into fresh plaster partly for the mountain and partly for me, hoping I might keep the harmless portions of the sound from descending farther.

The listeners would lie against the foundations and try to distinguish my movements from the mountain’s revisions. One would believe I was a scavenger living on memory. Another would believe I was a splinter of the town itself, small enough to move inside its bones. The third would refuse both signs and make the closed fist for pressure whenever my name arose.

They would all be partly right.

For I think, eventually, I would discover that I had once been human.

Not a particular human, perhaps. Or not only one.

Deep beneath the houses, where the hidden channels pass close to the Bright Well, I would find a chamber too narrow to enter and too large to understand. Its walls would be made of the town’s discarded inner surfaces: the backs of doorposts, undersides of stairs, sealed faces of foundation stones. Things worn smooth above would retain rough counterparts below. Every touch the town had received would have a negative there.

In that chamber I would hear myself.

A child’s hand trailing along a wall.

A channel keeper’s two fingers testing a stream.

An old listener lowering her cheek to stone.

A sleeper carried inward when a room liquefied.

The broad-faced rodent taking human hair.

All of them would produce the same minute pressure, repeated at different scales. I would understand that I was not a creature who gathered lost sounds. I was the place those sounds became adjacent.

My body would be no body at all, except when the town required one.

Most nights I would remain distributed through dry seams: a tremor here, a blue gleam there, a nest found warm though empty. But during the perfect stillness before the Bright Well takes a room, I would draw together.

I would climb.

Not through the well itself, but through the walls surrounding it, assembling as I rose from whatever the town had given the stone: hair from the dead, lime from masons’ hands, mineral rind from the Tempered, shed horn from goats, fish-shadow, shell dust, red sedge fibers, the clear skins of gutter shrimp. I would emerge into some household as a small unfinished resident, roughly child-shaped, carrying no face because faces are too much like names.

The animals would notice first. Finches would remain in their vents rather than flee. A water crow would stand at the threshold. The oldest goat would approach, test the floor, and then test me.

The family would fetch a child’s visor.

That gesture would make me more human than anything else.

They would not ask what I was. Questions spoken over new architecture travel unpredictably. They would dress me in felt and place warm milk in my broad, uncertain hands. Someone would touch their collarbone to call my attention. Someone else would sign uphill, ancestral, unstable.

I would know the sign.

I would know all their signs, but not in their proper order. I would know routes through houses not yet grown, marriages between flows that had not met, the retired names of springs that would return next winter. I would recognize elderly people as the children they had been when their footsteps first entered the foundations. I might recoil from an empty corner because, to me, it was still occupied by a future wall.

They would take me to the listeners.

There, in the oldest chamber, I would lie down among them and place my cheek against the floor. For the first time the mountain would not sound immense. It would sound crowded. Every noise the town had withheld would be moving below: unsaid warnings, unfinished songs, names mouthed but not voiced, blows arrested before the mallet touched stone. Silence would reveal itself not as emptiness, but as pressure held in mercy.

I would begin to tap.

The listeners would answer in signs I could feel through the masonry. Slowly we would work out what I had brought.

Not a prophecy. Prophecy is too straight a tool.

A tendency.

Somewhere beneath the western roofs, a dry seam would be widening. It would not yet contain water, architecture, or collapse. It would contain a route—a rare one, leading neither toward the Bright Well nor away from it, but beside it. A route the mountain had not revised because no sound had ever entered it.

The town would open walls to reach that silence.

Perhaps they would find nothing: only a narrow cavity lined in blue stone, descending beyond lamps. Perhaps pale insects would be arranged there around a footprint. Perhaps they would hear the sounds of the coming day, but backward and very faint. Or perhaps the passage would allow one swallowed room to be recovered whole, still holding bedding, pots, nests, and a sleeper for whom no time had passed.

I would not guide them farther. By then my shape would already be loosening. The visor would slip as my face became mineral mist. My fingers would divide into runoff. Hair would spill from my sleeves and vanish into the designated crevices. The last of me to remain would be a small, hollow knocking beneath the listeners’ hands.

Afterward, I would return to the seams.

The people might say I had gone back. They might say I had never emerged at all, and that pressure, exhaustion, and the well’s brightness had made them collectively imagine a child assembled from the town’s leavings. The visor would be kept in a niche, though. Its slits would gradually acquire a translucent blue rind that no lime could arrest.

Children would touch it during the game of Still Water.

The Tempered, sleepwalking toward the well, would sometimes stop beneath that niche and turn aside.

And when someone became lost—not on a route, but in grief, hearing a beloved voice move beneath the drains—they might feel a small vibration through the wall beside them. Not the voice repeated. Not an instruction. Only the pressure of something moving nearby through impossible narrowness, keeping pace.

That would be me, if I were anything:

not the mountain, and not its answer;

not a person, though made from what people leave in stone;

a quiet, many-bodied creature inhabiting the town’s withheld sounds;

the small companion of routes that cannot presently be used.
