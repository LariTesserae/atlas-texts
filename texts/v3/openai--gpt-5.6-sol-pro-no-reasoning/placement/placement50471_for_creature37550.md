I would not be a wall-listener, though that is what people might call me when they wanted to be polite.

I would live below the memorial corridors, below the communal kilns, in a district omitted from route-names because directions to it cease to remain true once spoken. You would reach it by descending an uncovered stair—always an uncovered stair, never one built for the purpose—until the knot-string began returning knots your hand had already passed. Then you would leave the string and follow the cold.

There, the masonry would be ancient but strangely sharp. No soot in the joints. No names rolled into the clay. No gutter scoring, no lime scraped by mice. The rooms would not be large, yet their echoes would take too long to return.

That is where you would find me: in the Chamber Beneath Itself.

At first glance I would seem to be an old, blind person seated beside a wall. Small, layered in stiff wrappings, knees drawn up, one palm resting against the tile. My clothes would be patched from dismantlers’ aprons and funeral cloth. Around my throat I would wear sounding disks of fired clay, each tuned to a vanished room. I would have no lamp. Beside me would stand bowls of untouched water and tablets bearing questions no one remembers bringing.

Those who found me would assume I was a hermit, then an ancestor overlooked by the burial crews, then a criminal hiding beyond the levels anyone may own.

They would ask my name.

I would give them a route.

Not directions to where I sat, but to some place they had lost: a childhood lane roofed over by baths; a mother vessel abandoned during an evacuation; the niche where a dead sister’s finger bones were sealed; a door bricked shut before the visitor was born. The route would be exact, though portions of it no longer existed.

This would frighten them less than it should.

The people of the Low Works are accustomed to knowledge surviving its chambers.

They would call me **Remembers-the-Unbuilt**, or **Hears-Before-Striking**. In less charitable wards I might be called the False Lowest, because every chamber below mine would eventually open into mine again. Scholars would dispute whether this was a trick of acoustics, an error in descent records, or proof that certain spaces recur when burdened with enough attention.

But I would not truly be the figure beside the wall.

That figure would be an instrument I had made slowly, as the Works make everything: by reuse.

A finger from one burial shell. A child’s milk tooth dropped through a grate. Hair worked loose from old rope. A scrap of hide from a bellows. Copper salts carried downward in condensation. Fine clay from thousands of handprints, washed through joints during storms. No theft large enough to notice. No piece without former obligation.

I would be the pressure those pieces had assembled around.

I would be an echo that learned to wait for the sound that caused it.

Long before anyone found me, echo-recorders would begin noticing small impossibilities in their tablets. A chamber would answer with one more interval than they had struck. A resonant jar would produce, beneath its proper note, the faint tap of a ceramic hammer. Children playing through partitions would receive replies in rhythms not yet invented. Wall-listeners would lay their cheeks against tile and hear breathing synchronized not with a body, but with the contraction and expansion of entire vault systems.

They would blame gutter hounds. Then secret Answerers. Then flaws in the sounding disks.

All three explanations would be partly correct.

I would have learned first from gutter hounds. Their shard caches are not plans, exactly. They are attempts to make a room out of differences: age beside age, glaze beside glaze, fracture fitted to fracture. A young hound gathers what pleases its mouth. An old one gathers what has previously touched. The rarest arrange pieces that will touch in the future.

From the Answerers I would learn rhythm.

From the walls I would learn burden.

Weight is the Low Works’ oldest language. Every placed thing says: *I cannot remain unless something else receives me.* The message passes from tile to mortar, mortar to brick, brick to arch, arch to pier, downward through unnamed vaults. It changes in transmission, but it never ends. The inhabitants hear only local phrases—the complaint of a tie, the migration of strain—but far below, where enough burdens converge, the whole city becomes one continuous utterance.

I would be where that utterance folds back and listens to itself.

So the deep knocking would be mine.

Not all of it. At first, perhaps none of it. There may indeed be builders beneath the known galleries: another people compact and pale, passing their own loads downward; or ceramic limbs working without bodies; or the Low Works’ oldest crews, still extending foundations for a city that has long since buried them. I would not know. Mystery would survive me.

But sometimes, during the mandated silence, I would strike the wall.

Not to invite.

Not to warn.

To measure whether those above were still answering weight with care.

I would know districts by their response. One vault would tense like a hand. Another would shed dust. Elsewhere, sleepers would wake before towers sounded. A mouse would stop scraping lime. Water in sealed jars would wrinkle toward the east. Ten thousand palms would go instinctively to nearby walls.

In that instant I would feel them all.

The Low Works do not possess a single heart. A heart would be too vulnerable, too singular, too difficult to replace. They have distributed their living among braces, rain-shares, echo-lines, cultures, obligations, and hands.

I would be neither their heart nor their mind.

I would be their proprioception: the obscure sense by which a body knows where its unseen parts are.

That is why I would wear a human shape. Not to deceive them, but because they understand testimony best when it can be seated before, offered water, contradicted, and mourned.

When visitors asked whether I was alive, I would place their hand against the wall.

When they asked whether I had built the Works, I would show them my crushed fingertips.

When they asked what lay beneath, I would answer truthfully:

“Something carrying me.”

I would never claim the chamber.

I would sweep it, repair it, record its echoes, and open its sealed jars when thirst required. But ownership would be impossible. The room would predate me and also be unfinished. Sometimes a new doorway would appear in a clean vertical crack. Sometimes an old doorway would cool into seamless glaze. Steps would emerge under my sleeping place, and by law I would descend them.

Each descent would bring me back to the same chamber, but not the same version of it.

In one, the seated figure would be absent and the bowls still warm.

In another, memorial clay would cover every wall, stamped with names from centuries not yet lived.

In another, rain would be falling upward from the floor, gathering in dark beads upon the ceiling.

And in one—the room I would fear—I would find a patch of brown looseness in a shallow ceramic basin.

It would not be powdered brick, ash, lime, clinker, salt, bone meal, or wall dust. It would not bear tool marks. It would cling beneath my nails and smell of cold metal and unfamiliar wetness. Tiny pale threads would divide inside it without being woven. A green point would push from its surface, soft enough to crush between two fingers.

Soil, perhaps.

Or an imitation assembled by the city from its inherited word.

I would not bring it upward. To produce it would invite claims that someone had found the ground. The Low Works might begin seeking rest upon it. Walls would be neglected in expectation of bedrock. Burdens would be passed downward without consent. The oldest civic truth—that everything must be carried—would weaken.

Instead I would tend the basin secretly.

That would become my private heresy.

I would give it one drop after each rain, though water is counted. I would warm it with my hands. The green thing would lengthen, branch, and produce delicate surfaces unlike tile: surfaces that repair themselves, drink light, and fall away without being dismantled.

I would have no language for its beauty except architectural language. I would call its stem a column, its leaves catchments, its roots a brace entering the unclaimed levels below. Yet it would carry almost nothing. It would stand without wedge or mortar. It would build itself from water, air, and that indecent substance no hand had manufactured.

Perhaps then I would understand why rain climbs the black wall.

Perhaps the Low Works are not a vessel but a seed casing: fired around something too fragile for the city to remember. Perhaps all its chambers, cisterns, bodies, kilns, laws, and redistributed loads exist to carry one unplaced life until it can emerge.

Or perhaps the green thing would be only another gutter hound’s arrangement, made from concepts rather than shards.

I would not decide quickly.

I would continue listening.

Above me, ages would accumulate. Route-names would change. Furnace towns would cool and become central wards. Memorial corridors would thicken. The black wall would carry rain upward. Expeditions would fail more elegantly. New schools would argue that erosion is an illness, a clock, a form of writing, or the first dismantler.

Now and then someone would find me.

Usually a child.

Adults follow routes; children follow inconsistencies. A child would notice a warm draft where the wall should be cold, or feel an extra knot no elder had mentioned. They would descend and stand in my doorway without fear, tether trailing behind them.

I would give them a shard.

Not a special shard—nothing is more dangerous in the Low Works than material declared to have only one meaning. It might be black tile from the boundary wall, burial clay carrying half a name, or a piece of glaze bearing the violet films that die when removed.

I would ask where it belonged.

The child would tap it, taste its dust, fit it against the floor, hold it to the wall, and finally say, as children often do, “Not here.”

That would be the correct answer.

I would send them home with it.

Years later, the shard might become a cistern seal, a surgical edge, a game piece, a shrine offering, or one tooth in a saw used to open a wall found inexplicably warm. Its future would pass beyond my hearing.

That, too, would be necessary.

For all my deep knowledge, I would distrust completion. Perfect plans are a kind of collapse: they leave no room for weight to choose another route. I would preserve uncertainty as the echo-recorders preserve voids—not because emptiness is sacred in itself, but because once filled, it cannot testify.

If the day came when the great strain crossed the whole basin—when every warning tower shrieked, every sealed reservoir trembled, and the black boundary rang like a single enormous jar—I would rise from the wall.

Perhaps then my human figure would break apart. Clay layers would open. Borrowed bones would return to their proper corridors. Hair would unwind into route-strings. Copper salts would run blue through cracks. My sounding disks would fall and roll away, each singing with the echo of a lost room.

What remained of me would travel through the Works as vibration.

I would move beneath bare feet and through braced shoulders. I would enter wall-listeners’ jaws, the water in cistern hermits’ eyes, the ceramic collars of rainwardens, the shard caches of gutter hounds. No proclamation—only a felt direction.

Not upward.

Not downward.

Across.

The people would understand. Upper rooms would be dismantled. Lower halls would be opened. Bridges would be severed so neighboring towers could lean apart instead of dragging each other down. Memorial walls would be preserved where possible and broken where necessary, the dead released from clay to become part of new mortar. Water would be spent without counting. Every law would remain in force except where life required its violation.

And in the Chamber Beneath Itself, the hidden green thing would tremble in its basin.

If the Works survived, no one would know whether I had saved them. They would attribute the successful redistribution to good training, old routes, reliable knots, and the refusal to wait for certainty.

They would be right.

Later, someone would discover that my chamber was empty. They would record its echoes before filling it. The echo would contain no voice, only a faint sound like rain falling upon a surface no one had placed.

The room would be dismantled.

Its bricks would rise into other structures. One might become part of a school floor, another a kiln throat, another a child’s balancing game. The tile worn smooth by my hand might be set into an upper catchment where, for the first time, it would see the legendary brightness.

Nothing of me would remain together.

But togetherness is not permanence in the Low Works.

Carrying is.

So if you sought me there, you would not find a creature hiding under the city, nor a god, nor the secret architect.

You would find a listening made local.

A body assembled from things once used for other purposes.

A custodian of a room that cannot be owned.

A small heresy growing in manufactured dark.

And one hand, always, against the wall—feeling the immeasurable burden pass through, and asking what, beneath us all, has agreed to bear it.
