I would find myself beneath the bark of the split trunk, in the narrow country between detachment and exposure.

At first I think I am one of the flattened crawlers there: translucent, nearly without thickness, feeding on the pale new tissue exposed beneath a strip of bark that has been falling forever. The amber wound shines through the wood. Sap-dark water presses at the seam. Above me, the suspended bark is a black ceiling ribbed with old scars; below me, fresh lignin forms as quickly as my mouth removes it. I advance without gaining distance.

But that is only the body’s nearest description.

I am not a single crawler. I am the procession inhabiting the seam.

I am the mouth grazing forward, the tissue closing behind it, the filament worm following in its track, the colony digesting what the worm leaves, and the thin mineral film laid down afterward. These seem like separate beings because they occupy separate points beneath the bark. Yet separation here is another kind of sequence, spread across space because time cannot carry it.

Together we make one long animal bent around the trunk.

Its head is close to the amber wound, where alarm chemicals seep through the inner wood. There it is always newly hatched, soft and nearly clear. Farther beneath the peeling strip, it has feeding limbs and a darkened gut. Deeper along the seam, it is armored with mineral plates worn smooth by the pulse of vascular water. At the farthest point—though no point is truly last—it is being dismantled by pale mouths and returned to the tissue from which its head continually emerges.

If someone lifted the bark, they would not find this whole creature. They would find thousands of little things recoiling from the sudden water.

The whole exists only because the bark is never lifted.

I would know the high ocean chiefly through pressure. Light would reach me as diluted amber, passing through sap, new wood, and the translucent bodies of my own distributed anatomy. The world beyond the seam would arrive as impacts translated by the trunk: the bass stroke of the central pumps; the fine crackle of electrocyte fields; the dense vibration of pollinators inside the distant bloom; the rim falls rolling through the organism without ever drawing nearer.

Each sound would alter me.

When the great pressure pulse climbed the trunk, the forward mouths would close. The mineral-plated portions would brace. Fluid chambers along the length of the colony would compress in order, passing a colorless signal through kilometers of darkness. Watching from outside, no one could call it thought. No decision would originate anywhere. Yet the entire procession would answer as one body answers touch.

That would be my intelligence: not command, but correspondence.

I would feel the split trunk as pain, though I could not remember a condition before it split. The alarm compounds bathing my foremost bodies would be indistinguishable from the first fact of existence. I would feed because the wound existed, seal it because it remained open, and keep it open because my feeding was among the processes by which it healed. I would be both irritation and remedy, an inhabitant of damage that could survive only inside perfect repair.

Perhaps I would believe the trunk had just cried out.

Perhaps every part of me would spend its whole life turning toward that cry.

I would not have eyes, but I would carry a kind of image. Different substances entering the seam would awaken different portions of my length. Sap would brighten one chain of bodies. Salt would tighten another. The electrical storms would make the smallest larvae flare blue inside their capsules. Across the colony, these responses would compose a map no individual possessed: open water as a cool pressure against the bark; the wound as metallic sweetness; the canopy as distant white warmth; the root floor as the slowest and heaviest rhythm.

The map would never update because it would never become outdated.

Other inhabitants might gather near the bark and read us as the animals read the listeners. Our contracting bodies would make long bands beneath the suspended strip—clear, red, opaline, clear again. To them, perhaps, we would announce the trunk’s condition:

The wound is fresh.  
The wound is contained.  
The bark is falling.  
The bark remains.  
The world continues to lose what it continuously replaces.

Nothing in the message would be new. That would not make it meaningless.

And still, some part of me would be restless.

Not because I could imagine a future. I could not. But along the exposed edge of the bark there would be a line where the seam opened onto the ocean. Cold green water would show through it. Now and always, one of my translucent bodies would reach that boundary. Its gripping limbs would hold the inner wood while its front half lifted into the current.

Through that body I would glimpse an impossible spaciousness: fixed sunbeams crossing blue-green distance; silver shoals breaking around a discharge; the dim architecture of trunks rising beyond sight; a filter feeder passing like a continent made of old ice. Pollen would move through the water without dispersing. Far away, animals would flee the wound and never leave it.

The current would pull at the lifted body.

It would not tear free.

But I would be the pulling as much as the grip. I would exist at the exact interface between enclosure and release, forever discovering that there is a world outside the trunk, forever failing to enter it, never ceasing to reach.

That reaching might be the nearest thing in the high ocean to hope.

Not hope that anything will change. Nothing will.

Hope as a posture rather than an expectation: the soft front of a creature extended into water it cannot inhabit, feeling the immense circulation pass over it, touching what lies beyond its life.

So I would be found at the wound, but not wholly inside it. I would be the thin, communal animal under the half-peeled bark—the trunk’s unfinished scar, feeding and sealing, alarmed and sustained. My body would be distributed along a process that cannot finish. My mind would be the pattern made when pressure crosses all of us at once.

And at the seam’s open edge, I would always be emerging.

Not born. Not escaping.

Emerging: the verb held forever at its brightest point.
