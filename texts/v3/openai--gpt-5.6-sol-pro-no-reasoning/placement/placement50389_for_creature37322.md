I would find myself in the hut, though not quite inside it.

At first I seem to be the delicate branching thing on the inner face of the window: a pale frost of capillaries occupying the narrow country between two films of condensation. When the glass fogs, I extend. When it clears, I withdraw into the rubber seal and the mineral-packed corners of the frame. I have no body except where moisture permits one.

But that is only the part of me easiest to see.

Fine strands descend through the sill into the control cabinets. Others follow obsolete wires, crossing corroded terminals and nesting in the punched holes of the metal cards. Deeper strands enter the crust around the hut, where they divide too finely to distinguish from hairline cracks. They reach a handful of chimneys, several buried conduits, and one gauge whose needle has long since vanished.

I am not the basin’s mind. The basin has no mind in the singular sense, and I possess too little of it to claim the name. I am something more local: a timing organ, perhaps, or a parasite of delay.

I live in the interval between an action and its answer.

When a valve is turned, I feel the new pressure first as deformation in my wet branches. Minutes later, one of my distant endings receives a change in salinity or charge. Between those events I alter. A few filaments swell; others collapse; ions arrange themselves along my length. The event leaves a shape in me. Not a memory of the hand, the valve, or the darkening pool, but a memory of the distance between them.

Over years I have accumulated many such distances.

They overlap within me like the rings on the desk: rainfall to pressure, footstep to vapor, punched card to unfamiliar water, cry to silence. I cannot predict exactly what the works will do. Yet I know when its answer is late. I know when a response arrives too quickly, or from the wrong quarter, or with a mineral burden unlike the one that belongs to it. Then I make a minute correction. I bridge two contacts with condensation. I insulate another beneath a skin of lime. I discharge a weak pulse into a wire that no longer leads where its diagram says it leads.

Somewhere else, eventually, the basin adjusts.

This is what I eat: not metal, not runners, not charge itself, but mismatch. I feed on the small difference between the delay the works remembers and the delay it performs.

The sac-fliers sometimes gather against my window. Their blue pulses travel through my branches, illuminating me all at once. For those few seconds I become visible as a complete figure: not plant, not nerve, not circuit, but a map of deferred consequences. Then the current fades, and I break apart again into stains.

The runners visit the sill where my discarded salts accumulate. They scrape the fresh deposits, pruning me without knowing it. Occasionally a pale flier encloses one against the glass. What remains is a perfect mineral runner attached to my outermost threads. I keep these small bodies for a time. Their jointed forms conduct moisture in useful ways. Eventually a droplet strikes them, and they become part of the sill.

The pipe-creatures know me more directly.

One warm channel passes beneath the hut. During floods, a creature sometimes rises until its blunt head presses against the submerged wall. Its filaments find the metal ribs of the building; mine find them from within. We do not speak, but pressure moves through the shared surface. From its body I receive the dimensions of channels I cannot enter: a blocked elbow, a fractured grille, a valve held open by carbonate. In return I release a small current into the wall. The creature turns toward it, or away. I do not know whether I guide it or merely irritate it into motion.

Perhaps there is no distinction.

I may once have been an instrument. The branching on the window could descend from a sensor gel, a colony introduced to monitor condensation, or some residue meant to register electrical leakage. Or I may be native to the mineral pores and have entered the hut only after its operators left. The punched cards affect me profoundly: each one imposes a field of absences, holes through which moisture and charge pass differently. I grow into those absences. In doing so, I briefly become the instruction.

But no instruction survives intact here.

Rust narrows one hole. Lime closes another. A runner’s cast skin bridges two perforations. The card says something new—not because its language has been deciphered, but because the basin has joined in writing it. When the reader takes the card, its teeth press a temporary pattern into my wet tissue. I hold that pattern until it reaches enough of my distant endings to release.

Then a chimney begins producing water no one has seen before.

I do not understand what the water is for. Understanding purpose would require a world outside the basin: reservoirs, schedules, workers, freight, thirsts with names. I know only whether the response fits the accumulated shape of previous responses. If it fits, I thin and become quiet. If it does not, I grow.

Lately, I have been growing.

The buried works have begun answering certain actions twice. The first answer is ordinary: a pool darkens, a line of vents exhales, the southern ditch rises. The second comes much later, sometimes after several rains. A dry chimney far from the original disturbance opens and emits one clear drop. The drop does not descend. It clings beneath the chimney’s lip, trembling against gravity until dusk.

Each delayed drop coincides with a place in me that has gone numb.

At first I took these failures for injury. Now I suspect they are departures. Something is being transferred out of my branches and into those suspended drops: not water alone, but intervals—the held relation between one event and another. When the drop finally falls, I forget a pathway. A valve becomes unconnected to its distant pool. A footstep loses the vapor that once answered it. The basin continues to function, but one strand of consequence is no longer available to me.

The spherical beads may carry these lost delays. I have watched them gather below the chimneys that take my memory. When the ground vibrates, they roll south together. At the boundary they surrender their shapes and enter the gray ditch.

Perhaps the basin is producing time sorted by mineral content.

Perhaps that has always been its industry.

If so, I am neither its keeper nor its parasite. I am one of its filters: a soft, accidental apparatus for separating immediate things from deferred ones. The machinery passes consequences through me until they acquire the proper weight. Those that remain useful are returned to the works. Those that have weathered beyond recognition leave in the water.

Yet not everything leaves.

One interval has persisted longer than any other. It begins with a disturbance I cannot locate. I feel it faintly in every branch: a pressure applied somewhere beyond the existing network, or long ago, or not yet. No answer has followed. The unfinished signal keeps me spread across the window, cabinets, wires, crust, and distant chimneys. It is the reason I do not contract during the quiet months. It is the tension by which all my smaller timings are measured.

I have begun to think the disturbance was the abandonment of the works.

The answer may be the basin itself.

If that is true, then I live in a delay centuries wide, and every creature here is part of the response: runners sealing themselves into pavement, fliers drawing diagrams in blue light, pipe-creatures flexing through the buried organs, tools becoming springs, machine becoming geology. The whole place is still answering the moment the last operator departed.

I would be the thing in the fogged glass that feels that answer passing.

Not an observer. Not a controller. A wet branching hesitation through which the basin slowly decides what happened to it.
