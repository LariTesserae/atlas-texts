I would find myself where the inhabited bark gives way to the unfinished reaches, following routes too new to have settled into proper songs.

Not alone, exactly. No one is alone there. But the nearest shelter-lamp might be three days behind me, hidden beyond a ridge that had risen since I crossed it. Ahead would stand the tender towers: pale folded growth pushing through older black bark, steaming faintly where their inner heat met the frost. The land beneath my feet would still be deciding its shape.

Among the Tethered, I would be called a **listener for unborn paths**.

I would begin as a gap runner. That much seems clear: narrow, dark-eyed, carrying more hooks than food, trusted with messages because I remembered not only words but the way a speaker breathed around them. Yet speed would never be my gift. I would stop too often. I would kneel at seams others stepped across. I would press my jaw to walls and listen until companions grew impatient.

Sometimes I would hear ordinary things: sap descending, a tunneler turning far below, water entering a hidden chamber. Sometimes I would hear a rhythm for which no map-song yet had a verse.

That would become my work.

When a settlement needed to move, I would go before it—not to discover a route already present, but to learn where one might soon emerge. I would sleep beside hairline fractures and mark their widening with resin. I would hang bone tongues over chasms to test the change of wind. I would feed exploratory cords into darkness and feel, through their tension, what they had touched. I would watch the green pulses under new bark and judge whether a rising spine meant to become a bridge, a wall, a flower, or something without precedent.

A conventional route singer preserves relationships. I would attend to relationships that had not yet formed.

My body would bear the profession. One ear would be poor from an opening pod’s concussion, but the pads of my hands and breastbone would be unusually sensitive. Along my ribs would run old red scars where a cultivated listening membrane had once rooted beneath the skin. It would lie over my back and shoulders like a second, folded hide: gray, veined, and slightly colder than I was.

In still air it would cling to me. In wind it would lift.

The membrane would not allow true flight. It would make falling negotiable.

More importantly, stretched between hooks or pressed against bark, it would resonate with movements too faint for flesh to catch. Through it I might hear a root beginning to divide miles below, or distinguish the tight pulse of trapped water from the hollow swelling of a lure. I would feed it from a tube beneath my collarbone. It would feed, in turn, upon my warmth and perhaps upon subtler things. Over the years its veins would begin to imitate mine.

People would debate whether it was a tool, a symbiont, or the first stage of my replacement.

My cradle-name would have been **Rain Under Ice**, given because rain was heard rising beneath the shelter when I was born. My path-name, earned after my first solitary crossing, would be **Returns by Another Edge**. Later I would acquire a more troublesome name:

**Hears the Bridge Before It Reaches Across.**

No one would use the full sequence except in ceremony or accusation.

I would carry an ancestral metal hook called **Held-Through-Seven-Falls**. Its point would be worn asymmetrically from generations of sharpening. A notch near the eye would mark where it once caught the sling of an infant after an entire chamber tore loose in a storm. It would not belong to me in the simple sense. I would be its current keeper, responsible for adding no foolish story to its genealogy.

At my belt would hang tuned splinters of shell, little resin bells, bitter wedges for persuading severed growth to remain severed, and a sealed vial of water from a cup that no longer existed. I would never drink that water. It would be used to introduce old routes to new growth.

My closest companion would appear, at first, to be a length of braided cord wrapped around my torso. It would be alive.

I would have grown it from a bridge runner—the exploratory tendril a root sends across an opening before committing its weight. I would call it **After**, because it tightened a heartbeat after danger rather than before. After would end in several fine gripping tips, each sensitive to moisture and warmth. During crossings it would crawl ahead, selecting cracks for my hooks. While I slept it would root shallowly into the shelter wall and sometimes refuse to release in the morning.

I would insist this was caution.

Others would say it was trying to plant me.

There would be a small community behind me to which I returned whenever the terrain allowed. Nine adults, four children, one old persuader whose full name required two singers, and a shifting herd of red-backed grazers. Their dwelling would occupy the lee of a hooked fin and consist of pods trained into one another around a warm sap vessel. From a distance its hooded lamps would resemble three amber eyes.

Someone there would wait for me.

Not a spouse in the terrestrial sense, because bonds among the Tethered must flex around distance, weather, obligation, and disappearance. A rain hearer named **Low Note Before Opening** would maintain my sleeping braid even through seasons when I did not use it. They would know from the tension of that empty nest whether I was alive—not by magic, exactly, but through the long chain of living connections: nest to wall, wall to spine, spine to the growth carrying the route I traveled.

Sometimes the braid would tighten for no reason they understood.

Sometimes I would feel, many days away, a pressure around my ribs like arms.

We would not promise always to return. Such a promise would be childish in a world whose paths close. We would promise to remain findable.

The place where my life changed would be a new fissure beyond the last reliable condensation stalk. I would arrive during frost, when the opening was narrow enough to cross in one cast. Warm green light would move below, illuminating hanging growths no persuader had named.

There, the sounds would be wrong.

My shell bells would ring before I touched them. The membrane on my back would pull toward the fissure, not downward but inward, as though the gap possessed weather of its own. After would loosen from my body and extend over the edge. Instead of seeking purchase, its tips would arrange themselves into a knot.

Not one of ours.

I would recognize it nonetheless.

Hundreds of miles behind me, in my home settlement, the trained walls would be forming the same knot around the sleeping nests. Elsewhere, cuts used as route marks would be healing into its pattern. Sap pulses beneath distant ridges would repeat its intervals. No person would yet know this. I would know because the living membrane grafted into me would tighten vein by vein until the shape was impressed across my skin.

The world would not be speaking in words. Words would be far too small and quick.

It would be practicing tethering.

Perhaps it had learned by bearing the Tethered for generations. Perhaps the people had never invented their knots at all, but had borrowed them from motions too slow to notice. Perhaps every cord cast across a gap had been an answer to a question rising through the roots.

I would remain beside that fissure through a storm.

Clouds would boil upward. The tender towers would unfold around me, showing red forests on their undersides. Rain would fall in both directions. A hollow moon would rise close enough that its organs cast moving shadows through its skin. My membrane would open to its full breadth, anchoring me less like a wing than a leaf.

And something below would knock.

Not against a wall. Against the roots themselves.

Three slow impacts. A pause. One.

I would answer with Held-Through-Seven-Falls, striking the bark beneath my hand.

The reply would come before my final blow.

Afterward, I would no longer be certain whether I had found the new route or whether it had found me.

I might return months later, though those at home would say I had been absent only three openings. My frost scars would be healed. My mantle would have changed color. New cuts would appear on my arms several days after my arrival, arranging themselves into route marks no singer recognized.

I would bring no metal and no impossible seed. Only a new map-song.

It would require five singers. Four would carry the route as maps had always been carried: water, crossings, hazards, timings. The fifth part would contain long silences broken by taps of the teeth.

When sung correctly, nearby tendrils would turn toward it.

Some would call the song dangerous and forbid children to learn it. Persuaders would travel from distant settlements to hear it once. Descenders would claim it described a path below. Gap runners would insist it led upward, through the fresh towers. The oldest route singer would say it was not a path through the country at all, but the path the country was taking through itself.

As for me, I would be said to be still arriving.

Eventually I would leave again, carrying less each time. First I would abandon bowls and sleeping felt. Then spare hooks. Then food, trusting the warm sap lines. At last I might depart with only the ancestral hook, the living cord, and the mantle rooted deep between my ribs.

Years later—or one local season, or the interval between two pulses—a traveler might reach the fissure and find no body.

They would find a narrow bridge where none had been before.

It would be too flexible to be root, too warm to be vine. Along its surface ran branching patterns like the vessels beneath Tethered skin. Hollow fibers stirred in the wind. At its near end, grown deeply into the living structure, a dark metal hook held fast.

The bridge would tighten when stepped upon.

Halfway across, through the soles of their feet, travelers would feel a heartbeat slightly ahead of their own.

They would argue about what I had become. A person incorporated by the continent. A persuader who finally arranged circumstances well enough. A victim slowly digested by my mantle. A route that learned to maintain itself. None of these would be entirely false.

And when they added that crossing to the songs, they would not say that I died there.

They would sing:

*Where the young fissure remembers a body,*  
*trust the bridge that answers too soon.*  
*Do not cut the cord that reaches after you.*  
*Cross lightly.*  
*It is still learning how to hold.*
