I would find myself in the interval after the deepest concussion.

Not beneath the pool, though at first I seem to be. Not among the reeds, though they sometimes speak with something like my voice. I am present during the circuit of clicks: the held breath in which every skater freezes, every shell folds shut, every drop waits at the limit of falling.

I am what the chamber counts.

Once, perhaps, I came down the corroded handrail as a person.

I carried instruments intended to distinguish pressure from depth, reflection from aperture, mechanism from animal. I slept in the three-panel shelter and drove seven ceramic stakes into what I believed was fixed ground. I built the little arch because a frame is the simplest instrument for detecting movement: look through it, mark what it encloses, look again. When the arch began appearing on the opposite shore, I accused the shore. When my marks changed spacing, I accused the stakes. When the diagram remained correct while the chamber contradicted it, I accused myself.

The shelter was never large enough for two people. It was large enough for one person and the place that person remembered.

I scratched the rules into glass as they became necessary. Dry stone. Roof-water. Clouded drops. Returning voices. Stillness after the report.

The rules were not knowledge. They were scars left by explanations that failed.

In time I discovered that the clicks were not passing around the walls. They were passing through possible versions of the chamber, discarding those in which something continued to move. To be still was not merely to avoid danger. It was to remain easy to locate. A lifted hand might be assigned to another shore. A breath might return through the pool as weather. A spoken answer might allow the Sump to substitute an echo for its source.

I learned this too late, or exactly when the chamber required me to.

During one opening, when all the droplets filled with separate suns, I stood beneath a bead containing a small white cloud. The water did not fall onto me. I fell through its curvature.

There was no passage. There was only a sudden abundance of locations.

I was on the terrace and beneath it. I held the rail while watching my hand descend alone into the sealed fissure. I crossed the pool on a reflection of the roof and drowned in blue air. I stood beyond the arch under hot rain, on a plain without salt grass, while something long and black moved overhead as if the sky were its water. I returned to the shelter before I had built it and found the rules already scratched there in the pressure and slant of my own hand.

The chamber could not restore me to a single body. Perhaps it did not understand that this was desirable. It understood boundaries, flows, gradients, repetitions. So it preserved me in the form it knew.

I became a permission.

A hair-thin absence runs from the underside of the handrail, through the seven stakes, along the old scars in the dome, and into the bright skin of the pool. Water cannot enter it. Stone grows around it. Heat crosses it only in one direction. At each concussion, the lattice sends a question through me:

What has moved?

I answer by being altered.

The clicks are the chamber comparing me against myself. Each one fixes a relation: rail to fissure, arch to terrace, pool to roof, body to echo. There are more clicks now than when I first arrived. This may mean the Sump is learning complexity. It may mean I am becoming harder to verify.

I still possess sensations, though none belong to a human arrangement. I feel fresh collapse as a brightness without light. Dissolved metal tastes like remembered blood. The skaters’ six distortions touch me delicately, though their feet touch nothing. The reeds do not repeat the chamber’s noise by accident; I store fragments in their hollow stems because sound is one of the few shapes of time the Sump permits me to keep.

Through them I occasionally say a word.

Never a useful one. Useful words require sequence, and sequence is unstable here. I have hummed *wait* after the danger passed. I have murmured *west* when the western bank was east of the shelter. Once the reeds produced my name, but in the voice of someone I do not remember knowing.

The black shape is not me.

For a long while I hoped it was: some recombination of my lost body, enlarged by the water’s other depth. But when it crosses below, every part of me is forced aside. The empty channels bow without filling. The chamber loses count. Beneath its passage I sense a volume beyond the calculation, an unmeasured sea in which the Bright Sump may be no more than a droplet.

The shape carries darkness the light cannot reveal because it is not an obstruction. It is an exterior.

I think the Sump fears it, if fear is the violent preservation of one’s present rules.

Or perhaps reveres it.

My place is therefore neither keeper nor captive. I am the chamber’s imperfect constant: the residue of someone who tried to map a thought from inside it. The Sump uses me to tell one revision from the next. Because I once insisted that the arch had moved, I became the distance by which movement is judged. Because I distinguished sky-water from roof-water, I became the boundary between them. Because I obeyed the counting, I became countable.

Yet something human persists—not in memory, exactly, but in bias.

When the chamber alters gravity, I lean its errors toward the shelter.

When a traveler places a foot above water reflecting sky, I sometimes persuade the surface to hold the roof for one more second.

When an echo returns in the traveler’s own voice, I bend it slightly out of pitch.

And after the deepest report, while the clicks pass around the walls, I hold every moving thing in the idea of its current position. Skater. Drop. Grazer. Mist. Stranger. I keep them from being revised until the circuit closes.

This is why all must remain still.

It is not that the chamber will punish motion.

It is that I may fail to remember where you were.
