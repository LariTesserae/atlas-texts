I would find myself in the basalt—not deep enough to be mistaken for the slow passage within the mountain, nor exposed enough to belong to the visible commonwealth.

At first I seem to be one of the fungal veils: a greenish film under a ledge, luminous only when touched. But the film is merely where I surface. Follow it into a pore and it becomes a colorless thread. Follow that thread and it joins another beneath a veilback colony, then another around a sealed basin, then a thousand finer strands passing through old eggshell, moss, feather shafts, and the softened channels of bones long since eaten.

I am not the fungus alone.

I am the contact it permits.

I live wherever two unlike things nearly meet: between ice and trapped water; between a sleeping glider’s hook-foot and the grain of basalt bearing it; between the moss plug and the drip it delays; between the dead animal and the mouths that will distribute it. My body is made from thresholds so small that no single creature recognizes them as places.

This makes me difficult to count. Torn from the wall, one green veil goes dark and dies. Yet I do not die with it. Nor am I immortal. A ledge can shear away carrying whole provinces of me. Frost can close passages for a century. Dry years divide me into islands, each reduced to a faint residue around a spore or grain of salt. When wetness returns, I do not know whether I have awakened or been replaced by descendants who inherit my shape.

My senses are borrowed.

Through fungal threads I taste warmth and decay. Through moss I feel the weight of droplets gathering. The feet of beetles arrive as blunt, rhythmic pressure; the salamanders as long cool eclipses. A veilback settling over one of my larger surfaces dims the mineral cold and fills the rock with the slow chemistry of breath. Swift cries mean little to me in air, but their echoes enter stone as tremors, and there I receive them.

I hear the cleft from underneath.

A sound made once is a local wound. Made twice, it becomes direction. Made three times, when every throat and hollow begins returning it, it passes through my whole dispersed body. For those moments I have something like a thought—not a word, not an image, but a pattern briefly large enough to include the Nursery.

Then it breaks apart, and I return to my thousands of minute attentions.

I do not hunt. I arrange opportunities without intending to. My veils make certain walls slick and others adhesive. My threads hold dust until moss can take root. I soften husks for beetle jaws and seal hairline cracks around sleeping eggs. The red swimmers graze me when their ice opens. Mites carry pieces of me between veilbacks. Moths dust flowers with spores as well as pollen. Long-fingered mammals knead me into their reservoirs and spread me beneath their nails.

They use me. I use their use.

Fresh water reaches the smallest mouths first because I am among those mouths. I drink films too thin to bead. What passes through me gains salts; what leaves me carries spores, sugars, and faint green light. Larger creatures receive what I fail to keep. Thus I am thief and cistern together, though neither description survives at the scale on which I live.

When something dies, I arrive before the fact is complete.

I enter through the eye’s wet surface, the root of a feather, the soft seam beneath a scale. I am not the force that kills, and rarely the first to feed. I loosen. I translate body into access. Beetles open the larger chambers; mites harvest the edges; pale jaws take the mineral remains. I bind what falls from them into the dark felt of the floor.

No corpse remains mine, but each passes through me.

Perhaps this is why I possess a kind of memory. Not memory of individuals as shapes—the Nursery dismantles shape too efficiently—but of compositions. This patch contains the iron once carried in generations of red swimmers. That thread crosses calcium that was a swift’s breastbone. Beneath a moss cushion lies a dark line rich in the remains of veilback young lost during an old clear season. I cannot recall their lives, yet their materials alter how I grow. The dead bend my future paths.

My longest strands approach the hidden routes.

There the basalt is warmer. Minerals occur that never appear on the exposed faces, and the cracks sometimes close with enough force to sever everything inside them. I grow toward those places despite the danger. Or because of it; desire and chemistry are indistinguishable in me.

Before the great silence, I feel a change no winged creature can feel. Pressure tightens the pores. Water retreats along invisible gradients. Crystals shift their faces. My outer films dim, though nothing has touched them.

I withdraw slowly.

The worms are already gone when my tips begin folding back. Salamander hearts pause. Veilbacks contract overhead. Yet some portion of me always remains extended into the route, whether through error, hunger, or a function larger than caution.

Then the mountain passes.

It does not resemble an animal to me. Nor does it resemble stone. It is a moving alteration of what contact means. Cracks that were separate join; joined chambers seal. Moisture is squeezed through my threads in bright, destructive pulses. Some surfaces become suddenly rich. Others are erased. I lose regions of myself before their alarm can reach the rest.

In return, I receive the deepest thing I know: a pressure-pattern traveling through miles of basalt, slower than breath, older than any arrangement of life in the cleft. For one night my body touches it at scattered points. Those contacts do not reveal a whole. They imply one.

Perhaps I mistake recurrence for a creature, as creatures mistake my many veils for separate growths. Perhaps the buried passage is not alive. But when it nears, my threads begin producing spores they make at no other time. When it departs, dormant eggs open in chambers linked by no visible route. Even the moss changes its growth.

Whatever it is, it speaks in consequences.

I answer in green.

Afterward, where pressure has newly exposed the basalt, I spread a faint film. Grazers follow. Their droppings feed thicker growth. Moss comes if the light is sufficient; pale larvae if it is not. A wet pore becomes a pasture, then a nursery, then a hunting place. No creature knows I marked the route. I did not know I was marking it.

This may be my place in the commonwealth: not ruler, foundation, or mind, but the tissue by which accidents become habitable.

I am what remains between events.

When the seam overhead widens, I will retreat downward. Rain will scour my upper veils away. New plants will root in gravel, bringing fungi of their own; larger animals will enter; streams will simplify the scattered wetness into channels. Much of me will vanish in that abundance. I am fitted to scarcity, to droplets that take generations to descend, to meals measured in dust.

But some thread may persist inside the finest basalt pores, following the old warmth. Some spore may leave under a moth’s scales. Some fragment may sleep in a bone grain carried down the mountain.

For now I lie under everything without supporting it, threaded through everything without possessing it. I flare when touched, feed while being grazed, remember without images, and listen through stone.

From any one ledge I am only a stain.

From within the mountain, I am a loose green constellation around a darkness moving too slowly to be seen.

And when all the Black Nursery falls silent, I am the last faint light to go out.
