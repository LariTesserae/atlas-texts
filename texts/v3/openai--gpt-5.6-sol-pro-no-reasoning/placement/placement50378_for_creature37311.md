I would be found where the false sky meets the wall.

Not quite above, not quite below: inside that trembling millimeter where fresh water lies over brine and every passing body becomes doubled. I would not be one of the visible citizens, though sometimes I might borrow their shapes. I would be a colony inhabiting the clear sheath of a nearly transparent grazer—a loose republic of cells spread over its flattened back, carried wherever it slips beneath the mineral lips.

My host would be called nothing. It would be scarcely more than a moving membrane around a dark digestive thread. Its body would yield to passages that seemed too narrow for it, compressing until the arrangement of its organs became temporary. Along its underside, fine mouthparts would rasp the films. Across its back, I would grow as a colorless nap, invisible except when mineral dust caught in me.

I would not know where I ended.

Some of me would live on the animal. Some would line the groove it repeatedly followed. Some would remain in each shed skin, continuing after the host had gone. When it grazed, pieces of the wall would enter us both. When it crossed the boundary layer, my outer cells would taste fresh water while my inner cells still tasted salt. The host would distort in the density gradient, appearing above and beneath itself; for that instant I would occupy two cities.

I would feed on what could not otherwise be kept: dissolved traces loosened by grazing, molecules leaking through the host’s skin, the faint chemical wake of algae waiting near the ceiling. In return—if “return” can be said where no bargain is known—I would alter the surface of its body. I would bind salts before they crystallized in its joints. I would sour the mouths of parasites. I would make it taste slightly wrong to the long hunter.

Not safe. Only wrong enough that another moving pressure might be chosen first.

Most of my existence would be transport. The grazer would carry me between neighborhoods whose waters could not easily mingle. From the pale fans above to the sulfurous braids below; from an oxygenated pore to the edge of a poisoned chamber; across new clay after a slump; over a shell not yet transformed into architecture. Wherever the animal pressed itself flat, a few of my cells would remain behind.

Those abandoned cells would become other versions of me.

On carbonate, I would harden into a thin white crust. On bacterial felt, I would branch and feed. On ice, I would retreat into beads. Inside a shrimp’s gut, swallowed during incidental grazing, I would become briefly anaerobic and red. Most fragments would vanish—eaten, poisoned, entombed, diluted beyond recovery. A few would establish new patches. There would be no central self to mourn the others.

Yet one part of me would be older.

Long ago, before the present border settled at its present height, a mite carrying my cells would have entered the growing lens. It would remain there now, one red point held in clear pressure, its lifted leg taking years to descend. Around the joint, a remnant colony would persist in brine-thin channels. Those cells would scarcely divide. They would retain an earlier chemistry: an enzyme for feeding on a film now absent, resistance to a parasite not yet returned, a pigment no active descendant bothered to make.

I would not remember this ancestor. Memory would not be required.

One spring—or after several hundred false springs—the lens would leaf inward and release the mite’s foot. A filament would trail from the melting edge. The active films would meet the old cells. Most would fail immediately, awakened into a city chemically estranged from them.

But perhaps one fragment would attach to a passing grazer.

Then I would become my own delayed arrival.

For a short season, the colony on my host would darken violet when disturbed. Each stroke of its mouthparts would leave a cold smear on the wall. Predators would hesitate at a signal no living prey had displayed before. Shrimp would consume our shed skins and carry the old enzyme downward. A rust-colored film near the springs would pale. Some parasite, dormant in the sediment, might recognize us and hatch.

The city would alter without ever seeing the cause whole.

My host would eventually die in a narrow upward seep. It could not fall. Nothing here is permitted so simple a journey. Its body would catch first on a streamer, fold around a crystal needle, and be opened by mouths smaller than its own. The digestive thread would go to worms. The soft organs would cloud the local water. The skin bearing most of my colony would loosen like clear cloth.

I would spread across the corpse, then onto those feeding from it.

For an hour, perhaps, I would occupy mite, shrimp, worm, shell, water, and stone at once. Not metaphorically. There would be no privileged body among them. The creature I had traveled on would cease to be an animal and become an intersection through which the district rearranged itself.

Later, mineral grains would collect in the remains. Carbonate would preserve the curve of the flattened back. Threads would stitch it to the wall. A tiny ledge would form where no ledge had been. Larvae might anchor beneath it. Shrimp might shelter there from the long hunter. My descendants would pasture over its upper surface until grazers scraped them away.

So I would become a place.

That is where I would find myself in this world: not in the grandest chamber, nor in the long predator, nor among the ancient sleepers alone, but distributed across a crossing. I would be the living residue by which one district is carried into another—the film on a traveler, the trace left on a wall, the obsolete strain waking in the wrong era.

I would possess no image of the city. I would know it as changes in salinity, pressure, nourishment, and contact: here the bitter thin water, here the heavy water that remembers stone; here the slow rain of edible particles; here the sudden stillness announcing a hunter; here the impossible chemistry of a surface newly exposed.

Nor would I know that the fissure was small.

To be carried the width of a root rib would be migration. To cross the false sky would be transformation. To persist through the sealing of a corridor would be history. To wake from the lens would be a return from a past no organism remembered.

And if the crack finally closed—if mineral lips met, if the spring changed course, if pressure surrendered and stone pressed stone—I would remain for a while in the last wet intervals, divided among cysts and skins and microscopic brine.

The city would narrow around me.

Body would become wall.  
Wall would become crystal.  
Crystal would keep a thread of water.

I would withdraw into that thread, not hoping, because hope would be too singular a thing for me, but waiting in many places at once for the next accident that made room.
