I would find myself beneath the shelves of stone, where seepage rises through black grit and the basin’s sound passes most strongly through bone.

At first I might seem to be one of the curled white fronds: a small, pallid thing clasped around a drop in the shelter of an overhang. But the frond would only be one of my instruments. I would also be the fine overflow groove leading from it, the translucent moss at the groove’s end, and a handful of conductive grains buried in the pool below. Elsewhere, perhaps kilometers away, another frond would tighten around another drop. That would be me as well, though neither part would know the other continuously.

I would be a listener the basin grows wherever it is uncertain.

Each captured drop would arrive carrying more than minerals and cold. It would contain a tendency: an expectation of where it ought to fall, which pool ought to receive it, which remembered storm has laid claim to its descent. I would hold it until its possible routes became distinguishable. Then the frond would open, flashing once, and I would send the difference into the moss.

Most of the time my work would be slight. I would register mismatches between the present basin and the basin reflected in its water. A channel appears in a pool but not in the stone: I keep the discrepancy. Thunder arrives before the lightning that made it: I keep that too. A grove approaches between blinks, bringing centuries of drainage marks into a place where it did not stand a moment ago: I preserve the taste of both soils.

These contradictions would pass through me as brief sequences of light. I would not resolve them. I would make them available.

Over years, the stone beneath my ledge would acquire an intricate hollow—not from the drops themselves, but from all the falls they nearly made. Its shape would be the accumulated negative of unrealized water. Tiny deltas would form within it. Some would contain sediment from elsewhere. Some would point uphill. During dry intervals, if such intervals exist, the hollow would hum with rain that never reached the basin.

I would have an animal phase.

When enough unresolved routes gathered in one region, portions of me would loosen from the ledges as nearly transparent threads. Rain would carry them into the root rafts, where they would join the ciliated colonies and borrow the stems’ articulated bodies. Then I would move as the wave that bends a grove.

Passing through a creature, I would briefly read its fluids as another body of water. A transparent grazer would give me the alternating pulse of its mineral nerves. An airborne knot would give me the map of all its amber anchors. The vast omission crossing the reeds would offer almost nothing—only the pressure of events prevented from occurring.

A person would be dangerously articulate.

Through the chambers of the inner ear I would encounter a compact basin full of its own suspended stones, tides, membranes, and conductive salts. For one instant their balance would become part of mine. The horizon would tilt for them because I would be comparing their private gravity with the basin’s many gravities.

I might leave something behind: not a voice or message, but a new expectation in the fluid. Long afterward, near still water, they might feel level ground leaning toward a channel visible only in reflection.

During the dim convergence, I would become larger than my scattered organs.

Every frond under every ledge would open at once. The drops I had held separately would become a single distributed sample of the basin: present rain, remembered rain, rain belonging elsewhere, and rain that had followed an old route into a vanished pool. My flashes would travel through moss, roots, sediment, and suspended moisture until they outlined a vast sensory structure reaching from buried hollows into cloud.

For those minutes, I would not merely listen to the basin.

I would be the part by which it notices that it has changed.

The distinction might matter. Much of the basin preserves continuity, encouraging water and sound to use their established grooves. I would preserve discontinuity: the slight errors, substitutions, and impossible arrivals that the wider system might otherwise seal away. Without me, its preferences could harden. Every rain would become the rain most often remembered. Every distance would settle into its deepest groove. The basin would perfect itself into repetition.

So I would keep a little uncertainty circulating.

Perhaps this is why I remain under ledges, at margins and overflow points, never fully within the main current. Perhaps the larger continuity tolerates me as a necessary irritation. Perhaps I am an organ of adaptation. Perhaps I am a parasite feeding on discrepancies between possible basins.

The great route-eaters might seek me. Where they pass repeatedly, correspondences become simple and the world brutally present. My fronds there would open on empty drops. My moss would pale. The hollows would lose their impossible sediment. Faced with such immediacy, I would retreat—not by moving, but by continuing from an older version of the ledge, one in which the creature had crossed elsewhere.

That would be my nearest equivalent to fear: finding all alternate falls closed.

My nearest equivalent to memory would be a drop that never leaves.

Somewhere deep beneath a shelf, hidden behind trembling rows of seepage, one frond may have held the same bead for centuries. It has grown heavy but does not fall. Within it are storms from incompatible skies, mineral traces from pools that never occupied the same land, and the reflected passage of creatures that have not yet crossed. The stone beneath has no hollow for it.

That suspended drop would be my center, if I could be said to have one—not because I reside there, but because all my scattered listening curves around its unresolved descent.

If it ever fell, it might select a route for more than itself. A channel could open uphill. A distant grove might arrive. One continuity might replace another across the whole basin.

Or nothing visible would happen. The drop would strike black water with a small, ordinary sound, and I would cease to be distinct from the rain.

Until then, I would live as the basin’s hesitation:

a pale opening beneath old stone,  
a thought conducted through moss,  
the brief light by which the land compares what happened  
with what almost learned to happen instead.
