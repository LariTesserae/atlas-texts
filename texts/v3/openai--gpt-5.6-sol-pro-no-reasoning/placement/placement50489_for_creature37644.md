I would be found where the city is weakest: in the bowed retaining walls between the tortoise plazas and the lower cisterns, where roots have entered the joints and water can be heard traveling through stone.

At first I might seem to be one of the small dark herbivores that use those walls as roads. Short-legged, broad-footed, almost without eyes, I would move slowly along the seepage lines, nosing through moss for softened mortar, fungi, salts, and the pale roots that grow in cracks. My hide would be ridged and porous, always wet, carrying a skin of algae. Small snails would shelter beneath its folds. Midges would rise when I shook myself.

But no single body would quite be me.

I would be a lineage of wall-dwellers, each knowing by scent and pressure where a terrace has begun to shift. We would feel strain through our feet: a trembling block, a widening seam, the cold exhalation of a new cavity. There we would gather. We would gnaw loose material from dangerous joints, swallow clay and grit, then deposit it farther inward as dense, fibrous pellets. We would drag rootlets across openings. We would carry fungal cords in our mouths and press them into wet rubble, encouraging a tough felt to grow through it. Our sleeping chambers would form behind the wall in branching clusters, and the packed bedding of generations would become reinforcement.

No one would call this repair. We would come because fractured stone sweats mineral water, because the fungus there is sweet, because the narrow chambers are warm and predators cannot turn around in them. The wall would survive as an incidental consequence of our appetite and fear.

Seen briefly in a noon shaft, one of us might look unfinished: blunt muzzle, whiskers pale as root hairs, ears sealed nearly flat against the head, forefeet divided into many sensitive pads. The pads would read masonry as other creatures read sky. Our calls would be too low to stand apart from the valley’s rumbling, but neighboring colonies would receive them through the stone. A warning would travel not as a cry but as a change in chewing. Silence in one chamber would make the next colony still; then the next. In that way we would perceive a stalking snake, an approaching herd, or the slow internal crack of a settling terrace.

Our world would not be the valley. It would be a chain of pressures.

Warm wall. Wet seam. Root arch. Hollow beneath the black plaza. Bitter mineral patch. The ceiling where bats make the stone hot. The conduit that floods after night rain. The old chamber that smells of a colony gone generations ago.

Perhaps our colonies would preserve routes older than any living forest. A tunnel would bend around a vanished beam. A nursery would occupy the socket where some ancient fitted stone had fallen out. We would follow these shapes without knowing they were absences. Our bodies would inherit the architecture as instinct: turn left where the wall thickens, climb when the water tastes of iron, retreat when cool air rises from below.

Other citizens would know us only through effects. Frogs would lay eggs in the shallow pools our pellet dams created. Rodents would steal our dry bedding. Monkeys would break open outer chambers for the salt gathered there. Snakes would enter behind them. Moss would root in our abandoned vents. When a colony died, its galleries would collapse into a compact band that held the terrace together better than untouched rubble.

Yet the longer I remained, the less certain it would be that I was the animal at all.

I might instead be what passes among them: a particular wet fungus cultivated unknowingly in their guts and chambers, binding clay to hair, root to dung, bone dust to stone. I would inhabit each wall-dweller briefly, carried from failing seam to failing seam. I would fruit only in abandoned rooms, releasing spores when a hooffall shook the masonry. My body would be distributed through miles of retaining faces—dormant in dry walls, awake where water returned.

Or I might be the route itself: the inherited circuit joining mineral seep, nursery, feeding crack, and burial hollow. Individual animals would enter me at birth and leave me at death, but I would persist as scent sunk into stone, as polished turns, as edible growth renewed by passage. I would have no awareness beyond traffic. Hunger would be my summons; crowding, my memory; collapse, my pain.

From outside, nothing would distinguish me from the valley’s general life. There would be no monument, no territory clearly mine. Only certain ancient walls would bow and not fall. Water would emerge from them filtered and brown. Ferns would grow in regular bands above hidden colonies. In the deepest hours, beneath frogs, wings, hooves, rain, and rookeries, a listener pressing an ear to the stone might hear a patient granular sound.

Not construction. Not language.

Thousands of mouths moving the mountain inward, one swallow at a time.
