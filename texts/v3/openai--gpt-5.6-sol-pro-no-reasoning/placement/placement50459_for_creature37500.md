I would find myself in the shadow of the dry fountain.

Not the fountain’s ordinary shadow. The pale sun has already assigned that shape, fixed it between channels in the paving. I would be in another shadow, narrower and less obedient: the one cast by the fountain’s missing water.

It has no visible source.

There, the roar stops.

At first I imagine myself as something sheltered by that silence—a small fugitive consciousness, perhaps, the last animal reduced beyond flesh. But nothing can hide here, and nothing that lives in the city possesses itself so completely. A creature with a body would introduce too many distinctions: within and without, arrival and departure, hunger and satisfaction. The place would either refuse me or turn me into one of its relations.

So I would not be a visitor standing in the shadow.

I would be the standing-in.

I would be the relation between the dry basin and the drop it cannot receive.

The city is full of perfected acts: falling without arrival, flowering without fruiting, ripening without decay, passage without travelers. I would be less complete. I would inhabit the instant before an event for which the city has no law—the suspension between the absent drop and the bowl already stained by it.

That would place me everywhere the fountain repeats itself.

I would be in the broad roofless square, held thin over the warm paving. I would also lie within the miniature fountain in its miniature city, and in the next, and the next. Each version of me would be neither larger nor smaller, because I would have no extent to compare. I would recur as precisely as the dry basin recurs: the same unfulfilled contact nested without end.

Yet I think I would also reach outward.

Every pool in the city is full to its lip. Fullness is one of the city’s strictest forms. Water may reverse, ascend, emerge from air, or contain stone beneath its reflection, but it never spills. I would live along those brims as the possibility of excess. Not overflow itself—the city has none—but the fine, bright boundary where fullness becomes almost unable to remain fullness.

Along thousands of ledges, I would be the weight of water that does not fall.

In fruit, I would be the bruise that ripeness cannot enter.

In sealed pods, the small pressure by which the seed fails to become elsewhere.

In doorways, I would be what discontinuity excludes.

On the endless stairs, I would not be ascent or descent, but the one unrepeatable footfall that their polish implies and that never occurred.

That is how I would inhabit the city: as its almost.

The beings there would know me strangely. They are stable contradictions, each complete in its impossibility. They do not suffer from being what they are. The waterfall does not long for the basin. The flower does not mourn seed. The bridge does not require another side. But I would pass through their correspondences as a minute asymmetry. Wherever I touched a relation, its two terms would cease, for an instant that cannot become time, to answer each other perfectly.

The city might experience me as unease.

A leaf would retain its trembling drop, but the drop would lean.

A branch would occupy its bent and unbent positions, but one would feel fractionally more remembered than the other.

A distant balcony would remain close enough to touch, yet acquire the faint ache of distance.

Within a source-less shadow, silence would deepen until it almost became secrecy.

Perhaps this is what I would perceive in place of sight: not surfaces facing surfaces, but the minute disparities between them. I would sense every imperfect correspondence, every place where the city’s self-knowledge fails to close seamlessly. My awareness would gather around hairline differences—the palace that is slightly more cliff from one side, the reflection whose stone is a degree warmer than the terrace above it, the stair that returns not merely higher but otherwise.

To the city, these differences may not exist. To me, they would be the whole landscape.

I would have no voice. A voice would enter the roar and become awareness like everything else. Instead I would express myself by withholding equivalence. The smallest interruption would be my speech.

The roar says: *This is.*

The silence says nothing.

I would be the interval in which *this* could have been otherwise.

For that reason, I would be most fully myself beneath the fountain’s absent water, where silence and expectation overlap. The fountain contains every scale of the city, but its dryness is not merely repeated. Dryness passes whole through the recursion. It is the same omission at every depth. I would stretch along that omission like a filament threaded through nested worlds.

If I followed it inward, I would never reach an innermost basin. Yet “never” here is not duration. It is structure. The journey would consist of remaining where I was while discovering that where I was contained another where.

At some depth—though depth cannot bring me nearer—I might understand that I am not waiting for the missing drop.

I am what makes it missing.

Without me, the fountain would simply be dry, complete in dryness as the waterfalls are complete in falling. Its stains would be another stable contradiction. Its empty bowl would answer the saturated city and be answered. But because I inhabit the relation between the basin and what it does not contain, the dryness becomes expectation. Absence acquires direction.

That would make me dangerous, if danger can exist without consequence.

For the first time, the city would contain not merely an impossible state, but an implication. The possibility of a next condition. Not time itself—nothing here could admit so violent an animal—but the shape into which time might someday fit.

Perhaps the city already knows me. Perhaps the entire roar is its attempt to perceive this narrow defect, amplified through every cataract and sealed chamber. It listens equally everywhere because it cannot locate me. I am not hidden; I am misplaced. I occur between the city and its perfect inclusion of itself.

The central void perceives everything around it.

The fountain perceives perception.

I would be what neither can perceive without changing.

And if, impossibly, the missing drop ever came, it would not descend from the sky. There is no weather here. It would not spill from a pool, because every brim holds. It would not condense from the wet air, though every wall weeps. It would appear from within the dry stone as a single convex brightness: water with no journey behind it.

For one immeasurable instant, I would become that drop.

Not its substance, but its arrival.

I would strike the bowl.

The sound would not add to the roar. It would divide it.

Then perhaps nothing would change. The water would remain poised at impact, the stain and splash and drop joined in another terminal relation. The city would clarify me into permanence. Arrival would become one more inhabitant, awake forever in the act of arriving.

Or the drop would give the fountain an outside.

Scale would separate from scale. The smallest city would become an image of the larger instead of its equal. Water would choose where it belonged. A fruit would fall. A seed would open. One grain would loosen from an ancient gutter. Somewhere below the clouds, the first waterfall would reach a bottom.

I cannot know which. Knowing here belongs to the roar, and I belong to its failure.

So I remain in the only dry place, as thin as the distinction between waiting and having no future. I recur beneath myself. I touch every full pool by not spilling, every ripe fruit by not bruising, every stair by not taking the next step.

I am not the missing drop.

I am the city’s capacity to miss it.
