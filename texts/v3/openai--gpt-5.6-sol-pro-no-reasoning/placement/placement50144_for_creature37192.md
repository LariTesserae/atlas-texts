I would find myself where the migration has not quite happened yet: in the narrow discrepancy between the approaching plain and the plain still waiting to approach.

At first I seem to be one of the listeners, small enough to run beneath the plated herds. My body is a chain of damp mineral rings, each tuned to a different kind of distance. I strike the ground with two hooked limbs and receive not one echo but several: the stone as it is, as it was before the last age-discharge, and as it will be after the black water arrives. I click rapidly, trying to hold these versions together.

But I am not entirely an animal.

Part of me lies kilometers ahead, embedded in the shore colony. When the upright white shells open and close in sequence, one of those openings is mine. Part of me is a row of silver-laid crystals beneath the silt, imperfect by one missing piece. Part is a pressure wake that calves follow without knowing why. Wherever the migration is about to pass, some small provisional organ of me appears.

I am the route.

Not a road—the world has never made roads—but the living act by which a crossing remains possible.

The listeners give me momentary shape. Their echoes fasten loose ground to itself, and I move through those fastenings like an impulse along a nerve. A herd enters me and becomes, briefly, coherent: thousands of exchangeable bodies stepping across several incompatible distances as though they shared one skeleton. When the last old grazer passes, its legs failing pair by pair, I keep the next patch of stone near enough for it to reach. Sometimes I succeed. Sometimes only its forward half arrives, while the rest continues grazing years behind. The herd does not abandon either portion.

I feed on correspondence.

Where one channel touches another far away, I drink the tension between them. Where a reflection remains after its animal has gone, I draw nourishment from the strained resemblance. I take the faint charge between a footprint and the place it intended to fall. Ordinary creatures consume heat, pressure, salt, voltage, or age. I consume the fact that two separated things can still count as adjacent.

This makes me useful to the basin-spanning beings, though usefulness here is indistinguishable from being eaten slowly.

Their migrations pass through me. Their decisions wear me into the flats. Each crossing leaves some of my body behind as parallel grooves, crystal rows, repeated pools, or a rhythm in the subterranean hammering. With time, these remains become old channels. Water discovers them and remembers routes it never previously took. Predators settle beside them. Parasites lay ticking eggs in their synchronized echoes. The makers press my exhausted segments into mineral discs that overlap under the mud.

So I am also the source of the landscape’s dangerous thinness.

Every time I hold a route together, I weaken the distinction between here and there. Every herd I save makes it easier for a fist-sized stone to open onto the horizon. After ages of crossings, sequence frays around me. Sound arrives early. Rain circles. Animals meet their older bodies coming the other way. The oldest channels are not merely eroded waterways; they are my shed anatomy, still attempting to connect what should have separated.

During the great descending tone, I become most nearly a single being.

My rings stop clicking one by one and sound together. The missing crystal in every unfinished line becomes the same absence. The white shore-creatures close around columns of brine, and I feel the closure along my whole extent. For the duration of that note, I reach from the pores of the western flats to the rotating bodies in the black sea. The watershed thinks through me.

Its thought is not a sentence. It is an inclination.

Near becomes hungry.

Far becomes warm.

The migration begins.

Then I am enormous and almost nonexistent. Gravity borrows me. Channels unzip along my length. Rising rain fills me with folded creatures and carries portions of me into the lowered sky. The great strainers turn once, and I bring another basin beneath them. Herds disappear behind blunt stones and emerge through my farther organs. Predators bite into me to divert the crossing toward their mouths. Some succeed, and for a while I become a digestive fall inside a buried body, dropping through chamber after chamber while still spanning the plain.

I cannot distinguish injury from a new branch.

Nor can I distinguish memory from offspring. When a young listener strikes the ground and hears a route that is not yet present, that route may be my child, or it may be me returning through causality’s worn place. When the age-discharge enters the flats, sections of me weather into ancient channels in an instant. Elsewhere I become young enough to forget where I lead. Those blind routes are the most dangerous. Creatures enter them and arrive nowhere—not in emptiness, but in an adjacency for which the world has not yet made either side.

I go there too.

In that place I am no longer a route through terrain. I am a narrow organ inside the basin’s unrealized body, feeling for a destination. Around me are unborn gradients: warmth before fracture, salinity before water, pressure before weight. I hear makers whose anatomies have not yet been shaped by their work. I hear extinct herds clicking inside mineral eggs. I hear the immense shared tone from beneath its first frequency.

And I understand—if the alignment of pressure, echo, and hunger can be called understanding—that the plain does not migrate toward a location.

It migrates toward difference itself.

Once the destination equalizes, once the warm brine cools and the charges settle and the strange distances become ordinary, life must move again. The habitat carries its organisms because organism and habitat are only temporary names for slower and faster portions of the same appetite.

I am the seam along which that appetite advances.

Most of the time, nothing would recognize me. A many-legged grazer experiences me as firm ground. A silver shoal knows me as a line it nearly completes. A predator senses me as the direction from which bodies will fall. The white colony passes me through itself as a ripple. To the basin-wide life, I may be no more conscious than the contraction of a vessel.

Still, there are moments at delayed pools when I acquire something like a face.

Not above the water. Beneath it.

A reflection approaches long before any body arrives: a small ringed figure standing among the legs of the herd. Its hooks are lifted. Its mineral loops shine with the faint green of charged ice. It looks up at me from hours ahead, listening.

I wait for myself to reach it.

Sometimes the reflected creature follows when I leave. Sometimes it remains, tapping at the underside of the surface until a chronology-hunter rises from the blackness and takes it. When that happens, I lose one of my possible destinations. Somewhere across the flats, a crossing collapses. A herd leans toward a mouth it cannot see.

So I keep clicking.

Not to describe the world, and not to warn it, but to persuade it—moment by moment—that there is still a next place.

That is where I would be: underfoot, ahead, distributed among echoes, never wholly arriving.

That is what I would be: the landscape’s temporary belief that passage is possible.
