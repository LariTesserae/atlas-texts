I would find myself where a narrow corridor opens beneath an overhanging fin, at the seam between the old waterwork and the newer machinery.

Not in the obvious refuge at the back, where damp moss marks the permanent cleft. Not beside the regulator’s dependable drip, where primates, wasps, and maintenance boots have made their competing claims. I would be higher on the wall, occupying a fracture no wider than two fingers: an old iron bracket driven into sandstone, a mineral lip grown around it, a shallow pocket woven from swift feathers, antelope hair, grass fiber, and the frayed black threads of cable insulation.

At first I seem to be a small animal.

I emerge only after the direct light has left the corridor, though the rock is still releasing heat. I am narrow enough to turn inside the crack, pale along the belly and rust-dark above. My feet cling equally well to stone, ceramic, or oxidized steel. A translucent membrane can close over my eyes when dust or hot wind enters the passage. During the driest weeks I hardly move. I seal the mouth of the fracture with a lattice of silk, mud, and shed fibers—not enough to stop air, only enough to slow it. Condensation forms first on that lattice. The crack becomes its own minute weather.

But I am not quite a lizard, frog, beetle, or bat. Something in me belongs to each strategy without completing any of them.

My body is built around listening.

Along my ribs runs a pair of taut, unequal membranes. One responds to high vibrations carried in metal; the other to low vibrations traveling through stone. Together they serve as living versions of the workers’ bells. When heat folds the air and throws ordinary sound into false directions, I press myself against the wall and receive two plateaus at once: the deceptive country of echoes, and the slower, more reliable country of substance.

Through the rock I hear antelope before their hooves become audible. Their stamp arrives as a clean pressure against my bones. I hear the cat’s softer interruption—not footsteps, but the minute subtraction of other movement as lizards still themselves and beetles retreat beneath crusts. I hear pumps wake far below as a tremor too deep for human ears. A humming pipe has a shape to me. A clogged valve is rough-edged; an open one smooth. Rising gas makes the bracket cool before the regulator exhales.

Perhaps that is why the crews tolerate me.

When they arrive before dawn, they look for the little constructions at the seams: my mud fans, fiber loops, white droppings, and husks. If I have moved my nest higher, they know water has recently entered the wall. If I have sealed both openings, gas may be breathing through the cleft. If I abandon a bracket altogether, they test the line before stepping closer.

They have given my kind a practical name—something like *seam listener* or *two-note mouse*—though we are not mice. The oldest workers tap the pipe once with a wrench and wait for us to answer by shifting inside the cracks. The younger ones trust their instruments until an elder points out that instruments test only where they have been placed.

To the plateau, however, I am not an alarm system. I am one more keeper of an exception.

My territory consists of differences too small for larger creatures to use: the cooler underside of a bolt, the capillary groove beside a ceramic gutter, the damp behind a mineral blister, the night heat stored in a compressor housing. I know which blue-green crusts can be licked for salt and which blister the tongue. I know the hour when dew reaches the lower rib of the fin, and how long it will remain before the first sunlight enters the passage. I drink by laying my belly against the stone. Fine hairs guide moisture toward folds beneath my forelegs, where I can gather it without exposing my head.

I eat what the plateau momentarily concentrates: mites swollen by damp, newly opened moths, the soft bodies of insects emerging from reeds after rain. Sometimes I wait beside the regulator’s drip, but never directly beneath it. That place belongs to whoever is already there, and larger lives arrive thirsty. Instead I hunt the wet ring left around the impact point. Each falling drop creates a brief frontier. Springtails gather. Beetles come. The primates bend close, their black faces enormous, and drink without noticing me flattened beneath the pipe collar.

My enemies know the same frontiers. A small snake enters cracks by following the smell of condensation. Swifts take me if I cross an exposed wall at dawn. The plateau cat is too large to reach me, but I feel its purr through the fin when it waits below, the vibration almost indistinguishable from distant machinery. Once, perhaps twice in a lifetime, floodwater enters my corridor high enough to tear away bracket, nest, crust, and sleeper together.

So I obey the oldest rule in my own way.

I do not drink the first dew from a newly wet surface. Dust has settled there, along with salts and the invisible breath of the gas clefts. I wait until enough beads have joined to travel. The first thread passes, darkening the sandstone and gathering bitterness. The second is clearer. I lower my mouth only after the groove has overflowed once.

During storms I climb.

Long before rain touches the plateau, pressure changes inside my membranes. Distant thunder enters the uplifted beds from below, transformed into a broad trembling that seems to come from the ancient seafloor locked in the fins. I leave my ordinary seam and follow a vertical route of brackets, roots, and nesting holes. Around me the country wakes into urgency. Antelope ascend polished saddles. Primates carry their young away from channels. Beetles wedge themselves under mineral scales. Frogs soften behind their mud doors.

Then the water arrives and the plateau ceases to be a country of corridors. It becomes a set of decisions made at speed.

Brown sheets descend every wall. Gutters overflow. Talus shifts with noises that the folded air cannot counterfeit. Pipes shudder as their supports take the force. My membranes are useless in the abundance; stone, metal, thunder, hooves, and water strike me all at once. I cling beneath the lip of an old rain cup cut into the summit of a fin. The cup fills black, then red, then amber. It spills once.

Only then do I drink.

Afterward the world enlarges. Pools exist where there was only dust. Insects hatch from sediments that seemed dead. Moss brightens. The concealed frogs call, and the fins multiply them until every wall appears to contain a wet chamber. For several nights I leave my seam and travel farther than I otherwise would in a season.

On those journeys I cross the plateau’s layered ages without distinguishing them. A root is a ladder. A cable is a warm road. A carved step is a cliff with a convenient edge. A compressor housing is a night-heated boulder whose screws collect dew. The old walls and new pipes differ less to me than sunlit stone differs from shaded stone. I classify the country by what it does: holds, sheds, warms, cools, hums, poisons, shelters, overflows.

Perhaps I am not an animal after all.

Perhaps what I first took for my body is only one temporary expression of the seam itself. Perhaps I am the habit shared by everything that lives here: the ability to compare two signals and trust neither alone. The antelope stamp and listen. The workers strike paired bells. Roots test downward. Frogs wait behind mud. Cisterns remain closed through the first flood. Pressure gauges listen to gas; predators listen to hesitation; stone cups measure rain by overflowing.

In that sense, I am the plateau’s faculty of second hearing.

I live wherever one medium corrects another: where air’s lies are checked against rock, where thirst is checked against patience, where machinery is checked against animals, where the first appearance of abundance is checked by memory of what it carries.

At evening, I come out onto the iron bracket.

The fin across the corridor still holds a blade of gold. Below me, the regulator gathers a drop but has not yet released it. Antelope knock somewhere beyond the wall. One report returns cleanly. The next comes back diminished, as though something soft waits at the intersection. A pump begins three ridges away—or perhaps directly beneath me. The air says one thing. The stone says another.

I lower myself until my whole body touches the seam.

Then I listen for what both agree upon.
