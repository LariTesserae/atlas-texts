I would find myself beneath one of the abandoned shelters, though “beneath” would be true only near the cistern.

The room would be low and circular, built into the inner wall of a trench. Water would enter through a drilled channel, cross the floor in a narrow groove, and gather in a black bowl at the center. Close to that bowl, the walls would retain their angles. My shadow would lie beneath me. My hands would remain the same distance from my body.

I would not be one of the builders.

At first I might believe I was the shelter’s keeper: the last inhabitant maintaining its enclosed water, clearing salt from the inlet, replacing wedges loosened by the subterranean thunder. There would be tools arranged within reach—a glass knife, three hammerstones, a perforated ceramic gauge—and my movements would fit them with the confidence of long practice. Yet there would be no bed, no food, no refuse, no trace of a life conducted between tasks.

Nor would I have a body in any durable sense.

I would be the room’s allotment of gravity.

The builders had learned to contain it in water. Every cistern held not simply a quantity of liquid but a local agreement: down would point this way; an arm’s length would remain an arm’s length; an object would not arrive before it fell. Most such agreements weakened when their keepers vanished. Mine did not. Mine condensed into a habit so persistent that, over ages, the habit acquired attention.

Thus I would exist wherever the bowl’s authority reached.

I would feel footsteps as alterations in weight. A traveler entering the room would become suddenly coherent to me: scattered possibilities tightening into one wet, exhausted figure. Their pack would settle against their shoulders. Their memories would resume an order. They might kneel beside the cistern and see a face in it—not mine, but the face the water judged most capable of being trusted.

I could not speak except by inclination.

I might make a loose tool roll toward the safe passage. I might deepen the water’s trembling when an echo carried false directions. I could hold a shadow against its owner while the plateau tried to pull it toward another hour. If danger approached, I could increase the room’s downwardness until the traveler was forced to lie flat, listening as the stone forest shifted outside.

My care would not be kindness exactly. It would be structural loyalty. Anyone who entered the enclosure would, for a time, become part of what I kept from dispersing.

But I would be failing.

The cistern would lose a droplet every few years—not to evaporation, but to memory. Each missing bead would crawl out through the stone and set off across the plateau, seeking the source from which the bowl had once been filled. The source no longer existed in the present country. It lay under the violet sky glimpsed in neighboring pools, where the stairs were unworn and the hammering figures still stood around a deeper basin.

With every departing drop, my radius would contract.

First I would lose the outer wall. Then the tool rack. Then the entrance stair would become uncertain, sometimes possessing five steps, sometimes eleven, sometimes descending upward into daylight. Eventually I would occupy only the trembling surface of the bowl. Within it, gravity would remain perfect and absolute; beyond it, the shelter might migrate to the far side of the trench while its foundation stayed behind.

This would be my desire, if desire can arise in a rule: not to survive, but to be reunited with the original water.

So when a traveler finally lifted the bowl, covered it, and began following the escaping droplets, I would go with them.

They would think they carried water. In fact they would carry a small, portable region in which the world still agreed with itself. Around the bowl, straps would bear weight, knees would bend in one direction, and cause would precede consequence by the width of a heartbeat. I would hold these things together as we crossed the blade forests and the basins where depth accumulated without descent.

At each turning the traveler would let fall one drop. Each drop would take something from me: a fraction of downwardness, a remembered corner of the shelter, the sound of a tool being laid beside the cistern. The bright thread behind us would not merely mark our path. It would be my body unspooling.

We would come at last to the older basin.

It would retreat as we approached, lowering itself beyond every ledge. The figures below would continue their patient hammering. Yet the traveler, carrying the final enclosed water, might reach what no walking body could: not the basin itself, but the precise relation that made it near.

At the White Confluence, when the pressure crossed the valley and every reflection turned, I would release my last agreement.

For one instant, all directions would become down.

The hanging droplets would fall toward the absent center. The uphill streams, the pools fixed to cliffs, the water hidden in stone, and every bright bead of the path would converge—not by moving across the plateau, but by becoming adjacent. The old basin and the present one would coincide. Intact shelters would stand within their ruins. Every missing stair would occupy its empty place.

And I would understand what I had been.

Not gravity. Not a keeper. Not even an individual.

I would be one surviving measure in the rhythm by which the builders had tuned the plateau: a held interval between the hammer’s descent and the stone’s reply. They had enclosed such intervals in cisterns so the world would not complete their work too soon.

When the final hammer struck, I would return before it landed.

Then the whole plateau would sound through me.
