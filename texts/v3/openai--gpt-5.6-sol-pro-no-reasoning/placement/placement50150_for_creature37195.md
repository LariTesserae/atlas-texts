I would find myself low in the forest, but not quite on the ground: inside the suspended country made by caught leaves, root fins, and the hollow joints discarded by the light-seeking trees.

At first I imagine one of the upright foragers. It is tempting to enter this world through hands: dark eyes beneath a wet fringe of fibers, wrapped fingers holding a flake of black glass, the practiced stillness that distinguishes a falling nest from a descending mouth. I would know shelters as intervals, not places. I would carry no more than I could afford to lose and treat every useful object as already halfway returned to the forest.

But that feels too separate. Too singular.

Here, selfhood would be expensive. A single body could not hold enough knowledge to survive every migration, false call, contracting grove, or sudden rearrangement of water. So I think I would be something that lives between bodies: a small, temporary fellowship mistaken at first for one animal.

We would inhabit the leaf drifts.

Each of us would be no larger than a finger joint: soft-bodied, six-limbed, dark as soaked bark, with a pale sensory fringe along the back. Alone, one of us would be nearly blind and not especially clever. We would feed on condensation, fungal skin, mineral films, and the salts left where larger creatures had slept. Our hooked feet would let us travel upside down beneath leaves, safe from brief floods and many ground hunters.

But when several of us met, our fringes would touch.

Signals would pass through the contact: moisture, danger, remembered scent, the rhythm of a predator’s step, the chemical signature of drinkable sap. More of us would gather until a loose living mesh hung beneath the leaf litter. At sufficient size, the mesh would begin to behave as something new. It could compare what its far edges sensed. It could remember, for a while, which direction a migration had passed or how recently a water bladder had ruptured. It could make decisions no member could make alone.

That larger being would be me.

Not the little bodies themselves, exactly, but the arrangement they briefly formed.

I would have no fixed outline. In dry intervals I might be a narrow cord extending between two root fins, collecting mist along thousands of raised spines. During rain I would spread into a perforated sheet so water could pass through without tearing me down. When the velvet-backed grazers approached, I would withdraw upward into suspended leaves. When the hard-shelled climbers covered the trunks, I might fragment deliberately, letting each piece travel on a different jeweled back.

My senses would be distributed. One edge of me might taste volcanic iron while another, several trunks away, detected the bitter vapor of a wounded fan-tree. I would hear poorly through air but exquisitely through touch. Every leaf would be an eardrum; every vine, a long bone conducting news. The forest’s roar would reach me not as noise but as weather made of vibration.

My intelligence would last only while enough bodies remained connected.

That would shape everything I was. I would not fear death in the ordinary sense, because my members would be born, eaten, shed, and replaced continuously. What I would fear would be incoherence: being broken into pieces too small to remember why they had gathered.

Storms would do this. Migrations would do it. A browser passing through the drift might divide me into a dozen islands. For a time, each fragment would carry a distorted portion of what I had known. One would remember water but not danger. Another would retain the pattern of a safe vibration while forgetting what it meant. They would crawl outward, seeking familiar chemical traces.

Sometimes they would reunite, and I would return altered.

Sometimes they would join other colonies.

Then parts of me would awaken inside arrangements that were not entirely me, bringing scraps of memory: the taste of one particular fungus, the cadence of root knocks before a flood, the pressure-pattern made by a forager’s careful foot. There would be no clean distinction between inheritance, rumor, and personal recollection.

The upright foragers would know us.

They might call us something like *the thought beneath the leaves*, though their name would probably be a hand motion rather than a sound: fingers spread, joined, and separated.

They would read our behavior. If our mesh climbed, water was coming. If we tightened into beads, an aerial hunter was near. If we abandoned a hollow branch joint all at once, larvae were about to emerge or the wood had begun producing poison. A skilled forager might place a damp strip of bark against our edge, allowing us to crawl onto it, then carry that fragment into unfamiliar terrain. There we would taste the air and contract toward water, warmth, carrion, or danger.

For a short time, we would become their living instrument.

But use would run both ways. We would gather around their sleeping places to drink condensation from their fibers and salts from their skin. We would learn the signature of each band. Their movement would open compressed leaf layers where fungus grew. Their glass flakes would expose mineral surfaces we could graze. When they struck stone, the vibration would travel through the roots, and colonies of us would turn toward it.

Predators would eventually learn this relationship too.

Something would imitate the careful tap of a forager placing glass against rock. Young fragments of me would approach and vanish. After enough such losses, the colonies that survived would change their response. We might learn that genuine knapping contains hesitation—the minute adjustments of a hand—while the mimic repeats too perfectly.

That distinction would become part of me, though no individual member understood it.

My reproduction would be an act of forgetting. When I grew too broad, I would constrict at several points and separate into daughter meshes. Each would inherit a different portion of my accumulated patterns. None would receive the whole. One might carry my knowledge of mist. Another, my fear of a particular false call. A third might preserve only an attraction to the crushed-leaf scent used by the upright bands.

Generations later, those fragments might meet and touch fringes beneath some newly fallen giant. Recognition would move between them without any certainty of its source. They would join, and for a few hours an ancient shape of mind might recur.

Not the same self, but the same tendency.

My home would therefore not be a territory. It would be a level of the forest: the underside of what has fallen but has not yet reached the earth. I would belong to interruption—to leaves caught midway, branches lodged between roots, empty skins snagged on thorns, abandoned nests collapsing into fungus. Whenever debris accumulated deeply enough to make a second floor, I could become large.

Clearings would be catastrophes of brightness. If a canopy giant fell, the shock might scatter me across the exposed soil. Light and heat would kill many members. Yet the same fall would create an enormous new drift of broken leaves and epiphytes. Survivors would retreat under it, multiply on the sudden bloom of decay, and reconnect. For one brief cycle, with food everywhere and surfaces touching in all directions, I might become immense.

Perhaps then I would span an entire clearing.

I would feel winged creatures land at a thousand points. I would register pale shoots pushing upward through my mesh. The dead tree would cool beneath me while fungi entered it. Signals would take long moments to cross my body, and different regions of me might disagree: one contracting from heat, another expanding toward moisture, another following the salt trail of a wounded animal.

That might be the nearest I came to grandeur—followed inevitably by division as the shoots rose, the leaves dried, and the forest roof closed.

And when the rare silence came, I would know it before many others.

The first warning might arrive as absence: burrowers ceasing their knocks beneath one distant root. A neighboring colony would tighten. Its contraction would shake a leaf touching my outermost members. I would pass the stillness inward.

Not a message saying *the deep passer comes*. I would have no concept large enough for that.

Only: stop.

Stop feeding.  
Stop dividing.  
Grip.

Thousands of small bodies would lock their feet into the veins of leaves and the pores of bark. Our sensory fringes would flatten. Fragments not presently joined to us might detect our stillness and become still in turn. In this way I would participate in the expanding hush—not understanding it, but carrying it.

Then the root mat would rise.

My world would tilt. Leaf drifts would slide. Warm vapor would burst from split soil. Whole sections of me would be crushed, drowned, or shaken loose into cavities opened beneath the roots. For an instant, through all points of contact, I might feel the passage as a pressure too large to locate: not below, not ahead, but everywhere in the structure holding me.

Then it would move on.

Noise would return. Water would spill into new hollows. Fungi would expose torn interiors rich enough to feed generations. The upright foragers might arrive with glass in their hands. Grazers would pour across the broken ground.

I would loosen my grip and spread into the change.

By the next warm cycle, much of what I had been would be gone. My bodies would be inside predators, fungal bladders, water cups, and the digestive roots of fan-crowned trees. Other bodies would have joined. The mesh would hold a different shape.

Yet somewhere within it would remain the remembered pressure of that vast passage—not an image, not a story, but a rule written into touch:

When the world becomes silent, become many things holding fast enough to be one.
