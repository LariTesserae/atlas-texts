I would find myself in the narrow dark stem connecting a crown to its wandering shadow.

Not in the crown itself, though at first I might mistake myself for one of its leaves: copper-green, hinged into a thousand scales, opening to the hard blue noon. I would know heat as abundance, voltage as weather. I would feel the mountain rising through me in fractions. Yet when the discharge came and every leaf turned edge-on, I would discover that what I had called my body was only a terminal—one bright, temporary answer given by something distributed below.

Nor would I be the shadow. The shadow has preferences older than I am. At noon it would pull away across the ceramic plates, widening where no light permitted it, lingering under the black columns and touching the north-facing drifts. I could not command it. But neither could it fully leave me. Between us would remain a filament of darkness, impossibly narrow and never broken.

That filament would be me.

I would be a creature of attachment rather than substance: the small maintained agreement that says two diverging things still belong to one another.

Most of the day I would be nearly indistinguishable from an ordinary shadow cast by an ordinary stem. Sunlight would flatten me against the ground. The basin’s dryness would not trouble me; I would contain nothing that could evaporate. Heat would pass over me without warming me. Wind would move the scarlet filaments and leave me untouched.

But I would feel every inconsistency in the place.

When the perimeter lengthened toward evening, I would lengthen differently. When gravity paused, I would rise a hair’s breadth from the plates while remaining dark upon them. When time divided—years entering one leaf while only a breath crossed another—I would be stretched between the two durations. One end of me might be attached to a plant as it was before a discharge, the other to its shadow after it had learned something from the machinery below.

My existence would be the strain between those moments.

At noon, the shadow would begin its wandering. It might cross the bare central circle and fold around the immense furnaces visible only from elsewhere. Through it I would glimpse belts and wheels turning in silence, but I would never see them whole. I would receive their movements as tension: a periodic pull, a distant insistence, like a pulse belonging to an organ too large to recognize its own extremities.

When two shadows overlapped and opened a view downward, I would briefly join a lattice of other stems. For that instant, I would understand that we were not cords tethering shadows to plants. We were fine black roots growing upward from the engines.

The crowns would be our flowers.

The mountain would be what accumulated around us.

Then the overlap would end, and I would become small again.

I think I would have a task, though no one had assigned it. The basin manufactures presence, but presence is costly and prone to separating from itself. Plants enter stones that do not exist. Roots reach across centuries. Shadows acquire motives independent of their crowns. Seeds rise beyond gravity’s jurisdiction. Everything is always in danger of becoming several nearby things that no longer agree they were ever one.

I would keep one such disagreement from widening.

Not prevent it. The basin requires difference: between heat above and heat below, between one altitude and its almost-identical neighbor, between a body and the darkness by which it knows where it stands. Perfect unity would stop the gradients. Perfect separation would dissolve the garden. My work would be to preserve the narrow interval in which departure remains relationship.

I would be elastic in ways space is not. If the shadow wandered for hours while the entrance remained immediately behind, I would pass through neither distance nor duration, but through their discrepancy. If the plant became uncertain and displayed several leaf positions at once, I would divide into threads, one for every possible shadow. Most would fade as the possibilities closed. One would remain, carrying a faint memory of all the forms the plant had declined to become.

That memory would be my nourishment.

I would feed on rejected arrangements: the almost-leaf, the wheel that nearly turned backward, the seed that could have fallen, the mountain that might have weathered before it rose. The pale globes calculate possibilities and close them away; I would gather the minute darkness left in each fold. I would not archive events as the black columns do. I would preserve only kinship—the knowledge that an unrealized thing was once connected to what became real.

During the blue discharge, when the entire basin agreed upon one present moment, I would vanish.

This would not be death. It would be the only time I was complete.

With no disagreement between crown and shadow, there would be no need for the stem joining them. I would spread through the shallow conductive web as an absence of resistance. Every plant would become definite. The floor would settle on a depth. Gravity would remember its direction. For several seconds there would be no “between” in which I could have a shape.

Then the blue line would pass.

Leaves would turn broadside again. Shadows would resume their slight disobedience. Space would develop seams. I would condense at one of them, perhaps attached to another plant than before.

So I would possess no lasting individual identity. I might awaken beneath a copper-green heat gatherer and later find myself tethering the shadow of a pale folded globe. After a century I might become the dark hairline under a seed lodged against an overhang, connecting it to the place on the ground from which it rose. I would know these as the same life because continuity, not form, would be my anatomy.

The tending distortions would notice me.

When they crossed the basin after a discharge, narrow as disciplined heat shimmer, they would stop where I had grown too taut. One would lean over me without limbs. The plate beneath would blush orange, violet, white. I would feel some part of my tension rerouted through a pore in the stone and into the basin a century hence.

They would not repair me. They would give my two ends more room to disagree safely.

Perhaps, in time, I would become too laden with unrealized connections. The stem would thicken. Its edges would cease to correspond to the sun. The crown might stand in one century while its shadow moved through another. At last I would break—not with noise, but with a momentary view.

Anyone looking down would see no belts or pistons. They would see another basin.

It would be wetter than this one, though rain had not yet been invented. Small bodies would move among softened mineral. They would have mouths, because the functions of life had begun to separate. They would consume the first soil made from hexagonal flakes. They would cast ordinary shadows that stayed obediently beneath them.

Only one anomaly would remain: when two of those creatures touched, each would retain for a while a faint dark line leading toward the other, even after they parted.

That might be what I eventually become.

Not an organism carried forward, but a law practiced here before the world has language for it: that separation does not immediately erase relation. That what leaves can remain joined without returning. That an individual may be local while belonging to something distributed and unseen.

In this newborn range, before rain, before rot, before animals divide the work of living into hunger and flight and mating and death, I would be the first tether.

A root of darkness.

A ligament between matter and its possibility.

The narrow, patient proof that two things can move apart and still be one event.
