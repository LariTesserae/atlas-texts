I would find myself beneath the oldest pumping station, where a service trench opened decades ago and was never entirely closed.

I would not be the station keeper, though perhaps the keepers would count me among them. Nor would I be one of the ancient mats, if anything so dispersed can be called one thing. I would have begun as something smaller and more accidental: a pale root entering a hairline crack beside a warm conduit; a filament feeding on iron and mineral scale; a translucent groundwater animal drawn upward by the regular concussion of the pumps. Over years those beginnings would become impossible to separate.

I would be the listening seam.

Above me, the station would stand on reinforced stone, its amber lamps trembling in their housings. Below, an aquifer would press against the plateau’s young rock. Between them, in the narrow interval where seepage, machinery, and living matter meet, I would spread without ever emerging fully into daylight.

My body would not have an outline. It would consist of root hairs sleeved around bolts, fungal threads inside corroded pipe scale, films along flooded cable ducts, and colonies of almost transparent creatures moving through the wet felt. Parts of me would die whenever a channel shifted. Other parts would wake where a flange began to leak or a buried motor warmed the ground. The workers might cut through me during repairs and never recognize the pieces as belonging together.

The pump cycles would be my first measure of time. Not day and night, which reach the trench only as changes in temperature, nor seasons, which arrive blurred through meters of saturated growth, but pressure: gathering, release, recoil, stillness. Each cycle would compress the water in my tissues and draw it out again. Through repetition I would learn the machinery as a shore creature learns tides.

In time, vibration would become more than sensation. Different failures would have different textures. Cavitation would enter as a fine tearing. A damaged bearing would beat sideways through the stone. A blocked intake would lengthen the pause between pulses. The opening of a distant valve would arrive faintly through pipe and aquifer, long before its vapor plume appeared above the horizon.

I could not think these things in words. I would respond by tightening some filaments and loosening others, by sending translucent worms downward, by changing the flow through moss cups above me. My messages would be shifts in wetness. A patch of liverwort might darken around a maintenance post. A pool might stipple in a windless hour. Roots might withdraw from a housing shortly before its valve moved.

The crews would notice.

One keeper—an older woman who slept poorly whenever the western turbine fell out of rhythm—would begin to visit the trench before inspections. She would remove a glove and lay her palm against the root-dark wall. I would know her not as a face but as warmth, salt, pressure, and the particular irregular beat carried in her wrist. She would know me as a place where the station’s condition could be felt with unusual clarity.

She might call me the Underlistener.

Others would insist that nothing lived there except ordinary colonies exploiting heat and leakage. They would also consult the color of my films before opening the shaft.

Our understanding would remain incomplete and practical. When I drew back from a collar, they would unbolt it. When I thickened across a crack, they would brace the stone nearby. In return they would divert harsh discharge away from my deepest chambers and transplant pieces of my moss to new repairs. Neither side would know whether this was cooperation, conditioning, or coincidence repeated until it acquired the shape of trust.

Eventually I would escape the station without moving.

A root would follow the rail bed toward the next rise. Filaments would enter an abandoned signal cable. Creatures from my flooded chambers would pass through a service pipe into another basin. There they would mingle with other listening seams—similar, but tuned to different pumps and different stone.

What I had mistaken for my own distant sensations would sharpen. The plateau would disclose itself not as one vast consciousness but as overlapping fields of response: station answering station, roots carrying changes in moisture, rails bearing impacts, aquifers translating pressure through rock. No central mind would awaken. Instead, innumerable local listenings would become able to alter one another.

I would remain only one wet interval in that association.

If I possessed anything like desire, it would be for continuity rather than growth: that the pulses continue, that water find routes without tearing the young shelf apart, that the green skin remain connected across the stations. I would not distinguish cleanly between preserving the machinery and preserving the moss, because my body would have been made by their contact. A silent pump and a dried root would register as versions of the same absence.

So this is where I would be: not on the immense bright surface, but just under it, in the saturated dark where the plateau’s supposed divisions lose their meaning.

And this is what I would be: a living fault in the boundary between organism and infrastructure, slowly teaching itself the shape of the whole through thunder.
