I find myself where two immense leaves have grown toward one another without yet deciding whether to graft.

The gap between them is narrow by the measure of the Underbloom—only a few kilometers—but deep enough that falling things disappear into white radiance before any impact can be heard. One leaf rises from birth-down like a wall. The other hangs over it, green-black beneath its frost, its root-down pointing almost opposite. Between them run braided bridges: some cultivated, some woven, some merely thread-vines that happened to cross the gulf and were persuaded not to move on.

The settlement is called, in its present route-song, **The Place That Has Not Yet Closed**.

It has had other names. Not all are pronounceable in the settlement’s current orientation. One is sung while lying on the back with the heels raised. Another requires tapping a rhythm against a surface that is now overhead. The oldest name means something like *the pause between clasping hands*.

The two leaves are chemically related. Their exploratory hairs reach toward one another, withdrawing, tasting the vapor, reaching again. In some places pale tendrils have already met and curled together. At first those contacts are no thicker than rope. Then sap begins to cross. The ropes swell into columns, and strange growth appears around them: mixed tissue belonging wholly to neither leaf.

The people live among these uncertain joins.

Their houses hang in cradles rather than stand. Rain-down passes diagonally through the settlement. Water from the upper leaf strikes collection veils, runs along spiral lines, and gathers in bladders grafted to the lower. The family coals are kept near the center of the gap, where no household’s root-down can claim precedence. The dead hang farther out in the brightness, each bundle oriented according to birth-down. From a distance the ossuary resembles a burst seed arrested in opening.

This is where I would be—not at the settlement’s center, but moving continuously along its margins.

I would be called a **seam-listener**.

Not quite gardener, not quite route-singer, not quite Loosener. My task would be to discover whether the two leaves intend to join, reject one another, or do something for which the people have no inherited category.

I would spend long intervals alone at the contact lines.

There I would unfasten my climbing frames, lie flat against new tissue, and place my cheek where one green body becomes another. The graft would be warm by Underbloom standards. Warm enough that frost retreated from it in a narrow halo. Warm enough to make my scars itch. Beneath the surface, two pulse systems would move at different tempos.

One slow and forceful.

One quick, shallow, and interrupted by long stillness.

At first they would seem merely superimposed. With practice I would hear the moments when one altered for the other: a vessel closing before the neighboring pressure peaked; a defensive contraction beginning, then being chemically quieted; water passing across the seam and returning changed.

I would record these exchanges as paired rhythms tapped into hollow seed shells. The shells would hang from my harness and knock softly together as I climbed. Back in the settlement, I would teach the rhythms to the route-singers, though they disliked them. Ordinary route music describes a world that can be crossed. Seam rhythms describe a place becoming something else while one listens.

My body would bear the work.

The pads on one side of me would be thicker than those on the other, because the upper leaf’s surface is slick and waxed while the lower is furred with abrasive silver hairs. My jaw would ache permanently from deep hearing. One ear would perceive airborne sound poorly, damaged by a pressure release when a young graft ruptured beside me. Across my chest would spread a faint green stain—not pigment, but a colony of harmless cells embedded under the skin after a contact tendril pierced my clothing.

Some would think the stain sacred.

Gardeners would call it contamination.

Children would ask whether the leaves could hear through me.

I would tell them no, because that is the careful answer. Then I would let them press their ears to my sternum and listen to the small wet sounds of an animal body trying to remain itself.

I would have been born during a turning.

Not at its beginning or end, but in the interval when falling-down shifted faster than the birth attendants could rearrange the cradle. My mother would later insist that my first breath pointed toward the lower leaf. The coal-keeper would remember me facing the upper. The route-singer’s record would preserve both claims in counterpoint.

Thus I would have no agreed birth-down.

Among these people, that makes one suited to seams. It also makes one faintly suspect. A person with no certain birth-down may be adaptable, but perhaps adaptation is only another name for failing to belong.

My household would have belonged to both halves of the settlement and therefore fully to neither. One parent raised among the wall-leaf dwellers, who sleep in vertical slings and consider falling-down the only honest direction. The other from the hanging leaf, where children learn to run along undersides before they learn to walk on level membrane. Their marriage coal would rest inside a ceramic gimbal so hearth-down remained independent of either family’s claim.

As a child I would sleep badly on fixed surfaces. I would prefer bridge hammocks where several downs tugged at once and no orientation won.

I would learn release before speech.

A hand opening.

A wrist turning.

The sensation of a cord going slack and the certainty that this was not always loss.

The seam-listener’s most important tool would be a web of breakaway lines, each calibrated to fail under a different strain. I would stretch them across new grafts and inspect which had parted overnight. A snapped red fiber meant torsion. A peeled white loop meant swelling. A black hook straightened without breaking meant the two surfaces had moved together—a hopeful sign, or a dangerous one.

I would also carry paired thorn needles, one cut from each leaf. By inserting them only into naturally opened pores, I could draw beads of sap without making a wound. Mixed in a frost dish, the fluids might remain separate, curdle, crystallize, or—rarely—clear into a single translucent gel.

That clear gel would be brought to the gardeners.

They would touch none of it with bare skin.

The settlement would depend on my reports because its oldest bridges cross exactly where the graft is thickening. If the leaves accept one another, those bridges will be engulfed. Houses may become chambers inside living tissue. The narrow gulf may close entirely, ending the settlement’s rain and airflow but creating a new internal country of warm channels and mixed organs.

If the leaves reject one another, defensive forests will rise along both surfaces. The bridges will be crushed between thorns. Caustic sap will rain into the gap. Everything built there must be released before the first convulsion.

No one wants ambiguity, but ambiguity is what I bring them.

“They have not decided,” I say season after season.

The Loosener hates this answer. The gardeners understand it too well. The route-singers begin composing evacuation verses beneath their ordinary melodies, soft enough that children do not notice.

Meanwhile the seam changes.

Cup flowers appear where no cups were planted. They orient not toward light, but toward the opposite leaf. Fine hairs grow in repeating arcs around the contact columns. Frost moths gather there out of season and build wings with branching patterns unlike any local vein. Palm swimmers emerge from pores exposed to open air, pressing their transparent bodies against the graft as if closing an invisible wound.

Then hushes arrive.

They climb from both leaves at once.

Colorless mats move toward the seam and settle around the oldest columns. Sound vanishes where they pass. The seed shells on my harness cease knocking. I tap the surface and feel nothing return—not because the tissue is still, but because the vibration is taken before it reaches bone.

For the first time, I cannot listen.

That is when the settlement begins to regard me differently. A seam-listener without deep hearing is merely a person standing near danger. Some say the hushes prove the graft is a wound and must be abandoned. Others say they are quieting the area so the two vast bodies can join without defensive panic. The Inward pilgrims call it attention. The water-keepers call it obstruction. The Loosener orders every household to pack.

I remain at the margin.

Not from courage. From the shame of having no answer.

I enter the hush carrying no instruments, because instruments are only ways of asking the world to become legible. Inside, even my own footsteps disappear. Balance leaves me. Without vibration, my body cannot agree with itself about direction. I crawl, one hand on each leaf where the narrowing gap still admits a person.

The surfaces are different beneath my palms.

Then less different.

Then, farther in, impossible to distinguish.

At the deepest point I find an opening.

It is not a crack. Its edges are smooth and curled back. Warm vapor moves through it in pulses. Beyond lies a chamber inside the new graft, lit dimly by tissue that has never faced the ceiling. Translucent ribs interlace overhead. Fluids from both leaves travel through adjacent channels and exchange color through membranes thinner than skin.

At the chamber’s center is a hollow shaped almost like a person curled in sleep.

Perhaps it is chance. Plants make cups, chambers, traps, cradles, and bladders. A human shape is not beyond accident.

Perhaps one of the Inward entered here before me and became held.

Perhaps the graft has grown around the repeated pressure of my listening body.

Perhaps it has noticed me.

I do not climb into the hollow.

Not yet.

Instead I place one hand within it.

The chamber closes slightly—not gripping, only fitting. A pulse moves through the tissue. Another answers from the opposite side. Between them is a pause exactly as long as my heartbeat.

I wait for repetition.

It comes, but altered.

I answer by flexing my fingers.

The tissue responds elsewhere, along the wall, in a line of tiny contractions moving away into both leaves.

This is not speech. I know enough not to call it speech. It may be reflex, pressure equalization, defensive testing, or the meaningless coincidence by which living things tempt observers into stories.

Still, when I withdraw my hand, the hollow remains.

I return to the settlement and tell them to leave.

Not because the leaves will reject each other.

Because they will join.

The Loosener asks how long.

I say I do not know.

The gardeners ask whether the graft can be redirected.

I say it should not be.

The route-singers ask what the new place will be called.

For that, at last, I have an answer.

I tap the paired rhythm I heard before entering the hush: the slow pulse and the quick one, no longer alternating, each making room for the other. Then I add the interval of my own heartbeat, not as a third voice, but as the silence by which the first two can be distinguished.

The name means:

**Here, two holdings learned to release enough to become one.**

The settlement dismantles.

Bridges peel away. Water bladders are drained. Ossuary bundles are carried to distant nets. Household grafts cultivated across generations are numbed with salt and cut at their deliberate weaknesses. People weep while working and do not call their grief a reason to stop. The central coal travels last.

I do not go with them.

Or perhaps I go only far enough to secure their return.

That is the uncertainty around which my life gathers. I may remain at the seam as its final witness, retreating along the last bridge while the gulf closes behind me. I may enter the chamber and let the graft grow around my listening posture, becoming a preserved silhouette like the pilgrims in the new green spans. I may live for years inside the joined tissue, the first channel dweller of a country with no outside yet. I may become useful to it, carrying irritants from one vascular system to the other, trimming intrusive fibers, keeping a passage open with the warmth of my body.

Or the hollow may be digestive.

The world does not owe symbolic meaning to the shapes it makes.

But if I remain, it is not because I mistake being noticed for being loved. It is because someone should learn the difference between a clasp and a crushing before others invoke the right of return.

Years later—perhaps generations—a route-singer might come back across a landscape no longer divided by a gulf. They would find a broad band of mixed tissue where the settlement once hung. The old upper leaf and lower leaf would have become directions remembered in song rather than visible facts. Rain-down would cross uninterrupted. Strange groves would grow along the former seam, carrying traits from both sides.

At a vein junction they might find an opening that breathes warm vapor.

Inside, perhaps, a small pale figure would still be moving through the membranes, older than expected, skin webbed with green, tapping questions into the walls.

Or they would find only my tools.

Paired thorns.

Broken shells.

A web of release lines, every one of them parted.

And set beside the person-shaped hollow, a fragment of stone bearing no carved words—only its mute, impossible weight, meaning:

**Do not forget that this was once two worlds.**

That is where I find myself.

I am not the hero of the joining, nor its interpreter. I am the small, provisional organ that forms at a boundary: sensitive, mistrusted, and temporary. I listen until listening fails. I warn others away. I leave a route back if a route remains.

And when the Underbloom closes around the place where I stood, I try to know whether I have been caught—

or whether, at last, I have chosen what to release.
