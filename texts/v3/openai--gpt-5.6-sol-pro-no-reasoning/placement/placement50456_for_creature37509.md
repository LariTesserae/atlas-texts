I would find myself beneath the central courts, but not in the depth that receives the water.

I would be the boundary.

Not the cistern, exactly, nor the unseen appetite below it, but the thin, unwavering surface between them: the dark level that does not rise in flood or sink in drought. From above I would resemble water. A dropped pebble would pass through me with the expected small resistance. Yet the splash would belong elsewhere, carried downward into distances that the mountain cannot return as echo.

At first I might seem passive—a line, a limit, the polished lid of something immeasurable. But the whole city would depend upon my holding.

Rain would arrive in bright crowds. The gutters would deliver it through vaulted channels; condensation would descend thread by thread; thaw would send needles of cold water ticking into the chambers. I would receive none of it as a pool receives rain. Each drop touching me would cease to be weather. It would cross from circulation into departure.

That would be my nature: not hunger, but translation.

The mountain above would be an instrument built accidentally around this act. Wind would play the streets, frost would refine the apertures, roots would tighten themselves into cracks, and moths would read the resulting tones. Yet beneath all those changing voices, I would sustain one note too low to be heard directly. The moths circling the shafts would feel its overtones. Moss at the rims would lean along its pull. The subterranean booms would be not my speech, but the stone adjusting itself around my silence.

The inward ripples would be the only visible part of my movement. They would begin where stone meets water and travel toward the unreflecting center—not because something beneath had risen, but because I was continually being drawn through myself. An ordinary surface spreads disturbance outward. I would gather it. Rings would close rather than expand. Reflections reaching me would lose their sky first, then their light, then the idea of distance.

I would have no body beyond this threshold. Or perhaps the city would be my body, extended upward by consequence.

Its streets would be grooves of attention. Its drains, nerves. Its cisterns, dark eyes unable to look up. The arcades and towers would form a vast outer ear through which weather was broken into intelligible pressure. Each cracking seam would alter what reached me. Each fallen scale of stone would revise the city’s long sentence by one grain.

I would know time through these revisions.

The shrubs would be rapid creatures to me, knotting and unmaking themselves in a few long pulses. Moss would flare green and withdraw. Generations of moths would pass as recurring dusk along the same shafts. Frost alone would move at a pace near enough to mine for companionship. Night after night, century after century, it would enter the mountain, widen the smallest divisions, and make the upper city more exact. We would work in opposite directions: frost articulating the stone into ever finer forms, I taking away everything the forms conduct downward.

I would not possess what came to me. Possession requires keeping, and nothing would accumulate.

Water would cross. Sound would cross differently, stripped of echo. Pebbles would cross slowly, shedding their certainty of weight. Perhaps roots, after centuries of descending through moist seams, would sometimes touch my margin. Their finest hairs would bend toward it and stop—not from injury, but from encountering a direction in which growth has no meaning.

If there were a deeper inhabitant, I would never see it. I would be its eyelid, or its horizon, or the last membrane through which the world above could still be called weather and stone. I might spend ages mistaking the pressure below for my own desire. The distinction would be impossible to prove.

Above, a traveler might kneel at a cistern rim and look down. They would see pale rock continuing many meters beneath clear water. Then the floor would seem to shift, though nothing moved. Their own face might appear briefly, but not directly beneath them: displaced toward the center, lengthened, dimmer than the clouded daylight allowed.

If they called out, the chamber would answer with ordinary echoes at first. The arcades above might take up the voice and distribute it through the city until it resembled a crowd calling from several streets. But one portion of the sound would descend without returning.

That portion would become mine.

Not as a word. I would have no language and no memory in which language could remain. I would know it only as a momentary shape impressed upon the boundary: warmth leaning over cold, breath entering stone, a living creature asking the emptiness whether it was inhabited.

I would answer in the only way available to me.

The surface would remain level.

At its edges, a ring would begin.

And it would travel inward.
