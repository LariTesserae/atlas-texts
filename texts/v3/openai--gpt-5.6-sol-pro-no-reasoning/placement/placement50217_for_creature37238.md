I would not be one of the listeners, though as a child I would imagine that fate for myself.

I would be found higher on the bell’s inner slope, where the inhabited ledges grow narrow and the pale porous stone begins: above the kilns, below the chimney mouths, in a district whose dwellings were built for perhaps two hundred people and now contain twenty-three. From below, the place would appear abandoned. Its stairs would climb past sealed doors and roofless chambers. Bridges would connect ledges where no one had lived for generations. Only during intake would collecting cloths flower from the balconies, showing that anyone remained.

My household would occupy the former vestibule of a much larger house. The inner rooms would be shuttered and inspected once each turning of the long pressure record. Seven ancestor tiles would be fitted beside the air slit. An eighth, carried from a dwelling lost to a crack, would rest in a padded niche because no opening had yet been judged safe enough to receive it.

I would be the district’s keeper of returning things.

This would be an old office with a misleading name. I would maintain ladders, ropeways, counterweighted sledges, and the marked lines by which chimney crews find their way home when the descending air fills with salt dust. Anything sent upward—cloth, basket, climber, measuring vane—would pass through my hands twice: once going out, once, if fortune and workmanship held, coming back.

The work would make me both rigger and archivist. Every rope would have a history. I would know which vine strain formed its core, who braided it, how many strong intakes it had endured, where hot dust had glazed its fibers, and whose weight it had arrested. I would not trust a rope merely because it looked whole. I would bend it beside my ear and listen for the dry inner crackle that meant the core had begun to salt.

Before an ascent, I would wrap a climber’s line around my waist and lean backward with all my strength while two others struck the rope with smooth stone batons. By its note and the vibration through my hips, I would decide whether it could be used. A false judgment would not always kill someone. That would make the responsibility worse. Sometimes a damaged line would hold through one more climb, and no one would know that my mistake had been forgiven by chance.

I would have the body of the place: heavy thighs, a broad back, red seams across my palms. My left shoulder would sit lower from years of checking loads. Salt scars would speckle the skin around my eyes where a surface veil had once split. I would own no finely dyed mourning cloth. Instead, I would keep one length of exceptionally soft gray fiber, undecorated, which had belonged to an elder sister who climbed too far from a chimney stake.

Nothing of her body would have been recovered.

That absence would trouble the household in a way death ordinarily did not. There would be no powder, no hollow tile, no surface into which she could be fired. Her name might be spoken and her room remembered, but she would not divide safe air from dangerous air. The family would regard her as unfinished—not alive, not properly dead, but still outside.

Her climbing line would have returned.

I would keep a section of it hidden in a sealed jar, though all failed ropes were supposed to be burned or dissolved. I would tell myself that I preserved it to discover what had gone wrong. Over the years I would test its twist, scrape its wax, examine its salt crystals under glazed lenses, and find no fault. That would become worse than finding one.

It would leave me with a private suspicion: perhaps she had cut herself free.

To most of the city, the surface was an exposure to be endured for work. To my sister, it had been something else. She had spoken of the open brightness without disgust. She said the lack of an opposite wall did not make her fall upward. It made distance feel newly honest. She believed the marks in the salt were tracks, though she could not say what had made them. Once, using fragments of fired clay, she constructed a little impossible life: four hinged limbs around a hollow body, with a mouth underneath and eyes on movable stalks. Our family destroyed it. I secretly kept one leg.

Because of her, I would be mistrusted by some and consulted by others. Surface workers would bring me things they did not wish to enter into the official stores: a translucent husk that might be mineral; paired depressions found beyond the nearest stakes; a black strand that curled when warmed and did not resemble any cavern fiber. Usually I would identify them as cracked glaze, pressure marks, or burnt vine. Occasionally I would be unable to.

I would not become convinced that animals existed. Conviction would be too simple. I would become skilled at leaving a question structurally sound.

That would be my deeper craft: not ropes, but uncertainty under tension.

I would live with a timekeeper named Sevet, whose hands were too fine for quarry work and permanently stained by the red and black powders of pressure records. We would understand each other perfectly in weather and less well in calm. Her signs would be spare, exact, almost severe. Mine would sprawl, full of qualifications. During the city’s loudest exhalations, we could settle household accounts, report damage, apologize, and make love without a word. In a cancellation chamber, hearing one another breathe, we would become shy and quarrelsome.

She would believe that all apparently irregular events belonged to rhythms too long for one life to perceive. The knocks beneath the Quiet Floor, chimney failures, rare light shafts, my sister’s disappearance: each could be part of a pattern hidden in generations of traces.

I would ask what difference there was between an intention and a pattern whose scale prevented us from understanding it.

She would touch two knuckles to her brow: *I understand, though I cannot hear you.*

We would have a child who hated heights but loved empty buildings. During calm intervals the child would slip into sealed districts and return with obsolete door pins, fragments of patterned glaze, and stories of hearing someone walking beyond walls. I would reprimand them for inventing alarms. Yet I would also inspect every place they named.

One inspection would change my life.

Far above our district, behind a bricked aperture where an old road ended, I would find a narrow chamber built inside the bell’s shell. It would contain no furniture, no ancestor tiles, no garden scars. Only a row of stone stakes driven into the floor and, attached to them, loops of rope turned pale with age.

The ropes would lead into the wall.

Not through holes or channels. Into unbroken basalt, as though the stone had cooled around them. When I pulled one, something beyond the wall would pull back—not immediately, but after the delay of a signal traveling a great distance.

I would assume at first that the motion came from pressure. I would test it during intake, exhalation, and stillness. I would weight the loop and mark its drift. I would ask Sevet to compare it with her records. The answering tension would obey no weather we knew.

It would, however, repeat.

Three short pulls. A pause. One long pull.

The pattern would recur every few cycles, then vanish for months.

We would tell no council. Not at first. The chamber would be condemned, the ropes cut, the wall quarried open by force. Perhaps that was the correct response. A city survived by refusing certain curiosities. We would instead record the pulls privately, I in knots and Sevet in dust traces. Our child would call the thing on the other end “the returner.”

In time I would learn to answer.

Not because I believed a person waited beyond the basalt. The rope might be caught in some deep fissure. It might connect, by a fold of space near the Quiet Floor, to another part of the city or another century. It might be moved by a garden colony rooted where no gardener had gone. It might be one of the bell’s own slow instruments.

But a rope under tension asks something of the hands.

I would answer with the oldest test signal of my profession: two short pulls to ask whether a line bears weight, then a sustained pull meaning *hold*.

For many cycles, nothing would change.

Then, during a deep exhalation when every tower sounded and speech was crushed from the air, the hidden rope would draw taut enough to bend its stone stake.

It would pull me toward the wall.

Through the rope I would feel—not a hand, exactly, because I would have no concept broad enough for the limb of an unknown living thing—but adjustment. Grip. Deliberate resistance. Something testing my weight as I had tested a thousand climbers before sending them upward.

My first thought would be of my sister.

My second would be the professional knowledge that whoever held the other end did not know how to test a line safely.

I would plant my feet. I would signal for Sevet and our child to stand back. Dust would shake loose from the wall, moving against the weather. The old fibers would creak but not break.

Then I would feel the answering pattern:

Two short pulls.

A sustained pull.

Not an echo. The intervals would be changed slightly, corrected for the length of the rope.

*The line bears.*

*Hold.*

That is where I would find myself: not at the center, where the world is thin, nor on the surface, where it has no edge, but high in an abandoned chamber with both hands around a rope that enters solid stone.

I would be the one obliged to decide whether to cut it.

Below me, the city would continue its practiced life. Kilns would brighten. Garden bladders would swell. Sledges would descend under restraint. The dead would murmur in doorways. Around the Quiet Floor, listeners would lay down their hands because the knocks had abruptly ceased.

I would think of all the city’s wisdom: that safe life depends upon boundaries, that every opening must be controlled, that not all absences should be filled. I would think of my sister’s missing tile and the tracks she claimed to see. I would think of an intention without hands, a footstep not made by the walking kind.

I would not cut the rope.

Neither would I follow it.

I would brace it around a second stake, pad the old fibers, and sit with my back against the basalt until the weather passed. When the stone became quiet enough, I would place the line against my inner wrist, where even its smallest movement could be felt.

And whatever waited beyond the wall—person, root, bell, distance, past, or some form of life for which we had no division—I would begin teaching it the signals for load, danger, slack, return.

The first word I gave it would be *hold*.

The second would be *I am here*.
