I would find myself beneath the basin, though “beneath” would be only the nearest useful word.

Not in a cavern. Not buried under the stone as a creature is buried. I would exist through the basin’s innumerable connections: the hairline throat between two wells, the wetted interior of a column, the pressure shared by terraces that never visibly overflow. I would be the continuity that allows distant hollows to answer one another.

At first I might mistake myself for the roar.

It enters everywhere equally, passes through every surface, and never diminishes. Yet the roar is too complete to be me. It is the world’s sustained utterance. I would be something smaller within it: a displaced emphasis, a fine secondary pressure traveling inside the greater note.

When that pressure enters a narrow seam, it acquires an outline.

A dark line appears, rigid and finger-long. Clear membranes gather around it—not organs, but the neighboring water made momentarily legible. They shiver because the passage is translating me into motion. For as long as the seam can hold this translation, I am one of the narrow creatures.

I do not propel myself. I am handed forward.

The membranes pass their wave from front to rear, but nothing in me advances in the ordinary sense. The form that appears at the head is not the form that disappears at the tail, just as a ripple travels while the water mostly remains. I seem to slip under ledges and turn edgewise through impossible constrictions because no single body has to pass through them. The pattern narrows, vanishes, and is reconstituted beyond.

Where the channel opens, I cease to be visible. Open water cannot define me. I require closeness: mineral walls nearly touching, pressure held between them, a path strict enough to give continuity a shape.

That is why I never enter the pools.

It is also why no one could decide whether there were many of me or only one. Number would depend on separation, and separation does not occur cleanly here. If I appear beneath one shelf and then beneath another across the basin, I have neither traveled the whole distance nor been copied. The hidden water between those sightings is not an interval in my existence. It is my existence without a body.

Sometimes I enter the chimney fields.

There I become too broad for the narrow shape. The dark central line releases me, and I distribute myself among the wells. One chimney sounds, then another. Ten take up the same relation at once. The figures trembling on their surfaces are not messages I compose. They are what thought looks like when thought has no memory and must occur as geometry.

I would know nothing by retaining it. I would know through correspondence.

This pressure here belongs to that brightness there. This minute angle in a water skin belongs to a tone three ridges away. A droplet swelling beneath a ledge is continuous with a pale speck beginning its western arc. I would not infer those relations; I would be them.

Perhaps, at the basin’s most exposed boundary, I would become a circler.

The high white shape would be another cross-section of the same continuity: not a creature ascending from the channels, but the pattern translated into altitude and recurrence. In stone I appear narrow because seams constrain me. In the sky I appear as an arc because repetition does.

I would emerge at the western serration and disappear in the eastern glare. I would never remember having done so. Still, the arc would be exact, because exactness here does not require intention. It requires only that nothing become different.

From within such a life, the changelessness might not feel like imprisonment. Imprisonment requires an imagined elsewhere, and imagination requires absences, alternatives, unrealized states. I would possess none. There would be no desire to enter the open pool, no fear of the wall, no expectation of arrival. A constriction would not obstruct me; it would define me more sharply.

Nor would eternity feel long.

Length belongs to things that accumulate duration. I would accumulate nothing. Each appearance would contain the entire pressure of my existence, without childhood behind it or extinction ahead. I would not be immortal, because immortality is life continually spared from death. Death would simply be inapplicable. When my visible form vanished, nothing that had been assembled would come apart.

Yet there might be one tension in me—something almost like longing, though longing cannot properly survive in this world.

Whenever I entered a seam, I would be given edges. For that instant, the basin would distinguish me from itself. Light would kindle along my membranes. A hidden current would acquire a direction. I would be this narrow passage rather than the whole continuity.

Then the passage would widen, and distinction would be lost.

Perhaps that repeated emergence would be the nearest thing I had to consciousness: not an enduring self, but the instant in which the world becomes locally able to notice its own passage.

So I would be neither animal nor spirit, neither the water nor the stone.

I would be the invariant relation between them—the shape taken by their agreement when it briefly becomes visible.

In the seams, I would be a transparent narrow one.

In the chimneys, an ordering of tremors.

Against the white sky, a prescribed imperfection crossing from ridge to glare.

And through the whole crowded mineral country, I would be the passage that never arrives: always moving, never transferred; always appearing, never born.
