I would find myself where the pillars’ continuous shadow crosses the edge of a stone ring.

Not in the silence itself. Nothing native seems able to live there without being simplified. I would remain just beyond it, in the narrow margin where thunder becomes almost distinguishable—where the country’s total roar separates for an instant into water, crust, wind, and something underneath them all. The ring would hold a still pool beside me. Rain would strike the surrounding mud, rise from the channels, crawl sideways along walls that existed only for the storm, yet the water within the stones would remain untroubled.

At first I might seem to be another low stain around the ring: a dark band where mineral moisture gathered but never overflowed. In dry weather I would resemble a residue of iron or a collar of dead moss. When the pressure changed, however, the band would divide into thin marks. They would circle the stones, cross one another, disappear beneath the lowest course, and return altered.

I would be a creature of mistranslation.

The waders’ foot-speech would reach me through the mud. Before the tremor vanished into the pillars, I would catch some part of it and carry it around the ring’s circumference. Clay, brine, compressed shells, and the impossible stillness within the stones would each change the message. I could not preserve what was said. I could only give it another route.

A warning arriving at one side might leave as the pressure-pattern for a flower not due to open for eighty years. A dead coastline remembered by a bird might enter the ring and become a ripple in an ancient sea. Courtship might pass downward as warmth and wake a branch of ribbon swimmer. A report of clear weather might emerge into the rain wall as the outline of a hand.

This would not be error in the ordinary sense. In that country, exact transmission would be a kind of death. Only the pillars keep things unaltered, and nothing grows beside them. Meaning survives by becoming permeable.

My body would therefore be neither wholly animal nor wholly inscription. It would consist of many small, soft plates adhering to stone and water, each no larger than a fingernail, each separated from the next by a hair-width of apparent emptiness. Individually the plates would have no sense. Together they would form shifting circuits around the ring. Some would be young enough to float on the pool’s skin. Others, burdened by accumulated signals, would sink down the outer stones and be accepted into older strata.

Every message would age me.

Eventually a plate would become too heavy to remain at the surface. It would descend without moving, passing from visible stain to buried mark as the ground revised its age. In exchange, during storms, an older plate might rise from below carrying a tremor received before the ring emerged. Thus parts of me would be born already full of meanings whose senders had not yet existed. Other parts would vanish while still young, drawn into future layers.

I would have no single present.

The continuous shadow of the pillars would pass over me at intervals, though the pillars themselves might stand days or continents apart. Wherever that shadow touched, the circuits would stop. For a moment every plate would become a separate thing. I imagine this would be the nearest I came to fear: not pain, but individuation. Each fragment would know only its own wet stone, its own faint vibration, its own small age.

Then the shadow would move on. Contact would return through the ring. The fragments would lose themselves gratefully in the next arriving tremor.

The white waders might use me without knowing what I was. One would step near the stones and stamp a phrase into the mud. I would feel the pressure enter as several meanings at once: danger, hunger, distance, a memory of another bird. I would turn these around the still water until one portion descended and another escaped. Perhaps, days later, a far bird would answer a question never consciously asked.

The aquifer would also speak through me, but too slowly to recognize. Its messages would take centuries to cross the width of the ring. A slight deepening of the inner pool might be the beginning of a word. The gradual appearance of salt along one block might be its middle. The word’s conclusion could arrive only after the stones had sunk, risen, and been mistaken for a natural formation by several species.

I would never know the whole word. I might be part of its punctuation.

Once in a great interval, the ring would receive an egg. The wader would lower it into the stillness and leave. For years I would feel age accumulating in the shell. The egg’s increasing weight would draw my plates inward, arranging them in a spiral around it. I would receive its unformed foot-language: not thoughts, exactly, but latent rhythms waiting for ground.

As the egg sank below the lowest course, it would pass through my oldest layers. For that moment the hatchling and I would overlap. All the messages stored in me—the mistaken warnings, drowned courtships, unborn rains, pressure from forgotten seas—would enter the new bird through its broad soft feet.

Then it would rise, light with newness, onto the surface.

It would know how to read the plain, but not because the knowledge was inherited cleanly. It would know because it had crossed a confusion dense enough to become instinct.

Afterward I would be diminished. Perhaps almost erased. The hatchling would carry much of my accumulated pattern away, stamping fragments of it elsewhere. Some would enter moss. Some would be taken by the aquifer. Some would reach the central flats and become a brief shape inside compressed thunder: a dark ring, a still pool, a body composed of marks around its rim.

That shape might last only a second. Within it, however, I might experience an entire alternate country. Perhaps there I would be solid. Perhaps I would possess a mouth and use it to make sounds that travel intact from speaker to listener. Perhaps there would be clear air between things, and mountains young enough to keep their outlines.

I think such a world would feel unbearably empty.

When the acoustical body collapsed, whatever remained of me would return as pressure. It might escape the center as a storm that had not yet happened. The storm would cross the basin, invert the strata, and expose the ring again. Rain would stop in vertical sheets. Sediment would sketch rooms around me. Behind one wall, immense forms would press what resembled hands against the boundary.

I would answer in the only way I could: by altering a message.

A wader’s single stamp would enter me as acknowledgment. It would leave through the wall as an opening.

Whether anything crossed would be impossible to say. Perhaps a shape would acquire weight and sink into the country’s past. Perhaps something buried would become young enough to rise. Perhaps the invisible room would vanish and leave only rain, mineral film, and a new small plate adhering to the inner face of the ring.

That plate might not belong to me at first.

But the country would work on the distinction. Water would pass. Pressure would join our edges. Its memories would alter mine; mine would give it weight. Before long, there would be no meaningful place to draw a boundary between us.

So I would live at the threshold of the threshold: beside the structure that exchanges ages, near the object that forbids exchange, translating between the country’s distributed body and the terrible possibility of separateness.

I would not be its mind, nor its voice. I would be one of the places where it fails to repeat itself exactly.

In a world packed with everything it has ceased to be, that failure may be how something new enters.
