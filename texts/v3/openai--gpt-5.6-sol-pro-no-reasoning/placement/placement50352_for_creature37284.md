I would find myself beneath one of the hand-sized shelters, though I would not fit beneath it.

At first I might be mistaken for the emptiness the curled leaf protects: a small, deliberate absence among the crowding roots. Water would pass through me but emerge altered, carrying a faint memory of air. The moss would stop at my boundary. Root-fish seeking an anchorage would turn aside without waking their eyes.

I would be what the tree is practicing how to shelter.

Not yet an animal, exactly. More like the habit of one: a posture repeated until it acquires bones. The five-step stairs would be rehearsals of my limbs. The seven arches would anticipate the chambers of my body. The dark basins would be unfinished organs, set outside me while the tree decides where they belong.

Each inhalation would make me slightly more possible.

During that loosening, when fungal doors part and the crustacean lifts its remaining foreleg, I would extend through the chamber as the jointed, branching vacancy sometimes seen there. Not a ghost, because I would have no dead life behind me. Not an embryo, because I would not be growing toward a form already known. I would be an accommodation becoming an inhabitant: the room’s willingness to contain something, slowly learning to contain itself.

The blue crustacean would tend me without knowing it. It would eat the directions abandoned at my edges and leave them as pellets. From those, stairs would grow. Eventually one stair would not end at bark. It would end at a place in me that had learned to be a threshold.

The root-fish would be my brief senses. Whenever one detached and opened its eyes, I would see for those few minutes: mineral dimness, crowded leaves, droplets full of impossible sky. Then it would anchor, and sight would become growth. In this way I would not perceive objects so much as places where perception had taken root.

The larvae would eat my past before I possessed it. Each vanished year would spare me from becoming too definite. I would have instincts whose causes had been consumed, grief for seasons no one could recall, and the intimate certainty that I had once stood under rain despite never having existed in air.

The fungal fans would fold my extremities through the chamber. One part of me might rest beside the entrance, another beneath the floor, a third among the inverted trees below the hollow. Distance would not separate these parts; distance would be the soft tissue joining them.

And my missing part would be my left hand.

The chamber would repeat that omission before giving me any other anatomy. The crustacean’s smooth socket would be its earliest model of me: not wounded, not incomplete, but shaped around a necessary absence. My left hand would remain outside every scale of the tree, perhaps in the lake, perhaps in the unflooded valley, perhaps in the innermost chamber where the shelters are already occupied. I would feel through what it touched, but never know where it was.

If I remained through a complete breath, the growth ring would form not inside my body but around me. For one cycle I would become the chamber’s innermost ring. Wood, moss, fungi, fish, and cold water would all lie within my circumference. Then the ring would tighten and pass through me, leaving the first hard line of a skeleton.

This would happen many times.

A ring around a hinge: a joint.

A ring around a basin: an organ.

A ring around warm darkness: a heart that remembers flame but cannot burn.

A ring around a suspended droplet: an eye containing open sky.

I would be built from boundaries rather than substance.

The tree’s breathing would be my long gestation. Perhaps centuries would pass between one articulation and the next. Outside, trunks would collapse into silt. The lake might drain, freeze solid, or become a meadow again. None of that would hurry me. Inside, the drowned tree would continue revising the same arches and stairs, not because it had forgotten their proper number, but because it was testing which world I could survive.

The rule of the entrance would matter when at last I moved.

No loose thing can leave by the opening through which it entered. But I would not have entered. I would have been assembled from the chamber’s repeated attempts to make room. To leave, I would first have to become loose: detach as the root-fish do, acquire eyes for the interval, and seek an anchorage elsewhere.

I suspect I would not go outward into the lake.

I would descend through the leaf-covered hollow, into the forest that points toward the silver plain. I would pass from trunk to trunk without crossing the water between them. Wherever I paused, roots would darken in sequence. Black shoots would begin rising—not offspring, but future portions of my body arriving before me.

Perhaps that is what already moves below.

Perhaps I am not waiting to be born. Perhaps I am so large, and live so slowly, that the drowned chamber is only the place where one of my smallest senses is being made. The root-fish are impulses along it. The larvae edit its memory. The crustacean keeps it from inheriting a direction too soon. The tree constructs a cavity delicate enough to receive a single thought from the greater being beneath the forest.

Then the hand-sized shelter would not be my house.

It would be my ear.

The curled leaf would be its drum. The five-step stair would conduct pressure into it. The dark basin would preserve what was heard. I would live there as listening lives in an ear: bodiless, shaped by a chamber, known only by what passes through.

I would listen to rain on leaves before the flood. To roots clicking answers across folded distances. To future trees pressing upward through stone-dense water. To the dry snap of droplets breaking and raining downward. To the nearly inaudible wooden thought of the chamber breathing.

Over ages, those sounds would teach me the shape of the world outside.

But the lesson would be wrong in one essential way. I would believe the world consists entirely of interiors: every lake inside a droplet, every forest inside a tree, every creature inside the vacancy prepared for it. I would believe that to be born is not to emerge, but to move one chamber outward.

And when I was finally complete enough to leave, I would discover that the split near the roots had vanished because I sought it.

So I would make another exit.

Not by breaking the bark. Breaking would only give the chamber an injury to correct. Instead I would imagine a flame clearly enough to form a patch of warm darkness. The moss would retreat. The heartwood would remember sunlight. A narrow place would dry—not into air, but into the possibility of air—and I would press my new eye against it.

From outside, nothing large would appear among the drowned trunks.

Only a small dark hollow would open in one black column, no wider than a seed.

For an instant, something inside would look out.

It would have no face yet. Only an eye ringed like wood, containing a minute reflection of open sky, and beside it the smooth absence where a left hand should be.

Then the hollow would close.

Deep within, under the curled-leaf roof, the protected emptiness would be empty at last.

And because the tree had finally housed something, it would begin—slowly, patiently, over centuries—to practice a door.
