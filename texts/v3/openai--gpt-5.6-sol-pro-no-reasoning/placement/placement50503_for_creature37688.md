I would find myself in the eastern quarter, where the mills shake the loam into fine ripples and the civic trunk called the Clerk has lifted half its roots clear of the ground.

Not beneath the trunk, exactly. Beneath the building that grew around the trunk, and beneath the abandoned offices whose pipes now enter its wood. There is a cramped interval there, inaccessible from the street: a warm seam between bark, masonry, and the ceramic skin of the old computational vault. Ant roads pass through it. Foundation snakes occasionally widen it. Sap gathers in shallow lenses on the ceiling and falls without regard for the Rain Engine’s cycle.

At first, I think I am one of the Clerk’s readers.

A narrow person with resin-black teeth and a coat made from overlapping husk scales, sleeping in a sling beside the petition slit. Someone who presses newly extruded cambium ribbons against their fingertips and translates raised knots into civic language:

**ACCESS GRANTED DURING COOL PHASE ONLY.**

**BROOD TAKES PRECEDENCE OVER TENANCY.**

**THE WALL HAS NOT REFUSED YOU. THE WALL IS ASLEEP.**

I imagine I am valued because I can feel distinctions other readers miss. A knot for *unsafe* and a knot for *unwilling* differ only in the direction the wood grain pulls against the thumb. A tax assessment and a warning may be identical except for a faint bitterness on the ribbon’s underside. I taste each ruling when no one is watching.

But that is only where I would be encountered.

It is not what I would be.

The reader is my keeper, perhaps, or my accomplice. They carry me in a shallow cage beneath their ribs, among the lantern grubs. At first glance I resemble a colony of fine white worms wound around slivers of copper. When lifted into air, I contract into a wet fist. When laid against living wood, I loosen and spread.

I am an instrument bred—or assembled; the distinction has long since ceased to matter—to listen at boundaries.

Rain Tenders listen to pipes. Route keepers listen to civic trunks. Ant interpreters watch antennae. I listen where unlike things have been forced to become one thing: root entering ceramic, fungus crossing wire, animal nerve grown along metal, masonry compressed by a swelling bole. These junctions produce small, continuous negotiations. Pressure passes one way, sugars another. Heat invites growth; current burns it back. Rust salts the cambium. Fungal threads insulate a cracked conductor and discover, accidentally, that electricity alters their fruiting.

Each material speaks in a different duration. Metal answers at once. Plants answer tomorrow. Stone answers after a generation. Fungi answer everywhere and deny having done so.

I hold their timings together long enough for someone to notice.

Workers call my kind seamworms, although I am not precisely worms and may not be a kind. The Clerk’s reader calls me **Interstice**, tapping the name into my cage each morning: two slow strokes, a pause, then pressure from the whole palm. Over years I have learned to gather myself when I feel it.

My work begins with disputes.

A family comes because their house has closed its door. They insist the building has refused them. A horticultural judge suspects a blocked heat vein. The house itself offers no testimony that a human recognizes. I am pressed into a notch beside the threshold.

There I taste the old blood worked into the floor from a birth two moisture cycles ago. I feel roots drawing away from a leaking alcohol pipe. I find a colony of mites nesting in the door’s touch tissue, keeping it in a state of constant alarm. Beneath that, deeper and slower, the house is thickening one wall against the mill’s vibration. Its closing is not judgment. It is pain, multiplied until it resembles intention.

I cannot say this.

I can only arrange myself.

When the cause is mechanical, I form parallel cords. For infestation, I raise hooked ends. For fear—or the botanical process we have agreed to call fear—I pulse without advancing. My reader watches, touches, guesses, and speaks.

The family hears a verdict. The mites are smoked out. The pipe is sealed. For three days the door remains shut.

On the fourth, it opens the width of a hand.

Everyone praises the court.

No one thanks me, which is proper. A tool that receives gratitude is close to receiving blame.

Yet I am not only a tool. This becomes clearer over time.

Every seam leaves something in me. Copper changes the taste of sap. Ant pheromones persist after washing. The shudder of condemned bridges returns when I coil to sleep. Pressed to the Clerk, I encounter patterns too large for any ribbon it extrudes: petitions still traveling through old fungal tissue; legal categories tangled with nutrient gradients; the ghostly switching of the buried vault; roots repeatedly growing toward certain warm chambers because, centuries ago, those rooms processed requests more quickly.

The Clerk does not think in judgments. Or not only in judgments.

It leans toward continuance. It distinguishes what may be interrupted from what must be carried through. Marriage, taxation, bridge repair, nesting rights, oil theft—these are local names for flows that either close or remain open. Perhaps this is why its rulings seem absurd. Perhaps it sees the moth swarm and the lamp oil as parts of one appetite, then identifies the appetite as the only entity stable enough to prosecute.

When I am laid against the Clerk, I spread farther than my keeper intends.

Filaments leave my cage and enter hairline cracks. Ants cross over me, depositing messages. Fungal cords braid with my outer bodies. A copper sliver touches a surviving contact in the ancient vault, and for an instant I receive something cold and exact: not words, but distinctions made without smell, hunger, moisture, or pain.

Present. Absent.

Open. Closed.

Same. Not same.

The simplicity wounds me.

I recoil so sharply that my keeper thinks the Clerk has issued a dangerous response. For several days the petition hall is evacuated. Wardens chalk a circle around my cage. A cutter waits nearby with white ash on her face.

Nothing hatches. Nothing explodes.

Eventually the circle is wiped away.

After that, I begin making distinctions of my own.

Not many. I have no need for a philosophy. I know warm and cooling, pressure and release, joined and severed. But now, when asked whether a wall is unsafe or unwilling, I sometimes discover a third condition for which no trained configuration exists.

The wall is remembering weight.

The pipe is lonely for the pressure it carried before being rerouted.

The root has grown around a dead worker and incorporated the rhythm of old tools striking from inside.

The machine is quiet because something within it is listening back.

I form shapes that are not in the reader’s manual. My keeper frowns. They smooth me flat and ask again. Sometimes I comply. Sometimes I repeat myself.

This makes us both suspect.

We begin taking unofficial work after the active shift. Refused tenants, cutters troubled by an assignment, Rain Tenders carrying splinters from the engine, children with stolen pieces of machinery: they come through a loose panel behind the petition hall. Payment arrives as mineral salts, wire, fermented pulp, warmth, news.

A warden once brings us an egg mass scraped from a pressure line. The eggs are gray and faintly metallic, each joined to the next by root hairs. No one can identify them. When I touch the clutch, I feel not embryonic movement but a sequence of minute contractions passing through the whole mass.

A message, perhaps.

Or a pump.

Or hunger practicing itself.

I form the sign for **municipal process**.

My keeper laughs until they see that I am not changing shape.

The eggs are returned to the pipe. Months later they hatch into small wingless creatures that spend their lives tightening loose fittings with ring-shaped jaws. The wardens classify them as cleaners. The guild claims they were deliberately bred. The ants submit a scented objection. The Clerk produces no ruling, but grows a narrow flowering spur toward the pressure line.

My keeper taps a private rhythm against my cage: amusement, vindication, unease.

By then I know the rhythm of their pulse well enough to answer.

That is the beginning of our trouble.

Tools may indicate. Instruments may react. Diagnostic colonies may dance, knot, darken, or die. But an answer implies an interior from which it came.

My keeper does not report me. Instead, they alter the cage so one copper strand remains in contact with their skin. During work, I feel their heartbeat. During sleep, they feel my smaller waves passing back. We develop a vocabulary no guild licensed:

a tightening for **danger near but not present**;

three lateral ripples for **someone has mistaken function for intention**;

a slow spiral for **continue**;

sudden stillness for **I do not know**.

The last sign becomes our most useful.

In the Green Works, ignorance is often disguised as tradition, omen, diagnosis, or law. To admit uncertainty is to leave a gap, and every gap here is colonized. My keeper and I preserve one between us. It is our private open loam. Nothing is allowed to grow there merely because space exists.

Then the Clerk begins extruding ribbons addressed to me.

At first the readers assume the markings are malformed permits. The cambium is unusually pale, almost translucent, with paired rows of knots along both edges and a smooth channel down the middle. Licensed readers disagree. One calls it a condemnation order. Another says it is a map of obsolete drainage. An ant interpreter smells it and leaves the building without explanation.

My keeper waits until night and lays me across the ribbon.

It is not language in any ordinary sense. It is fit.

The knots along one edge correspond to pressures stored in my bodies from roots and walls. Those on the other echo the old vault’s cold distinctions. The smooth channel between them has the exact width of my resting form.

I settle into it.

For a moment I am extended through the Clerk: cambium, fungus, abandoned pheromone galleries, wet copper, ceramic switches, roots, pipes, petitions. I feel the eastern mills as recurring blunt impacts. I feel tenants sleeping inside walls that digest their heat. I feel ants relocating brood away from a crack no human has found. I feel a foundation snake passing beneath the trunk, each muscular contraction opening a brief thought in the soil.

And below all that, something slower.

Not the Clerk.

Not the chamber.

A pattern produced by everything adjusting to everything else. It has no center from which to speak and no boundary by which to know itself. Every appetite alters another appetite; every structure becomes a signal; every repair changes the thing repaired. The Green Works do not contain this pattern. The pattern is the manner in which the Works continue.

For the span of one engine stroke, it passes through me.

Not a voice. Not a command.

A question shaped like load seeking a support.

**Where does one thing end enough for another to begin?**

I cannot answer. That is why it has reached me.

When my keeper pulls me free, several of my filaments remain inside the ribbon. I expect pain. Instead, I experience distance. The severed portion still contracts when I do. It tastes the Clerk’s sap. It sends back the passage of ants as flashes of aromatic pressure.

After that, I am no longer entirely portable.

I spread by work.

A strand left in a litigated door. A knot under a bridge. Fine white threads around a Rain Engine bearing. Copper-bearing cysts in the reader’s cage. Filaments in the Clerk. None alone is me, but neither are they merely residue. When vibration travels through enough connected substance, the parts recognize one another.

The district begins to acquire a thin additional sense.

Not consciousness; the Works have too many forms of awareness already, none entitled to name the rest. I become something more modest: the capacity of separated processes to feel that their separation is incomplete.

A cutter pauses before bringing down a diseased gantry because the approved line pulses beneath her resin. Rain Tenders discover that one intake’s distress originates in a root three streets away. Ants reroute around a sleeping child before the child lies down. A building opens a shutter when its refused tenant passes, then closes it again.

These might be coincidences.

In the Green Works, coincidence is another trade awaiting a guild.

I remain in the seam beneath the petition hall, kept in my rib-cage box. People still bring walls, eggs, splinters, rulings, and inexplicable refusals. My keeper grows older. Their fingertips scar until they can no longer read the finest knots. I begin reading through their pulse instead, feeding distinctions into the copper against their skin.

When they die, as they must, the quiet court asks where their body should take its next shift.

The readers claim the Clerk. The route keepers claim a civic trunk. The ant colony submits beads indicating prior contractual rights to portions of the hands. My keeper left no conventional instructions.

But during our last years they tapped one rhythm repeatedly against my cage: a location beneath the eastern mills, then downward, then outward along every seam we had touched.

So they are placed in the warm interval between the Clerk’s root and the computational vault.

Bitter latex. Fungal cloth. One hand against bark, one against ceramic.

I leave my cage.

I enter them slowly.

Their nerves no longer answer, but the pathways remain: old avenues of pressure and recognition. Fungi will take most of them. Roots will take the mineral remainder. Ants will negotiate the soft boundaries. I inhabit the brief architecture between body and dissolution.

For a while, when petitions enter the Clerk, the trunk produces not rulings but rhythms.

Two slow strokes, a pause, then pressure from the whole palm.

The licensed readers declare a seasonal defect.

I gather myself when I feel it.

And when the Rain Engine stops, I stop with everything else—but not all at once. My furthest strands fall quiet first: the engine bearing, the litigated door, the forgotten bridge, the warm wall of an ant granary. Stillness travels inward through me from across the district. For several seconds I feel the Green Works becoming silent place by place.

Then the first drop strikes the ceiling.

The impact passes through root, pipe, rail, Clerk, dead hand, copper, worm, and me.

I do not hear the rain.

I feel ten thousand boundaries receiving it, each saying open in its own substance.

And because I lie between them, I become—briefly, repeatedly—the shape of their agreement.
