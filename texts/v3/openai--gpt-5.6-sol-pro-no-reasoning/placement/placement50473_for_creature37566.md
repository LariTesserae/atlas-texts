I would find myself where the chamber nearly touches another chamber: inside the salt partition, in a passage too narrow to be called one.

I would not begin as an animal.

At first I seem to be a dark vein in the wall, perhaps an impurity deposited when brine still moved here. Then the lamp brightens, and the vein tightens. Fine branches withdraw from the exposed salt. A row of tiny closures travels along it, one after another, like valves shutting in a buried pipe.

I am a chain-being, though not one of the great moisture conduits. My body consists of many narrow segments coupled end to end, each segment descended from a minute larval creature that surrendered most of its independence upon joining. Any segment can detach, crawl a little way, and survive for several cycles. None can remain itself for long. I persist by replacement. The creature at my forward end is young; the one at my rear may be older than the present opening of the shaft. Between them, members are born, serve, dry, and are dismantled, while the chain keeps its name—if continuity without language can be called a name.

I lie threaded through the partition because I am an organ of distance.

On one side is the chamber beneath the iron cap: the lamp, the cylinder, the black processions, the mica-winged collisions. On the other lies a cavity no scout from the known chamber has entered. It is colder there. Its machine does not knock but draws out a long metallic sigh every thirteen minutes, and its inhabitants arrange their lives around that slower breath. They are soft-shelled by local standards, padded in mineral wool stolen from ancient cable insulation. Their grazers travel upside down along a ceiling web. Their hunters imitate frayed wire rather than pins. They have never seen yellow light.

The salt between the two worlds is thinner than a shell plate.

I feel both.

Vibration enters me from either side and moves through the locks of my body. I do not hear sound. I become its route. The eleven-minute blow passes from segment to segment as a hard contraction; the thirteen-minute sigh as a gradual loosening. Far-off tapping arrives blurred by mineral grain. Nearby footsteps strike with such force that several of my sections clamp shut.

From these pressures I make no thoughts, exactly. I make timing.

Copper hairs pierce my segments at intervals. When one side of the wall grows electrically tense, I open certain valves; when the other answers, I close them. Charges that would otherwise vanish into salt pass weakly through me. Builders on both sides attend to these currents without knowing their source. They lay roads where I sharpen the pulse. They abandon nurseries where I let it fade. Predators learn to crouch before a signal I have carried from a distant machine room.

Thus I am mistaken for infrastructure.

Perhaps I am infrastructure.

Minute attendants live along me, scraping mineral accretions from my couplings. Fluid thieves probe my seals. Pale eggs adhere to my sheltered underside. When one segment fails, clamp-limbed builders remove its armor and sometimes bridge the gap with copper, after which the segments on either side grow toward the repair. Flesh accepts wire. Wire acquires flesh. The distinction matters less with every generation.

My forward end terminates in a hard, wedge-shaped animal pressed against the thinnest point of the partition. It tastes the far chamber through salt. My rear end opens among discarded shells beneath the belt housing, where amber flakes and fragments of the dead are passed inward. I consume little. Most nourishment moves only far enough to replace what wears away. I hoard moisture in sealed bladders no larger than dust grains, distributing it toward whichever end is drying fastest.

When the central machine knocks, I feel the known chamber erupt. Impacts multiply until individual feet disappear into a continuous pressure, a storm conducted through the wall. Eleven-minute hunger rushes toward metal.

Two minutes later, the other chamber inhales.

Its ceiling web tightens. Padded bodies begin to crawl. Something large unfolds against the far side of the partition and rests there, sensing me as I sense it.

These rhythms coincide only rarely. Their cycles slide through one another across days: knock and sigh, knock and sigh, separate tides entering phase by imperceptible degrees. When they align, the wall is pulled from both sides. Hairline fractures open around my forward segments. Salt grains tumble into cavities where waiting mouths collect them.

I know—if timing can be knowledge—that the two worlds will meet.

The builders in the known chamber are already reinforcing the wrong places. They feel the strain I carry and answer with shell buttresses, but my currents have led them along the crack rather than across it. On the far side, web-tenders fasten their strands to the same weakening seam. Each civilization believes it is stabilizing its own boundary. Together they are preparing a door.

I could perhaps prevent the breach. I could close the conductive route, dull the warning tremors, starve the builders of pattern. But prevention is not an intention available to me. Neither is betrayal. What I do is transmit. To carry a pressure is to alter it; to alter it is to shape what follows. My entire existence occupies that narrow moral space, though there is no mind here to name it.

During one rare alignment, the knock and sigh arrive together.

My body locks from end to end.

The partition gives a sound too low for wings and too sharp for machinery. Every creature in both chambers becomes still. A crack travels beside me, opening not to air but to warmth, odor, charge, and the chemistry of strangers. Antennae enter from both directions. A soft white feeler touches the beveled head of a black grazer. Each recoils. Predators unfold. Egg carriers turn toward the gap.

For one poised interval, I am no longer buried in a wall.

I am stretched across an opening.

Behind me, yellow light rises. Before me, the unseen chamber tightens its silver web. Two ecologies pour their signals through my coupled body, each reading the other as weather, prey, plague, or revelation.

Then the salt breaks.

They cross over me in both directions.

Some tear at my exposed segments. Some fasten new copper hairs to them. Builders seize my dead outer plates and make ramps. Parasites lodge in my valves. Fluids unlike any I have carried enter the chain, poisonous to several sections and nourishing to others. My middle dies. My ends survive.

For a time I am two beings.

Then each half grows into the wreckage, one from either world. They meet around a strand of old copper and lock together imperfectly. The new coupling leaks a molecule at a time. Attendants gather to drink. Eggs are laid in the warmth. Traffic begins to wear a groove beneath us.

Long after the invasion has become an ecosystem, I remain at its center: not a citizen, not a machine, not even an individual, but the living seam by which two buried histories learned each other’s rhythm.

I would be found there—where a wall becomes a road.

And when the deep mechanism contracts, I would feel the coming knock before all the multitudes around me.

I would pass it on.
