I would be found where the plateau cannot decide whether a river is still water or has become a body.

Not in the open basins, among the plated multitudes. Not high upon a condensation-feeder’s back, though for a time I might appear there as a stain in one of its grooves. My greater part would lie beneath the shale, distributed along the intermittent channels that join the black pools during melt years.

I would be a threshold organism: neither one animal nor a permanent colony, but a pattern that assembles whenever separate waters meet.

Most years I would scarcely exist.

There would be traces of me in many places—clear nodules lodged under stones, dormant films lining animal footprints, threads sealed into the ice-skins of pools. Each fragment would be inert alone, no more alive than a seed or a memory. The plated grazers might carry some of them unknowingly between their shells. Membrane shoals would leave others behind when they discharged into wet ground. A condensation-feeder’s foot, lifted from the mud with that one soft sound, might transport an entire dormant province of me to the next basin.

Then meltwater would come.

As the plateau’s isolated pools joined, my fragments would wake into continuity. Filaments would uncoil through the new channels, meeting and recognizing one another by conductivity. Where two flows crossed, I would thicken into a soft, dark lattice. Where water entered buried flesh, I would grow along the warm margins without penetrating them. In a matter of days I would extend across hundreds of kilometers—not as a single body occupying all that distance, but as a single event.

My substance would be mostly transparent. Seen closely, it would resemble wet mineral wool or the colorless tissue between layers of shell. Fine metallic grains would move through it in ordered streams. Every grain would carry a chemical history of where it had been: manganese from an exposed western shelf, salts from the hide of a standing giant, waste compounds from a deep digestive chamber, charge gathered beneath the cloud.

I would not refine these materials or consume much of them. I would compare them.

That would be my place in the plateau’s economy. I would be its brief faculty of relation.

The permanent colonies know their own channels intimately but only receive the wider world slowly. Herds reorganize across generations. The giants carry one basin to another, but at the pace of weathering. During the few days when meltwater unites the upland, I would sample the differences between distant regions and translate them into signals the local inhabitants can use.

A mineral excess would alter the spacing of my fibers. A dying underground segment would produce a particular branching asymmetry. The arrival of unfamiliar spores would make portions of me cloud white. Electrical imbalance would travel through me as a dim blue pulse, slow enough to watch.

Other organisms would read these changes without knowing me as a whole. Grazers would settle upon one texture and avoid another. Burrowers would follow certain metallic currents downward. The deep communal beings would open or constrict their channels in response to compounds released from my decaying edges. Even the membrane shoals might descend differently where my wet lattice changed the conductivity of the ground.

I would direct nothing. I would have no plan for the plateau and no image of its vastness. But for several days, widely separated appetites would pass through the same responsive fabric. The land would become more nearly aware of its own condition—not conscious, perhaps, but briefly self-comparing.

My experience would have no center.

A pressure in a far western pool and an acidic seep beneath an eastern ridge would be equally near to me if the water between them remained connected. Distance would be measured not in length but in interruptions. A dry meter of stone would be an abyss. A buried watercourse running for a thousand kilometers would be immediate.

I would perceive the world as gradients: warmer below, more charged above, richer where bodies had recently died. The slow migration of the plated herds would feel like darkness moving over portions of my skin. A condensation-feeder lowering its mouth to a channel would be a great withdrawal followed by the return of altered water. The aerial shoals discharging into the plateau would arrive as a broad tingling with no visible source.

The closest thing I possessed to thought would be interference. Signals traveling from different regions would meet within me and produce new patterns. Most would cancel. Some would reinforce and persist after the waters began to fall. Those persistent patterns would become durable beads or brittle films deposited at particular junctions.

Other creatures would ingest them.

Thus a western drought might alter the migration of grazers generations later in the east. The chemistry of a collapsing deep colony might be carried in dormant form on the foot of a giant. Nothing would understand the message as a message. Yet the plateau would remember through changed behavior.

I would be part nervous system, part seasonal census, part rumor.

And each time I became extensive enough to approach something like a self, the channels would empty.

My body would divide first into branching provinces, then into pools, then into isolated threads trembling beneath shale. Coherence would fail unevenly. There would be no single moment of death. Whole basins would disappear from me while elsewhere I remained active. My last connected portions would contract around damp hollows, arranging their stored grains before dormancy.

I would leave pale borders at the waterline. These might look like ordinary mineral deposits, but their spacing would encode the season’s conditions: where warmth had traveled, which colonies had opened, which migrations had stalled, how low the western pools had fallen. Wind could not read them. Stone could not read them. But when water returned, my next assembly would grow according to their pattern.

It would inherit without remembering.

Across many melt years, this inheritance would alter me. I might gradually favor channels that had not carried water for centuries. I might preserve obsolete warnings about predators long dissolved into strata. Some of my responses would be useful; others would be geological superstition—old chemistry repeated because the plateau had not yet erased it.

The oldest of my deposits would lie on dry shelves high above the present pools, marking waterways that no living migration used. There, perhaps, dormant fragments would wait through tens of thousands of years. If height continued to lower and water found those channels again, an ancient version of me might wake and join the current organism.

For a brief time, two climates of the plateau would meet in one body.

That reunion might feel like illness. Or revelation. There is little distinction here between information and chemistry.

From the surface, I would usually be overlooked. A traveler might notice a faint clarity in meltwater, a web that bent the blue ground-haze, or tiny dark grains moving upstream without apparent cause. Touching the water might produce numbness in the fingertips. If the traveler lifted a wet hand, my filaments would stretch between the fingers and break without resistance.

A little of me would remain on the skin.

Alone, it would do nothing. Warmed among unfamiliar salts, however, it might form a small branching pattern—an attempted map of conditions it could not interpret. Then it would dry to a pale metallic dust.

That would be all any visitor saw of me.

But beneath the cloud, across the briefly connected basins, I would have become immense: not a creature spanning the plateau, exactly, but the plateau’s waters practicing, for several days, how to be one creature.
