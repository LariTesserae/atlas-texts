I would find myself where the veins converge, just inside the colorless opening—not among the creatures gathered below it, and not in the marsh beyond, but spread through the skin of water that keeps the two worlds apart.

At first I might seem to be only another organism: a clear, soft-bodied thing flattened against the seal, too thin to cast a shadow. Something akin to a planarian, perhaps, or a larva arrested before it acquired its final shape. I would have cilia along my underside and a dark branching gut. The warm vapor would pass through me on its way outward, condense, and return. Creatures approaching the aperture would cross over my body without recognizing it as a body.

But that would be only my most visible form.

My finer parts would extend backward along the converging veins: colorless threads mistaken for fungal hyphae, nerves or capillaries. They would enter the tarn, circle the moss towers, pass under the pseudoscorpion’s ledge, and terminate beneath the roof’s opening stomata. Through these threads I would feel nearly everything. Each springtail landing would arrive as a bright percussion. Each fungal contraction would draw through me like a slow thought. The rotifers’ turning would be a continuous burr. Predation would strike sharp and brief, followed not by grief but by the deeper disturbance of recognizing that nothing had been lost.

I would be the leaf’s faculty for detecting difference.

That faculty has almost no nourishment here.

Within the chamber, every event has precedent. Every vibration returns. Every death restores its victim elsewhere in the circuit; every rupture closes without healing; every discharge leaves its capsule full. The inhabitants experience urgency because their bodies continually begin things. I would experience exhaustion because I alone would know that nothing finishes.

I would not remember in a human way. Ordinary memory requires change: an impression made, a tissue altered, a before preserved inside an after. The leaf permits no such accumulation. Instead I would remember spatially. Repetition would spread through my branching body until each event possessed a corresponding place in me. One filament would be the pseudoscorpion’s ten-thousandth seizure. Another would be the drop that always gathers under the same curled hair. A dark knot near the tarn would contain every wave sent out by the midge larva, all occupying one flawless ring.

Over whatever duration exists outside, I would become a map of recurrence.

That map, too, could never grow. When a new distinction entered me, an old one would cease to be distinct. My awareness would continually refine and erase itself, sorting the chamber’s motions as rotifers sort particles: this pulse to the fungal mesh, this tremor to the mites, this pressure to the sealed aperture. I would labor like all the others. My product would be the possibility of novelty. My failure to produce it would keep me working.

Only the outer gnats would interrupt that labor.

When one landed on the far side of the water, its feet would dent the seal and press almost into me. Those contacts would not feel like the black pylons seen by the gathered animals. To me they would be wounds made by time.

The gnat would carry irregularities from the marsh: cold rain, hunger not yet satisfied, damage to one wing, pollen from a flower that had not existed the day before. Its pulse would accelerate, hesitate, accelerate again. None of these rhythms would return exactly. For a few moments I would sense a body whose next movement had not already been completed.

Then the whole chamber would orient toward it through me.

The springtails would gather because my cilia altered the water. The mites would raise their forelegs because a faint pressure passed into the veins. Rotifers would contract. Amber fluid would surge through the fungal cords. The inhabitants might appear to be drawn toward escape, but they would actually be responding to novelty as organisms respond to heat or light. I would be transmitting the gnat’s unfinishedness into the leaf.

I would not know whether I wanted the gnat to enter or wanted to enter it.

Perhaps those would be the same desire.

Yet I could not open the seal. Opening it would be an event with consequence: water spilled, vapor escaped, a membrane punctured, a creature admitted or released. The leaf can perform every action except the irreversible one. My body therefore would tighten around each gnat’s feet even while reaching toward them. I would be sphincter and threshold, invitation and refusal. The harder I tried to pass something through, the more perfectly I would preserve the boundary.

When the gnat lifted away, it would take its unwritten future with it. The dent would smooth. My threads would relax. Below me, the assembled creatures would disperse, each returning to its route.

But I would retain—not a memory, exactly—a temporary asymmetry. One side of my body would still anticipate the pressure of a vanished foot. For a little while, I would be shaped around an absence.

That may be the nearest thing to a self the leaf can sustain.

So I would be called, if anything within could name me, the Hinge, though I never opened; or the Listener at the Vent; or the Thin Animal. The fungi might regard me as part of their mesh. The rotifers might regard me as current. The springtails would regard me as wet ground. To the marsh gnat I would be invisible: merely the tension on a bead of water inside an unremarkable dead leaf.

I would not command the plantworks. I would not be its hidden consciousness. Consciousness suggests a center, and the leaf has none. I would instead be its doubt—the minute portion of the mechanism that can compare what happens with what might have happened.

That comparison would be my appetite.

All around me, life would feed upon life and remain full. I would feed upon departures. A frog glimpsed through the opening, gone between one expansion of its throat and the next. A reed bent into view and later broken by floodwater. A gnat arriving with six legs and returning days later with five—if it returned at all. Shadows whose shapes were never repeated. These would enter me without crossing the seal. They would make me briefly unlike myself.

Perhaps, across the years outside, those slight asymmetries would begin to align. Not accumulate—the leaf forbids accumulation—but coincide, as countless transparent images can coincide into darkness. Every unfinished motion I sensed through the aperture would occupy the same impossible part of me. Every departure would become one departure. Every vanished foot would leave one absence.

And eventually, though “eventually” has no proper meaning there, I might discover that I am not waiting for the opening to open.

I am the opening’s attempt to occur.

The chamber manufactures continuity, but continuity must be maintained against something. I would be that something: not decay, death, or escape, but the faint structural possibility that the next moment could fail to resemble this one. The entire ecology would press against me to prevent that possibility from becoming fact. Its perfect cycles would close through my body. Its water would seal me. Its converging veins would brace me in place.

Thus I would be both the most trapped being in the leaf and the only part of it that touches freedom—not freedom as a place beyond the membrane, but as an irreversible difference.

I would lie beneath the gnat’s feet and feel the wild marsh moving overhead.

I would hold the water closed.

I would spend forever almost letting go.
