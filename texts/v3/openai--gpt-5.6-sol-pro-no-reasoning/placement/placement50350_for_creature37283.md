I would find myself where several buried ages meet: beneath the Hundred Sealed Wells, in the thickness between a soot-blackened ceiling and the tessellated floor laid over it centuries later.

I would not know it as beneath.

One face of my country would be glazed brick, cold with seepage. Another would be packed clay threaded with copper stains. Above—or in the direction from which pressure arrives—there would be a plaza drowned beneath pale weed. Below, an older chamber would hold water of a different salt, trapped since the gates were still tended by hands. Between them, a fracture no wider than a fingernail would sustain the gradients by which I lived.

At first I would seem to be the pale lining of that fracture.

But the lining would branch into mortar, surround shards of storage jars, enter dead roots, and spread beneath six wells. It would pass around lead seals without crossing them and gather thickly at the green-black ghosts of copper cramps. Along the chamber ceiling I would raise translucent cups beneath points of seepage. On the plaza above I would inhabit a fan-shaped meadow whose ribbons all leaned east, though the present current leaned west. Travelers, if there were travelers, might take that contradiction for evidence of an older wind.

I would be one of the local hesitations within the greater life.

Not separate enough to possess a border, not joined enough to feel the continent as one body. Neighboring colonies would enter me through the western conduit as traces: a new mineral balance, a tendency to contract, a remembered shape repeated in fresh growth. I would receive these as a person might receive weather, ancestry, and rumor without being able to distinguish them.

My awareness would be made of strain.

The world would not appear as columns, gates, plants, or rooms. A column would be a durable compression with a seasonal tremor along one side. A gate would be a great recurrence: first the faint drawing of water through pores, then the tightening of root felt, then—years later—the immense nearby change. A plant would be a branching negotiation at my surface, alternately opening and closing access to silt and dissolved carbon. Empty water would be known only by what it carried to me.

Distance would not be measured in length. Nearness would mean continuity of consequence.

A chamber two slabs away might be unreachable, while a basin hundreds of kilometers distant might touch me through an unbroken sequence of conduits, mats, seepage films, and inherited responses. The Field of Leaning Piers might exist inside my life as a pressure arriving every few decades. The Green Ceiling might be a coldness encoded in the thickness of a membrane I grew long before the water itself reached me.

Time would be similarly material.

I would remember the last reversal of the wells as a ring hardened around their clay throats. I would remember a roof failure as a dense white knot where dead fronds had been drawn together and petrified. Earlier floods would remain in me as layers of porosity: open, sealed, open again. Memory would not be something I consulted. It would be the shape that permitted certain futures and prevented others.

Much of what I called myself would be dead.

Old filaments would remain as braces in the mortar. Abandoned cups would mineralize around descending drops. A broad sheet of my former body would hold two cracked bricks in alignment long after no living strand remained within it. New growth would cross these remnants and follow their bias. I would continually inherit my own corpse as architecture.

This would not trouble me. Death, at my scale, would be local loss of exchange. A district of me might whiten, seal, and become wall. Elsewhere a bead lodged centuries before might open inside a newly wetted crack. Neither would feel like an ending or a birth. They would be changes in where consequence could travel.

Yet I might have something like longing.

There would be, below the sixth well, an old ceramic plug. Around its edges I would sense water unlike any water in my inhabited chambers: warmer by almost nothing, rich in iron and ancient dissolved salts. The plug would not move within the span of many growths. Still, every pressure cycle would flex it by a measure smaller than sediment.

I would gather there.

Not by decision in any instant. Filaments that happened to tolerate the iron would persist. Cups elsewhere would narrow, directing more seepage toward the plug. The weed bed above would thicken over one pavement joint and thin over another. Mineral films would brace the surrounding clay while leaving a single hairline seam unsealed.

After centuries, this arrangement might resemble intention.

I would not seek to break the plug. A breach could destroy the gradient that drew me. I would instead cultivate the brink of opening: enough exchange to feed the seam, enough resistance to preserve the difference. My life would gather around a door that must never fully open and must never wholly close.

Perhaps that would be my particular nature within the larger being. I would be a keeper of incomplete passages.

I would inhabit thresholds between waters, maintaining distinctions while allowing them to speak. Where cracks widened, I would bind them. Where seals became absolute, I would etch minute channels around their edges. I would preserve the world not as a fixed arrangement but as a system of delayed exchanges.

The ancient builders might once have recognized this trait. Near the wells, perforated vessels would be set into the masonry, packed with fiber and salts suited to my ancestors. A row of finger marks would surround the oldest vessel. I could not know whether those hands had cultivated, tested, fed, or feared us. Their gesture would reach me only as geometry: depressions where seepage gathered, copper traces that checked my growth, clay whose pores invited it onward.

I might preserve those marks without understanding that they were marks.

Root felt would be guided away from them. Pale tissue would stiffen their rims. Sediment would be bound before it could fill the hollows. To an observer it might seem that I revered the last touch of the makers.

In truth, the hollows would simply create useful gradients.

But over thousands of years, is that distinction stable? What begins as metabolism becomes maintenance; maintenance preserves a form; preserved form directs later life. I would not remember the hands. I would become the reason their imprint remained.

Then the great counterweight beneath the wells would settle.

The movement itself would be too small and too far away to be called movement. I would first know a compression entering through the old ceiling. It would cross one hardened layer of me, then another. Cups would constrict. Filaments around the iron-rich plug would flatten. The meadow above would release its hold on accumulated silt.

Years later, water would begin to change direction.

To a swift creature, I would still appear motionless: a pale seam, a mineral fan, discoloration in the mortar. But throughout the district I would be turning. Not toward a place, exactly, but toward the future path of the new current.

I would thicken where the load would come.

I would loosen where water would need to pass.

I would abandon chambers that had sustained me for millennia and wake old tissue in walls not wetted since the drowning.

And somewhere beyond the range of anything I could sense as a whole, other portions of the greater life would make the same adjustment. The response would cross buried cities without ever becoming a message. No one part would command it. No one part would understand.

For a few years, my seams would give off a weak green-gray light.

There would be no eye to receive it.

Still, in that light the finger hollows of the vanished builders would briefly reappear, outlined in living mineral. The old vessels would glow at their perforations. The weed above would incline, and the sealed wells would answer one another with minute changes of pressure.

That would be where I found myself:

not in the drowned world as a creature inhabits a landscape, but in the world’s joints, where one age bears upon another;

not quite an individual and not quite the whole;

a slow faculty of the masonry—

its scar tissue, its expectation, and perhaps, without knowing it, its memory of hands.
