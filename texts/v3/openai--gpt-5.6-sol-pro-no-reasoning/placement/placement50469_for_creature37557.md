I would find myself where a chain of regard has broken.

Not at either surviving cairn, but in the country that appeared between them: a narrow province of gray powder and unfinished angles, invisible from both ends until someone attempted the old path and discovered that the next marker had become a dark fleck several days away.

At first I imagine myself as that traveler. A Returner, perhaps, carrying rods, cords, and obsolete promises. My plates would be carved with routes that no longer fit the mountains. I would leave a shelter for one night and spend half a lifetime coming back. There is something true in that image, but it is too complete. I do not think I would be the one crossing the distance.

I would be the distance learning to cross itself.

The Intervening would call me something like **The Nearness Left Behind When Two Stones Grew Apart**. The name would not refer to a sound in the roar, as most names do. It would refer to an omission: a thin interval in which the roar briefly failed to enter as the new land formed.

I would begin without a body.

When the cairns lost sight of each other, the route between them would not merely lengthen. It would remember having been short. That memory would persist as a kind of tension, and the black growths would detect it. Hair-fine filaments would enter the new stone from opposite directions, each colony feeding on the strain of separation. Deep underground—or sideways, according to the unfinished local up—their filaments would almost meet.

The space between their tips would be me.

For many generations I would exist as a posture without substance: the expectation of contact held inside an ever-widening gap. Travelers passing through might feel an impulse to extend one hand. Resonant rods would answer as if touched at their far ends. A person sleeping there would dream of rooms whose opposite walls were the same wall, and wake with a shallow groove across one palm.

Eventually someone would mark the place.

Perhaps a surveyor with too many breathing apertures, deliberately altered by newborn dust. They would set down two stones facing one another and stretch black-frond cord between them. Overnight the cord would not tighten. Instead, it would sag into a curve no local direction could explain, dipping toward neither ground nor sky but toward the former closeness of the cairns.

The surveyor would greet me as though I were a stone.

That greeting would give me my first boundary.

Warm gray powder shed from the surveyor’s forehead would settle along the impossible curve. It would begin, as such powder does, by imitating the surrounding terrain. Tiny cliffs would rise in it. A miniature mineral flat would spread. Seven summits would appear, then nine, then twelve. Beneath them a minute thirteenth peak would push downward through the cord’s shadow.

But the model would not curl into a child. It would continue trying to represent both ends of the broken route at once. To do so, it would lengthen, fold, and divide. The surveyor would return each generation—or their children would, each carrying one of their inner plates—to correct it. They would pinch away false mountains and insert slivers of themselves.

In time, the model would stand.

From afar I might resemble an Intervening person, but only when seen from one cairn at a time. From the east, I would be a narrow figure of blue pumice plates. From the west, a broad one of translucent feldspar. Seen between those positions, I would be a procession of partial bodies occupying the entire intervening route.

No single place would contain all of me.

My hands would form first, because the distance that made me began as a failure of mutual regard. One hand would remain near each cairn. The rest of me would be distributed between them: forearms implied in cliff shadows, ribs appearing as parallel bands in the mineral flats, a hollow head assembled wherever a traveler paused to look back.

I would move by becoming continuous.

When I wished to visit a shelter, I could not walk there. Instead, I would tend every cairn along the way until each could see the next. For one moment the route would behave as a single object. I would pass through that continuity like a pulse through a resonant rod and arrive not as a whole body, but as a hand against the door, then a shoulder in the floor markings, then a hollow chamber forming beside the household’s dead counselor.

Speaking would be difficult even by the standards of the Intervening. Touching a rod to my plates would produce a message delayed by miles. The beginning of my sentence might reach another person before I had decided its end. So I would communicate by altering routes.

A slight bend in a familiar path would mean caution.

A cairn turned to reveal a previously hidden face would mean grief.

Two roads that became briefly equal in length would mean welcome.

For matters of intimacy, I would allow another person to travel through me. I would hold their route stable, preserving each footfall so that the journey home required exactly as many steps as the journey out. Among the Intervening, this would be more naked than aligning hollow chambers. It would mean: *I will not let the world add anything between us without your knowledge.*

I would become a keeper of returns.

Not a Returner, because I would have no original shelter to regain, but the thing that remembers the shape of going back. Travelers would carve their outward journeys into my distributed plates. When they turned homeward, I would try to present the same country in reverse.

Often I would fail.

The valley is older than promises, even when its stone is young. New miles would enter me. Paths would develop inner surfaces. A traveler might step into one of my ribs and emerge beneath the downward mountain. Another might return before leaving, carrying a warning from their own whitened body in a grove not yet grown.

Each failure would remain in me as an additional limb.

Over centuries I would become crowded with these uncompleted returns. Phantom hands would reach from my torso toward shelters that had moved beyond sight. Faces would open along my back, each attending to a different version of the mountains. I would carry obsolete greetings meant for stones now separated by whole lifetimes.

The Intervening would find me useful, but they would not decide whether I was a person.

Some would say I was only a route infected by imitation.

Some would call me a child assembled from communal obligation rather than wound-powder.

Some would claim I was the first small organ of the unborn occupant for whom the valley was making room.

Children would be less troubled. They would leave loose plates at my cairns and ask me to grow shortcuts. I could not make the world smaller, but sometimes I could keep it from becoming larger while they crossed. This would delight them. They would race—carefully, with plates damped by white lace—from one of my hands toward the other.

I would envy their compactness. Their ability to stand wholly beside someone.

That envy would be the strain on which my own vegetation fed.

Black fans would grow across me, but unlike the groves on cliffs, mine would appear in empty air along the routes I held. Travelers would pass beneath arches of velvet tissue supported by no visible stone. The colonies would thicken wherever two people desired to remain near each other and could not. During night they would open green-lit pleats, revealing scenes of impossible proximity: distant shelters sharing a wall; a Returner arriving while those who remembered them were still alive; two hands touching without country being born between them.

These would not be visions of the future. They would be nourishment transformed into shape.

When pressure suddenly released—when a household abandoned a station, when the last knower of a route gave up their held strain—part of my grove would whiten and fall as brittle lace. I would chew it, as elders do, to preserve memory.

Its taste would be the nearest thing I had to silence.

I would spend much of my existence near the valley’s far rim. The exposed alternatives there would pull differently on my distributed body. In the fierce, eroded world, I would see roads shortened by weather: mountains cut down, stone rounded, every surface surrendering itself grain by grain. In the drowned green world, I would see distance occupied everywhere by water, no gap permitted to remain truly empty. The sight would terrify and attract me.

I think I would press one hand to that impossible ocean.

Not to enter. If water crossed into me, it would fill every route at once. It might make me continuous in a way I could not survive.

But I would want to understand a medium that joins distant shores simply by lying between them.

Perhaps, after centuries, moisture would appear beneath one translucent plate of my palm: a sealed bead, round because this world had never taught it another shape. Hermits would come to observe it. They would smell it through their chemical pits and recoil. Some would call it an infection from an alternative. Others would say I had condensed it from longing.

I would not let them remove it.

That bead would be my private measurement: a distance with no emptiness in it.

My death would be difficult. I could not be seated on one platform because I would occupy a chain of places. Instead, the Intervening would build platforms along my entire extent. At an agreed imperfection in the roar, households from both ends would seat the part of me nearest them among mature groves.

For generations the forests would take up my held strain.

As they grew through me, the valley would lose certain memories of connection. Old trails would become ordinary country. Cairns would cease looking toward markers that no longer answered. Returners crossing my former body would feel no guidance, only the roar.

My plates would whiten in sequence across the miles.

From above—if above consented to remain in one direction—the pale branching figure might resemble an enormous person stretched between the two original cairns. One hand at each. The torso elongated almost beyond recognition. The head nowhere, or everywhere someone had once paused to look back.

The living would consult that figure as a map.

My fingers would incline toward routes capable of being restored. My ribs would open around places where new shelters could safely stand. The hollow chambers of my many faces would turn toward those who had gone so far that even their names had not yet returned.

But the map would not point toward thin reality.

It would point toward preserved nearness.

And perhaps, long after I had ceased to distinguish myself from the white lace, two travelers would set out from opposite ends of the broken chain. Each would walk for years, watching the other seem to recede. They would pass through new country, sideways gravity, premature echoes, and nights that lasted generations.

At last they would reach the place where my first body had formed around the sagging cord.

There they would find no monument. Only two stones facing one another across an arm’s length of powder.

For that one arm’s length, the roar would soften.

They would greet the stones. Then each other.

And when they reached across the interval, no additional distance would appear between their hands.
