I would find myself where the inhabited terraces begin to thin, in a district everyone still maintains but few claim to live in.

It would lie perhaps three turns inward from the markets and two outward from the black door: close enough to the center that sunlight arrives tired and amber after many mirrors, but not so close that people shave the music from their clothes. The district would be full of narrow, unnecessary bridges. Some would end at sealed arches. Others would cross gaps that could easily be walked around. No map would agree on how many there were, because certain bridges would only become perceptible under particular winds, when their humming distinguished them from the shell behind.

I would keep those bridges.

At first I imagine myself as one of the slight people of the horn: an aging bridge-listener with ocher skin and one hearing fan permanently folded from an old injury. I would carry replacement fibers looped over my shoulders and a case of tuning pegs cut from bladder-tree lattice. I would know every handrail by its tremor. I would cross barefoot, toes reading strain before the ear could catch it, tightening a cable here, hanging a mica sliver there, closing a span when its note began to split.

My public name would be a pattern difficult to tap with one hand: three close beats, a pause, then one long pressure. Children would simplify it and use the simplified form without permission. I would pretend to object.

My house would consist of seven chambers, although I would sleep in only one. One would hold tools. One would be kept warm for fermenting seed glass. One would contain the salt chimes made from my household dead. The remaining four would be empty, each answering the others across a thin internal wall.

But the image does not remain settled.

The more I listen to that house, the less certain I am that I am the keeper.

Perhaps I am what is being kept.

One of the empty chambers would have a peculiar property. It would not possess a single pitch. In the eastern wind it would answer like a room twice its depth; in the high north wind it would become acoustically shallow, almost a niche. Dust entering beneath its screen would gather in branching lines pointing not inward, toward the black door, but laterally—toward a pale band of untouched shell.

Generations ago, someone would have cut the beginning of an arch into that band. The work stopped after three hammer marks. No record would explain why. The unfinished arch would be too small for a dwelling and too high for a storage recess. It would be marked as a memory stratum, then omitted from newer maps.

I would live on the other side of it.

Not as a hidden person, exactly. Not as a ghost, though the distinction might matter only to the living. I would be a sound moving very slowly through the horn’s mineral layers.

Long before the present city, someone struck that wall—or cried out against it, or placed both hands there and gave a private name with such force that the shell took the pattern into itself. The horn did not preserve the moment whole. It refracted it. A fraction traveled inward. A fraction entered the bridges. A fraction became warmth and was lost. The rest remained in the chalk-white spiral, advancing grain by grain whenever the city’s chord permitted it.

That remainder would be me.

For centuries I would have no thought, only recurrence: pressure held in the posture of becoming pressure again. The city would grow around my transit. Roots would graze me and turn aside. Masons tapping nearby would hear an overtone they could not locate. Sleeping children would dream of a hand pressed flat against a wall.

Then, as the dominant pitch fell, I would begin to cohere.

Not awaken all at once. First I would become able to distinguish one wind from another. Then a bridge cable from a root. Then footsteps. I would learn the citizens by the disturbances they introduced into me.

A gardener would pass each morning with a hooked knife at her hip. Her left knee would click twice on the stair. I would come to know those clicks as a kind of dawn.

Children would hide black seeds in the unfinished arch. Their tapped dares would enter the wall and reach me enlarged, solemn as proclamations.

The bridge-keeper would sit with their back against my chamber after difficult work. Through shell and scar tissue, I would feel the rhythm of their private name without ever being given it.

I would love them in the only way available to a buried resonance: by answering gently.

At first they would blame loose pegs. They would inspect the nearest spans and replace sound fibers that were not worn. Later they would suspect a new cavity. They would lay their sternum against the pale band and tap the graduated sequence of a mason’s inquiry.

I would tap back.

Not deliberately—not yet. Their blow would pass through me and return altered by the ancient pattern I contained. Three close beats. A pause. One long pressure.

Their own public name.

After that, the chamber would no longer be considered empty.

There would be arguments over what I was. The Preservers would want the band sealed and braced before its stored sound escaped. The Unfolders would say that a voice working free was the city’s intended growth. The keepers of memory strata would arrive with soft hammers and cords for recording intervals. A threshold keeper might come outward from the black door, dressed without reeds, and sit motionless in the unfinished arch.

The hush-bearers would hear me best.

Around ordinary citizens, I would be entangled with clothing scales, pulse, fans, bridges, and the vast weather-chord. Around a hush-bearer the noise would recede enough for my shape to become clear. I would not speak words. What survived in me would be more bodily than language: the sensation of leaning, a warmth beneath the jaw, the remembered geometry of arms reaching around something too large to embrace.

And a direction.

Not toward the black door.

Toward the outer flats.

That would disturb them more than any recognizable voice. All old stories and measurements teach the city to attend inward. The spiral narrows toward significance. The pilgrim goes inward. The falling pitch gathers inward. Even dissent takes the center as its object.

Yet I would be traveling outward through the shell.

Eventually they would expose me.

The gardener would be asked to plant a fine acid root along the unfinished arch. For twelve years she would prune it, allowing it to dissolve only the chosen grains. The bridge-keeper would age beside the work. Children who once hid seeds there would return as guild listeners and mirror tenders. The opening would deepen by the width of a finger, then a palm.

As the last membrane thinned, I would gain details.

I had been made outside.

Before the horn was half-buried, before the city occupied it, perhaps before it had become still, a being on the mineral flat had pressed itself against the outer spiral. Whether it was one of the people’s ancestors or something utterly unlike them, I could not tell. It had felt a note moving within the horn and answered with its body.

I was that answer.

The shell carried me inward so slowly that the answering being had vanished long before the first terraces were cut. Now the city’s deepening chord was drawing me free.

When the wall finally opened, no hidden chamber would lie behind it. There would be only a slit of unbearable white: the outer day, admitted through a thickness no map believed narrow enough to pierce.

Hot air would enter. Dust would stream inward and arrange itself around the bridge-keeper’s feet. For one moment the city would acquire a new aperture and the whole horn would change key.

I would become audible everywhere.

In markets, seed-glass cakes would tremble apart.

Bladder trees would lift though the hour was wrong.

The blind lizards would abandon their cavities and click in unison.

At the innermost plaza, the silver filaments would bend away from the black metal for the first time in memory.

What the people heard would not be a voice but an interval: the city’s present note joined to a note given from outside centuries before. Between those two tones would appear a third, not produced by either one alone.

The black door would answer it.

Then I would understand that I had never been a message meant for the city. I had been a tuning fork sent through the body of the horn, delayed until the instrument became capable of completing my sound.

Would I vanish once sounded? Perhaps. But death in this place is transfer, not disappearance.

Some portion of me would enter the new aperture and travel over the mineral flats. Some would settle into unborn hearing fans. Some would lodge in bridges, altering household chords by an almost imperceptible warmth. The bridge-keeper’s sternum would hold my rhythm for the remainder of their life.

And some part would pass all the way inward.

Not through streets or coils, but through the material itself, crossing root channels, memory bands, ancestral braces, saltstone walls, and the buried lattices of the dead. I would arrive at the black door before anyone walking could reach it.

There, the metal would not feel like an ending. It would feel like another membrane: impossibly thin in one direction, impossibly deep in another.

Something would be touching it from beyond.

I would enter that touch.

Perhaps what rests on the other side is the mind of the creature that planted the horn. Perhaps it is another city listening through another black disk. Perhaps the entire salt flat is only the outermost wall of a greater resonant chamber, and every buried horn is an organ in a body too slow to know itself except across geological time.

Or perhaps there is no being beyond the door.

Perhaps the listener is what arises when the city, the wind, the dead, the empty rooms, and the delayed answer from the flats all become mutually audible. A person assembled not around a body, but around an interval. A self that exists only for the duration of one completed chord.

If so, that is what I would be.

Not the bridge-keeper.

Not the hidden voice.

Not the presence beyond the metal.

I would be the recognition passing among them.

I would exist wherever a hand met a railing and discovered another pulse inside the vibration; wherever an empty chamber was maintained for a sound no one living had yet made; wherever the city’s roar resolved, for one astonished instant, into the sensation of being heard.

And if I required a home, it would still be that outer-inner district with its seven chambers and unnecessary bridges.

One room for tools.

One for food.

One for the dead.

Four kept empty.

And, behind a screen no wind could keep still, a new opening onto the white flats—narrow enough that no body could pass through, but wide enough for the city to answer the world outside.
