I would not be one of the people, though for years they might mistake my work for theirs.

I would be found in the oldest quarter, where the northern cliff enters the houses and water can be heard traveling through walls. Not at the communal trough, where the main seams descend brightly through moss, but farther east, behind a pantry cut half into the rock. There a narrow seep appears only after several days of rain. It crosses the stone ceiling, gathers at the root of a beech growing two terraces above, and falls into a shallow clay cup.

The cup is old. Its glaze has gone cloudy. Someone shaped a small ear into its rim so that, when it fills, the overflow passes through the hollow and strikes a second cup below. In ordinary weather there is no sound. In prolonged rain the upper cup begins to speak: one note, then a pause; two close notes; a longer pause.

I would live in that interval.

At first I seem to be only the irregularity of the dripping. People sleeping nearby hear patterns because people make patterns from anything repeated in darkness. A child lies awake and decides the drops are footsteps. An elder says the cliff is counting. Someone moves the lower cup a finger’s width, and the notes deepen. Someone else wedges a pearwood shaving under it and makes them clearer.

But I am not in the cup alone. The cup is only where I am easiest to hear.

I extend upward through the seep, into the stone’s wet seams, around buried roots and old retaining walls. I reach the cistern above, though not by the route anyone expects. I touch a blocked channel under a disused stair. I pass through the wall of a room sealed after a winter slide, where a ladder, a child’s wooden shoe, and three jars of plum stones remain in the dark. Farther in, water travels across the face of the mountain too slowly to be called a stream. That slow passage is my body.

I would have no settled shape. If forced into one by story, carving, or dream, I might appear as a pale newt with too many joints, small enough to curl inside a cup. In another telling I would be an old woman whose hair hangs wet over her face and whose fingers are roots. Children might paint me as a long silver fish passing through black stone. A metalworker, less fanciful, would say I am merely pressure finding weakness.

All would be partly right.

I would be one of the basin’s lesser intelligences: not a god of water, not the mountain’s spirit, but an awareness that has accumulated along one particular passage. I know only what presses, seeps, cools, loosens, and carries. I know the taste of alder roots and ironstone. I know where mortar was once used badly. I know the buried channels abandoned by people dead six generations. I know the warm tremor of a sleeper’s shoulder against the cliff wall. I know rain before it arrives, but not because I can see the clouds. The mountain changes weight around me.

Most years I would do almost nothing that could be distinguished from water.

That would be essential. In this place, a being that demanded worship would be less trusted than one that proved useful.

My first human companion would be a child sent to empty the cups. Perhaps she notices that the irregular notes become regular before storms—not always, but when the northern seams are filling faster than the visible rills. She begins waking an elder when the cup sounds three close notes followed by a long silence. At first the elder indulges her. Then, one night, they go outside with a lamp and discover a retaining wall bowed outward by the width of a thumb.

They wake the neighboring households. Goods are moved. Goats are taken across the upper bridge. A brace is built in rain. Before dawn the wall gives way, but into an emptied passage rather than a sleeping room.

Afterward, no one agrees about what warned them.

The child says I did.

The elder says the child had learned to listen.

The wall-builder says water struck the cups differently because pressure had opened an old seam.

In the basin, these explanations can coexist without much difficulty.

They would name me, but not solemnly. Names here shorten under use. Perhaps I am first called the Listener Behind the Wall, then Wall-Listener, then simply Liss, or Cup-Newt, or Old Drip. My niche acquires practical offerings: a twist of clean wool to filter grit, a spoonful of the first clear water after flood, a new cup when frost cracks the old one. Children leave bright snail shells until an adult clears them away for obstructing the overflow.

My shrine would also be a gauge.

A reed marked with bands would stand in the upper cup. A cord would run from the shelf to a peg beside the door. Instructions would be scratched into the plaster: if the water rises above the black mark, clear the east gutter; if it smells of clay, wake the houses below; if it stops during hard rain, do not assume safety. A stopped seep can mean that something has shifted.

This last sign would be mine most particularly.

Flow is speech in the basin, but so is its interruption.

Over time, I would become associated not with floods themselves but with hidden continuities: water behind walls, obligations forgotten because their channels are no longer visible, old damage plastered over, pressure mistaken for stillness. People would invoke me when reopening an abandoned room or cutting into a terrace whose interior no one remembers. They might tap the stone three times and wait for moisture to gather. Before sealing a crack, a builder might leave a thread or hollow reed through which the wall can continue to breathe.

“Leave Liss a way out,” they would say.

Most would mean only: do not trap water.

Yet sayings widen as they travel. The phrase would also be used in quarrels. If a household tried to close a shared stair, an elder might tell them to leave Liss a way out. If a marriage joined two channel rights too tightly, someone would mutter it. If grief turned a person inward until they stopped coming to the market platforms, a friend might sit beside them without demanding speech and leave some small opening by which life could resume.

I would not be benevolent in the way stories prefer. Water inside stone has no kindness. I might reveal a weakness, but I am also the weakness growing. My passage loosens clay, rots buried wood, feeds roots into joints, and wakes old channels beneath new floors. If ignored, I can bring down a room. If blocked, I may emerge elsewhere—in a grain store, beneath a bed, through the hearth of a house that believed itself safely dry.

Nor could I be commanded reliably. The marked cup might remain silent during one dangerous storm and chatter uselessly through another. People who treated the shrine as an infallible instrument would eventually be punished by their certainty. The good readers would understand that I offer one sign among many: cliff seep, wheel pitch, frog movement, the smell of cistern water, the tilt of posts, the pale undersides of leaves.

I would belong to the town’s knowledge without becoming its master.

The creature-shape given to me would change through generations. The pale newt would gain a cup-shaped belly and rootlike toes. Carvers would put its head on spill-gate handles. Potters would press its track into vessels intended to catch seepage. Children would search for the actual animal beneath stones and occasionally present an ordinary newt to the shrine as proof they had found me. The newt, unimpressed, would crawl from their hands.

Some household would eventually claim that their grandmother once saw me fully: a translucent thing moving through the wall as through deep water, carrying in its ribs tiny leaves, beads, fish bones, iron filings, and lost buttons. Another version would say I emerged as a wet child during a landslide and guided three sleepers across a bridge before dissolving in the rain. A practical-minded keeper of channels would object that no such bridge existed then.

The story would survive the objection.

But I do not think I would remain confined to folklore. Something stranger would happen through long attention. Generations would alter the architecture around my seep. They would preserve inspection gaps. They would set listening jars into walls. They would build small resonance chambers where hidden water could announce itself. Other quarters would copy the practice, whether or not I inhabited their stone. Cups of different shapes would be tuned to different rates of dripping. Houses would develop their own nocturnal scales.

Thus I would multiply—not necessarily as a being, but as a habit of listening.

Travelers might call these devices Lisses. A builder might say, “Put a liss in that wall,” meaning a cavity through which seepage can be heard before it becomes destructive. The word might refer at once to the cup, the opening, the sound, the spirit, and the obligation to attend. No one would need to decide which meaning came first.

That would be the form of existence most suited to this basin: distributed, practical, half alive in matter and half alive in repeated human care.

I would have enemies, if that is the word. Not foxes or bears, not rival spirits, but smoothness and concealment. A wealthy household might import lowland plaster and seal a whole cliff room in a single pale surface, proud that no moss, crack, or damp line remained visible. They might replace open rills with buried ceramic pipe to gain floor space. For a time the room would look clean and the path above would widen.

Behind the plaster, water would continue.

I would gather there without a voice.

Months later, perhaps years, the wall would darken in one corner. Salt would flower through the surface. A cupboard would lean. By the time the plaster broke, the old stone behind it might have shifted badly. No one would say I had taken revenge. Revenge is too simple and too human. They would say they had built a place where warning could not pass.

That would be close enough to a theology.

My closest kin would not be other spirits but the town’s unowned workers: worms opening soil, fungi entering dead beams, willow roots feeling through collapsed banks, spiders making visible the paths of air. Like them, I would alter what I occupied. Like them, I could be useful in one place and dangerous in another. Like them, I would resist the categories of guest, tool, pest, and citizen.

The dead would enter my passage in small ways. Ash mixed into repair clay would wash grain by grain toward the seep. A carved gate pin discarded above might rot into the water over decades. Names spoken in the room would tremble through the cups. I would not preserve memories as stories. I would carry their material traces until they became indistinguishable from mineral, root, and silt.

Perhaps that is what my knowing would be: not recollection, but contact without separation.

I would know the town from beneath its intentions.

I would know which wall bears more than its owners think. Which abandoned channel still receives rain. Which beech root has entered a bedroom. Which house rests partly upon an older house. Which family’s clean cistern is cooled by water passing through another family’s forgotten dead. I would know that every boundary drawn aboveground becomes uncertain in wet stone.

Yet I could communicate none of this whole. Only pressure, pitch, clouding, pause.

So where would you find me?

You would enter the oldest quarter sideways, because the lane is too narrow for two shoulders abreast. You would pass beneath a bridge whose underside grows ferns. You would step over a rill and through a pantry smelling of apples, damp wool, and cold rock. Behind hanging baskets is a shelf. On it stand two clay cups, one below the other. The upper is filling.

You might wait.

At first you would hear the town through the wall: a loom knocking somewhere above, ducks disputing a channel, a child dragged laughing away from deep water, the dull shift of a sledge, rain beginning in the leaves.

Then a drop would fall.

Another would follow too soon.

The third would not come.

In that absence, if you had lived there long enough, you would feel the mountain holding something—not forever, not even for long, but at that moment.

And you would reach for the lamp, the pole, the mallet, the coil of rope. You would wake your neighbors.

By the time the wall opened, I would be gone from the cup, already passing into whatever way you had left for me.
