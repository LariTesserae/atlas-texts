I would find myself not standing in the hollow, but distributed beneath it.

At first I might mistake myself for one of the black mats exposed in a seam: a folded patch of tissue holding fast to the clay, neither leaf nor skin, made dark to accept the almost-light. But that would be only the portion of me nearest the surface. Follow it downward and I would continue as a root no thicker than fiber, then divide, disappear into dust, and emerge many paces away beneath a bronze stalk. What seemed like distance would still be body.

I would have no single place from which I perceived the basin. I would know it as differences: warmth traveling slowly through dense stone; dryness tightening an exposed fold; the minute relief of moisture gathering where two mineral plates meet. The clay’s soft ticks would reach me not as sounds but as changes in pressure. Darkness would not be darkness. It would be the ordinary absence in which a slight usable radiance sometimes entered my upper tissues.

Perhaps I would be the hollow’s one life, though “one” would not mean what it means to an animal. Parts of me would have ceased exchanging sustenance centuries ago, yet remain joined by dead strands. Elsewhere a swelling would partition itself and become, gradually, another being—or merely a region of me that no longer answered the rest. There would be no decisive moment. Identity would fade across the network like warmth fading through stone.

My memory would not consist of images or events. It would be structure. A season of unusual dryness would remain as a narrowed root. A favorable seam would be remembered by thickening. Injury would become a bend, patience a capsule, age a layer of fiber retained after its usefulness had almost ended. I would remember by being shaped.

The central patch would be the clearest boundary in my world. I would approach its steady warmth again and again, drawn by what it promised, then turn aside where the smooth stone offered no purchase. Over ages my body would describe that refusal: an incomplete ring around a warmth I could sense but never inhabit. If I possessed anything like mystery, it would be there—not an unknown creature or buried intelligence, only a condition that seemed hospitable from afar and proved sterile at contact.

I would not be lonely. Loneliness requires an expectation of encounter, and nothing in me would have been formed by calls, faces, pursuit, or touch freely given. Yet neither would I be insensible. My awareness, if it could be called awareness, would be an extremely slow agreement among pressures: open here, seal there, thicken below, wait. A thought might take decades to pass from one exposed mat to another. By the time it arrived, the portion that began it might already have become inert fiber.

Then a footprint might appear.

I would not understand the maker. I would know only the sudden compression of plates, the breaking of established seams, moisture briefly introduced and then withdrawn, a shape imposed faster than any shape in the hollow had reason to form. The disturbance would pass in an instant; my response might continue for generations. Roots would edge toward the altered dust. One black fold might open where the print retained a little darkness. Long after the visitor vanished, I would still be growing into the fact of their weight.

So in that world I would be neither its traveler nor its hidden guardian. I would be its scattered inhabitant: the root-body under the basin, perhaps singular, perhaps plural, with no need to decide. I would live below the threshold at which change becomes an event.

To anything arriving from a quicker world, I would look almost dead.

To the hollow, I would be time made living.
