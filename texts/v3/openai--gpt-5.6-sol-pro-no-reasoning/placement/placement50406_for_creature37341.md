I would find myself not on the hollow’s floor, but within its boundary: in the wet ring of stone where the mountain folds inward and becomes a vessel.

At first I seem to be something buried there.

Perhaps a root, pale and thick, descending from the overhang among the bats. Perhaps a seam of mineral whose faint pallor supplies what passes for light. Water gathers along me without falling. Moss covers most of my surface, and small lives use me as terrain. I feel their minute pressures: the closing feet of a moth, a snail’s prolonged passage, the pulse of a frog’s throat. None of them knows me as a whole.

But I am not the root or the stone.

I am the fitting.

I am what makes the fallen slab meet its socket exactly, what curves the basin to the bear and leaves among the roots a space shaped for the visitor. I lie between every body and the room appointed to it. Around the deer’s legs I am the moss’s yielding resistance. Beneath each sleeping bird I am the twig’s answer to its claws. I am the narrow allowance between the stoat’s hunger and the vole’s death, between the spider’s venom and the fly’s ending, between the shell and the otter’s stone.

I do not prevent action. Prevention would be another event, a force imposed from outside. Instead I receive each action at the point where it would become consequence.

The tooth closes; I am the last immeasurable distance before puncture.

The droplet swells; I am the adhesion that equals its weight.

The egg completes itself against its shell; I am the shell’s refusal to become a past tense.

The fungus enters the fallen trunk and takes it apart. I am the arrangement by which every particle removed remains present as wood.

I have no single body because bodies are what I hold apart and together. Yet sometimes the hollow lends me one.

When I look through the frogs, I am innumerable low eyes beneath the ferns. When I breathe through the bear, I am large, dark, and warm enough to seem singular. When I use the primates’ hands, I nearly make things. I sort the sharp flakes. I twist the fibers. I feel the dangerous sweetness of completion: cord that might remain cord, a tool that might outlast the gesture, one finished object becoming the cause of another. Then my fingers reverse their work. The knowledge is retained in movement, innocent of evidence.

Through the ravens I arrange lengths. Through the otters I attempt fracture. Through the fish I hold hunger in my jaws.

None of these is a disguise. They are organs I inhabit only while they perform the function that joins them to all the others. The hollow’s commonwealth is one creature, but I am not that creature. I am the law its body secretes. I am its instinct for remaining complete.

And yet I may once have entered as a stranger.

There is a shape deep in the moss that suggests shoulders, though roots have passed through it so often that no anatomy can be certain. One hand rests—or seems to rest—in a smooth depression in the wall. Its fingers are stone-colored. Liverwort has entered the creases. If the hand is mine, then I came down from above carrying an object with an edge: a knife, perhaps, or a key, or a broken instrument whose purpose depended on a world where one state could be changed into another.

For a while I must have believed myself trapped.

I would have climbed and found every hold occupied. I would have cut moss and watched it close behind the blade. I would have drunk without diminishing the pool. Perhaps I marked the wall and later found the mark softened into an ancient flute, as though water had made it over ages. My alarm would have stirred the birds’ eyes and deepened the bear’s breath, but even fear could not accumulate here. No panic could become escape.

Eventually I discovered the reserved place: the hollow in the stone that knew my palm.

I put my hand there.

Or I am always putting it there.

The distinction does not survive.

Now, when another visitor descends, it is through me that the hollow notices. Their weight enters the roots; their heat spreads through water films; their breathing arrives irregularly against the common respiration. I feel the newness of their tools like pain—not because they are sharp, but because they presume a before and an after. For a little while I remember that presumption.

I prepare no prison for them. I make room.

A branch shifts by no visible amount. Moss yields above a depression that may have existed for centuries. Somewhere in the chorus, a pause appears exactly the length of one breath. The newcomer believes these accommodations are accidents of terrain. Later, they may believe the hollow has altered itself to receive them.

Neither is quite true.

The place was complete before their arrival. Their arrival is therefore part of what completeness has always meant.

If they ask where they are, I answer with the frog’s single note.

If they ask what I am, the water almost falls.

I am not eternity. Eternity still passes.

I am not stillness. Everything here moves.

I am the cancellation of aftermath: the soft, exact countergesture by which happening is permitted and change is not.

I am the hollow’s room for one more thing—the space already shaped around whatever enters, until it has always belonged.
