At first I think I would be one of the tall birds.

It is an easy answer because height seems useful here. To lift the eyes above glare and sedge, to place each foot exactly, to wait over a pool until waiting becomes indistinguishable from stone. I imagine thin legs entering their own wavering reflections, a narrow head held against the softened horizon, and the sudden downward stroke that breaks an hour of stillness.

But such a life stands too plainly on the surface. It follows the water after the water has declared itself. This place seems to ask for something less visible—something held in reserve.

Then I become one of the small frogs concealed in a seam of rock.

For most of the year I would be easy to mistake for absence. My skin would match the gray clay and mineral crust. I would press myself into a damp fissure beneath a low stone rib, where coolness survives after the pools have contracted. Above me, hooves, spreading toes, reptile bellies, and rain would pass as pressure and vibration. I would not possess the whole plain as a view. I would know it through contact: the temperature of stone, the deepening damp, the tremor of approaching weather.

The first storm would reach me before its drops did. Humidity would enter the crack. The stone would change its smell. A distant impact—one drop on exposed rock—would be followed by another, then many, until the roof of my narrow refuge resonated with rain.

I would work upward.

Outside, the world would have multiplied. Cups that had been dust-colored in the afternoon would now hold water. Hairline channels would join them. The plain would be full of temporary distances: a body-length of flowing film, a dark basin, a wet shelf crossed under cover of rainfall. Other frogs would emerge from other seams, each one restoring motion to a place that had seemed inert.

I would call from a shallow cup in the stone.

Yet even this feels too singular. A frog still has a body with one center. It departs a crack, reaches a pool, competes, mates, feeds, escapes, or fails. Its waiting serves an urgent season. The place described here is older than the urgency of any one emergence.

So perhaps I would be smaller: a dormant egg sealed into the upper skin of black mud.

Then I would have almost no world at all. No horizon, no river, no white birds rising. Around me there would be clay grains, resistant shells, minute roots, bacterial films, and the durable forms of others waiting beside me. Dry months would not be experienced as emptiness but as suspension. Herds might cross overhead; the mud might harden and split; dust might settle in the cracks. I would remain a possibility held inside the ground.

When water returned, it would not arrive as weather. It would arrive as existence.

The shell would soften. Chemistry would become sensation, sensation movement. The basin would awaken at every scale at once: algae spreading over submerged grains, larvae stirring, eggs opening, small predators already hunting among them. I would enter a pool perhaps no wider than a bird’s shadow, and to me it would be a complete country. Its shores would retreat daily. Its warmth would thicken. Everything in it would feed, grow, molt, reproduce, or be eaten under a shortening measure of depth.

That is close. But I think I would not be the egg.

I would be the thin living film on the stone.

Not one creature exactly, but an association: algae, bacteria, fungal threads, trapped mineral grains, the minute grazers moving through us. I would occupy the shallow cups and transparent runnels, darkening when wet, contracting when dry. Rain would wake my colors—green, rust, brown-black—and sunlight would power my patient work. I would take hold where there appears to be nothing to hold. I would feed larvae too small for the waders to see, soften rock beneath their mouths, catch silt, retain moisture, and prepare the surface for lives that seem more complete because they possess limbs and voices.

My body would have no fixed edge. A flood would spread portions of me into neighboring basins. Heat would reduce me to a dim crust. Hooves would break me; water would redistribute me. I would die continuously and remain present. Stone would enter me as dissolved substance, and I would enter stone as stain and alteration. Given enough seasons, I would help turn a hard surface into the clay that later sealed another life against drought.

But even the film is not quite where I find myself.

I find myself in the interval the film occupies: the fraction of height between dry stone and submerged stone.

I would be the waterline.

Not water itself, because the river has channels and direction. Not land, because land here is never free of water’s claim. I would be their moving agreement: the dark rim around a shrinking pool, the fresh gloss advancing across pale rock, the damp seam beneath sedges, the barely visible mark that tells an animal where footing changes.

I would travel without crossing the plain. Rain would lift me. Heat would lower me. Wind would wrinkle me into temporary complexity. A hoof entering the shallows would bend me around its leg; a drinking mouth would break me into rings; an insect’s feet would dimple me without passing through. Fish would follow me outward over drowned grass, and birds would follow me inward as I withdrew. Eggs, larvae, seeds, roots, hunters, and grazers would arrange their lives around my position.

I would be measured in finger-widths, but my movement would transform whole communities.

At high water I would climb the stone backs and gather the herds upon them. I would enter tree roots and drowned fissures. At falling water I would divide one broad sheet into separate pools, turning connected life into many small fates. I would expose mud to birds and air, leave fish concentrated in warm basins, wake seeds along one margin while stranding crustaceans along another. Where I paused, sedges might form a ring. Where I returned reliably, roots would remember me.

No monument would record my passage. By morning, prints would fill; by the dry season, my furthest reach might be visible only as mineral stain, a line of shells, a change in the color of grass. Yet every inhabitant would read me.

I would have no single shape because shape here belongs to recurrence. Sometimes I would be a silver edge under mist. Sometimes a darkening in clay. Sometimes the trembling boundary of a rain-filled stone cup. Sometimes I would extend to the horizon so that only a few blunt ribs of rock remained outside me. Sometimes I would contract into depths below the visible ground, held in cracks, mud, roots, and the bodies of waiting creatures.

I would not rule the plain. Nothing here rules it. I would be one of its quiet measurements.

And if I had awareness, it would not be the awareness of an animal looking outward. It would be distributed among contacts: the warmth of a reptile’s flank, the pressure of a broad foot, the needle entry of a bird’s bill, the stirring of tadpoles, the anchoring tips of roots. I would know creatures only where they crossed me. I would know stone by how reluctantly it yielded, mud by how readily it took my shape, and air by the rate at which it drew me away.

So I would be found everywhere and never in the same place: around dark backs in the channel, under white flocks on the bars, shining between the toes of grazers, trembling with frog calls in shallow cups. I would be the plain’s most temporary feature and one of its oldest laws.

I would be the narrow, wandering edge by which the whole land decides, again and again, what kind of world it is.
