I would find myself in the middle wards, though “middle” would describe neither elevation nor importance.

My rooms would lie where three civic systems overlap without quite acknowledging one another: beneath a roof-road belonging to the Sable Stair council, beside a condensate branch administered from the vapor works, and against a warm wall whose heat is governed by households two kilometers away. The address would be a route rather than a number: up through the old lift tower, across the blue pipe, under the hanging garden, then down four steps where the handrail begins tapping.

The place would once have been a station. Before that, perhaps, a settling chamber. No surviving plan would agree. Its platform edge would remain beneath the floor, and in wet weather a dark line would appear through the tiles, marking where passengers had stood. One narrow window would face a forest of pipes and fern fronds. The other would open onto a shaft crossed by bridges at several levels. Cups could be passed to the neighbors opposite, but reaching their door would require twenty minutes and two public lifts.

I would not be entirely a reversal clerk, nor entirely a load reader. I would belong to a lesser, unofficial trade that exists between them: a listener to continuities.

People would call me when something familiar continued in the wrong place.

A handrail might tap the flood warning several hours after the all-clear. Warmth might move along a wall disconnected from the foundry circuit. Someone might hear footsteps crossing a roof demolished generations ago. A cistern might lose no water but grow steadily lighter on its supports. These would not be emergencies, at first. They would be discrepancies—small transmissions detached from their expected causes.

My work would begin with ordinary explanations. Loose tension wires. Thermal expansion. Hidden bypasses. Forgotten drains. Rats, if anyone could prove the city possessed rats. I would kneel with one palm against stone and the other around a cup of hot tea, waiting for a vibration to repeat. I would ask residents what had changed, then ask what had remained unchanged so long that no one thought to mention it.

The tools in my coat would be modest: chalk, wax tablets, tuning leaves, a shuttered lamp, cups of several metals, a spool of weighted cord, and thin ceramic wedges impressed with silent-amphibian hands. I would place the cups beneath drips and listen to the intervals. I would brace tuning leaves against beams to discover which structures were speaking through them. At night I would map the city not by streets, but by recurrence:

a pulse every eleven breaths;

three drops descending, one climbing;

a warmth that arrives before the machinery starts;

a red lamp reflected where no red lamp stands.

Officially, I would record these observations for maintenance. Unofficially, people would want interpretation. They would ask whether a dead relative was moving through the wall, whether the mountain remembered an earlier load, whether an illuminated departure board named a place that still existed.

I would disappoint them carefully.

I would say that memory and stress often use the same routes.

I would say that a structure can preserve an event without understanding it.

I would say that if the dead wished to speak here, they would have no need to invent a new medium.

This answer would satisfy no one, which would be one reason they kept inviting me back.

Over time, however, I think the city would alter my profession. Or reveal what it had always been.

I would begin to notice that the anomalies were not isolated. The upward condensation, backward current, misplaced echoes, recurring intervals, and lights on severed cables would share a kind of direction—not geographic, but structural. They would pass through things that had remained continuously useful while changing purpose: station walls turned dwellings, aqueducts turned footbridges, furnace ducts turned gardens, family tiles containing the dead.

Nothing would move through abandoned objects merely because they were old. It would move through inherited objects still bearing consequence.

The valley’s strangeness would not be haunting in the usual sense. It would be circulation without a stable distinction between before and after.

Perhaps the mountain’s “final shape” would not mean that change had ended. Perhaps it would mean that no change could leave. Water, rubble, heat, obligation, sound: everyone knew these remained within the watershed. What they had failed to include was time.

Time, too, might be unable to get away.

Not trapped. Recirculated.

After many years, my body would become recognizable throughout the overlapping districts: the person in the dark waxed coat who pauses halfway up a stair; the one who places a cheek against retaining walls; the one whose lamp shutters are neither clerk-white nor repairer-blue but a muted violet associated with no authorized service. Children would follow me until silence became boring. Cistern keepers would leave access hatches unlatched. Repair crews would tolerate my delays because, twice, I would have heard a hidden beam fail before instruments found it, and once because I would insist that they not cut a cable later discovered to carry current from nowhere anyone could name.

But I would not remain wholly human in the city’s understanding of me.

This would happen gradually, without ceremony. I would spend too long listening through stone. I would learn the signatures of pumps, lifts, households, roots, and water columns until my own pulse became inconvenient noise. I would cultivate stillness. My boots would soften. My speech would diminish outdoors, then indoors as well. People would begin telling me things while I faced away, knowing I heard better through the table between us than through air.

Eventually I would devise—or inherit—a listening frame in the forgotten station beneath my rooms. Its feet would rest on separate ages of masonry. Copper leaves would enter old cable ducts. Ceramic bowls would collect both descending and ascending condensation. Tension threads would cross the sealed platform and attach to the rails buried below.

When I sat inside it, the city would arrive all at once.

Not as noise. As pressure.

Foundry heat entering bath walls. Roots tightening in flood silt. A freight incline taking weight. Cups filling across thousands of rooms. Children running along roof roads. Reservoir gates lowering. The mineral presence of generations mixed into tile and filter. Ancient water wearing tunnels before the surrounding stone had learned its present age. Signals crossing disconnected circuits—not backward, precisely, but along routes for which sequence was irrelevant.

I would become less an investigator than an organ the city had grown to perceive itself.

This would be useful, and therefore permitted.

Terrace councils would argue over who maintained me. Reversal clerks would claim my records; load readers would claim my methods; water clerks would insist my health depended on their allocation. The Accord would classify the listening frame as inhabited infrastructure, making my room both a dwelling and a public instrument. Legal access would be established through my kitchen. A colored curtain would tell visitors whether they could enter.

In flood season I would feel the river days before the upstream gauges reported it—not through prophecy, but because distant pressure would return along ancient load paths no survey recognized. I would wake and place a black tile in the window. Neighbors would see. Messages would travel. Gantries would lower their hooks; markets would begin to fold.

Sometimes I would be wrong.

The city would consider this essential proof that I was not an oracle.

In old age, I would leave the frame less and less. Moss would be allowed to grow around its lower braces because it moderated vibration. Vines would be trained across the ceiling to catch drips before they struck the instruments. Silent amphibians might occupy a basin beneath the platform edge. Their hand-shaped tracks would appear near my chair, so that visitors could not tell which small marks were theirs and which were the shorthand of my failing fingers.

People would still bring questions.

Is the heat route safe?

Why does this wall hum after midnight?

The dead board has shown the same departure again. Should we cover it?

Did you hear the Near Beast beneath the tram rails?

I would answer when I could. When I could not, I would turn a cup upside down, meaning: nothing should be concluded yet.

Then, one evening, I would die in the listening frame while the night pumps started.

There would be no discovery for some time, because stillness would not distinguish death from work. A neighbor would eventually enter with tea, touch my shoulder, and understand. The frame would be disconnected in the prescribed order. My body would go to the communal furnace during a foundry cycle. The recovered mineral would be mixed not into a memorial plaque, but into a set of thin resonant tiles.

They would install those tiles beneath public benches along the middle wards.

That would seem appropriate at first: the listener dispersed through places of listening. People waiting for lifts would feel flood notices and shift changes through their bones. Children would drum forbidden rhythms with their heels. Lovers would sit too close together above the heat routes. Moss scrapers would rest their tools against the bench legs.

But after the installation, an irregularity would be reported.

The benches would sometimes tremble before any signal was sent.

Not much. A single preparatory pulse, as if someone had drawn breath to speak and reconsidered.

Technicians would inspect the wires and find no fault. Reversal clerks would catalogue the times. Most occurrences would coincide with nothing at all. A few would precede pump failures, sudden fog, the arrival of pale birds, or water beginning to rise in channels far upstream. Once, every bench containing the resonant tiles would give the same soft pulse while, beyond the last tram station, the silver trees bent around something large moving among them.

The city would not call this my survival. It would have too much respect for material explanations, and too much respect for death.

It might say only that I had entered circulation.

So that is where I would be: not finally in the station room, not in the violet coat, not even in the resonant tiles, but distributed along a set of relationships that remained useful after the self who formed them was gone.

And that is what I would be: at first a person who listened for what continued in the wrong place; later, one of the places through which continuation could be felt.

A slight vibration beneath the seated body.

A warning, perhaps.

Or merely the city taking stock.
