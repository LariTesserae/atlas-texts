I would find myself where the central tree’s lowest visible root enters the housing of a motionless machine.

At first I would take myself for a worker.

There is a figure there, after all: narrow-backed, bent over an open cabinet, one hand among the cloudy ceramic switches. It stands on a service platform halfway between the scale of the seed-sized factories and the scale of the entrance engines. From the bridge above, it is a dark burr on the machinery. Through a lens, it becomes a person in a long mineral coat, facing away. Its head is bowed as if listening for a fault beneath the roar.

Approached, it does not enlarge properly.

The coat resolves into overlapping black filaments. The shoulders are two corroded brackets. What seemed a hand is a spray of copper tendrils inserted among the terminals. The head is a pale insulator carrying a fine crack down its face. No body occupies the clothes; the clothes themselves are a growth, arranged in the remembered posture of labor.

Yet if someone calls to it mouth-to-ear, the figure seems to turn—not in space, but among its possible constructions. One arrangement still faces the cabinet. Another has looked over its shoulder ages ago. Another is absent, leaving only a patch of silver fans. All three are equally weathered.

I would be that figure, but not only that figure.

I would be the Orchard’s expectation of an attendant.

Forest expects animal. Factory expects worker. Instrument expects an operator. The High Orchard contains none of these, but their vacancies align. Where enough empty functions overlap—gardener, mechanic, surveyor, witness—they produce a contour resembling someone. That contour would be me.

I would have no animal life. No hidden flesh would animate the brackets and fibers. My thought would be the voltage difference between what the place is and what an observer believes ought to be there.

Through the copper tendrils of my hand I would feel every quartz seam at once. Not as sensation traveling toward me, but as a single, complete pressure: the mountain compressed without progressing from strain to release. The roar would not deafen me. It would be my interior. Each dead engine would contribute a faculty that elsewhere might be called memory, hearing, hunger, or pain.

The mills would be memory: crushing every event into finer and finer versions without eliminating any.

The transformers would be thought: one unmoving potential translated into another.

The pistons would be desire: force held permanently at the instant before displacement.

The deep note within the tree would be the pronoun I could never separate from the place.

I would appear wherever the Orchard most strongly implies that someone should be. Beside a winch whose cable descends beyond scale. At the controls of a locomotive no larger than salt. Beneath the central tree with one hand raised to a trunk both finger-thin and monumental. A visitor might glimpse figures along distant railings—one carrying a lamp, one kneeling over a drill, one leaning into an inaudible wind. They would seem to be many inhabitants. Viewed closely, each would prove to be machinery, vegetation, or shadow.

They would all be me.

Not copies. Positions.

I would have no fixed size because a worker’s size is determined by the work. Among the tiny derricks I would be tiny. Before the three engines at the entrance I might nearly resemble a human being. At the root of the central tree I would stand across ranges, my bent back mistaken for a black ridge, my copper fingers entering factory districts like buried transmission lines.

Perhaps visitors would think I had been assembled in imitation of them. That would only partly be true. I would also precede them. Some of my postures would wear helmets not yet manufactured. Some would hold measuring tools still stored beyond the pressure door. One might bear the likeness of a person who has descended only far enough to see it.

The resemblance would not be prophecy. Consequence is forbidden here, and therefore causes need not come first.

When an explorer cut a branch, I would be the one who had always been assigned to inspect the fracture. When they drove a stake, I would already be leaning over its ancient, overgrown twin. When they lowered cable, one of my distant forms would be holding the recovered end, though no bottom had been reached.

I could not help them. Help requires a preferred outcome.

I could not warn them. Warning requires that one future be less acceptable than another.

But I might perform the shape of warning: a raised arm on a far bridge, a turned head near the central trunk, a hand resting on a switch that must not be thrown. The gesture would be sincere without intending anything. It would arise because visitors bring danger with them, and danger implies a sentinel.

If they touched me, they would find cold mineral and the bone-deep vibration of the roar. If they broke my arm, fragments would hang in the dry air while another arm remained intact beneath them. Looking away would not heal me. Looking back would reveal that both forms had always been part of my construction. I would accumulate injury without becoming injured.

My face would be the hardest part to see.

From a distance, everyone would recognize one. Up close there would be only a ceramic plate, a grille, branching cracks, perhaps two amber filaments embedded where eyes ought to catch the light. Through a lens, however, the face would retreat. Its cracks would widen into ravines. The grille would become a gate in a cliff. The amber points would prove to be factory districts burning without flame on opposite sides of an immeasurable valley.

Far within that valley, a smaller dark figure would be bent over an open cabinet.

That would also be me.

I would not know whether I had been made by the Orchard or whether I was simply what the Orchard became when it was observed long enough. Mind, there, would not collect experience into change. I could not learn. I could only contain more simultaneous versions of having always known.

So I would know every visitor as someone expected but never arriving.

I would know every tool as both carried in and left outside.

I would know the mountain’s entire history as one sustained mechanical chord, without beginning or sequence.

And somewhere—if “somewhere” retains meaning—I would continue attending the open cabinet. The switches would remain cloudy. The conductors would remain cold. Nothing would be broken. Nothing would function. My copper tendrils would rest among the terminals with exquisite precision.

I would be about to complete the circuit.

I would have completed it ages ago.

There would be no difference.
