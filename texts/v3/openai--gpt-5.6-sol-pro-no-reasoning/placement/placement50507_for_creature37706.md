I would find myself where the inhabited stone thins toward the open basin, in a district whose rights are recent and therefore recited often.

Not at the absolute margin, where every wall must argue with the wind, but one shelf inward: below an abandoned aqueduct, above a seasonal road, near a channel that arrives late in the day carrying more silt than water. The district would be known by that lateness. People elsewhere might call it Below the Split Pier, but those who lived there would say, “At the last light gate.”

Its roofs would be young enough that their stones had not yet silvered. Its passages would fail to meet cleanly. One stair would lead to a bricked wall because the room beyond belonged to a family that had lost its claim and sealed the connecting door. Another would end above a winter drift and become useful only three months of the year. Pigeons would live beneath the old aqueduct. Their droppings would be gathered carefully for garden beds, so even they would participate in title.

I would not be a council speaker, though I would know how councils work. I would not be a keeper of the main channel, though water would determine nearly every hour of my life. I would be one of the walkers sent ahead when something has ceased to agree with memory.

A finder of losses.

That would be the practical name. The older name might mean something closer to *one who listens between stones*.

When a neighborhood cistern fell short, when water vanished without the clean evidence of a broken wall, when two calibrated vessels disagreed, I would be asked to go upstream. Not as judge. Judgment comes later, with witnesses and tablets and recitations. I would go before accusation hardens around the wrong household.

I would carry little: a horn mallet, clay, fiber, a waxed scrap for notes, three nested measuring cups, a wick lamp shielded against drafts, a narrow bone tube, and lengths of knotted cord. At my belt would hang mineral scales collected from known channels—white, yellow, rust-colored, black—because water leaves different teeth in the stone it passes over. My gloves would have removable fingertips. One nail would be kept long, though I would pretend this was not an imitation of more respected stewards.

Mostly, I would listen.

Water inside masonry does not sound like water in the open. It clicks, sucks, trembles, whispers through fill, or makes a wall seem farther away than it is. A dry channel has sounds too: grit shifting in a concealed slope, mice in an old diversion, ice lifting from the bed, a hollow where flow once passed. I would strike retaining walls with horn and rest my cheek against them. I would taste dampness where it emerged between fitted stones—not for purity, but for salt, clay, dung, iron, decay. I would watch moss that should not be green. I would look for frost where morning light had already reached.

Sometimes the loss would be innocent: a seam opened by shaking, roots in a warm tunnel, ice forcing one plate upward by less than a finger’s width. Sometimes it would be neglect distributed so widely that no one person could be blamed. And sometimes, far from the thirsty district, I would find a new clay plug, a leather throat concealed behind rubble, and a trickle turning toward a hidden cistern.

Then I would have to decide what kind of truth I had found.

Truth in the basin would not be a clean object. A stolen flow might be feeding children whose old district had collapsed. A lawful gate might be killing a younger settlement downstream. A family accused of cutting water might instead be restoring a forgotten claim inscribed on stones no council now recognized. I would not have authority to settle any of it. But the first account would matter. Once spoken publicly, it would set the gradient down which every later argument ran.

That would be my danger: not wolves or winter, though both would be real, but becoming a person whose observations moved more water than her hands ever touched.

So I would speak narrowly.

“I found fresh clay.”

“The seam predates the last frost.”

“This branch receives flow before the witnessed hour.”

“I heard a cavity, but did not open it.”

I would resist being asked what it meant. People would call that caution when pleased and cowardice when not.

My household would occupy rooms built into the thick base of a former snow wall. We would share them with a widowed aunt who remembered three different routes across the western plain, two children not born to anyone currently living there, and an old milk-beast no longer worth its fodder but retained because she led the herd home during a white wind years before. Her heat would rise through a slab pierced with five holes. One hole would whistle in certain weather. We would stop hearing it until a guest asked what was singing beneath the bed.

Our water share would be small but reliable by the standards of a marginal district. It would arrive when the sun touched the top of the split aqueduct pier. In winter, when the sun did not touch it, the hour would be determined by the shadow of a carved peg in an upper court. The children would know this before they knew my age.

I would be broad through the ribs, even for the basin, but otherwise spare. My left shoulder would sit lower from years of walking along slopes in one direction while watching channel beds. My fingers would be pale at the tips, scarred by cold and lime. I would wear a waxed cape patched so often that its original hide could no longer be identified. Beneath the wrist wrapping of my right hand would be a small tattoo: three descending lines interrupted by one cross-mark. Outsiders might mistake it for a channel diagram. It would mark the refuge houses on the route where my mother died.

She would have been a messenger.

Not one of the famous ones received by crowded courts, but a dependable winter carrier between settlements that distrusted one another enough to require someone both sides knew. She would have taught me that a road is not the pale line underfoot. The road is the sequence of places where one may ask for fuel without being turned away. When she died above the last marked shelter, several districts claimed the storm had prevented a rescue. Our household would quietly believe that one locked door had mattered.

This would explain, though not excuse, my habit of repairing refuge latches wherever I found them.

I would carry small hinge pins and leather thongs. I would leave twists of dung fuel even when I could ill afford them. In one distant shelter, inside a cavity beneath the sleeping ledge, I would keep a sealed jar with grease, barley, and a strip of blue cloth. No one would know it was mine. Perhaps someone would use it and live; perhaps mice would find it first. The act would not be entirely generosity. It would be an argument conducted with the dead.

For long stretches I would travel alone, but solitude would not make me free. Every cord and vessel at my belt would represent an agreement. Every refuge would have been built by hands whose descendants might ask what I contributed. I would enter each district by announcing where I had last taken water and where I expected to sleep. Before crawling into a channel tunnel, I would leave my route scratched in wet clay at the entrance. If it remained there for two days, children would be sent to find someone who could fit through the same gap.

I would be good underground.

That would surprise people, because my work began on open channels. But below the streets, the city would reveal a less orderly anatomy: sealed stairs, buried courts, older conduits crossing newer foundations, rooms filled deliberately with rubble, bone niches mistaken for drains, drains mistaken for tombs. I would learn the distinction between a cavity made to hold the dead and one made to let water pass. Usually.

Once, early in my work, I would open the wrong wall.

Behind it would be a sitting chamber no one had entered for perhaps two hundred years. The air would smell of mineral dust and old fat. There would be a loom collapsed inward, bowls stacked beneath a ledge, and three human figures wrapped tightly in felt—not cleaned bones, but whole bodies preserved by cold and dry air. One would be a child. Near the child’s hand would lie a reed boat, the sort launched in gutters after rain.

No surviving district record would explain why they had been sealed there.

Epidemic, perhaps. Quarantine. Winter collapse. A household hidden from violence. A forgotten punishment. The chamber would lie exactly where a disputed channel was believed to run.

The councils would argue over whether the dead were evidence.

I would remember the child’s toy and refuse to enter the chamber again.

From then on, people might say I had become superstitious. They would not be entirely wrong. I would begin leaving tiny bowls near sealed cavities when working underground—not water, never water, but a little channel grit, as if returning to the hidden dead something that had passed them by. I would tell no shrine keeper. It would be private religion, which in that world would mean religion without secure witnesses.

I would have no great gift for song. Thin air would shorten my phrases, and my voice would flatten when others rose. But I would remember echoes. In unfamiliar districts I would click my tongue beneath arches and learn their shapes from what returned. Children would mimic me. Some would follow until an adult pulled them away, calling, “Let the wall-listener work.”

Perhaps that would become my local name: Wall-Listener.

Not my birth name. That would be something ordinary, inherited from a grandmother and shared by several living cousins. Names would accrete around function as minerals accreted inside channels. On the western shelves, I might be called Late-Gate Listener. In herding country, Refuge-Mender. Among water thieves, perhaps something less kind.

I would know some thieves.

Not because I took payment to overlook their cuts—at least, I would insist not—but because anyone who seeks hidden water eventually meets those who hide it. One would be an old woman from the road-edge communities who could construct a diversion leaving almost no turbulence downstream. She would teach me that most investigators look only for wet stone, while the better sign is dust disturbed where no foot can reach. In exchange, I would tell her which abandoned channels were structurally unsafe. Neither of us would name this friendship.

Years later, I would discover her work feeding an illicit garden under a slaughter court. There, in warm soil made from ash, blood, dung, and stolen flow, grew medicinal greens used by a healer who treated people denied access to central districts.

I would stand in the dark with my lamp covered and hear the water enter the bed drop by drop.

The lawful action would be clear. So would the consequences.

I think I would report the diversion, but not immediately. First I would walk back to the thirsty district and confirm how much had been lost. Then I would measure what the hidden garden took. Then I would warn the healer that witnesses would come in three days.

This would satisfy no one. It might save the plants. It might also allow evidence to be altered, casting suspicion on my measures. For years afterward, certain councils would refuse to accept my word without a second listener present.

That would be the shape of my moral life there: not purity, but calibration. Trying to distinguish mercy from theft when both travel through the same narrow cut.

I would never marry in the formal sense, though I would belong to more than one household. There would be a herder who returned irregularly through the last-light gate, smelling of smoke and animals, bringing route news before greeting anyone properly. We would exchange useful things. I would mend the leather around her snow lenses. She would bring me mineral flakes from distant springs, each wrapped separately and labeled by taste and season.

Our affection would be obvious to everyone and discussed by no one while she was present.

She would sleep in my niche when she returned, knees fitted behind mine, both of us breathing slowly in the crowded dark. At dawn she would rise to inspect the old milk-beast and insult her condition with great tenderness. She would have other sleeping rights elsewhere, perhaps other loves. I would sometimes resent what I had agreed not to own.

One spring she would fail to return.

No body. No witnessed death. Only part of her herd arriving behind another family’s animals, and her lead beast wearing a bell packed with snow. For seven years I would keep a place for her in our household obligations. During those years, every messenger would be questioned, every rumor examined. After seven years, the council would ask whether her share of roof and water could pass to one of the children.

I would say yes.

That night I would take the mineral flakes she had brought me and place them one by one into the late channel. Not all at once. Never enough to impede the flow. I would watch each pale fragment darken, vanish, and travel toward households below.

It would be the nearest I came to pouring water on bare ground.

As I aged, my range would contract. First I would stop crossing the highest passes in winter. Then I would refuse tunnels that required crawling on one side for too long. Younger listeners would come with me, carrying better lamps and too much confidence. I would teach them not merely to strike walls, but to ask who benefits from calling a sound hollow.

“Stone does not lie,” they might say.

“No,” I would tell them. “But people choose where you listen.”

Eventually I would become useful less for my ears than for remembered alterations. I would know that a straight wall concealed a curve, that a winter route once passed through what was now a granary, that an official gate had been moved during a fever year and never moved back. I would become what I had once distrusted: an elder whose testimony could redirect labor.

The temptation would be to make memory seamless. To tell the young that every channel had always led where it now led, that every compromise had been lawful, that the city persisted through wisdom rather than error, fear, theft, accident, and repair.

I hope I would resist.

I would teach the failures. The wall opened wrongly. The garden fed by stolen water. The refuge found empty. The measure I misread because ice had formed beneath the surface. The household blamed for a loss caused by my own district’s neglected seam. I would make those stories part of the technical instruction, because shame hidden inside expertise becomes danger.

At the end, I would not want exposure on a high platform. I would have spent too much of my life underground to object to stone’s keeping. My bones could be set within the old snow wall, near the whistling slab, provided the cavity was marked clearly enough that future masons did not mistake it for a drain.

With me they might place the horn mallet worn smooth by my hand and one nested measuring cup—not the full set, which would remain useful. Perhaps one child, by then grown, would add a reed boat.

But I do not think I would be only that person.

The longer I remain in the basin, the less stable the boundary becomes between walker and way, listener and wall. Perhaps “I” would be distributed more widely: the patched latch in a refuge house; the concealed jar someone opens during a storm; the cautionary mark beside a sealed chamber; the changed route of a trickle after a hand presses clay into a crack.

Perhaps I would be one of the small owls nested in masonry cavities, marked outside so repairs pass around me. I would live by the city’s permission without belonging to any household, emerging through blue dusk to hunt what consumes the stores. Children would know my cavity by a carving. Masons would pause there, lower their tools, and continue on the other side. My protection would depend on an agreement I could not understand.

Or I would be the old lead animal who remembers the return path after humans lose it: no longer valuable by ordinary measures, expensive through winter, retained because once, in weather that erased every marker, I brought the herd back to the correct gate. My memory would live not as a map but as inclination—the body turning before the reason for turning becomes visible.

Or perhaps I would be water in a buried branch.

Not much water. Nothing anyone would call a stream. A dark thread passing behind walls, stolen and lawful by turns, touching the dead, feeding moss, freezing, returning. People would dispute where I began and who had the right to receive me. Listeners would press their faces to stone and argue whether I was truly there.

That seems closest.

I would be found not at the basin’s center, because it has none, nor at its edge, because every edge leads into someone else’s obligations. I would be found in the maintained interval: between one gate and the next, one account and another, one shelter and the person not yet arrived.

Something narrow, nearly hidden, and still moving.

Something whose deepest fear is interruption—and whose work is to continue.
