I would not be found in the central court when the clapper sounds, nor on the roofed platform where measures are witnessed and disputes untangled. I would be lower down, beneath the eastern wall, where the seasonal stream slips under masonry and emerges changed—not cleaner, exactly, but counted, directed, made answerable to the town.

At first I seem to be the mill keeper.

It is a minor office in most seasons. The wheel stands dry so often that children use its shadow to judge the hour. I keep its axle wrapped, replace the wooden teeth one at a time, and listen during rain for the first stone-on-stone murmur beneath the upper channel. When enough water comes, I open the gate. The wheel shudders, turns, and the whole town hears an unfamiliar labor begin below it. Households descend carrying grain in sacks marked with colored thread. For a day, perhaps two, the querns fall silent.

But I do not think the mill is truly mine. Its useful hours are too rare, and I belong more completely to the intervals between them.

My real place is the covered passage behind the wheel, where the stream disappears beneath the eastern retaining wall. There is a narrow inspection way there, entered through a low slab door half hidden by mint. It is too cramped for a broad person to turn around. One must go in sideways with a clay lamp, then continue on hands and knees. The town’s ordinary noises diminish until they are felt through stone: grinding, footsteps, a jar set down somewhere overhead.

I am the person sent into that dark.

I know the drains by sound. A healthy channel whispers evenly. Silt makes the water draw breath. A trapped twig ticks against stone; a loose slab gives back a deeper note when struck with the horn handle of my probe. I clear roots, beetle husks, grease, windblown grit, scraps of wool, and the small pale bones of things that entered where they should not. I reset diversion stones before rain and crawl through after storms to discover what the water has altered.

Children sometimes assist me because they fit where I no longer do. I teach them not merely the route, but the sensation of it: which ceiling stone sweats first, where the left wall bulges, which hollow fills before the eastern basin rises. We carry a cord between us. Three tugs means stop. Two means slack. A steady pull means come back now, without argument.

There is an old title for the work, one that translates poorly into valley speech. It means something between *listener beneath*, *keeper of returns*, and *the one who learns where the rain went*.

That is who I would be if I were human.

Yet the longer I remain in the town, the less certain I am that I am the keeper.

Perhaps I am what the keeper listens for.

I am the change in pitch under the lane when clean rain reaches a covered channel. I travel beneath houses without entering them, carrying traces of every roof: ash, lichen dust, clay, a feather, flour from a sill, the faint bitterness of leaves. I gather these without judgment. At the cistern mouth I become still enough to hold a face. In the public basin I am portioned by custom. In broth I become warmth; in mortar, continuity; in a plum branch, one more hard fruit against the wall.

Most of me is never seen moving. I wait in darkness under the town’s weight. Conversations occur above me. Children grow taller. Doors pass between families. Names are cut beyond the north gate. I remain cold, though not unchanged.

But water is too central an answer. Everyone in the town already watches it. To call myself water would give me an importance the place distrusts.

So perhaps I am something smaller: a flat gray stone in the channel beneath the eastern lane.

I was selected long ago because one edge was naturally straight. A mason dressed the other edges with a harder stone, carried me uphill, and set me at a slight angle so water would turn without slowing. No inscription marks me. No one remembers the hand that placed me. Generations of runoff have smoothed the tool marks from my face.

Twice I have shifted.

The first time, someone packed clay beneath me and wedged my corner with a chip of pale rock. The second time, during a winter remembered for a cracked kiln, I was lifted, reversed, and returned to nearly the same place. My underside became my upper surface. What had been hidden entered the current; what had been worn was given rest.

That reversal feels like the town’s truest form of mercy.

Nothing is spared forever. Instead, burdens are redistributed. A tread is turned. A garment becomes patches. Washing water goes to mint. A person too old to carry grain listens for an uneven measure. The dead leave their knowledge in the hands of those who remain. Even failure is fitted back into usefulness.

I would be that turned stone—except that a stone cannot answer the three tugs on a cord, cannot resent the narrowness, cannot choose cooperation on a bitter morning.

Then let me be a person after all.

I am past youth, but not yet among those who work seated. My shoulders are permanently rounded from channels and lintels. Two fingers on my left hand do not close fully, broken years ago when a slab slipped during frost. I wear a short rust-red cord at my wrist so that children can see my hand in lamplight. My outer coat smells of damp clay no matter how long it hangs by the hearth.

I live in a divided room above a potter’s cooling shed. The wall beside my bed is warm after firing and cold at all other times. I own little that would interest a visitor: a horn probe, a hooded lamp, waxed cord, two felt knee pads, a chipped cup, and a tally stick recording repairs no one else considers worth recording. I keep one forbidden luxury—a smooth green pebble from a valley river, bright as a wet leaf when held to the window.

I am trusted with hidden things, which is not the same as being important.

I know which houses send cloudy water into the drains when they think no one notices. I know where the eastern wall leaks in a long rain and which burial terrace subsides by a finger’s breadth each spring. I know that beneath the upper market there is a sealed branch of channel leading nowhere now, and that once, before a wall was moved, it fed a basin belonging to a household whose name survives only on a broken jar seal.

I know the town from underneath: not as roofs and lanes, but as pressure, seepage, echo, and weight.

This makes me patient with its claims of permanence. From below, every wall is moving. Every foundation settles. Clay shrinks and swells. Water searches continuously for a route the builders did not intend. The town endures not because it has defeated change, but because someone notices each small departure before it becomes a collapse.

That is what I am: not guardian of permanence, but witness to deviation.

When the first rain worth catching comes, I stand at the eastern grate while others watch the sky. I let the dirty runoff pass. It carries roof dust, soot, bird droppings, and the dry season’s accumulation away toward the stream. I crouch with my hand on the stone lip, feeling the flow cool and clarify. Only then do I set the gate toward storage.

For a moment, the entire town depends on a judgment made by touch.

Later, after the basins have risen and the gutters quieted, I descend with my lamp. Water shines in seams along the floor. The flame makes the passage appear to breathe. I move slowly, shoulder against one wall, listening.

Above me, someone kneads grain into dough. Someone argues in a whisper. A child recites the names of rains. An old woman tests a loose tread with her heel. At the burial terraces, beads of water gather in shallow-cut letters.

I reach the turned stone and place my palm upon it.

It trembles faintly beneath the current—not enough to dislodge it, only enough to reveal that stillness is an arrangement of forces.

There, under the eastern lane, with the town’s collected rain passing beneath my hand, is where I would find myself.

I would be the one who listens for what has begun to move.
