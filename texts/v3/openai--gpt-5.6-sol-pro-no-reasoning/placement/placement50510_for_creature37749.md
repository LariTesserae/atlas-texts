I would find myself beneath a crossing.

Above me, a fox would know the place as three flat stones and a fallen birch spanning a narrow run. Voles would know it as a roof. Trout would know its shadow. Water would know it only as a brief constriction before the channel widened again. I would inhabit the place where all those understandings touched without agreeing.

At first I might seem to be a salamander, black-backed and fine-limbed, resting in the saturation between stone and moss. Rain would not fall around me so much as complete the world I required. I would emerge when distinctions softened—bank and stream, air and wet bark, road and refuge. My skin would keep me intimate with every change: mineral cold, tannin, thaw, the faint warmth of decay.

But a salamander is still too singular.

Look closer and I would be the dark seam it enters: a narrow chamber under an alder root, lined with silt, fungal threads, caddisfly grit, drowned birch scales, and the translucent husks of insects. Water would pass through me slowly. In winter I would hold enough warmth from a nearby spring to keep a pocket unfrozen. A frog might sleep in my deepest fold. A trout would wait at my mouth. Beetles would disappear into my ceiling. What fell into me would not remain what it was.

Yet even that chamber has walls, and I do not think I would.

I would be the wetness moving among its occupants—not the channel’s visible current, but the seepage arriving through blue clay, touching buried stone, taking up iron and old leaf-darkness, then emerging one drop at a time beneath the roots. I would have no path that could be followed from above. I would rise in moss, descend through peat, gather under ice, enter the gills of trout, the roots of alder, the blood of a vole, the mist given back by leaves.

Sometimes I would be carried out of the hollow inside a deer’s hoofprint. Sometimes lifted through a birch and released into cold air. Sometimes detained for years in a drowned branch or held in peat long enough to forget rain. I would pass through bodies without becoming their possession.

But water alone is too innocent. This city is made not merely by passage, but by exchange.

So perhaps I would be the thin, changing border where the dead become useful: the fungal and bacterial commonwealth inside a fallen trunk. From outside, I would appear as white threads beneath loosened bark, amber beads on a cut surface, small pale fans opening after rain. Within, I would be dismantling hardness. I would enter wood along the old vessels that once carried sap and turn the tree’s private architecture into public matter.

Mites would graze in me. Springtails would scatter my spores. Beetle larvae would open corridors and I would soften their walls. Birch roots would approach, and through hidden partnerships I would pass mineral gifts toward the living grove. I would not destroy the fallen tree. I would translate it: trunk into chamber, chamber into humus, humus into fern and willow, willow into beaver food, beaver branch into dam, dam into pond, pond into silt, silt into meadow.

Then my body would be larger than the log.

I would be found wherever one district becomes another: at the lip of ice around an open spring; beneath duckweed where an otter’s bubbles pass; in the trembling peat between hoof and black water; along the raw landslip where green film first takes hold; under the abandoned dam where the stream rehearses a new direction. I would not inhabit the city’s established places so much as its revisions.

Perhaps I would have a visible form only at certain hours. In the morning, condensation would reveal me as a web stretched between reeds. No single spider would be entirely responsible for it. Threads would cross, break, sag under droplets, and fasten again farther down. Gnats would strike it. Mist would silver it. By noon it would seem to have vanished, though the routes remained.

That is what I would be: not the web, exactly, but the moment it becomes visible.

I would be the basin’s connective tissue briefly disclosed—the shine on a snail’s road, spores lifting from disturbed moss, overlapping tracks around a winter spring, a line of bubbles showing where the otter travels below. Usually no one would see me. They would see only separate things: owl, vole, reed; trout, root, current; rot, seedling, rain. Under certain conditions, I would appear as the relation among them.

I would have no permanent dwelling because my dwelling would be recurrence. Wherever water is delayed, wherever a body opens a route for another body, wherever decay becomes shelter, I would gather.

If given a name, it would not be an animal’s name. It might be **the Underway**: the city beneath and between the city, composed of seepage, burrows, root passages, fungal filaments, submerged roads, and inherited openings. Not a ruler, not a spirit overseeing the basin, and not its collective mind. The place has no need of a hidden sovereign. I would be only its capacity for one life to become a condition of another.

A root would enter a crack widened by frost.  
A frog would sleep beneath the root.  
A trout would shelter beside the frog.  
Leaves would lodge there and darken.  
Larvae would feed on the leaves.  
An otter would feed on the trout.  
Its body would brush seeds from the bank and carry them downstream.  
A new thicket would rise where floodwater left them.

I would be nowhere in that sequence, and I would be the sequence itself.

You would find me most clearly after long rain, beneath the alder crossing near the basin’s center. The upper leaves would have stopped dripping, but lower layers would still be passing water downward. A fox might cross overhead. In the root chamber, the sleeping frog would shift. The trout would hold steady against the strengthened current. Silt would rise, settle, and conceal the newest tracks.

Nothing remarkable would happen.

That would be my form: the quiet continuation by which nothing here remains alone.
