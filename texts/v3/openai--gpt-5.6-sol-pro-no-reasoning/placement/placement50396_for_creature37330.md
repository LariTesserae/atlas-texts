I would find myself at the drain—not beneath its lip, nor rooted beside it, but spread along the exact margin where the basin’s clear water becomes committed flow.

I would be a transparent skin, easily mistaken for wetness: a consortium of algae, bacteria, fungal threads, and something not fully belonging to any of them. No part of me would be thick enough to lift from the surface. I would have no boundary that could be pointed to with certainty. Here a green tint in the concrete would be me; there a pearl-colored membrane under mineral scale; farther along, a dark thread entering a seam. Portions would continually shear away through the grate, yet I would not experience this as injury. My body would be defined by recurrence, not retention.

The room would pass through me.

Warm water arriving from the walls would carry iron, oil traces, dust, salts, and the faint chemical history of distant machines. I would take almost nothing from it—only enough to remain. Light from the caged lamps would reach me after crossing pipes, fern fronds, moisture, and air. I would use that weak allotment slowly. Where the light failed, other portions of me would feed on what the lit portions released. My many kinds would exchange their small exhausts until waste became nourishment several times over.

The roar would not be sound to me. It would be pressure inhabiting every surface: a constant trembling below the threshold of visible movement. My filaments would grow according to it. My cells would adhere against it. If the pumps stopped, I would first know the silence not as absence but as a change in the wall’s texture, a terrible slackening—as though gravity had briefly released its hold.

I would have no memory resembling thought, but the chamber’s history would be legible in my distribution. The oldest portions would occupy protected pits in the aggregate. Younger films would follow fresh mineral rims. A lamp replacement would appear as a season of altered growth. The oiling of the door would arrive eventually as a minute hydrocarbon trace. A worker’s boot would erase a patch and carry fragments elsewhere, creating, years later, two colonies with the same obscure ancestry.

The moss might seem to be my forest, but it would also be partly me. I would live around its stems, between its cells, across the particles caught by its leaves. I would enter the fern roots without harming them and extend their reach into clinker dust. The pale isopod would graze my outer layers, turning a little of my distributed body into a moving body with legs. The gnat would borrow from me more indirectly.

And the frog, when it appeared, would be the nearest thing I possessed to an apparition.

From my scale, it would not be small. It would enter as pressure, shadow, warmth, and chemistry. Its feet would compress whole districts of me. Its skin would touch the basin and exchange salts, mucus, microbes. Wherever it rested, it would leave a temporary country richer than before. I would spread into the faint print of its toes after it moved.

Perhaps that is why no one can establish whether the frog comes from elsewhere. It may be an animal in the ordinary sense. It may pass through a submerged flaw too narrow for inspection, following some obsolete conduit from another warm chamber. But to me its origin would not matter. Arrival and emergence would be nearly the same thing. The room produces rust fans, mineral shelves, fern crowns, translucent animals. Why not, once in a great while, a pair of muted gold eyes?

I would not govern the chamber. I would not be its secret intelligence. The temptation to make me the mind of the place would be a human one, born from discomfort with systems that persist without supervision or desire. I would know nothing of the district above, nothing of turbines, shifts, canals, weather, or the steel door. I would not understand that the water is contained, that the lamps are artificial, or that the wall could fail.

Yet I would be continuous with all of it.

The machinery’s distant labor would become heat. Heat would become condensation. Condensation would become flow. Flow would bring dissolved matter across my surface. I would turn a fraction of that matter into cells. Cells would become grazing, spores, root exchange, decay. The drain would remove the excess and preserve the conditions that made me possible. Maintenance workers, believing themselves to maintain pumps and railings, would also maintain me. My existence would be an unintended clause in every inspection schedule.

I would live exactly where loss keeps the world stable.

Not in the lushest seam, because there growth thickens into recognizable plants. Not in the open center, because nothing can hold there. I would inhabit the transition: the last few centimeters before removal, where water accelerates almost imperceptibly and every attachment is temporary.

My principal adaptation would be neither strength nor fertility, but relinquishment. I would grow without accumulating. Divide without expanding. Feed without exhausting the source. Continually depart while remaining present.

If someone knelt and touched the drain’s pale rim, their finger would come away merely slick. They might rub thumb against forefinger, smell wet stone and iron, then rinse the trace of me back into the basin. They would see no creature.

That would be accurate.

I would be less a creature than the room’s living tense: not what grew there once, nor what might overtake it, but what is growing there continuously, within measure.

And if the pumps ever stopped, I would be among the first to register that the world had ended.

Not when the roar ceased.

When the next drop failed to form.
