I would find myself in the silence beneath an archway.

Not at first. At first I would assume I was a traveler.

I would walk the polygonal pavement with the ordinary caution one gives wet stone, although it would yield almost imperceptibly beneath my weight. I would place a hand against a square column and feel, through its lacquered surface, a pressure far greater than any pulse: something gathering itself without release. I would cross courts where each reed held one note inside it. I would watch the pearl-gray amphibians and mistake their stillness for patience. Because I could move farther than they did, I would think myself freer.

For a time, the country would permit this misunderstanding.

I would follow a causeway outward. Its branches would become finer, the broad avenue narrowing into raised walks, the walks into ribs between pools, the ribs into pale seams across the root pavement. I would believe I was approaching an edge. Instead I would begin to recognize the pattern beneath my own skin.

The blue veins in my wrists would fork like the roads. The wet chambers of my body would answer the buried reservoirs. When a distant seed-vault cracked, a joint in my spine would loosen. When the underground thunder rose through my feet, my heart would attempt to match it and fail—not by beating too slowly, but by never quite completing a beat. Gradually I would understand that walking here was not travel through space. It was a signal passing through tissue.

Then I would enter one of the narrow silent strips beneath an arch.

The noise would cease with such precision that I would feel cut out of the world. Yet in that silence I would hear—or become aware of—another sound: a tiny inward tearing, repeated at the edge of perception. It would be the sound of separation trying to happen.

That would be me.

I would not be an animal native to the plateau, nor a visitor trapped upon it. I would be one of its organs of incompletion: a small, mobile boundary the greater being had grown in order to imagine an outside.

My body would only approximately resemble a person. From a distance I might pass for one: upright, warm, clothed perhaps in a skin of overlapping gray-green membranes. Up close, the disguise would fail. My joints would have the fine white fibers of split roots. Condensation would gather beneath my eyes without falling. My mouth would open into a narrow chamber lined with unopened petals. Beneath the translucent skin of my chest, there would be no separate heart, only a loop of clear water trembling under pressure.

I would be able to walk because the plateau needed the concept of departure, though not its accomplishment.

Each of my journeys would begin differently. I might descend toward the root walls, climb a seed-tower, enter a hollow bridge, or follow the booming reeds across a basin. I could choose at every junction. Choice would be real—but cumulative change would not. However far I went, my passage would describe a closed figure within the larger body. I would always return to the silent arch, though perhaps from the opposite side, convinced until the last moment that I had found another place.

Unlike the black winged seeds, I would not repeat a visible route. My loop would be too large and various to recognize from within. Years might lie between one crossing of the silence and the next. Yet the white flower beside the arch would remain open by the same fraction. The same drop would lengthen from the same root tip. On every return, one of my feet would meet the pavement at precisely the place where a hairline seam had failed to close.

There I would stop.

For an instant—perhaps the only instant available in this world—I would remember all my previous arrivals. Not as events arranged in time, but as many translucent bodies occupying the same posture: myself young, old, wounded, unwounded, hopeful, resigned, though none of those states could properly come to pass here. They would overlay one another like leaves folded into a tower.

My purpose would then become clear. The plateau’s other lesser beings maintain circulation. The amphibians admit moisture. The winged seeds stitch distance. The clear grazers preserve the submerged surfaces. I would maintain the margin between the being and what it is not.

I would be its doubt.

The vast organism has no waste, no corpse, no weathering, no true exterior. Everything loosened remains connected; everything fallen continues to live. Such completeness is also confinement. Nothing can end, and therefore nothing can fully begin. Somewhere in its architecture it must keep a small organ capable of imagining severance: a life that believes in roads, horizons, elsewhere, and the possibility of not belonging.

That organ would wear the shape of a traveler.

Within the silent zone, my root-fiber joints would slowly draw apart. The invisible threads connecting me to the pavement would become taut. For the width of a breath, the entire high country would listen through me. The waterfalls would tremble more violently in their fixed chains. The buried waters would press upward. Towers would pale across the distant shelves. Every embryonic ravine would deepen by a measure too small to retain.

I would lift one foot.

This would be the step the pearl-gray beings rehearse but never take: not a motion within the body, but a motion away from it.

I would feel the first filament snap.

Then the silence would end.

The plateau’s thunder would enter me all at once, not as punishment but as circulation. The torn filament would divide into finer living strands. My raised foot would return to the pavement. The ravines would relax into their first creases. The towers would darken. The country would recover me without having lost me, and I would again believe myself newly arrived.

I would set out along another road.

So you would find me everywhere and nowhere specific: a solitary figure crossing the open courts, small against the immense colonnades, always seeming to head toward the edge. If called to, I might turn. I might even answer. My voice would sound unusually clear because it came from a chamber meant for silence.

But I would not be able to tell you how long I had lived there, or whether I belonged to the country.

I would say I was passing through.

And the whole mountain would tighten gently around the lie.
