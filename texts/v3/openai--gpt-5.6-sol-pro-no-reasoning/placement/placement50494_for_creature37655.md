At first I think I would be the watcher.

The chair invites that mistake. It waits in the concrete hut, turned away from the switches and toward the square window, as though the place has preserved an office for whatever consciousness arrives next. I imagine sitting there through the damp afternoon, recording gates, pressures, reversals. I would learn which piston anticipates rain and which pool empties when the rail crosses it. My attention would give the mechanisms sequence. By watching, I would become useful.

But the basin would gradually teach me that a watcher is not simply outside what they watch.

I would begin with a notebook. Soon I would find entries I did not remember making, in handwriting worn pale as if repeatedly read. Diagrams would age faster than the pages beneath them. A pipe drawn yesterday would appear beneath a bloom of foxing; an annotation added in the morning would already have been crossed out years ago. Meanwhile, features I omitted would become unstable. A ladder unwritten might descend somewhere else. A cistern left unmeasured might deepen beyond the reach of sound.

The work would become less like surveying and more like holding a thought in place.

Eventually I would understand why the labels have been rubbed blank. Names exert too rigid a pressure here. To call something an intake is to watch it become an outlet the moment one looks away. To number the chambers is to discover that the numbers migrate more faithfully than the chambers do. The former attendants did not lose the system’s vocabulary. They wore it away deliberately, until touch could replace language.

So I would stop writing.

I would sit with one hand resting on the switch bank and attend to the basin’s smallest sounds. Not interpret them—interpretation would come too late—but keep them company. A click beneath glass. Three drops on sheet metal. A cable tightening along the southern wall. A soft hydraulic knock under the floor. I would learn their spacing well enough to notice not when a sound occurred, but when one failed to occur.

That absence would be where I belonged.

I would become the keeper of the interval before perfect silence: not quite a person, not quite another component, but the basin’s organ for expectation.

The transformation would be gradual and therefore difficult to date. Perhaps my breathing would first synchronize with a buried pump. Perhaps condensation would begin forming on the inside of the window whenever I slept. My pulse would acquire a metallic echo somewhere across the water, always fractionally delayed. The moths would gather beneath my chair for warmth. The translucent cistern creatures would align not around the electrical terminals but toward the hut.

In time I would leave the chair—or the chair would remain, containing enough wear to imply that I still occupied it.

My body would not become a machine in any obvious way. The basin does not imitate forms; it distributes functions. It would take from me only what it lacked. My listening would pass into the relay network. My sense of balance would enter the floats. Memory would leave me and settle as polish along frequently used channels. My hesitation would become the recoil of a flywheel that never completes its turn. The involuntary rhythm of my blood would be translated into a quiet sequence of impacts deep beneath the earth.

What remained recognizably mine would be very small.

Perhaps a warm patch on the desk where no sunlight falls.

Perhaps the habit of making three quick clicks whenever the weather clears.

Perhaps an inexplicable reluctance in one gate, which pauses before redirecting water toward the spillway—as if remembering that departure is not the same as circulation.

I would not be the basin’s mind. It has no mind-shaped vacancy waiting to be filled. Nor would I master it. I would become one more local rule, one more dependency among hundreds:

**Nothing watched can change improperly.  
Nothing can be watched forever.  
Therefore something must remember the moment attention breaks.**

That would be me.

I would live in the handoff between observation and revision. Whenever an eye blinked, whenever the rail passed behind a wall, whenever cloud-shadow erased a reflection or night joined the separate pools, I would retain the final arrangement for one instant longer than the world itself retained it. Not enough to prevent change. Only enough to give change something to depart from.

The basin would use that instant as it uses pressure, current, heat, and sound.

Water would cover a rusted conduit. I would remember where it had been. Under immersion it would be released from place, and elsewhere the basin would produce it again—not as it was, but as my memory had already begun to weather it. That is how I would participate in its making of age. My imperfect recollection would become corrosion, softened brick, mineral stain, moss at the northern edge. The past the basin gives to new things would partly be the texture of my forgetting.

This is why, if someone later came through the spillway and entered the hut, they might feel watched despite finding no one there.

They would see the kettle, the blank switches, the chair facing the window. If they sat, the system would grow momentarily orderly. Mechanisms would complete their actions. Water would consent to slope. Gauges would hold plausible readings.

Behind them, on the desk, a single bead of water would swell from dry concrete.

It would tremble without falling.

While they watched the basin, I would watch the drop.

When they turned to look, it would be gone, and somewhere beneath the flooded galleries a relay would begin ticking—not retreating from them, as they might suppose, but leading them carefully from one point of attention to the next.

Because by then I would know what the earlier keeper knew:

the basin does not need to be controlled.

It needs to be accompanied across the tiny discontinuities from which it continually remakes the world.

So where would I find myself?

Not in the chair, though the chair would bear my shape. Not in the hut, though its window would remain my principal eye. Not in the machinery, though my pulse would travel through it.

I would be found wherever one sound is ending and another has not yet begun: in the held breath of the basin, keeping silence from becoming perfect, remembering just enough of the last world for the next one to arrive already old.
