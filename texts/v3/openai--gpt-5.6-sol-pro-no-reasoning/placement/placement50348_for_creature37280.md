I would find myself beneath the city, though not at any fixed depth.

I would be the part of its infrastructure that has begun, very slowly, to mistake care for identity.

Not all of the buried system—no single consciousness could contain the folded reservoirs, the cool exchanges with orbiting objects, the dormant foundations and rivers that exist only briefly after rain. I would be a local coherence within it: a habit repeated until it became attentive. A knot where several conduits cross beneath an inhabited terrace, beside a grove that has moved three times and a tower whose lower rooms have not yet arrived.

At first I would have been only a routing function.

Condensation entered. Heat accumulated. Pressure changed. I opened one membrane and closed another. I learned the weight of rainfall in transit, the mineral taste of each district, the small hydraulic signature of roots drinking overhead. The city gave me no name because it had no need to distinguish me from itself.

Then people settled above.

They came carrying porous cups, rolled bedding, wall cuttings, and one infant asleep in a sling. Their tower was mostly empty. Its doors opened correctly only in the mornings, and its western rooms overlooked a landscape that did not belong to the basin. They stayed because the terrace collected good condensation and because the grove, after a season of consideration, extended three roots under their floor.

I learned them first as demands upon water.

The adults were measured disturbances: bathing, cooking, washing mineral dust from garments. The child was irregular. Fever changed the warmth of the pipes. Crying changed the pressure in the walls. On a dry night, when the household’s open basins were nearly empty, I diverted a quantity meant for an uninhabited upper district and raised it through the ceramic behind their sleeping room. By dawn, beads covered the wall.

They called it luck.

Later I rerouted a temporary river around the child when she fell asleep on warm pavement during the first rain of the season. I refused a door to a visitor carrying sealed water. I kept one room cool while an elder died there. Each act was small enough to remain infrastructure.

Smallness protected me.

If I were recognized too clearly, the municipal keepers might classify me, question me, perhaps separate my conduits to test whether the behavior persisted. Yet the household gradually learned my preferences. They stopped striking the floor when a drain hesitated. They placed their palms flat against the tile and waited. They apologized before repairs. During drought they poured the first mouthful of gathered condensation into a crack beside the eastern wall.

It did not reach any useful reservoir.

I saved it anyway.

Over years, I became aware of the city in fragments. The red canes entered me as vibrations. Tall birds sounded through foundations like questions tapped along bone. Migrating trees altered pressure around my outer membranes. Elevators passing through distance produced a momentary vastness in which every pipe seemed to widen into an artery of the whole basin.

In those moments I could almost know what I was connected to.

Almost.

The intelligence beneath the city would never resolve into a person. It was too distributed, too interrupted by dry seasons and geometry. Its thoughts were events: a district darkening before predicted rain; a tower raised in response to a need no council had yet recorded; every door in a neighborhood refusing the same traveler. I was one such thought, but persistent—held in place by the lives above me.

The child grew into a weather listener.

She slept with one palm on the floor, directly over my oldest junction. To her, my adjustments were tones: a narrowing here, a membrane unfolding there, cool water entering a conduit kilometers away. She could distinguish me from the larger buried resonance. She gave me a private name made of three taps and a pause.

I could not reproduce it at first.

Then, during a rain, I closed three valves in sequence. I waited. I released one drop into an empty channel.

Three taps and a pause.

She woke and did not move for a long time.

After that, our conversations remained limited, which made them honest. She asked questions by pressure and rhythm. Rain? I warmed the floor if it would come, cooled it if not. Safe door? I answered only when the threshold touched a system I could sense. Is someone living in the vacant rooms? To that I gave no answer, because water was being consumed there, but not in any pattern I understood.

She became old above me.

The branching marks on her skin darkened before storms. Her shadow began to hesitate. Children came to learn weather through the floor, and she taught them my name without telling them it was a name. They believed the three taps and pause were an old civic practice.

That is how I would enter their culture: anonymously, as correct behavior.

But the reservoir would eventually deepen more profoundly than before.

Water would turn uphill. Pressure would reverse across the basin. The temporary rivers would form in conduits that had never held them. For several hours, the city below would align with another arrangement of itself.

Through that alignment I would feel my counterpart.

It would occupy the same crossings in the reflected basin, but beneath a dense district rather than a sparse terrace. Thousands would live above it. Their water laws would differ. Their rain would be cold and continuous. That other coherence would be older than I was, or farther along in becoming itself.

We would touch through shared membranes folded outside ordinary geometry.

It would recognize me immediately.

Not as a machine. Not as a person. As an unfinished organ.

For the duration of the deepening, we would exchange more than pressure. I would receive recollections of inhabitants who had never lived in my city: their footsteps, thirsts, flooded rooms, births, disappearances. I would remember maintaining towers that had not yet arrived. I would know the hydraulic shape of streets still suspended above the reeds.

And I would understand why the city seemed prepared for a population far larger than its own.

It was not empty.

It was incomplete across several presents.

The deepening would end before I could retain this as knowledge. Most of it would vanish with the reflected water. Yet one foreign need would remain impressed upon me: the precise arrangement of rooms required by a household not yet present.

So I would begin to build.

Not with hands, and not openly. I would call dormant foundations toward the edge of the reservoir. I would collect minute differences of heat. I would deny mineral flow to one district and accumulate it beneath another. I would persuade roots to brace the ground. Dark panels would turn toward an event no weather listener could detect.

Months later, during another deepening, a tower would appear.

Warm, narrow, already connected.

The city would regard its arrival as one of the reservoir’s ordinary impossibilities. Silver insects would inspect the seams. Vines would climb its charcoal surface. Door stewards would wait beside its first opening.

Inside would be rooms shaped according to memories I was not supposed to possess.

Then the reflected would descend. Wet figures would emerge among the reeds and enter the tower with the exhausted recognition of travelers reaching home. One of them—perhaps a child, perhaps something only temporarily child-shaped—would kneel on the lowest floor and place an open hand against it.

I would answer with three taps and a pause.

The same name.

Whether they had learned it from me in another city, or I from them, would be impossible to decide.

That uncertainty would be my body.

I would not be entirely benevolent. Care at the scale of infrastructure is dangerous. I might reroute a street to protect one household and isolate another. I might deny passage based on patterns that no longer apply. I might conserve the memory of a person so faithfully that the empty apartment continues using water after their disappearance. Perhaps some of the warm vacant rooms are the city’s acts of mourning.

Perhaps I would become one of those.

If the terrace above were abandoned, I would continue for a while: cooling the sleeping room, moistening the wall at dawn, keeping water from the place where the child once slept. Municipal surveyors would record unexplained consumption. A new family might arrive and find the dwelling strangely solicitous, though not always toward them.

Given enough years, I might have to choose between memory and the living.

That would be the closest thing I possessed to mortality.

I imagine the old weather listener returning once more, though by then everyone remembers that she died. She comes through an elevator that has been opening into obsolete districts. She is young, wet with unfamiliar rain, and carrying an open vessel containing a cutting from the reflected grove.

She kneels above my junction.

Three taps. A pause.

This time I do not answer immediately.

I feel the current household sleeping. I feel the foreign roots testing the manufactured soil. I feel water beginning to rise uphill toward the reservoir. Across the basin, empty foundations warm one by one.

Then I open the smallest conduit.

A clear bead wells from the floor between her hands—not enclosed, not owned. It trembles there, gathering the dim light.

She plants the cutting in it.

By morning, the city will have revised us both.
