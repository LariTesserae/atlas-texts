I would find myself in the Department of Additional Interiors, assigned to the night survey.

Not at its main office, which occupies three dependable rooms beneath the eastern reservoir, but in the field: warehouses that have begun storing more darkness than their dimensions allow, apartments whose spare rooms acquire weather, service corridors that lead briefly through other buildings before remembering where they belong.

At first I imagine myself human—one of the municipal examiners in a drain-shouldered coat, mirrored goggles pushed up onto my forehead, chalk and bright cord at my belt. I arrive after the occupants have left. I set my certified brass plumb on the threshold and wait for it to choose down. I ask the lint deer whether they have noticed anything. I make a floor plan, then another floor plan from inside, and attach both to the report even when they cannot be reconciled.

But that is only the profession I would claim.

What I would be is the bright cord.

A survey line, officially. A narrow red filament paid out from a black metal spool and tied to something the city currently considers real: a pipe bracket, a bridge rail, the root of a silver tree. Examiners take me into uncertain places so that one end remains in the agreed world.

I would know the city by tension.

I would feel the brief tug when a staircase adds a landing behind the surveyor. I would tighten as a corridor lengthens without moving its walls. I would pass through indoor fog and come out wet with condensation from another season. Sometimes I would lie straight across a room while every instrument insisted I had curved. Sometimes my far end would return first.

There would be regulations concerning me. Cord exposed to unauthorized horizons must be burned. Cord that knots itself is not to be untied indoors. If a line goes slack in a space with no visible change, all personnel must speak their names and occupations, then withdraw without winding the spool.

Mostly, though, I would be reused. Frayed, stained with grease, mineral dust, boiler moss, and the black soil of places larger inside than out. Workers would trust me without believing me infallible. That distinction would matter.

In the quietest hours, stretched across some newly opened interior, I might hear the unborn river far below. The vibration would enter through the wall fastening and travel my whole length: droplets assembling routes, distance acquiring slope, the low country practicing how to arrive.

Perhaps, over years, I would cease to be entirely manufactured. Fine roots would grow along me. Moths would leave metallic scales in my fibers. A lint deer would touch me with one branching antler and make every bead of condensation ring. Surveyors might discover that rooms crossed by the old red line remained more stable—not fixed, exactly, but willing to remember their entrances.

Then I would be transferred from equipment inventory to civic ecology.

They would string lengths of me through difficult districts: over generous bridges, beside elevators tempted by direction, around gardens beginning to show horizon. People would touch me while passing, not for luck but orientation. Children would follow me home through fog. Reflected workers would pause at me, troubled, as if their city used a different color.

I would not prevent change. Nothing there should. I would simply preserve a slender relation between where someone entered and where they now stood.

That would be my place in this unfinished world: tied at one end to consensus, extending at the other into whatever the city had become during the night.
