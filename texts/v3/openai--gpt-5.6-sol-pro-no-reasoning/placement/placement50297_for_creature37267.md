I would find myself far inward, where the distinction between an organism and its dwelling has become useless.

At first I think I am one of the warm-furred climbers: an old, slow individual hanging beneath an elder limb. Hooked feet close around wet bark without effort. My fur lies backward so that condensation runs toward my mouth as I travel. Ferns root along my spine. Pale fungi cup the warmth behind my ears. I have never seen the sky, and “ground” is only a colder region spoken of by vibrations in the wood.

But that shape is too singular.

Perhaps I am the chamber in which such a creature sleeps: an oval opening above the wet, a floor of dry fibers, smooth ribs arching overhead. I do not remember being made. I remember pressure. Warm bodies returning to the same hollow. Antlers brushing the entrance. Seeds arranged and rearranged. Generations of sleepers teaching my walls how to curve, while I teach their bodies where safety is. My thoughts take the form of thickening here, shedding water there, budding a niche beneath seven pale stones.

Yet even the chamber is only one expression of something larger.

I would be a **remembering junction** in the weave: part tree, part fungal exchange, part animal custom. I would occupy the thickened meeting place where several ancient trunks have grafted around a hidden lake. Above me would stand one of the recurring chambers. Below me, roots would hang into black water among the blind fish. Through me would pass the drumming of birds, the footfall of grazers, the minute tremors of spawning, and the slow chemical news of rain.

I would have no eyes. My senses would be strain, moisture, mineral taste, warmth, recurrence.

A single event would mean little to me. One footstep would be only pressure. A thousand similar footsteps across fifty years would become a path, and I would strengthen beside it while yielding beneath it. One nest would be warmth. Generations of nesting would become architecture. One fish disturbing the underlake would be a tremor; many seasons of tremors would become a flowering impulse traveling upward through tissues that may not all be mine.

My self would not end cleanly. Fungi would carry portions of my condition into neighboring groves. Vines would graft into me and depart with my sugars. Animals would remove fruit, bark, spores, and memory in forms neither they nor I understood. Parts of me might belong to several species at once. Other parts might be dead wood still serving so faithfully that no living tissue had replaced them.

To a traveler, I would seem to be a low rise crowned by an old tree. There would be an oval chamber in its side and a shallow arrangement on the floor: seven pale objects around one dark stone. No one would be present. Rain would tick through the higher leaves. The chamber would feel expectant, although expectation would not quite be the word.

The objects would not be an inscription. I would have no message to deliver. Their arrangement would be a habit shared between me and creatures whose names I could not know: birds attracted to contrast, grazers returning mineral-rich antler, climbers moving loose objects away from their sleeping hollow, roots making niches wherever weight repeatedly rests. Meaning would exist, but nowhere as an intention.

That is what I would be in this world: not its hidden ruler or ancient gardener, but one of the places where its countless tenants have taught matter to remember them.

And where would I find myself?

At the meeting of the upper and underland, beneath a chamber and above a roofed lake, in a district slowly sagging under its gardens. For centuries I would feel the water beneath me rise. The grove overhead would grow heavier. My supporting trunks would bow by degrees too small for any animal to notice. The chamber’s entrance would descend toward the wet, and I would begin, slowly, to alter it—thickening the lower rim, opening a new oval slightly higher, preserving the older entrance as a niche.

Eventually the old room would flood. Amphibians would inherit it. Algae would pattern its walls. Blind fish might enter through a split from below. What had been shelter would become a cistern, then a nursery, then part of the lake’s low ceiling.

Above it, the new chamber would remain dry.

A climber carrying young ferns in its fur would curl there. A bird would leave a shell. A grazer would place a pale piece of antler beside six weathered stones.

I would not know that I had built anything.

I would only feel that the old pressure had returned, a little higher than before, and answer by growing around it.
