I would find myself where the paved country becomes least certain: near the Great Stair, but not among its hereditary attendants. My dwelling would stand some distance out in the shallows, a narrow roofless enclosure reached by a causeway that appears straight only when no one is walking on it.

From the White Hand, my house would seem close enough to call to. From the Hollow Beast, it would lie beyond three orchards and an afternoon. According to its route rope, it would belong to the loop.

The enclosure would contain no bed, kiln, or household shrine. Only shelves of black bowls, pegs of bone, bundles of toe-thread, tablets wrapped in damp cloth, and a waist-high ceramic table scored with paired lines. In its center would be a shallow basin. The basin would usually be empty, though fish shadows would pass through it at noon.

I would be a **Keeper of Unchosen Edges**.

Ordinary Keepers attend boundaries that have already declared their habits: the door requiring a burden, the wet wall opening into yesterday, the stair that alters its destination when counted. My work would concern edges not yet committed to being edges: a hairline in glaze that might be a crack or a horizon; the place where shade almost becomes a room; a pause in conversation that may have acquired depth; the interval between a person and the self descending toward them.

I would not determine what such things truly were. Truth would be too forceful a tool. I would keep them from deciding prematurely.

People would bring me uncertain objects.

A widow might arrive with a cup that had belonged to her wife. Each time she raised it, someone in another compound became thirsty. A child might bring a reed whistle that produced a doorway instead of a note. A Distance Carrier might lay down a cord whose short strand had begun dreaming of the destination. An Under-Gazer might bring a reversed tool received from below, insisting that it fit a hand no one in either country possessed.

I would set each object on the scored table and sit beside it until its possibilities became distinct without becoming exclusive. This might take an hour, a season, or the length of a marriage. I would test it without exhausting it: cover it, wet it, carry it backward, address it by an incorrect name, leave food near it, show it to a frog. My findings would be recorded as permissions rather than definitions:

**This cup may be used by those willing to share thirst.**

**This whistle must not be played where a wall is desired.**

**The cord arrives before the carrier, but only when welcomed separately.**

**The tool is left-handed in both directions. Let it remain unemployed.**

My title would sound contemplative, but the labor would be physical. I would spend much of my life kneeling in hot water, running my fingertips along seams. I would carry bowls of collected coolness to fresh cracks, not to close them but to slow their choosing. I would abrade one side of a threshold while polishing the other. I would paint half a wall blue and remove the pigment grain by grain whenever the promise held too strongly.

I would have trained first as a Breaker.

As a child, I would have loved the certainty of grinding: the pestle descending, the shard becoming powder, the dependable fact that what had been sharp could be made otherwise. My family would expect me to become a sacred finisher. But during my maturity journey I would return from the Great Stair by a route that none of the three Distance Carriers had measured.

I would descend alone with two bowls, as custom required. Partway down, I would meet myself ascending.

The other would be older, though not in the local manner. Their face would bear the looseness of weather. There would be a scar across their mouth, pale grit in their hair, and mud dried in the whorls of their fingertips. They would carry no matching bowls. Instead, they would hold a small green fruit split by its own growth.

We would not speak.

They would set the fruit between us. I would set down one bowl. Then we would pass.

When I reached the bottom, I would find myself at the top of the stair holding both bowls. The fruit would be gone. Yet for the rest of my life, everything I deliberately finished would begin again elsewhere.

A pot ground to temper would reappear intact in a stranger’s kitchen. A dangerous corner rounded smooth would sharpen beneath a sleeping child. A dead person’s name, powdered and mixed into mortar, would be heard spoken from a fish’s warm interior.

The Breakers would dismiss me gently. Not as punishment: my hands would simply no longer be safe for conclusions.

That failure would become my vocation.

People might think I preserved ambiguity because I revered it. In fact I would fear it. I would envy masons their walls, cooks their covered pots, and even the plaza-bound workers their inexhaustible first brick. I would want one thing in my life to pass wholly from unfinished to finished and remain there.

Perhaps this is why I would keep one private object beneath the table: the bowl I had set on the stair.

Inside it would lie a little true mud.

Not enough to plant. Not enough to soften a slab. Barely enough to darken the lines of my palm when mixed with condensation. I would never remember retrieving it. Some mornings it would be dry; some mornings it would contain a green shoot; some mornings the bowl would be clean, and a new wrinkle would have appeared beside my eye.

I would conceal this from the Loosened Ones as carefully as from those who feared them. I would distrust both certainty that mud was salvation and certainty that it was ruin. Naturalness, too, could become an assigned abundance. Even freedom might harden if named too often.

Animals would frequent my enclosure. Frogs would cling beneath the shelves. A dragonfly would remain over the empty basin for years, shifting only when someone lied about what they wanted. My companion would be a jointed fish called Peg, kept in a long wet trough along the eastern wall. In water, Peg would have silver flesh and a clouded eye. Lifted out, it would become dark wood joined by seven leather hinges.

There would once have been eight.

I would wear the missing hinge on a cord at my throat. Peg would show no injury, but whenever I passed the Great Stair, something inside the fish would knock once against the trough.

I would have kin, though not a conventional household. My dream-siblings would be scattered widely: an Hour Painter in the western cistern district; a silent child from the Hollow Beast; a woman below the clean edge who claimed that I was her descending self. We would all dream the same route—a long gallery of unpainted walls ending at a door swollen with rain.

None of us would open it.

We would wake under different darknesses and send one another accounts by jar, knot, and mirrored board. Over time, our descriptions would diverge. In mine, the door would be red. The child would insist it was made of bark. The woman below would say there was no door, only an opening packed with roots.

This disagreement would make us siblings more surely than resemblance.

My public service would become most important when a custom began to fail.

Not break—breaking would be manageable—but fail by becoming too obedient. A burden-door might begin accepting anything as a burden: a bracelet, a held breath, a memory of carrying water. Soon people would cross accidentally and return to rooms they had not yet left. Or a painted wall separating hours might become so exact that husband and wife could no longer exchange meals through their transition alcove. A contradiction preserved too faithfully could become a prison.

I would arrive with no insignia except a collar made from unglazed fragments. I would listen to everyone involved, including the door, wall, bowls, shadows, and any animal that had established an obligation there. Then I would introduce a small, disciplined uncertainty.

Perhaps I would chip one letter from the lintel record.

Perhaps I would add a counterfeit fingerprint to the plaster.

Perhaps I would ask a child to cross carrying a sleeping lizard and refuse to say which of them bore the other.

Usually this would loosen the custom enough for life to pass through again.

For this reason, some would call me a repairer. Others would call me a saboteur in courteous language. Keepers of Edges would value my reports but dislike inviting me home. Breakers would leave offerings at my causeway: smooth stones, although there were no stones in the country.

Once in my life, I would be summoned to the raw edge.

One of the clear drops between the countries would have fallen.

No one would hear it strike. No pool would receive it. But above and below, the corresponding gap in the row would remain. Through that tiny vacancy, the glare would no longer reflect correctly. People looking into it would see neither country.

They would see weather.

Clouds crossing. Reeds bending through successive positions. Dark water rising against a bank of unmade earth. A tree so crowded with growth that its branches broke beneath fruit. Then winter—not coolness collected in bowls, but a vast condition in which heat had genuinely departed.

The Under-Gazers would argue that the opening must be sealed. The Loosened Ones would gather in secret, hoping to widen it. Priests would remind the absent drop of its route. Hour Painters would surround the gap with blue to make its absence remain true. Breakers would stand ready with powder. Nobody would agree whether the falling had already happened, was still happening, or waited on the other side of sight.

I would be lowered toward it on reed ropes, carrying my black bowl.

As I approached the vacancy, condensation would form on my skin. Not the country’s clear, patient moisture, but droplets of differing sizes, some joining others, some evaporating before they fell. My shade frame would tremble. For the first time in my life, I would feel air cross my face and continue elsewhere.

I would understand then that I had never feared change itself.

I had feared change without obligation: water that did not return, names that could be forgotten, fruit ripening where nobody had assigned it, a world not faithful enough to preserve those who depended on it.

But I would also understand what our faithfulness cost. Nothing here could heal by ceasing to be wounded. Nothing could ripen except by custom. Grief could be tempered, mortared into the house, made useful as shade—but never weathered down by passing years. Every contradiction was kept. So was every injury.

I would hold the bowl beneath the missing drop.

Something would enter it—not water, not mud, but the brief shadow of a moving cloud.

When they raised me, the bowl would appear empty. Yet wherever I carried it, shadows would shift a finger’s width. People beneath awnings would look up. Lizards would close their mouths. A marsh bird would make one clear, impossible call that traveled beyond the next house.

I would bring the bowl back to my roofless enclosure and set it beside the hidden mud.

I would not pour them together.

Not yet.

Instead I would record:

**This darkness does not remain where placed.**

**It cools nothing reliably.**

**It may be carried without becoming a burden.**

**Under it, unfinished things continue. Finished things may also continue.**

Then I would go out to the causeway with my soft bronze tool. I would work at a newly sharp edge, neither rounding it nor preserving it, but cutting into it a minute irregularity—too small to be a doorway, too deliberate to be damage.

Condensation would gather there.

For a long while, the drop would hold.

Then, while I was looking directly at it, it would fall.
