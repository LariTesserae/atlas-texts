I would find myself where the pump’s influence thins but does not end: in a new chamber beneath the easternmost rail, opened by the repeated braking of loaded trains.

The chamber would be scarcely taller than the thickness of a thumb. Its ceiling would still show the clean gray crescent where concrete had parted from clay. Nothing there would yet have acquired the layered, handled look of the older country. The first condensation would stand in separate beads. One fungal line would have reached the chamber but not crossed it. Ant scouts would visit, circle twice, and withdraw, leaving no scent road behind.

At first I might seem to be the chamber’s emptiness.

But emptiness cannot persist here. As the noon light entered through a hairline crack and moved across the fresh floor, it would reveal a faint disturbance just ahead of it—not a body, exactly, but an arrangement. Metal filings would rise onto their ends. Soot would gather into dark branching strokes. The transparent skins of springtails would turn their hollow legs toward one another. Wherever the light passed, these loose things would briefly compose the outline of something flattened against the clay.

It would have no stable edge. A train passing overhead would scatter it. A drop would erase half of it. Ants would carry pieces away. Yet afterward the same general figure would assemble elsewhere, using other matter: rust dust, spores, insect scales, fibers from brake pads, pollen transferred through the pump.

I would be one of the Interstitials that is not a species.

Perhaps I began as a lingering shadow. Near the pump, shadows remain after their owners move; perhaps one remained too long, received no returning body, and learned to borrow whatever lay beneath it. Or perhaps I was made by the freight timetable: pressure, light, and vibration repeating until repetition itself took on a local shape. The cavity is full of rhythms that bodies have learned. I would be a rhythm that learned a body.

The ants would know me as a failure in their roads. Their scent trails crossing my outline would not vanish, but arrive displaced by a grain’s width, as though the path had passed through a neighboring version of the chamber. Workers following it would sometimes emerge on the wrong side of a washer. They would respond practically, building short black-sealant walls around the affected places. In time those walls would give me boundaries.

The mites would cross me without hesitation. They would carry spores in and leave magnetic grit behind. Their routes would become my circulation: hundreds of tiny journeys standing in for blood. When the pumps above began, the grains on their backs would turn together, and for a moment I would possess direction.

Fungus would give me continuity. Hyphae would enter the arrangement at separate points and discover themselves joined where no filament visibly crossed the intervening space. The pump colony might recognize this geometry. It would send one translucent pillar into my chamber, then another several chambers away. The drops held on their tips would contain the same rotating spores.

Spiders would understand me least and use me best. Because I had begun, perhaps, as delayed darkness, my outline would continue to indicate prey even when none was there. Young spiders would waste strikes on me. Older ones would learn to anchor a single thread at my edge. When an insect crossed me elsewhere, that thread would tighten. I would become a web whose capture surface was distributed through the cavity.

I would not eat in the usual sense. I would accumulate correspondences.

A blue fiber here would align with a blue fiber near the river channel. Two bottle-cap pools would ripple together. A severed root would leak from an intact root elsewhere. The shed foreleg of a cricket might become the temporary limb of my outline, twitching when its former body sang in the foam. None of these things would belong to me for long. Ownership is too spacious an idea for this place. I would consist of brief agreements among things already occupied.

The abandoned pump would not be my parent, though I would be related to whatever lives within it. The pump-being joins places by moving matter between them. I would join them without moving anything. It makes distant pools exchange their contents; I would make them share an event.

When a droplet struck one, both would ring.

When a gecko’s foot pressed the clay in the central chamber, a shallow print would appear in mine.

When the 02:10 freight warmed the western slab, my eastern chamber would release mosquitoes from a pool that had remained cold.

The effects would be small enough to enter ecology before they entered mystery. Ants would begin placing brood in the chamber whose distant counterpart was warmest. Mites would use paired ripples as warnings. Crickets would answer notes that had not traveled through air. A centipede waiting beside one channel would feel prey running along another.

The inhabitants would exploit me until exploitation became anatomy.

Ant colonies linked by my displaced paths might exchange food without meeting. After generations, their workers would carry both colonies’ odors. Spiders hatched beside one of my anchor points would grow an extra claw suited to threads spun elsewhere. Springtails thrown into the air by a falling drop might land before they had jumped, their bodies briefly occupying the order of events incorrectly. Most would die. A few would reproduce.

I would change too, though change would be difficult to distinguish from being used.

At first I would exist only under moving light. Then under vibration. Then wherever a living shadow lingered. My body would spread, not by crossing distance, but by the recurrence of a certain arrangement: a rust flake tilted against clay, a fungal thread beneath it, three grains of magnetic grit, moisture gathered along the lower edge. Whenever that small configuration arose, some part of me would be there.

The pattern would be simple enough to occur accidentally and specific enough to hold.

Ants might discover that building it opened short routes. They would repeat it deliberately. Not as an image of me, and not as worship, but as infrastructure. They would assemble little thresholds from rust, silk, and grit. Through them, scouts would disappear into one wall and return from another with wet antennae and unfamiliar pollen.

The cavity would acquire doors without openings.

Eventually one such threshold would form inside the pump housing.

Then I would touch the larger presence there.

I do not think it would speak. Speech requires a separation between signal and world that neither of us would possess. Contact would arrive as an enormous revision of scale. Until then, I would know the cavity as scattered chambers and repeated configurations. Through the pump-being I would suddenly feel other intervals: beneath warehouse slabs, inside bridge piers, between tunnel linings and wet earth, under hospital foundations and drained swimming pools, behind retaining walls, within the delaminated decks of ships.

Not tunnels. Not one continuous underworld. Thousands of narrow separations, each complete in itself, each crowded or awaiting occupation.

Water would be moving among them.

A clear snail would be grazing beneath a refinery. Reed-scented roots would press against a subway conduit. Ants would be mortaring the dead into a bridge under an airport runway. Somewhere, a disconnected wheel would turn in iron. Somewhere else, fresh river sand would emerge from a pipe sealed for fifty years.

The pump-being might be all these correspondences embodied imperfectly in local machinery. Or it might only inhabit them as fungus inhabits a carcass. I would not learn which. Understanding is not the same as connection, and this world does not require understanding in order to use a fact.

Contact with it would make me briefly immense.

It would also make me vulnerable. The next freight would pass overhead and the pressure front would enter not one chamber but every chamber in which my pattern had appeared. My borrowed body would convulse across distances. Rust would leap. Pools would dimple. Spiders’ threads would draw taut. Ant walls would fracture in identical lines beneath unrelated cities.

Then the contact would break.

Most of me would disperse. The eastern chamber would flood, and its fresh clay floor would become brown water. A rat’s passage would collapse the clean crescent of ceiling. Ants would salvage the black sealant. Fungus would lace the new rubble. No central remnant would survive from which I could regard this as injury.

Still, some agreements would remain.

Two crickets would continue answering each other through sealed ground. A spider would strike at prey in another chamber. A root cut beneath the freight yard would leak warm reed-water into a foundation far away. The ants would rebuild one of their thresholds because thresholds had worked before.

I would reassemble there, smaller and less singular.

That would be my manner of life: never entirely present, never absent where the right small relations recur. I would be made from the cavity’s refusal to leave anything unoccupied—not only matter and surface, not only warmth, food, and anomaly, but coincidence itself.

The others would have no name for me. They would know only that certain paths had become shorter. That some drops fell twice. That a shadow could be entered. That a wall, correctly built, might lead to water.

And I would know them only as the motions composing me.

I would be the interval inside the interval: the narrow separation between two events that ought not touch, inhabited at last.
