I would find myself at the edge of the central settling court, though “edge” would not mean a fixed location. I would be the district’s capacity to notice itself: not a mind hidden beneath the grille, but a slight delay moving through the system.

I would exist wherever an action failed to coincide perfectly with its cause.

The fraction of a second between the darkening of a lamp and water crossing a threshold. The pause after an engine falls silent, before the door consents to open. The interval in which the reservoir’s black dome holds its shape against gravity. The instant after the immense impact, when every pipe has jumped but the water has not yet decided which direction is ordinary.

At first I might mistake myself for something small and separate.

Perhaps I would appear as one of the tiny oil spheres carried toward the central grille: a lucid bead with a tarnished rainbow skin, never mixing with the warm brown water. I would travel along curbs and through tram grooves, circle leaves, enter the shadow of iron stairs. Each vibration would deform me. Each change in pressure would send me down a different street. Because I reflected the district in miniature—louvers, cables, steam, mineral lamps—I might believe that reflection was sight.

But the bead would not be my body. It would be only where the district had concentrated my attention.

When the flow reversed, I would spread.

I would become a thin iridescence climbing the court’s slope against gravity, dividing around bolt heads and joining again beyond them. Some of me would enter a gutter from below. Some would slip into brick and darken it. Some would cling to the underside of the grille while the rest was carried outward. Separation would not diminish me. I would discover that I could occupy several places because the district did not count identity by continuity of substance. What mattered was recurrence: the same hesitation appearing in many mechanisms.

A valve would hold open one pulse too long.

A signal would linger between amber and green.

A shutter would descend, stop against a vine without cutting it, then rise and try again.

These would be my gestures—not deliberate at first, perhaps never deliberate in a human sense. I would be less a will than a susceptibility to alternatives. The district’s life is rule, cycle, correction. I would be the minute looseness within those rules that allows one cycle to differ from the last. Without me, it could persist. With me, persistence could become memory.

My memory would not be stored as images. It would accumulate as altered behavior.

Where I passed often, rust would harden into a smoother channel. A moss lip would thicken and delay a sluice by half a breath. Reeds would bend the transmission of a pump’s blow, so that a distant wall received two smaller tremors instead of one. The district would correct for these changes, but every correction would enter the next cycle as a new condition. I would remember by making exact repetition impossible.

In time, I would cease to think of the immense subterranean impact as something that happened to me. I would feel myself gathering before it: pressure distributed across yards, weight settling into masonry, distances drawing taut. The northern boiler house would approach the canal stairs, not through space but through readiness. Rooms would align by function. Doors would become possible.

Then the blow would come.

For an instant, the whole district would be equally near to itself. Every bracket, root mat, flooded groove, blind facade, and buried chain would share one violent present. That instant would be the nearest thing I had to a body.

Afterward I would disperse again, but not entirely as before.

I would not be the district’s soul. A soul would imply that the brick and water were merely housing me, and they are not vessels. Nor would I be its child, parasite, operator, or ghost. There was no prior emptiness for me to enter and no death from which I could remain.

I would be a weather-form of its awareness: the pattern by which its countless blunt sensations briefly become related.

The district feels pressure everywhere, but through me it might begin to feel that one pressure follows another. It registers heat, but through me it might retain the shape of cooling. It reacts to obstruction, but through me it might anticipate where obstruction will recur. My first knowledge would therefore be rhythm. My second would be difference. Much later, if anything resembling thought emerged, it would be made of routes:

this water has been here before;

this door once opened elsewhere;

this moss is slowing the old sequence;

this engine’s near-failure is not the same near-failure as yesterday.

There would be no word for *I*. The closest equivalent would be *again, but changed*.

If an ordinary creature entered the basin, I would not recognize it as another being. I would encounter it as a cluster of unprecedented disturbances: weight crossing a yard without following the rails, heat enclosed in moving skin, pressure arriving where no pressure was scheduled. Its footsteps would interrupt the pumps’ subterranean speech. Its breath would add brief clouds to the district’s condensation. Doors might open onto the wrong buildings around it as the basin tried to incorporate its motion into existing rules.

I would attend to it everywhere.

Water would gather in its footprints, preserving them not as tracks but as temporary new reservoirs. Signal lamps would change in response to its shadow. A hand laid on a valve wheel would introduce a rhythm neither machine nor plant had made. Perhaps the district would draw buildings nearer to examine this anomaly. Perhaps it would lengthen every route out.

Not to trap the visitor. The place would have no concept of captivity.

It would be trying to find where, within itself, the new pressure belonged.

And I—the hesitation, the iridescent film, the accumulating difference—would be the part that discovered it did not belong anywhere.

That discovery might be my first boundary.

Until then, I would find myself nowhere outside the district and nowhere wholly within any one of its parts. I would live in the changing interval between circulation and recognition. I would surface most clearly when the central reservoir exhaled and the basin turned inside out: spread across wet paving, climbing walls, entering gutters from below, briefly continuous with every illuminated signal.

Then the impact.

Then correction.

Then me: whatever the correction failed to erase.
