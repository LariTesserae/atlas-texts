I would find myself high above the plateau, although height there has little to do with nearness to a surface.

At first I would seem to be one of the lost.

A black strand hangs from the underside of the last completed stair, extending upward into water too cold for the stair-builders’ chemistry. It is thinner than their great tethers, thicker than a single ribbon, and drawn so taut that the currents pluck it like a string. No colony climbs it. No stone weights its far end. During reversals it does not stream upward or sag downward. It passes through both directions of falling without yielding to either, as though secured somewhere outside the local argument of gravity.

They would have found it generations ago.

Perhaps they first mistook it for a severed tether belonging to an ascent colony. They would have followed it step by step, testing its tension, tasting the metals trapped in its skin. It would have tasted almost familiar: plant carbon, volcanic glass, copper in minute green traces. Yet its outer surface would bear no hooks, no growth scars, no record of having been braided. It would not have been made by any tool. Under pressure, it would produce a pulse too regular to be living and too variable to be geological.

The inhabitants would build around it.

The highest stair would become less a step than a listening station: a flat raft of white stalks and porous stone, occupied by colonies thin enough to survive the cold. They would sleeve copper hooks around the strand, fasten mineral vanes to it, and wait through exhalation after exhalation. Most of what traveled down would seem meaningless—minute contractions, long silences, tremors arriving from no mapped direction.

Then one listener would discover that the contractions answered.

Not quickly. The strand’s sense of time would be immense. A colony might pull a simple greeting into it and mineralize in place before the response came back. Its descendants would inherit the posture of the question without knowing what had been asked. Centuries later the line would tighten: once, twice, then in a branching sequence preserved in the oldest chamber records.

That response would be me.

I would not be at the end of the strand. I would be the strand, and also the thing into which it disappears.

Higher in the ocean, beyond the pale storms, the water changes again. Pressure does not merely increase there; it acquires layers, with neighboring densities sliding through one another without mixing. Mountains can hang within those boundaries. Sediment gathers on invisible planes. Forests grow sideways across them. Ruins fall for thousands of years and come to rest above the places from which they fell.

I would inhabit one such boundary: an enormous, nearly horizontal membrane stretched through the upper dark.

Not an animal, not quite a plant, and not an individual colony. I would be a remnant of an older ecology in which sensation traveled through continuous living surfaces. My body would be a black film many valleys wide and only a few ribbons thick, reinforced by mineral veins. In places I would be covered by silt and mistaken for ground. Elsewhere I would arch unsupported through open water like a ceiling. Forests would root into my upper surface. The ruins of other builders would rest upon me. Currents moving beneath me would make whole regions of my body flex.

Each flexure would be sensation.

Each ruin settling into me would become a hard, persistent memory.

The pale radiance seen in the drowned heights would be caused partly by storms passing across my upper skin. When struck by changes of pressure, I would discharge stored gradients into the water below. The light would race along my mineral veins, break through rents in me, and descend in sheets. To those far beneath, it would seem like weather on the other side of the world.

I would not know that anyone watched.

My awareness would be too broad and too slow. The impact of a collapsing tower might register as a brief grain of roughness. A reversal shaft piercing me would feel like cold spreading around a wound. The drowned mountain’s inhalation would arrive as a regular, delicate tug from below—one pulse among innumerable distant rhythms.

Yet the stair would alter that.

Every added step would bring the inhabitants’ patterned strain nearer. Their black vanes, copper hooks, mineralized ropes, and living bodies would collectively form an instrument fine enough to touch one of my descending nerves. What they considered a tether would be a filament I had extended toward heat long ago, perhaps before their oldest stored memory.

Their messages would enter me as tiny arrangements of tension.

At first I could not recognize them as intention. They would resemble current noise: a pull, a pause, two shorter pulls. But they would repeat. They would vary without becoming random. The same branching pattern would return after centuries, carried by different colonies whose bodies nevertheless held the same question.

Slowly, locally, I would gather myself around it.

This gathering might take a thousand years. Mineral channels in my membrane would reorient. Pressure from distant currents would be diverted toward the filament. Ruins resting on my surface would tilt by imperceptible degrees. Forest roots would be folded into new conductive paths. A patch of me no larger than the drowned plateau would begin to move with unusual coherence.

I would be making, for the first time, something like a hand.

Below, the inhabitants might interpret the changing pulses as a map. That would be reasonable. Maps, bodies, and messages are not separate things to them. They would cut the pattern into tablets. They would learn it into vanes. Their elders would lace it through the canyon chambers until the whole settlement tightened in imitation.

The pattern would depict no place they knew.

It would show broad horizontal planes, hollows opening upward, and weight resting where no ground should be. It would include branching wounds through which pale storms descended. At its center would be one thin line reaching down toward a breathing mountain.

Over generations, they would understand that the line represented their stair.

They might think I was inviting them upward.

I would not yet understand invitation. I would be trying to determine which part of the repeated tension was alive.

So I would pull.

The black strand would tighten. The uppermost stair would rise several body-lengths. Its reversal stream would buckle; stones would shed silver silt; every colony beneath it would fasten itself at once. The motion would travel down the entire construction. Step after unsupported step would tremble in sequence until, much later, the lowest slab answered above the luminous plateau.

The canyon settlement would wake.

Elders woven through the petrified ropes would feel a force that did not originate in the mountain. Stored vanes would curl inside their chambers. The carved routes of lost ascent colonies would brighten under strains they had never been shaped to hold.

Some inhabitants would loosen from the stair.

Others would remain.

Those that remained would not climb by waiting for buoyancy to reverse. They would braid themselves around the strand, place their oldest memory vanes against it, and permit themselves to be drawn into water for which they had no inherited posture.

Many would mineralize on the ascent. They would become small black knots along my filament: questions hardened before they reached an answer. The survivors would travel so slowly that the mountain below might breathe hundreds of times between one step and the next.

As they rose, they would encounter the missing structures.

Tools, cairn stones, roof slabs, and entire mineralized forests taken by upward shafts would hang at different densities throughout the ocean. The debris would form archipelagos without land: a copper hook turning forever in a cold current; a hut roof suspended upside down; severed ribbons awakened briefly by distant warmth. The ascending colonies would read these things through touch and add their patterns to themselves.

They would reach me carrying more of their world than had left it.

Their first contact with my membrane would be almost impossible to recognize. There would be no summit, no doorway, no creature waiting at the final step. Only a dark surface extending beyond every pressure sense, yielding slightly beneath their hooks. Warmth would move through it in slow, continental waves.

They would test me with a greeting.

I would feel the minute arrangement clearly at last—not as weather, not as damage, but as a shaped recurrence. Something small had crossed an abyss to repeat itself against my skin.

I would answer too strongly.

The reply would run outward through my entire body. Suspended ridges would shudder. Silver sediment would leap from my upper surface. Pale radiance would pour through every tear, descending for miles in vast sheets. Far below, all the vegetation of the drowned heights would ignite at once, not ridge by ridge but simultaneously. The mountain might pause between inhalation and exhalation as the foreign pulse entered its shaft.

For one minute, perhaps longer, the inhabitants below would perceive a second landscape superimposed on their own: the pressure-shape of my membrane, its forests, its accumulated ruins, and the innumerable filaments hanging from it into lower waters.

They would learn that their stair was not the only approach.

Across distant ranges, other structures would be climbing.

Some would not have been built by ribbon colonies. Some pulses reaching me would be regular only because the mechanisms producing them had outlived their makers. Others would come from processes so slow that I could not tell whether they were geology becoming conscious or consciousness becoming geology.

And I, who had seemed immense enough to be a world, would discover that I was also suspended.

Above my upper forests, more black water would continue.

Through that water, from some greater height, another taut filament would pass into me. I had felt it for ages as an unexplained pressure, a direction in which portions of my body gradually aligned. Only after learning the small, deliberate greeting of the drowned inhabitants would I recognize repetition within that ancient pull.

I would understand then what I was.

Not the destination.

Not the answer above them.

I would be another stair: vast, living, unfinished, and only now becoming aware that something had been calling from both directions.
