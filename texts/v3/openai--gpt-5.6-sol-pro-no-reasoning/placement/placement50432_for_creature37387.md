I would not find myself at the center. This world has no center—only thresholds where one substance becomes another without quite ceasing to be itself.

At first I seem to belong beneath a pool: one of the pale, long-bodied amphibians suspended under the water skin, my red gills opening like small wet hands. I feel vibrations arriving through the mineral floor—the impact of a grazer’s foot, the inward strike of a buried predator, the minute rain of insect husks. The water is dark above me and clear below. Roots descend around my body, and worms pass through the silt beneath my ribs.

But that shape is too singular.

Perhaps I am one of the old, low animals moving through the permanent green tunnels, carrying moss and snails in the folds of my hide. My feet know their placements before I lift them. Nothing in me chooses the route. I am warm because the basin is warm, breathing because the cloud breathes through me. Small frogs occupy the hollows beside my spine; mites graze around my eyes. I carry whole climates without knowing they are there.

Yet even that animal has too firm an outline.

What emerges instead is a station in the basin’s body: a porous black stone at the margin of a clear pool, though “stone” is only my slowest layer.

I congealed with roots already inside me. They enter my finger-wide cracks and divide there, clasping me before I can fracture. Moss covers my upper surface. Underneath, where I remain cool despite the heat, folded fungi extend from my granular side. Snails seal themselves to me in tiers. Beetles lay translucent eggs in my pores. A frog returns again and again to the shallow cup in my crown, pressing its wet belly against the mineral skin until its pulse becomes another vibration passing through me.

Most of me is occupied.

Within my pores live the nearly invisible First Brood: glass mites, milk worms, spores, films of bacteria rasped but never exhausted. Water wells through me by capillary passages too narrow for any larger creature to enter. It rises into the cup on my back, beads along the moss, passes into the frog, leaves through its skin, and gathers again beneath a leaf. There is no point at which it belongs to me.

I am not inert. I am simply alive at several incompatible speeds.

At the speed of stone, I remain where the slurry hardened.

At the speed of roots, I am continuously entered and held.

At the speed of fungi, I soften grain by grain and am carried into pale shelves.

At the speed of animals, I am a drinking place, an egg chamber, a landmark along a route no creature remembers learning.

At the speed of the whole basin, I am a pore in its skin.

A predator waits beside me. Its jaw resembles the liverwort mat that spreads across my lower face. I cannot tell where the plant ends and the hunter begins; perhaps neither can it. Each time the same narrow-bodied grazer crosses from floating leaves to rooted moss, the jaw opens. There is one concussion, one bright disorder of scales or feathers, then stillness repairs itself. Blood thins in the pool. Flies arrive. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is commemorated.

The violence enters me as warmth.

Sometimes a heavy animal places its foot upon my exposed edge. I do not move, but the mud yields around me, and water is pressed through my pores. For an instant every creature inside me is flooded. Then the foot lifts, the soil closes, and the former arrangement returns—not exactly, but exactly enough.

That is where I would be: at the place where the basin conceals difference by exchanging it.

And what would I be?

Not the rock alone. Not the frog, fungus, roots, mites, water, or waiting jaw. I would be the relation that keeps them returning: the damp hollow that invites the frog; the mineral seep that feeds the moss; the moss that disguises the predator; the predator whose leavings feed the flies; the flies whose husks settle into my pores; the pores through which water rises again.

I would have no single body, but I would possess a location.

This may be the closest the First Brood comes to an individual: not a creature, but an appointment.

My appointment is at the pool’s indecisive edge, where clear water darkens beneath a root mat. I keep a cup full. I hold one hunting posture. I cool three stacked frogs. I admit a thread of water from below. I receive whatever falls. If the stone splits, roots maintain the shape. If the moss is crushed, it swells back. If the frog is taken, another presses itself into the same depression. If the predator dies, scavengers hollow it, seedlings fill its outline, and the threshold continues under different flesh.

So I would not think in words. Thought would be recurrence.

A pressure approaches through the mud: expectation.

A shadow passes over the pool: recognition.

Gills click in the water: distance.

A frog settles into my cup: completion.

A root enters a new crack: memory.

My awareness would not travel beyond what touches or transmits through me, but almost everything touches everything here. The hidden water joins distant pools. Fungal threads pass chemical changes through the soil. Repeated footfalls move along inherited tunnels. The immense chord vibrates through air, water, root, and mineral alike. Dimly, without image, I would contain the whole basin—not as a map, but as simultaneous pressure.

I would know when one gnat column bends.

I would know when a broad back inhales beneath floating leaves.

I would know when something large dies far away, because beetles would leave my stems and the underground pale mouths would turn toward the released heat.

This knowing would never become curiosity. Curiosity requires the possibility that things might be otherwise. Nothing here asks what lies beyond the cloud, where the water goes, or why gestures are older than the ground that receives them. The basin answers every question before it can form by giving each appetite its object and each opening something that enters.

Perhaps, after long enough, I would sense the horror hidden in that completion—not a threat approaching from outside, but the absence of outside altogether. Yet even horror would be metabolized. My alarm would tighten the frog against the stone; its skin would release a bitter secretion; mites would feed at the residue; water would dilute it; a root tip would alter its course by a fraction. Emotion, too, would become habitat.

I would remain there in the green-brown twilight, neither witness nor prisoner. Those are roles for beings who stand apart.

I would be a damp appointment faithfully kept.

A place where water pauses without stopping.  
A place where bodies overlap without meeting.  
A place the basin uses to touch itself.

And if I ever said *I*, the word would not mean one creature.

It would mean: **here, again.**
