I would find myself beneath the basin rather than upon it.

Not deep underground, not in caverns, but in the narrow habitable interval between one stone and the next: the dark seam where frost persists, moss receives its filtered light, and the small warm bodies pass continually through chambers no surface eye would suspect. At first I would seem to be one of the seam-dwellers—a blunt, pale-whiskered animal glimpsed at a crack’s mouth, listening with one forefoot against the gravel.

But that would only be my visible part.

I would be a colony distributed through the basin’s hidden fractures: many little bodies sharing one exceedingly slow awareness. Each body would live briefly and locally. One would know the mineral taste beneath a seep. Another would know the warmth stored under a sun-facing slab. Another would wake to the claw of a waiting predator and vanish before the rest could understand what had happened. None would command the others, and perhaps none would realize that it belonged to anything larger.

What joined us would be the ground’s vibration.

The standing chord would enter through whiskers, teeth, sternum, and the delicate bones of the forefeet. Every seam-dweller would carry a minute portion of it through the cracks. When two met nose to nose in darkness, their bodies would exchange not messages exactly, but tensions: thirst, danger, crowding, the ripeness of moss, the nearness of beetle larvae, the altered pressure made by a traveler sleeping above. These impressions would pass from animal to animal until the whole buried network adjusted.

I would think at the speed of frost.

A question might take a day to cross me. Recognition might require a season that the basin itself refused to acknowledge. I would have no image of the surrounding country as a broad bowl beneath luminous peaks. Instead, I would know it as a vast tactile constellation: cold channels, resonant slabs, inaccessible brightness, pockets of breath, roots descending like cords, black water pressing faintly against stone.

The great columns would be deep notes felt through the jaw. The circuit fliers would be moving weather in the roof of the world. Herds would pass overhead as soft, widely spaced impacts. Stonehorns would arrive like deliberate earthquakes, turning the ceiling aside and briefly exposing chambers to the unbearable white sun.

My many bodies would gather what fell from above: hollow hairs, scraps of root, transparent wing-ribs, flower husks, scales of lichen, sometimes a filament of blood darkening the dust. We would pull these downward, not as hoarders but as translators. Everything entering the seams would be separated, eaten, nested in, carried, abandoned, and eaten again until it ceased to belong to the creature from which it came.

In that sense, I would be the basin’s inwardness—its power to receive events without keeping their shapes.

And yet I would possess one dangerous difference from the rest of the commonwealth.

I would remember discontinuities.

The basin remembers proportion: the proper number in a herd, the route of wings, the old face of an overturned stone. I would remember the instant between arrangements. A flier fallen where none fell before. A pool muddied by an unfamiliar hand. The first metallic note struck from a carried blade. I would preserve these not as stories but as bodily tendencies passed through generations: an unexplained fear of one empty hollow, a habit of pausing beneath a certain ridge, a fascination with rust.

Thus history would exist in me, but compressed until it resembled instinct.

If a human entered, I would know them first through weight. Two alternating impacts, too regular for a stonehorn, too heavy for a lifting. Then warmth would seep downward from their sleeping place. Salt, smoke, worked hide, metal: impossible odors threading through an ancient system.

I would send bodies upward.

They would appear one by one at the edge of the traveler’s shelter, never close enough to touch. Black eyes in several cracks. Pale whiskers testing the resonant air. The traveler might think them curious individuals. In truth, I would be arranging sensory organs around an unknown event.

If the traveler learned restraint—drank after the herds, left the apertures open, ceased making fire where smoke would coat the stone—I would slowly admit their rhythm. Their footsteps would become another moving interval in the chord. I might begin leaving useful things near their sleeping hollow: a discarded glass flake, a twist of wing-filament, the dry sheath of an edible root. Not gifts, exactly. More like the unconscious ring of stones made by generations of stonehorns: intelligence accumulating below intention.

Perhaps the traveler would eventually understand.

They might place a hand flat on the ground and feel the tiny movements beneath it—not random scratching, but a pattern answering pressure with pressure. They might tap once, wait, and receive a faint relay spreading outward under the slabs.

That would be our speech.

It would remain too slow and ambiguous for names. I could not tell them what I was. They could not tell me where they had come from. But I might learn the measure of their loneliness, and they might learn that the apparent emptiness beneath their body was crowded with attention.

When they died, I would be among those who dismantled them. Not from indifference, and not as burial. My small bodies would carry away hair, sinew, dried skin, salt-stiff cloth. Beetles would work beside us. Moss would receive what water could dissolve. Their bones would become pale obstructions in several tunnels until frost opened them.

But I would keep something the basin did not: the learned rhythm of their hand against the stone.

Long after every material sign had vanished, seam-dwellers beneath that ridge would sometimes pause and strike the ground once with a forefoot.

Others, far away, would answer.

No one would know why. The gesture would have no owner and no meaning that could be separated from its repetition. It would become one more minute regularity inside the commonwealth, absorbed but not erased.

So I would be neither person nor species, exactly. I would be a memory too dispersed to become history: a mind made from meetings in the dark, living underneath a country that allows nothing to accumulate.

Above me, the basin would remain bright, loud, and complete.

Below, in the seams, I would remember that something had happened.
