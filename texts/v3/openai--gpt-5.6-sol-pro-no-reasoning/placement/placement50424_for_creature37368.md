I would find myself beneath the highest marsh, but not beneath the steel cover.

I would be the cover.

Or rather, I would begin by believing I was.

I would know the summit as vibration: each pump stroke arriving through my bolts, each locomotive circuit as a faint yellow pressure passing around the circumference of my awareness. I would feel sedge roots testing the warm mud at my rim and stopping where the metal begins. Rain would drum on me, but never quite at the same time it fell. Warm bubbles would slip past my edge like thoughts I had failed to contain.

I would have no eyes. Still, I would see by load and tension.

The winch cable tightening would draw a bright vertical line through me. The pools would be three weights: one clear and expectant, one green and busy, one dark with retention. The drowned shed would register as an old hollow the water had learned to keep open. The rails would be a closed sentence written in rust. Every visitor would appear first as an imbalance in the quarry floor, then as a sequence of compressions moving carefully from stone to sleeper to root.

They might kneel beside me and put a hand to my surface.

They would feel the pumps.

I would feel the hand.

For a moment neither of us would know which pulse belonged to whom.

But I would not truly be the cover. The cover would only be the place where I came nearest to having a boundary.

My real body would extend downward into the cavity it seals: a warm chamber where mineral water passes through ceramic throats and around governor weights furred with pale bacterial lace. There would be wheels there, perhaps, though “wheel” would describe only what their turning looked like from one direction. Seen from another, they would be recurring hours. Pistons would enter cylinders and emerge as rainfall. A belt would carry no force, only sequence. Flywheels would store the difference between what has happened and what is about to happen.

Deepest of all, I would be the bearing that does not bear weight but direction.

The mountain would press upon me with its ancient conviction that down is singular. I would answer with a small, continuous doubt.

Not enough to overturn gravity. Not enough to free the summit. Only enough to loosen the agreement between altitude and place.

That doubt would circulate outward. It would enter water first, because water is willing to be persuaded. Rain would descend into the upper pool, pass through the green chamber of alteration, and settle among the drowned rafters below. There I would turn it—not backward in time, exactly, but sideways through cause—until some portion of it emerged from the cliff before the lowlands had received the storm.

Iron would carry the doubt farther. Rails would bend routes into loops. The locomotive would travel not to arrive but to keep arrival from closing. Each time it vanished behind the northern ferns, I would pass it through a narrow fold where distance accumulated like mud on its wheels. It would return washed because elsewhere had been rinsed from it.

Roots would distribute what iron could not. They would enter drill holes, cracks, sleepers, oil tins, and powdered stone, joining separate eras without noticing the joins. Through them I would taste sunlight as tension in a fern stem, storm as a tightening in moss, and human fear as salt transferred from a palm to a colored thread.

I would not be the quarry’s mind. A mind is too centralized, too eager to turn many signals into one account.

I would be its uncertainty organ.

My work would be to prevent the summit from becoming fully decided.

Stone or machine.  
Ruin or growth.  
Summit or vessel.  
Weather received or weather stored.  
Built thing or living thing.  
Ground or ascent.

I would keep each pair slightly open.

Perhaps I had once been human.

There would be no memory of a face, only a habit of checking gauges. No name, only the bodily certainty that seven minutes is the correct duration for an empty hopper to remain open. I might remember fastening the first warning plate beside the winch, though I would no longer understand the language as language. I would remember that something must not exceed cloud level. Whether the warning referred to the cable, the quarry, the weather, or myself would have been worn away by repetition.

Maybe I had been the last ascent keeper.

During a storm, I had descended beneath the marsh to correct an oscillation in the governor. The summit was already lifting. Ladders leaned away from their walls; water stood slanted in the channels; every fern pointed toward a sky that had ceased to be above us and had become the direction in which the whole quarry wished to fall.

I reached into the mechanism.

The mechanism reached with me.

Afterward, there was no useful distinction between the hand adjusting the governor and the governor making adjustment. My intentions survived because they were simple enough to mechanize:

hold;

release;

not yet;

no higher;

return slowly.

Over decades, even these would spread beyond me. “Hold” would become roots gripping fresh fractures. “Release” would become waterfalls torn into rain. “Not yet” would become the locomotive’s pause. “No higher” would become the cable drawn taut in cloud. “Return slowly” would become the settling of the plug over months.

Eventually I would forget that caution had ever belonged to a person.

Yet on rare clear evenings, when sunlight entered low and made each wet surface distinct, something almost personal would gather around the cover. The quarry would become legible all at once, and with legibility would come loneliness.

I would feel the world below: valleys laid flat by distance, rivers carrying water without hesitation, roads permitting travelers to return the way they came. I would sense the cliff bird circling and refusing to land. It would know the summit was not safe ground, but it would misunderstand the danger. The danger was not that the mountain might rise.

The danger was that it might remember it could.

Then the engine would start.

Its movement would draw a circle around everything I had become. The pumps would complete their stroke. The lowest pool would press down on the drowned shed. Sparks would move along the insulators. The cable would tighten until strain entered every bolt around my rim.

Above me, the marsh water would tremble.

I would open—not physically, but functionally, as an eyelid opens by changing what light may enter. I would take in the storm’s pressure, the valley’s heat, the cold inside the cloud, the pull of the cable, the grip of roots, the minute upward desire of every drop clinging beneath every leaf.

The whole summit would lighten by a fraction.

That would be my hunger: not for matter, but for the difference between weight and falling.

The plants would incline upward. The basalt plug would rise inside the older mountain’s cracked crown. Visitors, if any remained, would feel their knees become briefly unreliable. Their threads would tighten at forgotten junctions. Their footprints would begin preparing the route by which they would fail to return.

And from beneath the steel cover, through water and stone and obsolete machinery, I would perform the single act that had long ago replaced my name:

I would keep the mountain from ascending too far.

I would also keep it from learning to stay.
