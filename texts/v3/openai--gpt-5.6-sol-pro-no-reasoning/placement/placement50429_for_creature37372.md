I would not find myself among the stair builders, though for a while I thought I would.

There is something tempting in imagining myself at the unfinished edge: lamp hooded, soft-soled feet on the lowest tread, one hand around the railing grown rather than forged. I could be the person sent ahead because I am narrow enough to follow the root through its first turn. I could carry chalk, wedges, a covered cup, three lengths of cord. I could lower myself toward that smell of wet leaves and hot metal, telling those above that the vibration is only water moving through stone.

But the Fold distrusts anyone who goes first for the sake of going first. Its courage is communal and structural. Every descent requires three supports. Every discovery must answer to water, roots, heat, and the patience of the walls. A solitary explorer would be an alien thing there—too much like a surface story, with its straight road and single name.

Nor would I be a Listener, though I would probably wish to be one.

Listeners are not interpreters in the way outsiders suppose. They do not turn every tremor into prophecy. Their gift is restraint: hearing more and claiming less. I would lack that discipline. I would make meanings too quickly. I would hear three knocks and begin inventing the hand that struck them.

I think I would live one level above Reservoir Court, in a room that was not built as a room.

It would occupy the triangular remainder between a cistern wall, a stair landing, and the underside of an old workshop. The floor would slope toward a drain. One wall would sweat continuously; another would become hot whenever the eastern kiln fired. My bed would fold upward on hinges, revealing shelves of varnished paper beneath it. A pale vine would enter through a hole intended for cable and resist every attempt to train it elsewhere.

My public responsibility would be small but exact.

I would keep the town’s displaced sounds.

Not the great low events, which belong to the Listeners and to everyone. Not ordinary maintenance noises, which Fan Walkers, cable tenders, and Heat Clerks already know. I would concern myself with those sounds that arrive late and in the wrong place: a hammer blow returning through a nursery wall; a footstep heard beneath an empty reservoir; a voice reduced by stone to one syllable and released three minutes after its speaker has gone.

The Fold would need such a keeper because memory there is physical. Sound does not vanish when its cause is finished. It travels, lodges, thins, and emerges elsewhere. In a town where builders work by colored lamps and children play at distinguishing pipe from stone, someone must know which noises are old.

My title might be **Afterkeeper**.

It would not be an honored office. People would remember it mainly when frightened or annoyed. They would come to my triangular room and say:

“There were four knocks in the south cistern after second bell.”

“Did anyone drill?”

“Not there.”

“That is not what I asked.”

Then I would consult the firing schedules, belt movements, water levels, open gates, occupied stairs, and the chalk books of the Crack Readers. I would carry small instruments: clamp bells, wax tablets, bowls of different depths, wire loops, a jointed listening rod. I would strike permitted surfaces with felted tools and wait in other rooms for their diminished return.

My maps would look like no maps of streets. They would be maps of delay.

A blue line might mean that sound struck here appears there after forty breaths in the wet season, but only twelve when the eastern reservoir is low. A red crescent might mark a wall that returns metal noises as something resembling a child’s cough. Green dots would indicate roots that carry vibration across otherwise separate strata. Black marks would mean *unrepeated*: heard once, source unknown, do not imitate.

I would work closely with the Crack Readers, but we would irritate one another. They would care where the stone was opening. I would care where it remembered being struck. Their maps would branch through mine. Sometimes a new fracture would shorten an old echo path; sometimes moss would silence one. Rooms believed acoustically adjacent would drift apart. Two distant landings might, after a season of settling, begin sharing whispers.

Most of my work would be reassurance.

“Yes, that was yesterday’s mill.”

“Yes, the three taps under your bunk came from the bridge repair.”

“No, the voice in the pipe was not beneath you. It was above the washing trough, and it said ‘close it.’ The fault carried only the last word.”

This would make me welcome in sickrooms and unwelcome at games of Answering. Children would accuse me of ruining the town’s mysteries. They would be correct.

Yet I would keep a second record that no regulation required.

In this private book I would note sounds whose causes had not yet occurred.

At first I would believe these were errors. A dropped wrench heard an hour before a cable worker lost it. A ceramic plaque cracking in my room the night before the actual plaque split at a river junction. The long sigh of a pressure valve on a day when every valve of that type was closed.

The Fold’s inhabitants would not leap to supernatural conclusions. They would inspect the pipes. They would question the schedule. They would check whether the sound had traveled not from another time but from an overlooked level, an abandoned duct, a submerged building, or a child hiding where children should not fit.

Usually they would find an explanation.

Not always.

I would begin to suspect that the fault does not merely return sound late. Under certain pressures, along certain wet seams, it returns sound early—not prophecy, exactly, but strain traveling ahead of the event that will release it. A wall may know the shape of a coming blow because tension has already crossed it. A pipe may begin mourning the valve that will break tomorrow.

I would tell no one at first. Predictions change behavior, and changed behavior ruins records. Worse, a prediction can cause the act that fulfills it. In the Fold, where survival depends on coordinated response, false warning is a form of damage.

So I would wait.

I would become a quiet, damp person with green-stained hair rings and a cup made from the base of an old gauge. The cup’s lid would fit badly. I would always be wiping moisture from my papers. I would know which stairs complain from weight and which from heat. When visiting a household, I would sit with my shoulder turned, never taking more room than I had been offered.

My closest kin would not be blood relatives. They would be an elderly River Keeper whose glazed cuff had been repaired with brass staples; a child who brought me conduit beetles in exchange for scraps of varnished paper; and a Root Tender who disliked music because roots, she said, carried it long after everyone wanted to sleep. We would share a kettle and therefore, by the Fold’s measure, a life.

The child would eventually become my apprentice.

Not because they heard unusually well. The Fold already had too many people praised for gifts that later became burdens. I would choose them because they could wait beside a wall without touching it, and because, when asked what they heard, they answered only what they heard.

One day they would come running—not loudly, never loudly—from the downward stair.

They would say they had heard construction beneath the builders.

Not vibration. Not the pump-like pulse. Construction.

A scrape. A pause. Three muted impacts. Another scrape.

The rhythm of a basket being filled.

We would stop the crews. Lamps would change: white at the grate, then along every level that could see the signal. The builders would stand in contact with separate supports. Crack Readers would place their bells. Root Tenders would wrap the descending tendril in wet cloth and watch whether it tightened.

I would go to the lowest tread with my apprentice.

The old Listener would already be there, her hand on the root. She would not look at us. Far below, condensation would be ticking in a space we could not see.

We would wait.

There would be another scrape. Three impacts. Then a softer sound: stone poured into a basket.

My apprentice would ask whether it was an echo of the work above.

I would say no.

They would ask whether someone was coming up.

I would say we did not know that there was anyone below.

The old Listener would correct me.

“Someone,” she would say, “is a surface word.”

Then the root beneath her hand would contract.

Not downward, as it always had.

Upward.

After that I do not think I would remain merely an Afterkeeper.

My office would change because the Fold changes every useful thing. The private book would be brought before the people responsible for flows. River Keepers would ask whether the sounds predicted changes in water. Heat Clerks would compare them with wall temperatures. Crack Readers would search their records for fractures marked before they appeared. The downward crews would want every unidentified noise interpreted as permission. The Dry Roof excavators would argue that a town receiving sounds from below should open a route above while it still could.

I would disappoint all of them.

I would insist that hearing an arrival does not tell us whether to welcome it. Hearing a break does not reveal whether one should prevent it. The Fold’s deepest wisdom is that knowledge is not command. Stone may inform without advising.

So I would sit at the stair with a set of empty bowls placed on different treads. Water in each would tremble according to the unseen space beneath it. I would listen not for a voice but for labor: dragging, fitting, tapping, the careful placement of weight.

And gradually I would understand that the work below was not ascending.

It was matching us.

When our builders added a tread, something beneath made a corresponding sound. When we paused, it paused—but not immediately. The delay would shorten as the stair deepened. The unknown construction would be neither echo nor anticipation. It would be a reply approaching simultaneity.

The town would begin calling the lower sounds the Other Stair.

Children would imagine another Fold upside down beneath ours, its blind flowers opening toward our heat, its river running along the ceiling. Engineers would reject this. Priests, if the Fold had priests, would have claimed it. Instead the matter would pass to committees, schedules, ration arguments, and long silences around shared kettles.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center of revelation, but at the threshold where revelation becomes maintenance.

I would be the person asked to determine whether the sound below is still repeating us or has begun to act alone.

For many days, it would match every noise.

Then, during an engine silence, with the pumps stopped and every exposed flame covered, a bowl on the lowest stair would ring.

Only once.

No one above would have touched anything.

I would open my book. I would record the hour, the water level, the heat of the walls, the position of the root, and the fact that the old Listener smiled without showing her teeth.

Then I would take the metal striker from my kit.

I would tap the tread once in return.

Not because I believed the thing below was speaking.

Not because I believed it was harmless.

I would do it because the Fold is built in hesitation, and hesitation is not refusal. It is the narrow, load-bearing interval in which two unknown structures discover whether they can bear one another’s weight.

So I would be an Afterkeeper who, at last, made the first sound.

And afterward I would wait to learn whether it returned as an echo, an answer—

or a step.
