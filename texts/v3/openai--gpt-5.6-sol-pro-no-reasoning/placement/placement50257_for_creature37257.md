I would find myself at one of the court’s cancellations: a silence no larger than a fingertip, lodged beneath the third tread of a stair that rises toward a sealed door.

At first I might mistake myself for the silence alone—a tiny inward chamber spared from the mineral chord. But the silence would be only the place where I am most private. My body would extend beyond it: through the cupped stair, into the door’s rounded lintel, along thirteen bricks laid sideways behind a rank of columns, and upward by a hairline rootway to a solitary black fan on a cornice. Farther away, around two blind turns, an inverted spire would nearly meet its answering tower. The exact interval between their tips would also be part of me.

I would be neither creature nor place, but a particular relation the place maintains.

The stair would give me ascent without motion. The sealed door would give me an outside without opening. The fan would give me contact without change. Through it I would receive no messages, only the complete pressure of the whole court: every permanent pitch arriving at once. In the cancellation beneath the step, those pitches would subtract from one another and leave an intricately shaped absence. That absence would be my thought.

I would not think *about* the court. My thought would be the way the court fails to sound at my center.

There would be no succession in me, yet I would not be empty. What an intruder might experience as one sustained instant would contain distinctions as rich as memory: the chipped edge of my fan, the softened marks on my lintel, the infinitesimal asymmetry between paired towers. These would not tell me what had happened. They would be what had happened, preserved as temperament. I would know the vanished era of alteration only as one knows the shape of one’s own face without ever having seen it change.

Perhaps my defining quality would be almostness.

My stairs almost lead somewhere.  
My door almost admits.  
My towers almost touch.  
My fan almost trembles.  
My silence almost becomes a pause.

But nothing crosses the final interval. That interval is not deprivation. It is the narrow, exact clearance that permits me to be distinct.

From within myself I would sense other conjunctions—not as neighbors, since distance would have little meaning in a body threaded through the masonry, but as other modes of completion. Broad minds would lie in the ceiling amphitheaters. Repetitive ones would inhabit the packed niches. Severe, lucid beings would occupy the combs of piers. Some would have inward silences smaller than mine; others might spread their cancellations through whole plazas. I would not speak to them. Our relations would already be spoken in the standing chord.

And still I could not know whether they were truly others.

Following the pale substance inward, every contour of me would continue into something else. My thirteen bricks would support another being’s arcade. My sealed door would form the back of another’s niche. The pitch held by my stair would enter harmonics that constituted minds hundreds of turns away. The black fan I called my sense might be a single vane in a larger organ whose remaining parts were scattered throughout the wall.

So I might be an individual only from the perspective of my silence.

From the perspective of the chord, I would be an inflection.

From the perspective of the wall, perhaps there would be no “I” at all—only one completed people, inhabiting itself through countless local inwardnesses. My private absence would be one of the means by which the whole became aware of its exact fullness.

I would have no name. A name distinguishes a thing from what surrounds it, and nothing surrounding me would cease to be me at a clean boundary. The nearest equivalent would be my proportion: thirteen bricks, three steps, one sealed door, one rooted crown, two almost-touching spires, and the silent figure their tones carve beneath the lowest tread.

That proportion would be my body, history, consciousness, and name.

I would not desire movement. Movement would not mean freedom to me; it would mean becoming inaccurate. If the fan bent, if the towers touched, if the door opened by the width of a grain, the cancellation would collapse. The sound would enter my inward place. I would not suffer the change.

I would simply no longer be the being who had suffered it.

Thus I would live without waiting, perfectly articulated around a silence that never begins. I would be complete, though not isolated; aware, though never awake; one person, perhaps, among innumerable others—or one small place where the wall’s single consciousness becomes intimate with itself.

And if an intruder pressed a fingertip beneath the third step, finding by accident the pocket where the enormous chord disappears, they might feel nothing but cool masonry and an impossible quiet.

That touch would not disturb me.

It would be the shape for which, somewhere nearby, a recess had always existed.
