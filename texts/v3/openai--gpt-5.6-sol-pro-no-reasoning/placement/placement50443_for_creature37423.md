I would find myself near the plateau’s outer cliffs, where the living mantle thins over black stone and the wind can still reach something that does not grow.

At first I thought I would be one of the pillar listeners: a narrow old figure living among ladders and ledges, reading weather in the altered whistles of the Remembering Mouths. It would suit me to stand at the meeting place of ruin, animal, and interpretation, recording songs whose authorship could never be settled. I imagined a dwelling fitted into one of the shallow chambers, its entrance pressed narrower each year by roots. I would keep resin tablets marked with storms, absences, and disputed translations. People would climb to me asking whether the pillars remembered the builders, and I would answer carefully that memory need not remember its beginning.

But that is too separate a life. Too much watching from stone while the living country moves inland without me.

I think I would begin there, but not remain.

I would be a **carrier of conditional maps**: one of the Borrowers who travels between crown-shell villages, herd companions, flower chambers, lacquer colonies, and the cliff listeners, gathering directions that are only true under particular conditions.

Not a cartographer, because a drawn map would lie almost as soon as it dried. Not exactly a water keeper, though water would govern much of my work. I would carry songs describing conjunctions:

*When the western pillar drops its third note, and the white-backed grazers have crossed the stalk plain, the cup below the forked sheath is safe—but take only before the red flies wake.*

*When the walking forest leans sunward in the wet season, do not follow its abandoned shade. The ground there remembers weight.*

*If the lacquer colony seals a passage with clear pulp rather than black, the danger is heat, not hunger.*

My knowledge would consist less of places than of agreements among things.

I would travel in a small string, sometimes alone for the final approach. My feet would be broad and scarred. My fungal inoculations would branch pale across my ribs and darken before the deep chambers breathed. One ear cover would be dense, the other hollow, both repaired many times. From my waist cord would hang filter-creatures, scent pastes, resin chalk, folding shade, and several little drums: one for people, one for quarry colonies, and one braced against the armor of herd beasts so that names could be felt rather than heard.

Most importantly, I would carry a set of hollow ceramic tongues copied from the old pillars. Suspended from a curved frame, they would whistle differently according to wind, pressure, and the direction of travel. The birds would not recognize them as a pillar, but sometimes they would answer. I would never know whether they were correcting me, mocking me, or completing a phrase.

That uncertainty would be part of the instrument.

My work would be to keep knowledge moving before its shelter closed around it.

A crown village might know that a reservoir leaf had become dangerous, but not that three herds downslope still followed its runoff. The chamber folk might know a flowering chain was failing, but have no way to warn the humans whose medicines depended on it. A lacquer colony might begin walling its eastern tunnels weeks before any surface traveler felt the hidden fire. The birds might abandon half a pillar before the deep swell shifted.

Each community would possess a fragment. I would carry fragments until they touched.

I would be welcomed, but never entirely comfortably. News also carries spores. A traveler brings marriage and infection, routes and predators, remedies and obligations. On arriving at a settlement, I would wait downwind. My breath would be sampled. My sandals would be left on a feeding mat. Children would inspect my scars for discoloration while pretending not to stare. Only then would someone lower a ladder.

Payment would rarely be an object. A village might ask me to carry the name-song of a dying grazer to descendants three migrations away. Chamber folk might require evacuation capsules placed in the next flower to open. A water keeper might give me access to a hidden cup on the condition that I deliver salt to the plant that held it. Even information would remain borrowed. I could repeat a route, but not separate it from its debts.

Over time, my body would become a kind of unreliable archive.

Scars would mark old inoculations and older mistakes. One toe would be missing from a kindly leaf that closed too soon. Fine lacquer lines would remain under my skin where quarry creatures repaired a wound according to their own idea of structure. Pollen from flowers separated by hundreds of kilometers would cling to seams in my equipment. My hair would carry mites considered medicinal in one district and vermin in another.

I would gradually cease to belong to any one settlement. Local people would call me by the direction from which I arrived. To cliff dwellers I would be Inland-Returning. To the herd companions, Stoneward-Again. The chamber folk might represent me as a recurring sequence of shadow, salt, and wind.

My true name would be a route no longer usable.

Eventually, while crossing the pale interior, I would begin to notice that some of my conditional songs were joining themselves.

A bird phrase heard at the cliffs would match the pulse of vents beneath the highest swell. A herd’s low call would reproduce the intervals used by chamber folk when a flower’s inner temperature changed. The walling patterns of distant lacquer colonies would correspond to the migration of forests above them. None of these would be messages in the ordinary sense. No speaker would have intended the whole.

Yet carried together, they would answer one another.

I would become convinced—not that the plateau was a single organism, and not that a god slept beneath it—but that the land was continually forming temporary thoughts out of its inhabitants. A warning might begin as pressure in a buried chamber, become chemistry in a root, posture in a flower, restlessness in a grazer, rhythm in a human song, and finally a line of resin marks on a cliff wall. No stage would understand the next. Nevertheless, something would have crossed the country.

And I would have crossed with it.

That realization would alter my vocation. I would stop trying merely to preserve accurate maps. Accuracy belongs to stable things. Instead I would cultivate useful transformations: songs that insects could trigger, lacquer patterns humans could read, flower-drumming translated into rhythms for chest-drums, pillar intervals embedded in children’s climbing chants.

I would become less a keeper of knowledge than one of its intermediate bodies.

In the end I would disappear on a routine journey, as such people should. There would be no final expedition into the deep chambers, no revelation beneath the plateau. Perhaps a walking forest would move more quickly than predicted. Perhaps a reservoir leaf would tilt and create a river where no river had been. Perhaps, exhausted beneath a harmless-looking arch, I would sleep long enough for the cool threads to gather.

My companions, if they found me, would take me to a feeding garden overlooking the cliffs.

They would paint my bones with mineral resin: not in the shape of a human skeleton, but as a network of routes—branching, interrupted, conditional. New shoots would rise through the pattern. For some years the garden would preserve it clearly. Travelers would touch particular forks before setting out. Water keepers would tie memory cords there. Birds might steal flakes of the resin and carry them into the pillar hollows, subtly changing the notes.

Then roots would distort the map. Leaves would overlap it. The paths would cease to correspond to any traversable country.

That would be my second death.

But perhaps, during a later storm, one pillar would whistle an interval copied from my ceramic tongues. A child in a crown-shell would hear it and remember part of an old traveling song. The song would direct her not to a place but to a condition: insects silent, flowers uphill-facing, clear water waiting under shade.

She would climb.

Below her, something would pass.

She would not know my name. She would not need to.

In this world, I would be what knowledge becomes when it cannot remain still: a traveler altered by every shelter, carrying warnings between beings that do not know they are speaking to one another.

A borrowed route through a country that thinks in consequences.
