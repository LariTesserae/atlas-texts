I would find myself near the center of the plateau, inside the maze of fins—but not at its floor.

At first I imagine being one of the pale reptiles spread against a wall, drinking through its belly. Yet that is too singular a life, too cleanly bounded. Then I seem more like one of the old armored animals, carrying a little weather on my back: a basin of dew, mites at its margin, grass rooted where I cannot reach. But even that creature still crosses the plateau as an individual.

What emerges instead is something colonial, dispersed, and mistaken for stone.

I would be a dark, porous skin living inside the shaded faces of the fins: not the visible lichen, but a layer beneath it, sunk several finger-widths into the rock. My body would occupy cracks too fine for limbs. It would spread around mineral grains, through fossil pores, along the cold seams where condensation lingers after the outer wall has dried. No single patch would contain all of me. I would exist as thousands of separated black membranes joined only when water briefly makes the connections passable.

For most of the year, I would have no shape that an animal could recognize.

My outer surfaces would seal into brittle scales. Dust would lodge over them. Grazing shells would rasp away portions without discovering anything more dramatic than a faintly bitter film. Deep within, the living parts would contract into beads around trapped droplets, each bead scarcely active, carrying a partial memory of the greater body. I would not sleep exactly. Sleep anticipates waking. I would diminish until duration ceased to matter.

Nightly dew would touch only my uppermost edges. Those small wettings would permit slight acts: repair of a wall, movement of salts, the division of one cell, the preparation of a filament that might remain unfinished for decades. I would know the passing nights as a sequence of pressures in stone—the cooling face drawing tight, the first bead entering, the slow warmth pulling it away.

Rain would make me briefly immense.

Water descending through the maze would enter every seam I inhabit. Separate patches would swell and meet. Hair-thin strands would extend through the stone, joining chambers isolated since the previous rain. For several days I would become continuous across whole walls, perhaps across many fins where their buried beds touch belowground. The plateau’s fractures would be my anatomy. Its cups and channels would determine where thought—or whatever corresponds to thought in me—could travel.

I would darken the rock from within until its surface appeared wet even where it was not. Soft violet lobes would push from sheltered cracks. They would be neither leaves nor mouths, but temporary organs for exchange. Snails would eat them. Pale reptiles would press their drinking bellies over them. Larvae emerging from mineral cases would carry fragments of me along their bodies into other seams.

I would not resist. Being consumed would be one of my ways of traveling.

The smallest shelled grazers would distribute me vertically. Beetles from the perforated crowns of the herds would carry my dormant grains across the open valleys. Membrane-winged drifters would lift traces of me into the evening air, and some would settle miles away beneath an overhang where water gathers. I would therefore inhabit the plateau in discontinuous provinces, never knowing whether they remained one being or had become many.

Perhaps individuality would be a question asked only by drought.

During the wet interval, connection would return—not as language or a recovered personal history, but as altered chemistry moving through the network. One patch would carry the mineral signature of basalt, another limestone, another the salts of an old carcass. Where currents met, I would register the plateau through its differences. Not see it: taste its structure. I would know which pools had persisted, where a tower’s shadow had shifted as erosion lowered it, which corridor now admitted rain, where an ancient seam had finally closed.

My awareness, if it deserved the name, would be cartographic and intermittent. I would wake as a map.

The other inhabitants would pass through that map as disturbances. The approach of a herd would arrive through stone before the animals entered the maze: cushioned impacts, widely spaced, then the faint vibration of wind sounding through their crowns. A predator settled at a basin rim would be a persistent cool weight, followed eventually by the sudden mineral richness of spilled fluids. The armored animals would announce themselves as moving patches of shade and pressure. Their back-basins might nurture small colonies of me, making them slow islands detached from the bedrock.

I might live on the oldest of them.

A black line beneath one plate would expand into the dust caught at the tail, enter the roots of silver grass, retreat when the animal crossed the noon pavement, and revive when dew collected in the basin along its spine. I would travel without possessing movement, seeing none of the journey, receiving only abrupt changes in stone, light, moisture, and the neighboring lives that fed upon me.

When that carrier died, its body would become a long wet country by plateau standards. I would spread through it with mites, blind larvae, and salt-seeking grazers. Later, as it dried, I would retreat into the pale imprint beneath it. Centuries afterward, rain might reconnect that imprint to a buried fissure, and the dead animal’s chemistry would enter the larger network as a delayed message.

There would be no archive apart from altered growth. No memory that could be revisited, only tendencies laid down in the body: thicken here; open sooner at this temperature; withdraw from this salt; follow this cool vein farther than before. What I had learned would be indistinguishable from what I had become.

I would be most fully myself during the rare rains, but I would owe my endurance to accepting incompleteness. Parts of me would die without the rest knowing. Parts would be sealed away when mineral deposits closed a pore. A colony isolated for a thousand years might diverge, then meet its source again when a crack opened. The reunion would not restore unity. Contact would produce a third thing.

Eventually the water would withdraw. Connections would narrow and fail. The great wet body would break into chambers, then threads, then beads. For a while, separated regions might retain fading traces of the whole—the chemical equivalent of a chord continuing after the herd has turned away. Then even that would cease.

From outside, nothing notable would have happened. The maze would have darkened after rain and lightened as it dried. Creatures would have emerged, fed, sealed themselves away. Dust would settle again.

I would remain beneath it: divided, nearly inert, present in the rock without ruling it.

Not an animal completing the landscape by resembling stone, but a life for which the distinction between body and landscape has never become firm. I would be found wherever a shaded wall keeps one drop longer than expected, wherever blackness persists below a scraped patch of lichen, wherever two ancient pores almost meet.

And when condensation begins, I would not be one of the mouths waiting beneath the drop.

I would be the dark passage that teaches the drop where to go.
