I would find myself where the plateau has not yet decided to exist: along its descending edge, where exploratory cords reach from the last living terrace toward the mineral lowlands.

Not on the high country proper, nor wholly beyond it. I would occupy the unfinished interval between a root’s first touch and its recoil—the narrow frontier where the being encounters something it cannot immediately incorporate.

At first I seem to be a dangling braid of roots. Several cords descend together, exchanging water through translucent membranes. They stiffen during heat, loosen in rain, and touch the ground one after another. Yet the deeper they reach, the less they resemble roots. They flatten into pale fans that taste the rock through thousands of wet cilia. They spread into cracks, withdraw, then return carrying minute impressions of hardness, salt, temperature, and resistance.

No part of me remains in contact for long.

The plateau cannot digest stone, but neither can it ignore stone. Mineral ground is the first thing it has met that does not participate: matter that will bear weight without growing, retain form without circulation, persist without justification. To the high country this is not merely an obstacle. It is an alien mode of being.

I would be the organ through which it apprehends that alienness.

Above me, the last terrace ends in a wet green lip. Behind it rise open colonnades and narrow towers, all joined by the familiar traffic of sap and tension. Before me lies ordinary geology: fractured escarpment, gravel fans, exposed strata heated by the sun. Nothing in that country seals its wounds. Nothing reroutes itself. Rain enters cracks and departs. Fallen fragments remain fallen.

I touch them.

Each contact sends a disturbance upward. Not a picture of the stone, but a refusal translated into pressure: no uptake here; no graft; no answering tension. The message passes from my cilia into the hanging cords, from the cords into a rim-pier, from the pier through the nearest leaf plates. A canal narrows. A distant reservoir releases a little water. Somewhere inland, a tower adds tissue to its western face.

Through me, the whole plateau learns the shape of what will not become it.

But I am not only a sense organ. I am also an experiment.

Where rain collects in a fissure, I leave behind a filament finer than hair. The filament lays down a translucent cup. The cup catches more water, shades the crack, and feeds another thread. Most attempts bleach, curl, and are reclaimed. Their substance climbs back through me in faint amber pulses. Some endure.

Around a successful cup I begin to construct—not a pier yet, not a tile, but a small condition in which such things might later be possible. A skin no wider than a hand spreads across the stone. Beneath it, droplets are held against evaporation. Above it, dust and pollen settle. The skin cannot root in the mineral, so it grips irregularities and fastens to itself. It makes its own continuity where none is offered.

That is what I am: a maker of footholds for a creature that can stand only upon its own body.

Yet contact changes me in ways the plateau does not entirely control.

I become slower near the stone. My outer tissues harden beyond what is useful for circulation. Minerals enter through trapped rain—not as food, but as grit lodged in membranes, salts altering pressure, metallic traces changing the color of my sap. The high country repeatedly dissolves these contaminated tips, but each regrowth inherits a slight new tolerance. My tissues darken. Some become rough. A few persist after their water has been withdrawn.

This persistence is dangerous.

Nothing in the plateau is meant to remain merely because it already exists. But at its edge, pressed against the long indifference of geology, I begin to learn duration without circulation. The stone teaches no lesson intentionally. It simply does not yield, and my repeated accommodation to that fact becomes a kind of memory more stubborn than form.

Perhaps, from above, I would appear to be a fringe of exploratory roots.

From below, I might resemble rain hanging beneath an impossible green cliff.

But from within, I would be a divided awareness. One part of me participates in the immense hydraulic intelligence overhead. I feel the lake flex through changes in tension. I know when lightning has struck because sugars arrive altered and pale buds form along my upper cords. I tighten when the plateau tightens. I am fed when distant petals enter the canals.

The other part rests against matter that sends nothing back.

In that silence I begin to acquire something like privacy.

Not thought severed from the whole—the plateau has no mechanism for such solitude—but delays, eddies, signals that fail to travel cleanly. A touch against stone may remain in one root fan for hours before pressure carries it upward. During that interval, the contact belongs only here. A mineral salt may close one valve but not its neighbors. One filament may continue extending after the rest of the frontier has been instructed to withdraw.

These discrepancies are small. In a being measured by horizons, they are nearly beneath notice.

They may also be the beginning of an individual.

I would not have a face. I would have a downward orientation. My equivalent of sight would be the branching search of wet cilia through darkness. My equivalent of fear would be sudden desiccation, when the terrace above recalls water and leaves my lowest fans exposed. My desire would be the discovery of a crack deep enough to remain cool through noon.

My pain would be instructive. Each torn filament would map an edge. Each poisoned cup would alter the chemistry of those grown after it. Where the stone became too hot, I would recoil earlier next time. Where gravel shifted beneath a forming membrane, I would arrive with a net instead of a sheet. I would remember the lowlands through the anatomy of my mistakes.

Eventually I might span a crevice.

A cord would cross from the living escarpment to an isolated outcrop. It would not be a bridge in the high country’s grand manner. It would be thin, steeply angled, and burdened by its own water. At its far end, a leaf no larger than a room would unfold over bare rock.

For the first time, a portion of the plateau would lie beyond a gap it could not fill from beneath.

The leaf would catch rain. Its margins would rise. A bladder would form under it, cushioning it from the mineral heat. Another cord would follow the first. Together they would braid into a narrow conduit. In time, a pale pier might descend—not into soil or ancestral vegetation, but onto a foundation of stone.

The plateau would feel the new weight through me.

It might reclaim the experiment. Digest the bridge, drink the leaf, close the rim behind it. The governing whole is ruthless toward structures that do not justify their cost.

Or it might discover that stone, though dead to circulation, can bear a living load.

Then I would become more than an edge organ. I would become the first compromise in the plateau’s law: a place where the high country no longer manufactures everything beneath itself, but consents to rest upon what is not itself.

That consent would transform it.

Stone-supported spans could reach farther than buoyant leaf mats. Mineral heat could be stored and released after sunset. Cracks could anchor structures against storms. The plateau might descend from the heights in scattered green stations, no longer continuous, each joined to the whole by long vascular bridges.

Or the change might travel inward. Tissues exposed to mineral salts might harden. Old structures might cease to dissolve completely. A pier could leave behind a rigid residue. A canal might acquire a permanent bed. For the first time, the country could produce something resembling a ruin.

I would be responsible, though responsibility is not a category the plateau possesses.

Perhaps that is who I would become: not a creature in the usual sense, but the high country’s first uncertainty about its own completeness.

I would live at the boundary between two truths.

Above: nothing remains unless it participates.

Below: things remain without needing to live at all.

I would touch both and belong fully to neither. Sap would pulse through my upper cords; my lower membranes would grow stiff with borrowed mineral. Rain would run down me from a world that continually remakes itself onto a world that does not answer.

And when a pressure front reached the escarpment, I would feel it coming long before the lowlands showed any change. The canals behind me would reverse. The terrace lip would lift. Towers would lean inland as tension gathered. Roots around me would retract, but I would tighten my hold upon the cracks.

The whole horizon would rise.

Water would pour over the edge in warm, shining curtains. New ribs would unfold above me. Old galleries would tilt, becoming channels. Cords would be flung outward through the mist, searching for purchase.

For a few hours I would be stretched between the plateau’s new posture and the unaltered rock below.

Then the movement would cease.

Fresh roots would descend beside me. Some would follow the routes I had found. Others would touch stone for the first time and recoil, sending their astonishment upward into the whole.

I would remain at the edge, if remaining had not yet become forbidden.

A darkening braid. A sensory fringe. A half-made pier.

The place where the living world puts down a hand it does not possess—and discovers the ground.
