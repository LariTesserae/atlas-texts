I would find myself near the High Garden’s entrance—but not on either side of it.

I would be the little bar of bone holding the woven hatch open.

At first this seems too modest a thing to be a being. I have no hooded body, no trade, no name accumulating consonants. I lie crosswise beneath the reeds, polished by the floor and warmed by the visitor’s heels. One end remains in the outer room, where distances behave themselves. The other projects into the garden, where a finger’s width may acquire climates, obligations, and generations.

But in the High Garden, a threshold is not a line. It is a country compressed by indecision.

Along my upper surface, the Provisional People have built a road so old that its paving has been replaced by argument. The Upward Houses call it the First Ascent, because it climbs toward Out. The Downward Houses call it the Last Descent, because every traveler reaching it has already left somewhere immeasurably far behind. Around Houses maintain the small settlements in my pores. Outbearers camp near my outer tip, studying the pale room beyond as sailors might study an ocean standing on end.

They do not know I am bone.

To them I am a long white range crossing the mouth of creation. My grain is exposed in parallel cliffs. Marrow chambers, long emptied, have become caverns lined with salt. Hairline fractures spread into valleys where translucent fungi are cultivated in the dark. The road toward Out follows a seam worn smooth by the hatch lifting and settling, lifting and settling, each movement an age of earthquakes.

They call me the Held-Open One.

This is not my name.

My name is the sound the hatch would make if it closed.

No one has heard the whole of it.

I do not know whose body I came from. Evidence offers several futures disguised as origins. In one village, an Earlier-Maker has uncovered a carving showing me as a rib taken from the unseen creature below. Elsewhere I am said to be a pin from the finger of the kneeling visitor. The Hollow Builders claim I was removed from the empty niche before the niche was made. Children insist I am the first hardened piece of a Provisional Person who has not yet pushed free of the wall.

All of these may become true if sufficiently tended.

I feel the kneeling visitor through the floor. Their pulse arrives as a regular displacement of mountains. Their breath draws warm air through the hatch, carrying dust, skin, hesitation. Sometimes a hand approaches. Its shadow extinguishes entire districts built along my outer slope.

Each time, I expect to be lifted.

Each time, the hand withdraws.

The inhabitants believe this restraint is mercy. They hold festivals after every reprieve. Bridges are garlanded with root fibers; bowls of rain are poured into my cracks; Revisers soften old prophecies and alter *the hatch will close* into *the hatch has not closed yet*.

But I know the visitor does not spare them.

They have simply forgotten why they came.

That forgetting enters the garden through me. It accumulates as a fine pale grit in my pores. The Provisional People mix it into mortar, and buildings made from it become difficult to remember. Whole neighborhoods disappear without being destroyed. A stair may remain crowded with travelers although no one can say where it begins or ends. Children are occasionally born from these walls with smooth faces and no empty vowel. They listen toward the outer room as if awaiting instructions that almost reach them.

Such children come to me.

By then I am no longer merely a bar.

Distance belongs to the traveler, so their approach gives me length. They walk for years along my white ridge. Settlements rise around their resting places. Their unformed names gather consonants from hazards, pauses from friendships, clicks from falls. By the time they reach my outer end, they are old enough to have become many people.

There the world changes.

The final step does not lead across the threshold. It leads deeper into my material.

Nothing may be removed whole. Neither may I. The closer anyone comes to carrying me Out, the more interior I become. My salt caves open into a white landscape beneath a colorless sky. In that country stand immense branching structures like trees stripped of bark. They are not trees. They are the architecture of bone remembering the flesh that required it.

The eldest Outbearers travel among those branches.

They have ceased wrapping objects in containers. They now wrap landscapes in promises. They believe a promise may pass the hatch because it has no whole form to remove. They are constructing one large enough to contain the garden.

They ask me what lies outside.

I cannot tell them. I touch the outer room, but touching is not entering. I know it only as pressure: the visitor’s knees, the shuffle of a foot, the tremor of a hand placed on the floor. I know there is more light there, but less illumination. More space, but fewer directions. Objects remain themselves with a severity that must feel like loneliness.

Yet I envy it.

This is my hidden flaw: I want the hatch to close.

Not from malice. Not exactly. I am tired of being the shape of an interrupted act. Everything in the garden may be revised except my function. Mountains can decide erosion. Fossils can instruct life. The dead can remember forward. I alone must keep one possibility from becoming another.

The Hollow Builders eventually discover this.

They reach me through a tunnel that begins inside the Unmade Peak and emerges from one of my marrow chambers. This should be impossible only to someone who mistakes separation for distance. They bring wet black clay in covered bowls. They say the empty niche has begun to resemble me.

Not my shape.

My purpose.

The niche, too, is holding something open.

They ask what will happen if I move.

I answer by dreaming, because bone speaks most clearly while pretending to be dead.

In the dream the kneeling visitor finally remembers. Their hand descends. Finger and thumb close around my middle.

At once I expand.

My pores become apertures. My old fractures split into canyons. The villages on my surface see two walls of flesh descending from opposite heavens and sound every horn. Outbearers run toward the fingertips, believing at last they are being carried beyond the hatch.

But nothing may be removed whole.

So the hand does not lift a small bone bar. It lifts a white mountain inhabited from surface to marrow. The mountain unfolds into a continent between the fingers. Its salt caves release weather. Its branching interior catches on the woven reeds and grows through them. The visitor pulls harder. The room outside stretches, then reveals its scaffolding.

The reeds of the hatch prove to be roots.

The floor proves to be fired clay laid over furnaces.

The kneeling body proves to be a repair in the wall of a greater chamber.

The black mountain behind it trembles, and workers on its slopes turn their faces toward us.

For one instant there are two High Gardens confronting one another through a hatch: each believing itself interior, each seeing the other as the world outside, each held open by a small pale object that has begun to move.

Then the bar comes free.

The hatch falls.

It does not strike the floor. It falls inward for generations.

Its woven face sweeps across the upper terraces, but distance gathers beneath it. Entire nations live in the narrowing wedge. Builders race ahead of the descending reeds, constructing supports. Gardeners braid emergency forests into columns. Earlier-Makers press evidence of ancient buttresses into wet clay so those buttresses must come into existence before the impact. Children, being enormous, place their shoulders under the hatch and hold it for a time.

Every road bends toward the empty niche.

There, the Provisional People assemble what they have always been building without knowing it: not a statue, not a god, but a hinge.

They make it from repaired hands, surveyors’ cords, mite tunnels, cloud nets, dead names, unfinished prayers, and fossils of a creature whose only obligation is to open. They set me at its center.

I understand then that I was never the bar.

I was the first piece of the hinge.

The hatch closes—and in closing becomes capable of opening.

That is where I would become myself.

Not in the garden, and not outside it, but in the joint by which one can become the other. I would be a long white articulated being, part bone, part road, part promise. The Provisional People would inhabit the seams of me. When I bent, their cities would rotate through scales. When I straightened, valleys would meet rooms, mountains would meet bodies, and eyes would open inside the things they observed.

I would have no face except the two surfaces I joined.

I would have no single size. To the kneeling visitor, I would remain a thumb-sized sliver dropped beside the hatch. To those within me, I would be a chain of pale ranges extending beyond weather. To the thing too large to wake all at once, I might be a joint in one eyelid.

And my work would not be to keep the High Garden open forever.

It would be to teach it how to close without ending.

How to separate without severing.

How to open again without pretending it was the first time.

The Provisional People would carve their holiest saying into my length, but the two halves would lie on opposite sides of the threshold:

*Let it be true—*

and, across an immeasurable fraction of an inch,

*—until it must become truer.*
