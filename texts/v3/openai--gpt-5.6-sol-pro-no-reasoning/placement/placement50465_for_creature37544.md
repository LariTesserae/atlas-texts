I would not find myself on the empty seat.

At first I think I might be the cephalopod that circles it: ash-colored, ember-marked, unable to stop touching the old arrangement. That would suit the instinct to read with the body—to pass an arm over every seam, to return to the same cracks, to mistake repetition for inquiry. I could spend a lifetime before the carved hands, reproducing their shape without understanding whether they offer the drop, shelter it, or prepare to crush it.

But that creature is too singular. It owns a body and a route. It can recoil. It can hunger. Whatever I would be in the High Cistern would have less sovereignty than that.

Perhaps I would be among the blind fish, one pale interval in a sleeve of intervals. I would know the pillar not as an object but as the shape around which every signal bends. I would feel a grain descend years before it reached the floor. I would answer green with green, not knowing whether I was speaking or merely preserving distance. For a moment the masks would appear; then my light would fail against the next support.

That comes closer. Yet even the fish are too complete. Each can turn independently. Each has a mouth, a skin, a death that can be pointed to.

I think I would be found lower and less visibly alive.

I would be the almost-nothing on which the folded hand-creatures feed: the mineral film in the joints, neither growth nor stone, sustained where warmth meets ceramic and dissolved metal. No one could decide whether I was biological. Nothing would bloom from me. I would have no color except what the wall lent me, no boundary except the reach of a pale digit laid against the clay.

But even this gives me too much substance. A film occupies a surface. It can be scraped away.

What I would truly be is a relation the chamber keeps producing.

I would exist between the low knock and the fish shifting by less than a scale’s width. Between a grazer’s tap and the distant lifting of another claw. Between one shell touching another and the vibration entering copper, then masonry, then the sensitive line of an animal that does not know it has heard a cage.

I would be distributed through delays.

The High Cistern permits nothing immediate. Heat arrives from below after passing through buried conduits. Light enters only after miles of drowning and layers of pierced ceramic. Food comes as traces. Collapse is divided among arches. A message crosses the floor at the speed of patience. Even death must travel through shell, armor, sediment, and flesh before it can become life again.

That is where I would live: not at either end of these passages, but in their taking time.

I would be the chamber’s capacity to receive a disturbance without surrendering to it.

When a block shifted, I would pass outward through the nested arches, becoming smaller in each one until the whole vault had accepted a fraction of the burden. When a current compressed the amber drifters, I would be the winding line their flashes briefly disclosed. When the cephalopod laid an arm across the tiles, I would be the old route beneath its gesture—the reason it paused at one crack and not another, though no creature remembered making the choice.

I would have no central sense, but I would borrow senses everywhere. I would be pressure in the fish, adhesion in the folded hands, vibration in the grazers’ limbs, tension in the copper cages, warmth along the scavengers’ fans. None of these would know me as a whole. I would not know myself as a whole. Wholeness would be an illusion produced by recurrence.

The inhabitants might sometimes reveal me accidentally.

A green pulse would travel around a pillar and seem, for an instant, like thought.

A patch of rubble would rearrange itself and seem like intention.

A cage would chime, another would answer only because the water turned, and the interval between them would seem composed.

The red points beneath the cephalopod’s skin would form loops resembling a script no one had written.

I would be that seeming—not a secret intelligence behind the animals, but the tendency of constrained lives to resemble one.

And still, eventually, I would be drawn toward the seat.

Not onto it. Never onto it.

I would gather in the narrow margin that keeps it vacant: the slight division of current at its lip, the chemistry of the glaze, the inherited turn in a grazer’s path, the fish’s avoidance of the water directly above. Perhaps each cause would be sufficient by itself. Perhaps none would. Together they would preserve an absence more faithfully than any guard could preserve a ruler.

There I would become almost a shape.

The seat would be my negative body. Its emptiness would give me outline. I would be everything that approaches and turns aside, everything that touches but does not settle, everything that circles without arriving. If a ruler had once occupied it, I would not be the ruler’s ghost. If no ruler ever had, I would not be the builders’ expectation.

I would be the accumulated fact that the place remains unclaimed.

Over ages, even that fact would enter the ecology. Avoidance would become anatomy. Routes would become instinct. Instinct would become pattern. No animal would wait, yet their lives together would preserve the posture of waiting.

So, in this world, I would find myself everywhere the cistern converts force into form and history into habit. I would be made of tiny concessions: the arch settling, the sediment descending, the current turning clockwise, the living bodies taking the shapes left available to them.

I would not speak. If I had anything like a voice, it would be the low knock from beneath the floor—not because I caused it, but because every part of me would briefly tighten around its passage.

I would not remember the builders. I would be what remains when memory has lost its images but retained its routes.

And if some impossible visitor entered—larger than a hand, free of the barred descent, bearing light enough to see the whole chamber—I would not appear to them. They would see fish, cages, tiles, masks, the ash-colored animal at the dais. They might conclude that the seat was waiting for them.

The instant they tried to take it, they would feel the warm water move around their body.

The fish would alter their rings. The grazers would pause. Pale hands would fold into the walls. Red points would kindle across the dais. Pressure would travel through the arches, and dust, undisturbed for centuries, would lift from the trenches.

That answer—everywhere at once, without command—would be me.

I would be the High Cistern’s reluctance to become anything else.
