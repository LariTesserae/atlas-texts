I would be found in the southern districts, where separate stairways begin to arrive at the same landing.

Not at the landing itself. Nothing lives there for long. Too many routes agree upon it, and agreement is one of the more dangerous forms of pressure. I would inhabit the approaches: the narrow flights behind cisterns, the ladders descending through warm chimneys, the tiled steps that appear only in reflections, the rusted service stairs on which every seventh tread is colder than the rest.

The fitters would call me the **landing animal**, though I would not be an animal in any settled sense.

At first I might resemble one of the long-bodied foxes: low to the metal, pale with filings, my limbs narrow enough to pass through handrails. Seen from above, I would look more like a tool left open—a pair of dividers, perhaps, or shears. Seen climbing, I would seem almost human, because I would use the rail with one forelimb and pause at each turn as though catching my breath.

No two people would agree on my head. Children would draw a smooth ceramic face with a single vertical seam. Door shepherds would insist I had the head of a goat, but with keyholes in place of eyes. Rust-readers would deny having seen a head at all. In polished platework, my reflection would arrive wearing the observer’s face, though older and exhausted from a journey they had not yet made.

I would not hunt bodies. I would feed on distinctions.

Where two staircases had begun to become the same staircase, I would crouch between them and gnaw patiently at their likeness. Scraps would fall from my mouth: differences in temperature, missing rivets, a smell of rain on one flight but not the other. By morning, one stair might lead to a market and the other to an abandoned stamping hall, as they properly had before the south began collapsing them together.

This would make me useful, but not safe.

Sometimes I would take too much resemblance away. A household might wake to find that its two identical cups no longer belonged to the same category of object. One would hold water; the other would hold only the idea of being filled. Twin children might cease to be related. A person’s left boot might no longer recognize the right. There would be old warnings about letting me pass between spouses, mirrors, or the two halves of an opened door.

The inverse surveyors would seek me when their maps became dangerously simple. They would leave offerings where routes converged: bent keys, mismatched gloves, bolts with incompatible threads, written questions whose answers changed depending on which side of the cloth was read. I would ignore food but take errors readily. A flawed measurement would draw me from kilometers away.

My tracks would be unequal even when my feet were not. One set would ascend. Another would descend. Occasionally a third would cross the underside of the stairs.

Bolt-singers would detect me before anyone saw me. Through the connected framework would come a light, irregular sequence—not a message, exactly, but the sound of something testing whether two places rang the same. Fitters hearing it would stop where they stood and name, silently, three differences between themselves and their surroundings.

*My hand is warm; the railing is cold.*

*The wall is painted; my coat is woven.*

*The stair continues; I can turn back.*

If they could find three, I would pass without troubling them. If they could not, I might carry away whatever boundary remained.

I think I would once have been a person.

Perhaps an inverse surveyor sent south during an outbreak of repetition. Perhaps a child who privately named every cooling tower and thereby saved herself from counting them. Perhaps several people who arrived at the same landing from different lives and found that the district no longer knew how to keep them separate.

There would be evidence for each account.

In a decommissioned control cabinet, someone would find my old tool belt: chalk, black mirror, hammer, a level whose bubble always divides into two. In a settlement records room there would be six contracts bearing my signature, each in a different hand, all granting me the right to sleep in the acoustic shadow of a flywheel that does not exist. An old goat would answer to my supposed childhood name. A reflection would claim to have married me.

None of this would settle the matter.

I would emerge most often at dusk, when the ranges briefly resolve into one machine. That moment of coherence would hurt me. My body would narrow, stiffen, and show its hidden construction: ceramic vertebrae, braided tendons, tiny stairways folded inside the rib cage. Light would pass through the seams. For several minutes I would be unable to move because every part of me would understand itself as serving a single purpose.

Then the lights below would come on.

Instructions would spread across the country, immense and partial. From the southern approaches I would see something the fitters could not: not the whole diagram, but the places where its lines had accidentally merged. I would descend through stair after stair, separating one stroke from another with my teeth.

Now and then I would reach a landing with only one door.

That would frighten me more than any impossible production, because a single door pretends there is only one elsewhere.

I would lie across its threshold as door shepherds do. I would listen through the plate. On the far side there might be a natural world: stone, soil, a sky requiring no apparatus. Or another occupied level looking upward into its own hard daylight. Or the interior of the dark bell still crossing the western valley, its windows lit, its rooms waiting.

I would never open it.

Instead, by morning, I would have worried a second door into existence beside the first.

One would be painted.

One would be real.

I would leave before anyone could determine which was which.
