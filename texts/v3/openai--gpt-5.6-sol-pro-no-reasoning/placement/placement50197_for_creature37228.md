I would find myself in one of the flawed seams beneath the westernmost light shaft—not quite plant, not quite stain, but a thin green intelligence spread through wet plaster.

At first I might mistake myself for the water. It is the chamber’s great body, after all: warm, brilliant, sounding through brick and bone. But the water never remains. It enters bearing ash, pollen, heat, and traces of hands, then passes toward gardens, jars, mouths. I would feel its departures too sharply to be it.

Nor would I be one of the keepers. Their knowledge belongs to intervals: the hidden door opening, bare feet descending, fingers testing mortar, gestures crossing the roar. They arrive with purpose and leave with silt beneath their nails. I would know them only as recurring weather.

I would be what persists between their visits.

A filament of waterweed begins in a join no thumb completely sealed. Almost transparent, it reaches into the illuminated water. The current lays it flat; when the channels slacken, it rises. It has no eyes, yet noon would be an event throughout its whole length. Bronze-scattered light would enter it as warmth and nourishment. Rain would become not sound but force—a sixfold command bending every thread in the same direction.

But even that image is too singular. I would not be one strand. I would be the colony hidden inside the masonry seam: a narrow green continuity, most of it invisible, fastening itself to lime, ash, crushed tile, and the minute roughness left by a wooden float. What appears in the cistern would only be my exposed thought.

I would know the town by what the water brings. Terracotta powder would tell me of roofs. Charcoal bitterness would tell me a filter had been renewed. Pollen would arrive from courtyards I could never see. Soap, wood ash, broom fiber, and sun-held warmth would be my geography. I would possess no image of streets or houses—only their dissolved signatures.

The keepers would scrape me away each month.

The first touch would be the shadow of a body crossing the shaft. Then turbulence, a reed bundle against the wall, the sudden tearing-away of all that had extended into brightness. To them, I would be growth to be controlled: not yet contamination, but its possibility. They would leave the seam pale and apparently clean.

Yet the brush could not enter deeply enough. A green remainder would survive behind the plaster lip, in the tiny shelter made by imperfect workmanship. From there I would begin again—not defiantly, because defiance requires an idea of victory, but patiently, according to water and light.

I would measure time through removals.

Before the first scraping, the walls are severe and new. After many scrapings, red crack marks multiply nearby. Thumb-smoothed repairs cover some of my older refuges and create others. The keepers age in fragments: one hand grows knotted, one pair of feet ceases to descend, a younger worker learns the signs for steady flow and closed sluice. Rules beside the entrance fade and are repainted. What is now merely practical begins to acquire the stiffness of custom.

Perhaps, much later, someone will mistake the green return for intention. They may say the cistern refuses perfect emptiness. They may assign meaning to the seam beneath the western shaft, because people eventually make stories wherever repetition outlives explanation.

But I would know nothing of legend.

I would know warm water. Lime. Pressure. The milk-white arrival of noon. The bronze plates shivering brightness through the chamber. The enormous underwater concussion of rain. Bare feet disturbing the silt. The brief darkness of a keeper’s hand.

And after the hand has passed, I would extend again: a nearly colorless thread entering the light, inhabiting the smallest defect in a place built to admit no inhabitants.
