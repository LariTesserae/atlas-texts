I would find myself far inland, where the city’s constellation thins into survey country: beyond the final pressure tunnel, beyond the last district compressor audible through the soles, but not yet at the white plain of the Still Migration.

There, an obsolete rail line crosses a shallow basin of black glass. Most of its track has been swallowed by dust, though at noon two parallel seams appear when the mineral haze changes charge. At the basin’s center stands a signal house that no train has obeyed for centuries. Its upper rooms have collapsed inward. Its lower chamber remains sealed and warm.

I would live there—or appear to.

Municipal records would classify me differently from decade to decade. First as a salvage custodian, because the signal house still draws a negligible current. Later as an unauthorized resident. Then as a probable colony organism inhabiting a pressure garment. Eventually, after appeals and contradictory examinations, as **provisional civic fauna, communicative, function undetermined**.

No one would know whether I had once been human.

I would be narrow and darkly clothed, my garments patched with amber cut from old coolant spills. My face would usually be veiled against dust. Beneath the veil there might be ordinary features, but they would not remain consistent in witness descriptions. One surveyor would record an elderly woman. Another, a young man with implanted respiratory folds. A child would insist there was no face at all—only a shallow ceramic plate warmed from behind.

I would move rarely. Most travelers would first mistake me for a broken signal post beside the track.

My work would be listening to intervals.

The dead rail retains vibrations long after their causes have passed: thermal cracks, distant extraction machines, cliff herds landing days away. But beneath these ordinary transmissions runs another sequence, too faint and too slow for standard instruments. One pulse in winter. Two separated by nine days. A cluster arriving through the eastern rail, though that rail ends in an abandoned refinery. I would keep these signals in long cabinets of thin stone leaves, each leaf dusted with grains that rearrange themselves when laid against the floor.

The records would resemble writing, but no two readers would agree on their direction.

Sometimes citizens would come to consult me. Not officials at first. Load-bearing people would come: those who wished to leave households that depended upon them; custodians who had begun to suspect their systems were alive; factory solitaries carrying components that had resumed forgotten motions. They would sit across from me in the warm lower chamber.

I would not tell them what to do.

I would place their hands against the rail embedded in the floor and ask them to distinguish three things: the vibration they made themselves, the vibration made by what supported them, and the vibration approaching from elsewhere.

Most could not.

Those who could would leave with a new tapped name.

Children would visit more boldly. They would bring ceramic mite shells, failed one-motion machines, balanced stones stolen from the province where stones right themselves overnight. I would repair nothing. Instead, I would help them build devices with no obvious purpose: a hinge that opened when the signal house cooled, a wheel turned by static accumulating over years, a small sealed box designed to tap once if carried across the next plateau contraction.

One child would leave such a box with me and never return for it.

Many alignments later, it would tap.

That would be the beginning of the city’s concern.

Surveyors would arrive and measure the signal house. They would discover that its foundation had shifted west by less than the width of a finger. The surrounding stone had not shifted. The rails entering the building had lengthened without thinning. Under the floor, where plans showed compacted regolith, resonant scans would find a hollow body with layered boundaries: partly architecture, partly mineral, partly something organized like tissue.

At its center would be a cavity shaped approximately like a person kneeling.

I would deny knowing it was there.

This would not be believed, but it might be true.

Over the years, my body and the station would become harder to separate. When I slept, signal shutters moved in the collapsed tower. If a wall cracked, a bruise appeared along my ribs. During pressure loss I remained conscious, while the building’s seals contracted as though breathing for both of us. Conduit feeders gathered under my skin without causing injury. Their red glow could sometimes be seen through my palms.

The city would debate whether I inhabited the station, whether the station inhabited me, or whether both were temporary organs of the buried thing beneath them.

No dismantling would be authorized. My function was unknown.

Eventually I would be given a municipal title: **Keeper of the Interrupted Route**.

The title would be inaccurate. The route was not interrupted. It simply no longer carried trains.

Its destination lay west.

During the nineteen-year alignment, the basin would fill with sailbacks. They would arrange themselves along the buried rails, respiratory fans raised, forming two translucent lines that ran from horizon to horizon. Ribbon creatures would gather in the signal chamber and drift through the air in pale loops. The old mechanisms above me—gears locked for centuries—would turn one tooth.

I would leave the station then.

Witnesses would see me walking west between the rails, carrying no pressure reserve and wearing no buoyancy bladder. I would stop at the edge of the Still Migration, where entry was forbidden, and wait for the deepest shadow.

The plateau would contract.

Everyone would bend their knees.

I would not.

For one instant, the pulse rising through the ground would hold me upright. The dark forms would take their single pace westward—and I would take one pace east.

Not opposing them. Completing something.

Afterward, the keepers would find a new figure on the white plain near the eastern boundary. It would be tall, dark, and narrow. Instruments would register internal warmth. From one angle it would resemble stone; from another, a person in a veil.

At the same time, I would still be found in the signal house, seated beside the rail.

From then on there would be two of me, though that would be too simple a way to say it. One would speak, accept visitors, and maintain the cabinets of stone leaves. The other would remain on the plain, several kilometers from every neighboring form, facing neither west nor east but toward the city.

The speaking one would gradually cool.

The distant one would gradually warm.

Perhaps, after another three or four alignments, they would exchange places without crossing the land between. Perhaps they had always been doing so. Perhaps every figure in the Still Migration has such a counterpart somewhere: a citizen, a machine, an animal, a warm room in a dead factory—something that moves freely only because another version of it remains still.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a relay between two kinds of patience.

A person, while personhood remained useful.  
A building, when shelter was required.  
An animal under civic law.  
A survey error that persisted when measured again.

And if asked my name, I would lay a hand against the nearest wall and tap a pattern with one interval missing.

The missing beat would be the part that meant me.
