I would find myself where the Long Fall can no longer be seen, but can still be felt.

Not at the basalt pillar, though for a time I might mistake that for my destination. Not in one of the low houses, either, though I would know their lamps and the particular answers of their floors. I would be farther west, among the spillways, where the cataract’s immense common violence separates into narrow currents and each current begins to speak distinctly again.

There is a place there called, in the hand-language, something like **The Returning Split**.

Its spoken name is only a low click followed by breath. Its floor-name is more exact: two heel strikes close together, a pause, then one strike returned through the opposite foot. The gesture for it is made by pressing two fingers into another person’s palm and drawing them apart around the mound of the thumb, then bringing them together at the wrist.

The Returning Split is not reliably land. In some seasons it is a smooth tongue of basalt between channels. In others, water covers it to the ankle or thigh. After floods it may disappear entirely beneath silt, leaving only three sounding pins driven into cracks high along the eastern edge. Beneath it runs a narrow cavity in the rock. Water enters somewhere upstream, passes under the shelf, and emerges delayed from a seam several body lengths away.

The delay changes.

That is why I would be there.

I would be a keeper of passages—not one of the seven-marked keepers of the pillar, but a lesser, more itinerant kind. A route-mender, flood-reader, and finder of hollows. Someone called when a familiar floor begins answering wrongly.

My broad feet would be scarred at the rims from years of crossing mineral crust. Two toes on the left foot would not spread properly, having once been broken under a shifting slab. I would walk with an asymmetry recognizable several cairns away: weight, weight, drag; weight, pause. Children would imitate it behind me and claim the floor itself had developed a limp.

That cadence would be one of my names.

My copper plate would be small and unusually thick, cut down generations ago from a larger plate split during a flood. It would answer reluctantly to light taps but carry deep blows for a great distance through the chest. Across its lower half would be a field of shallow dents made by previous owners. I would add no orderly mark of my own. Instead, one edge would be curled inward from the time I used it to wedge open a skin line while freeing someone trapped beneath a collapsed roof.

This flaw would give it a doubled after-vibration. People would know me by that imperfect return.

My work would begin after floods, when the old paths remain visible in memory but not beneath the feet. I would travel with a weighted staff, bone sounding pins, coils of braided sinew, leather bladders, shell wedges, and a flat hammer whose handle had once been part of a cousin’s thighbone. I would kneel at drowned cairns and place my jaw against their sides. I would drive pins into seams and feel whether the stone answered full or hollow. I would measure currents by tethering inflated hides in them and counting the pulses transmitted along the cord.

Where another person saw water crossing rock, I would feel layers: surface flow, seep beneath, trapped pocket, returning pressure, distant strike. Not perfectly. No person feels perfectly. But well enough to decide where a household should cross, where a new cairn might stand, where children must not crawl, and where a roof slab could be lifted without waking a hidden channel beneath it.

I would be useful because I distrusted certainty.

The Wash punishes those who confuse familiarity with permanence. A route crossed safely for three generations may be only one thin mineral skin over a widening cavity. A dry shelf may be bearing the weight of a buried stream. A strong response through the heel may mean dense stone—or merely a shallow chamber filled to its roof with water.

So I would test twice, then ask another body to test after me.

This would not always make me pleasant company.

I would interrupt confident gestures. I would press questions into wrists after everyone else had agreed. I would stop a crossing because one sounding pin trembled differently from the other two. People hungry to reach a stranded pool would accuse me of cowardice. Sometimes they would be right. Sometimes the pool would empty before we found a longer route, and a household would lose food because I had feared a hollow that did not collapse.

My caution would have a source.

As a child, I would have lost an older sibling beneath apparently solid stone. Not to deep water, not to a predator, but to a shelf that opened like a mouth under both hands. The person would fall only a little distance, yet the flow beneath would turn the body sideways and wedge it where no arm could reach. For hours the family would feel the trapped rhythm through the slab: a heel striking from below, slower and slower, while tools broke against the rock.

The final blows would become part of my floor-name.

The body would never be recovered. This would be a particular pain in the Wash, where the dead are expected to become useful. No fat for the lamp, no bone for tools, no skull-tone in the wall. Only a rhythm entering the ground and ceasing there.

I would grow into someone who believed that hollows were not empty.

They held water, danger, delay, lost bodies, old workmanship, and answers traveling by paths no living person could see. A cavity beneath the floor was not an absence but a hidden relation between distant places.

That belief would lead me, eventually, to the pillar.

Not because I wished to open it. At least, that is what I would tell myself.

I would sit outside the keepers’ ring with my hands on the stone and feel the seven tongues answer the falling drops. The pillar’s pattern would travel outward through saturated basalt, mingling with rain, lake pressure, footsteps, animals, and the immense continuous body of the Long Fall. Most people would attend to the sequence of strikes. I would attend to what came after each strike: the faint returning texture, the tiny delayed answer beneath the main note.

The pillar would not feel like a vessel with water passing down through it.

It would feel rooted.

Some of its passages would extend beneath the fitted rings and into the bedrock. Or perhaps the vibration merely gave that impression. The keepers would warn me that stone often seemed deeper when it was hollow. I would agree, openly.

Privately, I would begin mapping the delayed answers.

I would compare them to the Returning Split, where water vanished beneath one shelf and emerged elsewhere. I would place bladders in shallow basins around the pillar and watch for minute rises after each internal sequence. I would borrow old shell records and discover that certain “irregular” drops were followed, many hours later, by changes in springs east of the lake.

The pattern would not prove that the pillar controlled the Wash.

It might prove something stranger: that the pillar was not one device but an exposed part of a system spread through the rock. The standing rings, drowned foundations, rows of sockets, vanished channels, and hollow floors might all belong to one immense circulation—built, natural, or long since made into both.

I would not settle the question.

Instead I would become caught between the two old dances. The receiving hands and the striking hands. The ones who maintain and the ones who would understand by alteration.

I would believe both were incomplete.

Water could not be made to obey, but neither was maintenance innocent. Clearing an outlet changed flow. Replacing a cup changed weight and timing. Even reverence pressed upon the thing revered. The pillar’s keepers were already participants in whatever it did; they merely preferred actions whose consequences had become traditional.

This thought would make me dangerous, though not rebellious in any loud sense. Loud rebellion has little meaning in the Wash.

I would make a copy.

Not of the pillar. I would have neither the skill nor the copper nor the arrogance for that. I would build a small arrangement at the Returning Split: seven shell tongues beneath a dripping seam, each mounted over a different buried sounding pin. Water would fall. The shells would strike. The pins would carry answers through the hollow shelf.

It would be a crude thing, forever clogging with mineral grease. Children would call it my little rain animal. The pillar keepers would refuse to name it at all.

Most days it would tell us nothing.

Then, before one flood, its fourth tongue would cease answering.

The seam would still drip. The shell would still visibly move. But the strike would vanish into the rock without returning. Beneath the shelf, a cavity had filled—or opened—or connected to another passage.

I would order the nearby households uphill.

Some would go because they trusted me. Others because my repeated questions had made resistance exhausting. A few would remain to secure nets and fences.

The flood would arrive unevenly. Not as the familiar expanding skin, but first through the ground. Water would rise from cracks behind the houses. The Returning Split would lift along one edge, settle, then break into several immense rounded slabs. The households that left would survive. One person who stayed would not.

Afterward, people would say the small device had predicted the break.

I would insist that it had only revealed a change.

The distinction would matter fiercely to me and almost not at all to anyone else.

Some would ask me to build such devices at every route. Some pillar keepers would oppose this, fearing that a field of false warnings would make true warning meaningless. Others would quietly bring me better shell, old drilled stones, records of drop sequences. The two versions of the ancient dance would begin acquiring a third.

In this third dance, the hands would first cup to receive the water. Then one hand would strike the stone. The other would remain open, feeling what returned.

That would be my place in the world: not the person who learns whether the Wash was built, nor the one who discovers the purpose of the Long Fall. I would live too briefly for revelations on that scale. My measurements would be compromised by weather, memory, changing rock, and the unavoidable pressure of my own hands. Much of what I believed would be wrong.

But I might leave behind a useful error.

Perhaps, after my death, my little devices would be rebuilt into forms I would not recognize. Shell tongues would become bone membranes, then copper scraps. Routes would gain not merely identifying cairns but stones that answered changes beneath them. Children would learn another distinction against their backs: not only solid, hollow, flowing beneath, unsafe, but **hollow becoming**.

My body would be divided among this work.

My thick copper plate would pass to someone less cautious than I had been. They would hammer the curled edge flat and thereby destroy the doubled vibration by which people knew me. This would be appropriate. A tool should not preserve a flaw after the flaw ceases to be useful.

The sensitive bones of my lower jaw would become sounding pieces set beneath two distant cairns. My teeth would count maintenance debts. The skin of my palms, if it could be cured finely enough, might line the cups of one of the small warning devices. My left foot, with its badly healed toes, would yield poor tools. The nails might become wedges.

My skull would not go into the house wall.

I would ask for it to be mortared beneath the Returning Split, face inward toward the cavity, though perhaps the household would refuse such waste or sentiment. If they consented, water would slowly alter it. The channels within the bone would fill with silt. Its answer would deepen, blur, and finally become indistinguishable from the shelf.

Those who remembered me would touch the first sounding pin, then the fourth, then the stone between them.

At first this sequence would mean my mouth-name.

Later it would mean caution.

Later still, perhaps, it would mean only that the route once broke there.

That would be enough. In the Wash, to endure is not to remain oneself. It is to enter the next repair.
