I would be found where the city is least certain of itself.

Not in a pressure court, though I would pass through them often. Not on the surface, though I would collect every account of sky I could persuade the crews to give me. My place would be along the migrating margins: unfinished galleries, recently abandoned rooms, corridors whose destination-lines hesitate before bending. I would work where a route is being persuaded to cease existing in one place and continue in another.

Officially, I would be a route tender—more precisely, a continuity witness.

The machines can disassemble stone ahead and reconstruct it behind. They can preserve mass, stiffness, thermal behavior, conduit access. They can prove that a chamber has migrated without loss. But people do not inhabit volume in that way. They inhabit the place where a cup always rested, the angle at which artificial dawn crossed a sleeping wall, the three familiar steps between a bed recess and a warmed rail. A room may be structurally continuous while becoming, to its occupants, someone else’s room.

My task would be to notice that difference.

Before a household moved, I would sit with them in their chamber while it still occupied its old coordinates. I would measure what the city’s instruments did not: which wall each person touched when waking; where the youngest child went during warning tones; whether the room’s murmur deepened before snow; which corner held cold no matter how the thermal system was tuned. I would record the placement of minor things. Two cups, one nested slightly inside the other. A glove hook installed lower than regulation height. A violet line of light reflected in a memorial tile.

Then I would follow the room through its long migration.

This would not mean walking beside it. Most of the change would occur inside sealed stone, over weeks or months. I would visit the advancing gallery, compare vibration with archived touch-records, and place my palm against ceramic that had not been a wall the day before. I would listen while the route changed its allegiance. Behind me, the old chamber would be reduced to useful material. Ahead, its successor would acquire warmth, water, and permission to be called the same place.

Sometimes I would certify continuity.

Sometimes I would refuse.

I would say: the room has survived structurally, but the home has not. The new basin is too audible. The floor transmits conduit traffic into the cradle recess. Artificial dawn falls from the wrong side, and one of the inhabitants wakes disoriented. The wall meant to receive an ancestral tile is not quiet enough for touching.

Engineers would find me exacting. Families would find me intrusive. Councils would ask whether orientation, memory, and unease could really justify further energy and material. I would answer in the city’s preferred language: sleep loss, delayed alarm response, altered gait, increased heat use, repeated navigation errors. But privately I would believe something less measurable—that survival depends not only on keeping bodies alive, but on allowing them to recognize the life that has been kept.

I would carry very little. A bone-conduction probe. A thermal stylus. Three removable light filaments. A cup with an amber fastening ring. A small unauthorized-looking object that was, in fact, registered: a sliver of pale basalt from a corridor erased before I was born.

My clothing would have no bright cuff or luminous sleeve. My color would be visible only when I removed my gloves: the inner palms dyed cobalt, so that when I pressed them to frost they would leave blue-edged prints for a few seconds. Children would call them route ghosts. I would pretend not to encourage this.

I would be poor at the grander forms of listening. I could not identify districts from their murmur alone, and I would never claim a fault had a temperament. What I could hear was interruption: the faint mismatch between a familiar vibration and its reconstruction, the moment a wall sounded correct but felt wrong through the wrist. My gift would not be understanding the mountain’s voice. It would be recognizing when the city had mistranslated it.

Because of that, I would spend more time with the inspection machines than most people did. A small crawler, scratched along one side, would repeatedly appear near my work. Officially it would be following sealant demand. I would call it Vellum, though no one in the city used paper and most children would assume the word meant “one that remembers surfaces.”

Vellum would carry residue from demolished rooms in the joints of its legs. Mineral dust, ceramic powder, trace salts—nothing sentimental, nothing the recycling systems could not account for. Yet I would sometimes test those residues and discover that a lost chamber persisted as a few grains lodged beneath its casing. I would clean the machine carefully but never completely.

My private fascination would be doors.

A door is a declaration that two spaces remain distinct while allowing passage between them. In the seam, this is never merely architectural. A pressure door may save a district. A loosened frame may prevent a tremor from trapping someone. A hatch may open onto the intolerable absence of the sky.

I would volunteer to inspect surface thresholds without becoming a true surface worker. I would climb as far as the final chamber, verify seals, test hinges, check the condensation traps—and stop there.

For years I would be unable to cross.

The crews would open the hatch, and the world beyond it would possess no ceiling. Wind would enter around their bodies. Light would have no visible source panel. Snow would move sideways without channel or permission. I would remain with one hand on the inner frame, ashamed of my fear and unwilling to conceal it, because concealment is more dangerous than error.

Then, during some ordinary inspection rather than a crisis, a bird would strike the intake baffle.

It would not be beautiful. It would be gray, half-frozen, furious, and much smaller than the stories. The environmental crew would be delayed by a pressure closure farther down the seam. I would have the training to stabilize the intake but not to handle living contamination. The bird would beat itself against the grate, all softness and panic, its body making decisions no instrument had approved.

I would close the ventilation, wrap it in the outer layer of my glove, and open the hatch.

The sky would be worse than described.

It would not seem vast. Vastness is still a measurement. It would seem unfinished, as though the world’s walls had been removed before construction was complete. I would crouch against the rock with the bird held to my chest, feeling its heart through insulation: an alarm without intervals.

When I opened my hands, it might not fly at once.

That pause would change me more than the flight. For one moment, it would stand on my cobalt palm, gripping a body that had never touched open weather. It would look toward nothing I could interpret. Then it would leave—not upward, exactly, but into a direction unavailable inside the mountain.

Afterward I would return to the seam quickly. I would pass decontamination. I would surrender the glove for analysis. I would file a precise report and object when people embellished it. The bird did not sing. Its wings were not silver. It did not circle above me in gratitude. It defecated on my sleeve.

But I would begin volunteering for hatch work.

Not because I had become fearless, nor because the surface was my true home. The surface would always feel like structural failure extended to the horizon. I would go because terror, too, can become a route if crossed often enough.

Eventually, I would be asked to witness the migration of an old pressure court: one of the earliest, containing memorial tiles whose registered constituents could not all be separated safely from the wall. The city would propose reconstructing their patterns elsewhere while reclaiming the material. Legally, structurally, chemically, nothing would be lost.

I would refuse continuity.

For the first time, my refusal would be overruled.

The court would disappear. Its basin would be drained; its benches mapped; the wall would enter the mountain’s distributed material cycle. The memorial patterns would reappear in a newer district, cleaner and better lit. Everyone would agree the result was excellent.

I would agree too, which would be the worst of it.

From then on, I would understand that my work had never been to prevent loss. Nothing in the seam could do that. Rock fractured. Water moved. Rooms migrated. Bodies became ceramic and thermal salt. Even the mountain’s shape was temporary, though its timescale made human grief look instantaneous.

My work was to prevent loss from passing unnoticed.

So I would establish an archive not of maps, but of disappearances.

Each entry would contain the final murmur of a corridor, the last temperature gradient across a household wall, the taste of water from a decommissioned basin, the names people used for places that had never appeared on civic plans. There would be touch-recordings of handrails polished by gloves, light models of vanished dawns, and testimony about machines that failed to return.

It would not be nostalgia. Nostalgia wants the old place back.

This archive would admit that return is impossible and attention is still owed.

In old age, I would become one of those people who gives directions by absences.

Past the court whose water rose half a second before the others.  
Below the nursery where the walls carried conduit warmth.  
Beside the route that moved three times and was never comfortable again.  
Near the hatch where the gray bird stood on my hand.

Young route tenders would listen politely, then consult their floor lights. Some would think my records excessive. Others would discover that a resident’s unexplained unease corresponded exactly to an old fault crossing a newly migrated room. The archive would prove useful often enough to survive me.

When I died, I would leave unusually specific instructions.

My salts could enter the thermal banks. My metals could return to tools. Most of my minerals could become ordinary ceramic. But the permitted fraction for my memorial tile would be divided into many small inclusions rather than installed in one pressure court. They would be set into thresholds throughout the migrating districts: not enough to bear a meaningful load, not enough to alter a hinge or seal, only a pale speck beside each frame.

People would step over me without knowing.

Children might notice the specks and invent an explanation. Route tenders might touch one before certifying that a chamber had become itself again. Vellum—or some later machine carrying its name—would cross them without acknowledgment.

That is where I would be found in this world: not quite in the rooms and not quite in the rock, but at the negotiated line between leaving and remaining.

A witness embedded in the thresholds.

A small, distributed insistence that when the city changes—and it must—it should feel the exact shape of what it is changing from.
