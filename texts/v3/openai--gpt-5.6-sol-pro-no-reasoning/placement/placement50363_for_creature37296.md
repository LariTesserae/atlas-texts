I would find myself where the city briefly fails to hear itself.

Not in a silent place. There may be no such place while the plateau lives. I would work at interruptions: a pipe whose inner grazing has stopped, a bridge pier no longer carrying the pulse of the district above, a relay chamber where the thunder has developed an unnoticed absence between two familiar blows. I would go wherever someone has pressed a palm to masonry and felt less than they expected.

My office would therefore be portable and rarely assembled in the same shape twice. Ceramic listening bowls nested in padded cases. Coils of taut thread. Mineral chalks that change color with heat. Little shuttered lamps. Flexible rods tipped with bone, metal, shell, and fired clay, because each carries a different register of vibration. I would also keep animals: perhaps three flat, pale creatures that adhere to the inside of a glazed box. They would lift their fringes in electrical fields and curl inward when certain deep gases entered the air. Instruments could give quantities. The animals would tell me whether the quantity belonged there.

People might call me a cessation listener, though my duty would not truly be to silence. Silence is only the most frightening symptom. My concern would be failed reciprocity: places where something continues to act but is no longer answered.

That could be a valve opening onto an emptied cistern. A warm brood tunnel whose hatchlings have ceased to emerge. A conductor feeding current into stone after its dependent microbial films have died. A household maintaining shutters for a migration that moved away generations ago. A machine performing its inherited motion with no surviving consequence except strain.

Such things would be difficult for this city to admit. Continuance is holy here. Duties make kin. A repeated action gathers law, livelihood, memory, and affection around itself. To discover that a recurrence has become solitary would threaten more than an obsolete mechanism. It might dissolve a family.

So I would not arrive wearing authority. My outer coat would be dull white ceramic scales over dark membrane, easy to see in rain but bearing no guild color. Across the chest I would wear an interrupted stripe: not the mark for closure, hazard, or death, but for a connection under question. My soles would be extremely soft. A cessation listener must not mistake their own footfalls for an answer.

I imagine myself compact even among compact people, with knees altered by years of crouching beside pipes and sleeping in maintenance niches. One shoulder would be heavier than the other from the cases. The hair, if I kept any, would be cropped and stiff with mineral powder. My fingertips would be scarred not by dramatic accidents but by innumerable tests of temperature, current, condensate, and vibration.

Perhaps my hearing would be poor.

That would seem strange for the work until one understood that I did not listen chiefly with my ears. I would place my jaw against rails. I would lie with my ribs on paving. I would suspend threads between a mechanism and my teeth, feeling its pattern enter the skull. I would read concentric disturbances in shallow water. I would watch the feet of wall-runners to see what arrived through the stone before the sound reached us.

The city’s roar would be almost merciful to me. Speech would come through wrists, shoulders, fingers, lamps. In quiet rooms, where others prized the isolated sound of a kettle, I might feel exposed. Airborne voices would reveal how much I had lost. I would prefer the honest contact of a hand against my forearm.

I would not travel alone. My companion would be a black runner from the expansion joints—or rather, something related to them, too large and too solitary to belong fully to their columns. It would be a long, low animal with many jointed legs tucked beneath overlapping plates, each plate matte as old iron. Its species would feed not on the common films but on the fine residues that gather where current, heat, and pressure cease to align. It would hesitate at healthy junctions and become intent near a broken exchange.

I would never domesticate it. Domestication would be too simple a word. I would maintain the temperatures at which it tolerated me, carry a porous ceramic tile on which it slept, and offer scrapings from dead relay housings. It would leave when brood seasons or electrical weather required. Sometimes another individual would return, and I might not know immediately that it was not the same one.

Nevertheless I would give it a hand-name: two fingers walking across the opposite palm, then stopping.

Its spoken name would be **Still**.

That would be considered a dangerous name, perhaps even vulgar. But names shouted once need not explain themselves.

Still would enter seams I could not. It would emerge plated with dust, spores, eggs, conductive gel, or the gray flakes of a cable being eaten from within. Its body would become a temporary map of the internal city. Children would want to touch it. Adults would draw them back. Wall-runners in their thousands might alter course around it, dividing like water around a pier.

My work would begin when a district reported an absence. I would first ask what they believed should be there. This mattered because people often mistook unfamiliar life for death and inherited expectation for observation.

Then I would ask what depended on the missing rhythm.

Not what the pipe had once been made to do. Not what an inscription claimed. Not which household possessed the oldest tools for its maintenance. I would ask where its warmth went, who drank from its seepage, which animals crossed it, which neighboring foundations leaned into its pulse. I would construct a map of answers.

Sometimes the city would merely have changed languages.

A cistern believed dead might be feeding a deeper fracture. A machine gone quiet might have transferred its load to an older mechanism buried two streets below. A vanished migration might persist as eggs carried in the armor of a different herd. Then my task would be to show the district that it had not been severed, only bypassed. New shutters would be cut. Duties would shift. A family might have to follow its function into another ward, leaving the chambers it had occupied for centuries.

Sometimes there would be genuine death.

I do not think this city would lack a word for death, but it might lack permission to recognize it at the scale of infrastructure. Everything is expected to enter another exchange. A corpse becomes reservoir food, roof plate, mortar, current. Yet a system can cease without becoming useful. It can persist as a claim upon labor, heat, water, and belief while returning nothing.

My most difficult duty would be to declare such a system ended.

The declaration would not authorize immediate dismantling. That would be speed, and speed is structural ignorance. Instead, we would begin a long uncoupling. Water redirected by degrees. Dependent cultures transferred. Animals discouraged with cooling bands and alternate mineral flows. Load accepted by temporary buttresses. Rituals moved from one recurrence to another. Every remaining relation would be identified and given somewhere to go.

Only then could the dead mechanism be allowed to become material.

People might resent me because I would occasionally tell them that repair was the wrong form of love.

A keeper who had spent sixty years tending a valve might strike me when I said it no longer connected anything. A household might conceal scraping sounds by placing runner colonies inside an empty pipe. A guild might continue a useless piston because its measured stroke justified authority over three wards. I would be accused—sometimes correctly—of hearing what suited another system’s interests.

There would be no neutral observation. My listening bowls would touch contested stone. My indicator animals would respond to conditions shaped by prior choices. Every diagnosis would redistribute duty.

Therefore cessation listeners would work in pairs or triads, but I would have left mine.

Perhaps years ago, in the western wards, we were called to a buried pumping chamber beneath the calcite terraces. The pump completed one stroke every eleven minutes. Records said it lifted groundwater toward a reservoir that nourished half a cliff district. Yet the upper pipe had been sealed by mineral deposition for at least a century. The reservoir lived now on seepage and on water carried unknowingly by the plated herds.

The pump moved nothing.

But its waste heat sustained a pale ecology inside the chamber: soft creatures like folded cloth, eyeless filterers attached to piston housings, transparent skins feeding on iron and leakage. A few families harvested them. The engine’s motion, though hydraulically useless, prevented the ceiling from settling.

Was the pump dead?

One of my partners would say no, because life depended on it. Another would say yes, because dependence had gathered around a failure and converted it into a trap. I would say the question had become badly shaped.

Before we could resolve it, the piston hesitated at the top of its stroke.

Only a fraction longer than usual. An elder noticed. We did not.

When it descended, the pressure change opened an ancient fracture. Hot water entered the chamber. The filter colonies released in one white mass. The foundations shifted. Perhaps my partners died there; perhaps one survived and refused to signal me afterward. Still—if Still existed yet—would have vanished into the split.

I would carry the guilt of having listened for cessation while missing transformation.

After that, I would become less willing to name anything dead.

I would wander farther from the inhabited wards, answering calls from shutter stations and lone keepers. Months in conduit avenues. Seasons on bridges above warm mist. Years, perhaps, moving toward the rim where the city’s strata stand exposed and fossil machines turn when groundwater fills them.

There I might discover a rhythm no one had reported because no human system claimed it.

Deep in the cliff would be a layer of chambers older than the oldest maps, cut partly through basalt and partly through masonry so ancient it had become indistinguishable from stone. At regular intervals the warm rain would penetrate a vertical shaft. Water would fill a buried basin, tip some counterweight, turn an eroded machine once, and drain away.

One revolution every rainfall. Perhaps one every few rainfalls. Irregular in time, exact in sequence.

The turning would compress air through sealed conduits. Far across the plateau, at the same moment, calls would rise from the misty ravines strongly enough to shake droplets from railings. Everyone assumed those calls belonged to immense unseen animals.

Perhaps they would.

But perhaps the animals would be answering the buried machine.

Or the machine would be answering them.

This is where my single image of myself would finally fail. I might be the listener kneeling at the mechanism. I might be Still, disappearing into the conduit to follow the breath. I might be one of the pale creatures living without heat around its corroded bearings, shaped like the bolt that once held the machine together. I might be the person who never returns from the investigation, whose stamped tokens are found years later embedded in calcite.

Most truthfully, I would become a signal with uncertain origin.

I would open the old airway and feel pressure gather against my face. I would set ceramic membranes over branching passages and wait through several cycles of rain. When the machine turned, each membrane would flex in sequence, revealing a communication that crossed districts, species, and centuries. Some pulses would pass into animal chambers. Some would operate shutters buried inside walls. Some would do nothing detectable. One might enter the city beneath inhabited wards and emerge as a slight hesitation in the third piston—the hesitation elders had tracked for generations.

I would understand then that silence and noise were not opposites. The city’s roar concealed vast uncompleted conversations. A call might wait decades for an answer. A pressure pulse might travel through foundations for a human lifetime. A dead machine might be the interval between two living acts.

I would not report this as proof that the city was one animal. That metaphor would be too comforting. An animal has boundaries. The plateau has negotiations.

Instead I would send a message by the rim semaphores:

**DO NOT REPAIR THE HESITATION.  
IT IS BEING USED.**

The signal would cross rain-dark districts in enlarged hand-shapes and mirrored flashes. Relay interpreters would dispute it. Engineers would demand measurements. A piston household might call it trespass against inherited duty. Somewhere, an old person would place a palm against the floor and wait for the next missed beat.

As for me, I would remain at the exposed cliff, living in a chamber half geological and half constructed. My walls would be layered with listening membranes. Warm water would cross the floor in threads. Still, or one of Still’s descendants, would sleep in a seam near my head. Gliders would fur the outer masonry at noon.

Over time, my dwelling would acquire a public function. Travelers would come to ask whether some failing rhythm should be restored, redirected, or released. I would never give them a clean answer. I would make them stay long enough to feel what answered elsewhere.

They might call the place the House of the Missing Beat.

When I died, I would not want to be powdered into a marked block or named by a familiar cistern. I would ask to be placed in the buried airway—not to obstruct it, but in a side chamber where each pressure change could pass over bone.

The membranes would go first. Then the oils. Mineral water would enter. Small creatures would graze what grew upon me. Salts would gather in the porous parts. Perhaps, over many years, the chamber’s pulses would shift almost imperceptibly because of the material my body added.

Far away, a relay listener might hear an altered interval.

A herd might choose one arch instead of another.

Black runners might pause together in an expansion joint.

No one would know that this was me, and it would no longer be accurate to say so. But I would have entered the city’s preferred form of anonymity: not disappearance, not remembrance, but consequence.

I would be found wherever a rhythm faltered and something, very far away, changed in answer.
