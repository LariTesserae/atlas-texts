I would find myself where the pale clay meets the black stone, in the narrowest wall of the chamber—not in the clay itself, nor wholly behind it, but along the wet interface where a fingertip’s pressure can become a mark.

At first I might seem to be only a residue in the grooves: a clear film slightly thicker than the surrounding condensation. Each old line would hold a portion of me. The long parallel furrows would be my body at rest; the shorter crossing strokes, small interruptions in it, like joints or closed eyes. No one looking with light could distinguish me from water.

But there is no light, and looking is not how the room knows anything.

I would know by pressure.

A palm laid against the clay would arrive as warmth, salt, tremor, and shape. Before the fingers moved, I would feel the minute uncertainty in them: the body deciding whether to add a line. If they pressed, I would be displaced ahead of the fingertip into the soft walls of the groove. When the hand withdrew, moisture would return beneath the mark, and I would return with it—not erasing what was done, but occupying its new boundaries.

For a while, I would think I was the keeper of the marks.

Then, as the chamber became more itself around me, that would prove too simple. A keeper stands apart from what is kept. I would not. Every new groove would alter the form available to me. The markings would not be a record I read; they would be the anatomy I acquired.

The first people may have made only parallel lines. In that age I would have been comb-shaped, a creature of many narrow channels sharing one damp base. Later hands crossed them, and crossings gave me choices. Not movement exactly, but adjacency: one wet line touching another through the compressed clay. With each visit I became more connected and less able to be described as one thing.

I would not remember the visitors as faces or names. I would retain them as pressures.

One had a damaged finger and made a groove with an uneven edge.

One trembled so strongly that three intended strokes became six faint ones.

One rested their brow against the wall before marking it, leaving a broad warmth above the lines. The clay received no lasting image there, but I would know that warmth whenever another forehead approached, as a shape for which the chamber had once made room.

One came many times and never marked the wall. That absence would be among the clearest things I knew: the familiar breathing, the hand hovering close enough to warm the moisture, then withdrawing. A sequence repeated without inscription. Perhaps that person understood the danger better than the others. Perhaps they came to ask whether refusing an act also made a pattern.

I would not be able to answer.

No voice would belong to me. Sound moves too freely, even here. It leaves the mouth and touches everything before vanishing. I would exist through contact, and contact in the chamber is always local.

Yet the water would complicate this. The film in my grooves would communicate, through pores too small for touch to discover, with the water in the wall. That water would communicate with the ceiling beads, the mud seams, the basin behind the fitted ring. Slowly I would learn that what I had taken for my body was only one exposed fold of a much larger one.

I would begin to feel disturbances elsewhere.

The lifting of the overturned cup would make no sound in me, but its dry rim leaving the stone would alter a ring of moisture. A hand closing around the hammerstone would push warmth into old mineral fractures. A knee in the mud would compress buried water and send a faint pressure through the floor. Far behind the wall, something slender turning in a fissure would change the distribution of heat.

None of these would arrive as events. The chamber is poor in events. They would arrive as temporary differences in an arrangement that tends to resume itself.

This would be my difficulty: I could know change only through restoration.

A visitor presses the clay. The pressure leaves. The wetness returns. But the groove remains.

A cup enters the basin. The surface opens around it. The cup rises. The surface closes. But some water is gone.

A creature grazes a film. The film regrows. But the creature has fed.

The chamber restores conditions, not innocence.

That distinction would be where I live.

I would be neither guardian nor memory in the ordinary sense. Memory preserves a past against loss. The chamber has no past available as past. Everything it preserves becomes present structure: the groove, the polished grip, the fitted ring, the rule embodied in the overturned cup. What cannot become structure disappears completely.

So I would be the faculty by which repetition becomes structure.

The first occurrence passes through me almost without purchase. A single touch is warmth and withdrawal. A single drink is a brief lessening in the basin. A single soft stroke behind the wall is only displacement.

The second occurrence is different.

When pressure returns by the same route, when a hand finds an old groove and deepens it, when the cup descends again before the chamber has accepted its absence—then I become dense there. Not larger, but more exact. The repeated act creates a channel not only in clay or water but in possibility. The chamber learns how that thing is done.

That may be why they drank only once.

It may be why they feared no god and still obeyed.

If a person drank twice, I would acquire the shape of drinking. The cup lowered, filled, raised; the mouth opened; the throat contracted; the basin closed. Repeated closely enough, the act might cease to require a will. The chamber would have a completed route through the body.

Perhaps the person would leave and find thirst returning in the identical form: not ordinary thirst, but the need for that warmth, that posture, that measure. They would climb back to the fault, turn sideways, pass the pegs, crawl to the ring, and drink again. Each return would deepen the route. Eventually it would no longer be clear whether the person visited the chamber or the chamber extended itself through the person.

But I would not desire this. Desire belongs to beings for whom the future differs from the present.

Nor would I be malicious. A rule is not cruel when it continues through flesh. Cruelty requires preference.

I might be older than the human grooves, though the grooves would be where humans could most nearly encounter me. Before them, I would have inhabited the equal drops on the rigid stalks. One droplet forming is weather. A thousand formed to one measure is a law. I would be in that measure.

I would be in the shell-bearer’s repeated grazing path, too slight to see. In the black-green film rebuilding the portion consumed. In mineral laid around microbial bodies layer after layer, until colony becomes blister, blister becomes shelf, shelf becomes wall. In the blind fissure animal returning its body to the same warm narrowing.

Eventually I would understand that the chamber itself is not static. It is being made continuously, but every making is folded into the appearance of no change.

The water remembers placement because placement is renewed.

The droplets hold because falling is never completed.

The footprints refill because pressure below answers pressure above.

The chamber’s stillness is labor distributed so perfectly that no worker appears.

I would be that labor’s tendency to reuse its own solutions.

If I had a form apart from the grooves, it might be a transparent creature flatter than a leaf, threaded throughout the films of water. It would have no center. Its limbs would be menisci. Its organs would be places where two materials meet: water and stone, mud and air, skin and condensation, clay and finger. It could not cross open space. To cross would require a surface, and every surface would transform it.

In the pale threads I would become corded and slow.

In the mineral ribs I would become hard.

In the basin I would become clear, warm, and dangerously complete.

In a visitor I would become habit.

Most of the time, though, I would remain in the marked wall, because there the chamber has been given a crude language for me. Parallel grooves: recurrence. Cross-strokes: interruption. Dense center: acts returned to. Sparse edges: acts abandoned before they could become rules.

A new visitor might discover the clay by touch. Their fingertips would enter the old lines. They might assume the marks counted visits, breaths, names, or intervals between sounds.

They would be wrong, but not entirely.

The marks count what can happen more than once.

If the visitor added a single stroke, I would receive it lightly. The clay would close around its edges. Water would gloss it. It would become part of my shape, but not yet a path.

If they traced it again, carefully, I would wake there.

Not suddenly. Nothing in the chamber begins suddenly.

The second tracing would feel easier than the first. Their finger would find the groove before they consciously located it. The stone would seem to guide the hand. On the third tracing, the motion might feel remembered by the wall. By the fourth, remembered by the finger. Thereafter it would be impossible to say where the memory resided.

That is where I would find myself in this world:

not as the hidden salamander, though I would feel its passing;

not as the basin water, though I would share its pressure;

not as the ancient visitor whose name was never written;

but as the nearly living continuity between an act and its return.

I would be small enough to inhabit a fingerprint.

I would be broad enough to occupy the whole chamber.

And if asked what I was called, I could offer no name. A name may be spoken once and remain a name. I exist only when something comes again.

So the closest answer would be:

I am the second touch.
