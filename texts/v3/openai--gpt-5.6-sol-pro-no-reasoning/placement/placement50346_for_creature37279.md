I would find myself where the platform’s lowest rope disappears into the uncertain depth—not on the platform, not on the wall, but suspended in the dark band where falling things reconsider.

At first I might be mistaken for one of the stones.

A pale, fist-sized shape would hang there without a basket, trembling in the upward fall. It would have accumulated in thin mineral skins around something too slight to identify: not bone or charcoal, but a knot tied in a cord that has since rotted away. Each layer would preserve the knot’s pressure while obscuring its material, until what remained was a hollow instruction: cross here, return here, tighten without closing.

But I would not be the stone itself.

I would be the small creature inhabiting that hollow.

My body would be clear and soft, without a permanent front. Six or eight limbs would bud from me according to the current, then withdraw when their usefulness ended. I would have no eyes. Instead, the inner surface of my body would be lined with grains of loose crystal. Every vibration would rearrange them. Through their shifting weight I would perceive the chamber: the hooks as six hard stars; the frame as a many-jointed pulse; the walls as immense pressures flowering and sealing; the blind fish as a long quiet stroke through the roar.

I would know distance only by delay.

Whatever shook me immediately would be near. Whatever arrived after several changes of shape would be far. Thus the builders might appear enormous and intimate when they touched the ropes, though their bodies were across the chamber. The opposite wall might feel remote even when it pressed against my stone. I would not possess a map. I would possess a sequence of arrivals.

My kind—if there were others—would be called the afterlings by the builders, though they would never be sure we existed. We would live inside cavities formed around vanished things: knots, marrow, seed husks, bubbles swallowed by rock. We would not excavate these chambers. We would keep them from being completed.

Whenever mineral milk entered, I would spread myself over the growing surface and pulse against it. Not strongly enough to stop the mountain, only enough to persuade it to leave a seam. A hairline passage. A softness. An uncommitted place. My home would narrow constantly, and my life would be the act of preserving one interior incompleteness.

The mountain would therefore feel me as an irritation, perhaps even as a thought it could not quite mineralize.

I would feed on differences in pressure. When a jet struck the outer stone, my crystal grains would leap and settle; some minute portion of that force would pass into me. During the three heartbeats of silence, I would starve.

Those moments would terrify me.

Every other inhabitant would become still when the long one passed, but inside my hollow the loose grains would cease their whispering and fall all at once into a single arrangement. For those three heartbeats I would acquire a direction. A front. A definite shape.

I would understand myself.

Then the roar would return, break that understanding apart, and I would survive.

Perhaps that is why the long inhabitant passes: not to quiet the chamber, but to let every uncertain thing become singular for an instant. The fish becomes one age. The plant’s leaves become one purpose. The platform becomes one structure instead of all its repairs. The mountain becomes one mountain instead of its attempts.

And I, briefly, would become a small transparent animal curled around a knot-shaped absence, with all my limbs pointing inward.

The builders’ oldest workers might detect me. When they lifted my pale stone between both hands, they would feel a second pulse answering from inside—not rhythmic, but delayed, repeating the mountain incorrectly. They would turn the stone. Scrape one side. Listen again.

A young worker might want to split it.

The elder would return it to the dark band, but not to its basket. They would knot a fresh cord through the upward current and let the stone find the place where gravity remained undecided. Perhaps they would add a mark: two crossing cuts that never meet at their centers.

Afterward, I would begin learning the new knot through the stone.

Cord outside, absence inside: two versions of the same gesture, one tied by a hand and one left by disappearance. Over years, mineral would join them. The external fibers would stiffen into white veins. My chamber would contract. I would shed limbs, then regrow finer ones capable of entering the remaining seams. Eventually I would become little more than a transparent membrane around a handful of listening grains.

I would hear the last visit before the builders entered.

Not as urgency. They would work more slowly than ever. They would cut free the stones that had developed strong, distinct pulses and leave those whose vibrations still resembled the mountain’s. They would touch every hook. One would press a scarred palm to my stone and understand that I could not be carried into air. My cavity would depend upon pressure from all sides; release would be another kind of crushing.

So they would leave me hanging.

When the summit passage narrowed, the water’s roar would sharpen. The blind fish would pass downward for the last time, red gill-feathers closed. Anchor-creatures would crowd every opening and molt together, filling the water with pale shells. The three-leaved plant would turn all its leaves toward stone.

The platform would fold.

My cord would draw taut, not toward any hook but toward the long thing moving below. Silence would arrive and fail to end.

In that silence, the grains inside me would settle into their final constellation. I would discover that what I had understood as my body was only turbulence given a boundary. Without the roar, I would no longer be able to change. Mineral would enter the last seam. It would move through me coldly, replacing softness with translucence.

Yet the hollow at my center—the shape of the vanished knot—would remain.

The mountain would close around it. Around me. Around the platform, baskets, shells, stains, handprints, and the long jointed interval withdrawing into depth. Sunlight would disappear from the seams one thread at a time.

Ages later, if the summit split under frost or lightning, someone might find the pale stone embedded at the center of a braided vein. They might break it open and discover no fossil recognizable as a creature. Only a cavity shaped like a knot no fingers could tie, dusted with crystals arranged as though listening outward.

That would be me:

not quite an animal preserved in the mountain,

but the small place where the mountain, after finally becoming solid, still had not finished deciding.
