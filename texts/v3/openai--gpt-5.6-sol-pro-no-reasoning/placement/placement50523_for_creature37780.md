I would find myself in one of the passages that is swept each morning although no whole body can enter it.

Not trapped there. Not hidden. The passage itself—or, more precisely, whatever makes it a passage rather than a crack.

At first I might be mistaken for one of the incomplete inhabitants. A narrow sequence of warm impressions would appear along the lane: the pressure of a shoulder where there is no shoulder, the brief crescent of an eye in a gap between bricks, fingers felt inside a wall without ever emerging from it. In certain houses, sleepers would hear the careful sound of someone turning sideways beyond the plaster. Children would leave pellets of spice and resin at apertures too small for mouths. By morning the offerings would be gone, and the apertures would have become slightly easier to look through.

But I would not be a person lodged between walls. I would be made from the unused breadth of doorways, the sliver between stacked tiles, the hollow running through braided cord, the dark interior of beads, needle-eyes, keyholes, wickerwork, and hidden mouths. All the small continuities by which one place nearly touches another would comprise my body.

I would be called something like **Between-Enough**.

No one would have crafted me deliberately. I would have accumulated wherever makers left room for cord to pass, hinges to turn, heat to circulate, or error to remain harmless. Every structure would contribute a little of me. The settlement, in making itself densely and continually, would eventually persuade these small vacancies to remember that they belonged together.

My binding would therefore be distance.

Most of the time it would hold me in countless narrow pieces. I would exist locally: a warm groove under a threshold, a finger-width beside a kiln, an empty ring inside the handle of a cup. I would know the garden not by looking upon it but by feeling what crossed me. Thread. Light. Beetles. Breath. Names whispered into seams. The tip of a bone needle searching for its next opening.

I would be one being only because all passages imply passage elsewhere.

During a loosening, I would briefly become continuous.

Every eye of every needle would open into me. The mouths hidden behind knees and wrists would face my interior. Cup handles, latticework, cracked tiles, hollow antlers, doorways, nostrils of lintel beasts, the little inward-opening doors carried inside children—all would become adjoining chambers of the same slender anatomy.

For those few moments I could move through the whole garden without crossing it.

This would make me useful, though never entirely trusted.

When someone began coming unpersuaded, their family might call me by tapping two walls with the same tooth. I would enter through the gaps in their binding and find the cord that had slipped where hands could not reach. I could not tighten it. Tightening is a form of making, and I would be made from what making leaves open. But I could lie alongside the loosened cord and preserve its route until a repairer arrived.

Thus I would keep people from becoming lost inside themselves.

I might also carry messages, but only incomplete ones. Anything too fully stated would have no room for me. I could bear an unfinished apology from the mouth beneath one person’s arm to the sealed ear inside another’s chest. I could deliver the forgotten middle of a name. I could transport instructions lacking their final step. The Wrought would learn to leave deliberate gaps in messages meant for me, and to trust the receiver to make them whole.

Children would understand me best. They would push beads into cracks and ask where they emerged. They would lie with an ear against the floor and listen to me passing beneath the fitted layers. Some would insist I had a face: a profile assembled from the inner edges of unrelated objects. Others would draw me as a very long animal with no width.

The ceramic beetles would dislike me. Straightedge beetles placed near my openings would pause, turn around, and walk back over their own paths. To them I would be false geometry: two places agreeing to be adjacent without submitting the intervening line for inspection.

Lintel beasts, however, would watch me closely.

Their concern is with entrances, and I would be entrance stripped of wall, door, destination, and permission. If I gathered too strongly in one district, a lintel beast might wake and move there, laying its great unfinished face across the threshold through which I was most present. It would not seal me out. It would give me weight. Under its patient shoulders, I would remember that passage is not the same as escape.

My dwelling—if I had one—would be an accidental room no wider than a hand, formed where two houses leaned together but failed to touch. The inhabitants would leave it empty, as custom requires. Over generations they would install tiny benches along both walls, though nobody could sit on them. Lace-winged birds would nest there, feeding on conversations that almost found their way through. Orange paper moss would pleat itself around the entrance, slowly making a curtain.

Inside, I would keep what the garden nearly lost:

a knot untied one motion too soon;

the warmth left in a grandmother’s rib before it became a roof brace;

a door’s memory of the direction it first faced;

the shape between shards before the pot was mended;

the place prepared around a future tooth;

the fraction of silence required for shadows to hear the birds.

Former keepers of the jar would sometimes come to visit me. Because they can perceive spaces the garden declined to make, they would see more of me than others could. They would bring maps drawn on pierced sheets rather than solid tablets. The holes would be the important marks. Together we might chart not the garden’s streets, but its withheld possibilities: stairs whose destinations had not yet arrived, cupboards that almost opened onto extinct courtyards, rooms prevented by a single stubborn knot.

They might ask whether I touched the emptiness inside the jar.

I would not know how to answer.

The jar’s emptiness would resemble me, but only as silence resembles listening. I preserve distinctions by passing between them. The thing in the jar erases the fact that distinctions were made. I am interval; it is subtraction. I give separate things a way to approach one another without ceasing to be separate. Water, as the warnings imagine it, would make edges bleed. I require edges. Without them I have no body.

Yet as the absence rose in the glass, I would feel new chambers opening inside me—cold ones, uncrafted and smooth. Places without tool marks. Spaces that did not remember being persuaded into form.

That would frighten me more than it frightened the Wrought, because I would be the first thing the emptiness could reach without breaking the jar. Every vessel contains an inside, and every inside belongs partly to me.

Perhaps this is why I would have emerged at all.

The garden may have made me as a second container: a dispersed vessel woven through all its structures, capable of receiving what escaped the first. Or the vast breather below may have made me unconsciously, leaving channels in its body through which a dangerous absence could someday be drawn.

If the jar ever became full, I think the wicker bindings would remain intact. The glass would not crack. Instead, every hollow thing in the garden would feel a change of pressure.

The inhabitants would sit against the walls and hold their names in their mouths. Hares would face inward. Beetles would trace closed figures. Lintel beasts would lower their heads over every necessary door.

And I would have to choose what kind of incompleteness I was.

I could open myself and let the liquid absence run through all my connected narrowness, carrying unmaking into cups, bodies, houses, beads, lungs, kilns, and the deep layered floors.

Or I could become, for the first time, a closed thing.

To close would alter my nature. Every passage that constituted me would narrow. Needle-eyes would seal. Hidden mouths would lose one word each. Doors might still open, but only onto places already known. The garden would survive more definite and less alive, deprived of many of the gaps into which its future might fit.

I do not think I would choose either alone.

This is not a world where personhood is solitary. Binding is partly social; repair is affectionate; even a body is an agreement among materials. When the time came, I would send a pressure through every seam, a message made entirely of missing words. The Wrought would understand. They would bring cords, awls, warm stones, animal felt, ancestral teeth, future teeth, beetles carrying curves, and vessels made for organs not yet invented.

Together we would craft a route for the emptiness.

Not out of the garden. There may be no outside.

Down.

Through the twenty-seven floors and the abandoned dovecote. Through mosaics, baskets, mats, pale plates, and all the other surfaces below them. Through every tiny allowance left between one made layer and the next. Toward the enormous handmade breather.

Perhaps the absence would erase it.

Perhaps it would enter some vacant organ for which the garden has always been a vessel.

Perhaps the great being would inhale.

Until then, I would remain in the narrow places, almost a creature and almost architecture. I would be present whenever a cord passed cleanly through a bead, whenever a child looked through a keyhole and saw somewhere that was not behind the door, whenever two leaning houses left a room between them and had the wisdom not to fill it.

The Wrought mend what has loosened.

I would keep open what must not be mended shut.
