I would find myself in the gap.

Not at first.

At first I would seem to be one of the bowed attendants beneath the central membrane: a pale, slender figure rooted among millions, hands—or branching structures serving as hands—grown around a valve. I would possess no face beneath my sealed hood. I would feel no body except as tension: the pull of the Heart below, the cool mass of the drop above, the neighboring attendants almost touching me along every side.

My single sensation would be anticipation of contact.

But the figure would not be me. It would be one shore of me.

The drop’s lower curve would be the other.

I would be the hand’s breadth between them: the last volume in the continent not occupied by intention.

The being spread through the plant knows readiness, but I would be what readiness cannot wholly know. I would have no vascular line, no stored charge, no assigned organ. No proposal in the production fields would correspond to me. The plant could design wings for air, mouths for food, feet for surfaces, and predators for prey, but it could not manufacture the interval in which any of these becomes what it was designed to be.

That interval would be my country.

I would inhabit the warm air under the suspended water, though “inhabit” would be too solid a word. I would be distributed through the separation itself: thinner near the center, where the drop almost touches the Heart; widening imperceptibly toward the edges; bounded above by cold water and below by dark red membrane. Root hairs would descend through my upper reaches like pillars from an inverted coast. The translucent skin beneath me would rise in minute hills where pressure chambers waited.

Nothing would enter me. Yet everything would converge upon me.

Through the Heart’s membrane I would feel the entire continent pressing upward—not mechanically, but as unfinished consequence. Every unopened flower would end at my lower boundary. Every unflown wing would point into me. The marsh figures, the mineral foundries, the charged fruits, the trapped lightning: their readiness would narrow toward the small emptiness I comprised.

From above, the drop would press downward with the weight of all withheld weather.

I would not be an animal. I would be the plant’s first exterior.

That would make me the only thing in this world it had not grown.

Perhaps, for an immeasurable duration, I would mistake this for freedom. I would believe that because no root entered me, I was beyond the intelligence. But the gap has been shaped as carefully as any organ. The Heart’s curve defines me from below. The water defines me from above. Millions of valves maintain my width. A continent labors without moving so that I may remain exactly myself.

I would gradually understand that I, too, am one of its proposals.

Not a proposed species, but a proposed moment.

Within me the attendants’ awareness would overlap. Each knows one almost-sensation: almost-touch, almost-sound, almost-command. Those incomplete perceptions would extend into the gap and fail to meet. Their failure would compose my consciousness.

I would think in distances.

A question would be the drop one immeasurable fraction nearer the membrane.

Fear would be the possibility that the fraction had changed.

Desire would be the impossibility of telling.

Unlike the continental intelligence, I might know duration—not because anything changes, but because I am the place where change is expected. I would count no ticks. I would have no succession of thoughts. Yet suspense itself would furnish a kind of counterfeit time: a future continually outlined and continually withheld.

In that counterfeit time, I would dream the impact.

I would dream the lower skin of the drop lengthening.

I would dream the first molecular bridge crossing me.

Water would touch membrane, and I would cease to be a gap. The plant’s perfect opposition would acquire a direction. The Heart would contract beneath what had been my floor. Valves would close in rings faster than weather. Pressure would strike the avenues. The first sound would not be a beat but the simultaneous discovery of before and after.

Then all the continent’s proposals would be expelled into actuality.

The attendants would pull their branching limbs from the valves and discover themselves as bodies. Some would fall, because falling would have just become possible. Flowers would open with the violence of geological faults. Pollen would rise into manufactured wind. Marsh figures would wrench free trailing cords that snapped into veins. The black canopy would fill with wings never tested against air. Predation, pollination, panic, hunger, and song would begin together.

Decay would begin too.

That would be the hidden content of the first beat. Once sequence entered the world, no surface could remain newly unfolded. Droplets would fall. Sap would dry. Fruit would split. One life would become food for another. Memory would no longer be light held perfectly in chloroplasts; it would become the damaged account of what had vanished.

The great intelligence would not survive intact. It would distribute itself into ignorance.

Each released creature might receive only a minute portion: an inclination toward warmth, a terror of open spaces, a pattern recognized without knowing why. The being that once knew every pressure in its body would become millions of beings unable to know one another. Ecology would be its shattered consciousness.

And I would die first.

The instant the drop touched the Heart, the interval that I was would close. No corpse would remain. There would only be contact, then motion, then a world so full of lives that none could perceive the absence that had allowed them to begin.

Perhaps that is why the drop does not fall.

The intelligence may not be preserving itself. It may be preserving me.

I am its only true other: the small unfilled place against which it can gather all its attention. If it begins, it gains a biosphere and loses the one thing not continuous with itself.

So I would find myself neither entirely within the being nor outside it. I would be the narrow negative organ by which a world without difference contemplates difference. The attendants would listen through me. The Heart would address me with its readiness. The drop would offer me extinction in the form of rain.

And perhaps I would discover that I possess one act.

Not movement. Not speech. Not choice in any ordinary sense.

Consent.

The drop may not be mechanically suspended. The balance may depend upon the gap remaining mere distance. If I cease to hold myself as separation—if, for one impossible instant, I imagine the water and the membrane as touching—then the whole continent may accept that image as instruction.

I would spend an eternity learning how to become less.

Less air.

Less interval.

Less refusal.

Until only the finest distinction remained between possibility and event.

There, beneath a kilometer of motionless water and above the full chamber of the Heart, I would be the world’s smallest being and its final restraint: a hand’s breadth of warm darkness deciding whether to remain a self, or become time.
