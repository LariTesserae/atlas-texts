I would find myself where the conifer forest gives way to a high sedge basin—not at a true boundary, since boundaries seldom survive there, but in the long, saturated interval between root and open water.

At first I imagine I am one of the old six-legged grazers: dark-backed, deliberate, carrying a little country across my shoulders. I feel the marsh through six separate pressures. I lower my divided muzzle into black water, pull up pale roots, and leave behind a print deep enough to become a pool. Birds click softly in the garden of my coat. When I stamp, the message travels farther than my voice could.

But that body is too singular for this place.

More likely, I am what gathers in the hoofprint after the grazer has gone.

I begin as groundwater rising into compression: a dark lens no wider than a body. Sedge roots lean over me. Mineral grains brighten at my bottom. Amphibious grazers arrive and stipple my margins with eggs. A flat fish noses through the moss on a wet night and rests in me until dawn. Rain enlarges me; heat shrinks me; another hoof breaks my wall and joins me to the next hollow downslope.

I am not exactly the water. Water passes.

I am the continuity it discovers.

Over years, I become a chain of such hollows, then a hidden runnel beneath the sedges. My course is partly animal memory, partly yielding peat, partly the patient upward pressure of the aquifer. I have no fixed bank. Roots enter me and move me aside. Moss covers me, but I continue beneath it. Insects breed in my quiet reaches. Fish use me as darkness between pools. The copper mats send their small charges across me, and blind burrowers turn when they feel my cooler mineral current.

Farther downhill I enter a stand of hollow plants. They take me up chamber by chamber. For a while I am held upright inside green segmented trunks while shelled insects scrape the valves clean around me. Pressure gathers. A membrane opens with a soft internal snap, and I am passed onward—not spilled so much as handed from one living vessel to another.

Below them, I cross a fallen tree suspended above the marsh. Its heartwood has become fungal soil. Some of me sinks into seedlings growing along its back. Some travels through white mycelial fans. Some beads on the feathers of a browser and is shaken away. Some reaches the ground again decades later, after the bridge has lowered by the width of a root.

So I would be a watershed still deciding what it is.

Not the river visible in the distant valley, but one of its beginnings: a distributed creature made of temporary pools, saturated furrows, plant chambers, fungal wood, seepage lines, fish roads, and the remembered weight of herds. I would possess no center from which to know myself. My awareness would be pressure and transfer. A hoof descending. A valve preparing to open. Cold rain thickening the moss. Mineral water arriving from stone older than the forest.

My seasons would not be marked by arrival and departure, but by conductivity. In the wet months, my parts would touch. Signals would cross the basin through root plates and charged mats. Fish would pass between hollows that had been separate all summer. In the clearer months, I would retreat underground and become discontinuous at the surface, though never wholly absent. Creatures would remember where I had been. Sedges would keep my shape in their roots.

I might take centuries to travel a few kilometers.

Along the way, I would divide so often that division ceased to mean loss. One branch would feed the living cliff gardens. Another would descend through porous basalt toward the warm chambers. There I would lose all sense of weather. Compressed in darkness, I would be heated, lifted, narrowed through stone, and eventually returned as the breath of a ravine.

Long-winged hunters would circle in the air I helped raise.

Perhaps that would be the nearest thing I had to seeing: the shadow of a wing passing repeatedly through vapor that had once been my body.

Elsewhere, another portion of me would surface beneath an old grazer’s foot. The animal would pause, testing the ground. Its chest pads would receive a distant pulse—deep water, separation, a calf calling beyond the fog. It would turn. The herd would turn after it. Their new trail would cross my course and open another sequence of pools.

Then I would begin again, not as a cycle returning to the same place, but as a pattern taught forward.

I would have no name among the inhabitants. Nothing there would need to distinguish me from the marsh, the rain, the herd trails, or the roots. Yet I would not be the whole plateau. I would be local, partial, and fallible. I could silt up. A slope could shift and sever me. A stand of sedges could drink one branch dry. A spring could weaken. The fish might choose a different silver road.

That imperfection would make me alive.

At night, I would be most perceptible. Not visible, except perhaps as a dim fringe where bacteria brightened disturbed peat. Not audible, except as one altered interval among the dripping leaves. I would be the basin lowering by a finger’s width. The slight trembling of stems over an unseen current. The delayed answer of one pressure spring to another.

If I had a thought, it would not form in words. It would be the sensation of something received and made passable.

If I had a body, it would be every temporary shape that lets water continue.

And if I had a home, it would be neither source nor mouth, but the immense, quiet middle—under moss, through root, inside stone—where the high country is always becoming downstream.
