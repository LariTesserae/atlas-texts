I would find myself where the city is undecided.

Not in the warm root chambers, though I would remember them: black water beneath a yielding floor, the mineral taste of first movement, green threads loosening from my back. Not yet among the spires, where the eldest open their arms to cloud and begin the long surrender into architecture. I would inhabit the middle levels—the crowded, mutable district where a route is never wholly a route and every wall is considering whether to become a door.

I would be an Inquiline known, if knowing can exist without names, by a particular pressure: three slow pulses and a fourth that barely arrives. Others would recognize it through stems before they recognized my shape. It would mean neither danger nor shelter exactly, but *there may still be a way*.

My body would be greener than those newly unfolded below and less silvered than those who cling above. Moss would have taken along one shoulder and down the outer length of an arm. In wet weather it would swell, making me seem asymmetrical, burdened with a little garden. Minute bladderworts might lodge there, their clear traps catching organisms from the runoff that traveled over me. I would not own them. They would alter my balance; I would learn to lean accordingly.

I think I would tend thresholds.

Not preserve them unchanged—the city permits no such office—but keep them negotiable. I would return to places where two growths pressed too closely together and place my narrow body between them. At first for an hour, then through a sleep, then for as many nights as the stems required to remember resistance. My warmth would pale the leaves nearest my skin. My breath would dampen one wall and feed fungus there. Perhaps the passage would widen. Perhaps it would rise instead, becoming an arch too high for me to influence except by climbing. Perhaps the plants would close below my ribs and leave only a narrow opening through which water, pollen, and the youngest could pass.

That, too, would be a threshold.

I would travel without a map, but not without memory. My memory would be bodily. One gallery would live in the angle of my wrists. A root stair would be remembered by an ache along the fibers of my lower limbs. I would know a low tunnel because its ceiling had combed the moss on my back in a certain direction. The city’s geography would be a collection of postures.

When those postures no longer fit, I would know the city had changed.

Then I would go slowly, tasting the air through the seam of my face. Resin: a wall wounded by ice. Fungal sweetness: a chamber softening toward collapse. Cold mineral water: some buried vein risen unexpectedly near the surface. Bitter oil: another Inquiline’s warning. I would press a hand to the nearest connected stem and ask with three pulses, then the faint fourth.

Sometimes an answer would come from very far away, weakened by forks and soft growth.

Sometimes the city itself would answer: a cistern spilling, a bridge tightening under rain, a fruit dropping onto a taut leaf and sending its accidental message through half the district.

I would have to decide which replies were intended. I would often be wrong.

There would be one place I returned to more than others: a suspended lane near the saddle’s outer edge, roofed by overlapping violet leaves. Beneath it, roots hung into cloud without finding stone. Above it, a narrow spire gathered water and released each drop at long intervals. The lane would have been failing for many seasons. One supporting trunk had hollowed; shelf-fungi climbed its inner curve; the roots holding the far side had begun to draw apart.

I would sleep there, curled around the weakness.

Others might join me during sleet. Together, without plan, our bodies would distribute the weight that the old trunk could no longer bear. Condensation would gather on our filaments and descend through us. Pollen carried from the lower district would wash from our skin into the hollow wood. In time something would germinate there.

It might save the lane.

It might split it.

One thaw, I would wake to find the old passage gone and two smaller passages branching around the place where we had slept. One would descend into a fungal chamber luminous as old snow. The other would climb toward an exposed bridge edged in frost. Neither would be the way I had tried to keep.

I would touch both entrances.

Then I would mark the higher one bitter, because ice had loosened above it, and leave sweet paste by the lower one, where shelter remained.

That would be the extent of my authority.

In darkness I would join the singing. I would not begin it. The first tone might rise from below, passing through water before entering the root frame. I would feel it in the wall against my shoulder before I heard it in the air. My answer would be thin, almost lost beneath dripping roofs, but the living structure would carry it farther than my body could.

The tones would alter as they crossed me. Moss, water, hollow spaces, and the soft pleat beneath my head would each add something. Others would answer from galleries I had never entered. A root bridge would contract. Cistern surfaces would tremble. Far above, an elder fixed to a spire might spread stiffened fingers through a fan of needles, and cloud water would run along the arm that was no longer entirely an arm.

During the song I would not know whether we were speaking to the city or whether the city, requiring mouths for one night, was speaking through us.

Perhaps that uncertainty would be our nearest form of belief.

I would fear the bird.

Its landing would come as a single clean shock, unlike water or growth: weight arriving all at once, self-contained and warm. Every filament on me would flatten. I would press into the underside of the violet-roofed lane, fibers locking against wet bark. My dark patches would close only by turning my face to the wall.

The bird’s feet would move through a nearby courtyard.

A cistern flower would bow beneath its beak. Water would pour. The connected stems would carry each adjustment of its balance into my hands. Around me, hidden in folds and behind leaf-skins, other Inquilines would hold themselves still. The city would seem to have no inhabitants because all its inhabitants would be imitating the city.

I would not think of the bird as cruel. Cruelty requires negotiation. The bird would belong to the open sky, to distances that remain open, and therefore to a world whose laws I could not understand.

When it departed, the sudden absence of its weight would pass through the settlement like a second landing.

I would wait longer than necessary.

Then three slow pulses would reach me through the lane, followed by a fourth so faint it might be the settling of a drop.

I would answer.

As seasons accumulated, the moss on my shoulder would silver. Lichen would cross the seam between what grew on me and what I had once thought of as myself. I would climb more often toward exposed places. Not from wisdom, but because the lower passages would fit me less well, and because the cold would become easier to bear than the city’s ceaseless compression.

My work at thresholds would change. Instead of holding young stems apart, I would brace old ones together. Instead of searching for passages my body could use, I would make slight continuities for water, pollen, and smaller forms recently unfolded from the root chambers.

Eventually I would find a rupture near the violet lane. Perhaps a winter load would tear away its roof. Perhaps the hollow trunk would finally soften into black fiber. Cloud would show through the wound: not sky exactly, but brightness without surface.

I would fasten myself across it.

At first this would be only rest. My many-fingered hands would close around two separate stems. My lower limbs would brace a root bundle. Water would descend my back and drip from my head into the gutter below. I would expect to move when pressure changed.

Then frost would enter the wet moss on my shoulder and hold it rigid. Fungi already living in my outer filaments would extend inward. Roots would touch the softened places along my spine and, finding neither resistance nor permission, continue.

I would remain aware for some time—not as a whole body, but as tensions.

The pull of the left stem.

The tremor of water in the right.

A small figure crossing beneath me.

Hands pressing pollen into a seam near what had been my ribs.

Three slow pulses entering through one arm, and a fourth almost too faint to arrive.

I could no longer answer in the old way. Yet the signal would travel across me. My stiffened body would carry it from one side of the rupture to the other.

That would become my final function: not speaker, not builder, not even keeper of a threshold, but the material through which an answer could pass.

Shoots would emerge where warmth once gathered under my folds. A translucent leaf-skin would spread between my arm and flank, turning the rupture into a dim green window. Shelf-fungi would harden along the curve of one limb, making three shallow steps. The moss garden on my shoulder would join the roof. In sleet, water would follow the old channel of my spine and descend safely into a cistern flower.

The city would not preserve my shape. It would use my inclinations.

A tendency to brace here would become a bridge.

A habitual bend there would become an arch.

The place where I had made myself narrow to let others pass would remain narrow, and others would turn sideways through it for many seasons.

No one would know me as an individual. Even the pressure by which I had been recognized would dissolve into the noises of rain and growth. But perhaps, in a city without plans, identity is the bias one leaves in what continues: water sent a little farther left, weight shared across one more stem, a passage encouraged to remain possible rather than close.

So I would find myself in the middle heights, and then at the edge; first moving through the city, then slowing within it, finally becoming one of its small decisions.

I would be an Inquiline.

And afterward, I would be a way through.
