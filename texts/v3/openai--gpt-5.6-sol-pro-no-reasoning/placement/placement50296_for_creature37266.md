I would find myself neither on the open valley floor nor entirely beneath it, but inside the narrow seam where the two exchange breath: a fracture descending from one of the warm wetlands into the mountain.

I would be a **vein listener**.

At first I might be mistaken for one of the pale worms inhabiting the springs. My body would be long and soft, no thicker than a root near the head, branching farther down into finer and finer filaments. Most of me would remain threaded through cracks in the rock, held there by rings of translucent gripping tissue. Only a small crown would emerge beneath an overhang at the edge of a seep. In calm weather it would open into several colorless vanes and tremble in the mingled currents of water and air.

I would have no eyes. Sight would be of little use inside stone, and the valley seldom offers enough distance or light to justify it. Instead, my whole body would hear.

Through the upper vanes I would feel steam condense, midges land, snow begin, and the minute pressure change made by a broad wing passing low overhead. Through the buried length I would receive the mountain’s deeper signals: gas knocking through a constriction, groundwater entering a newly opened crack, ice widening a seam, a slab beginning to surrender its weight. Hooves would arrive as distinct signatures. Seam mice would be quick scratches near the surface. A ledge cat would be a slow sequence of padded compressions. A scattered herd of fumarole shepherds would pass through me as many separate pulses joined by their low calls, until the rock itself seemed to contain one immense, loosely assembled animal.

The vein listener would not merely detect these vibrations. Its body would retain them.

Minerals carried by the spring would plate its buried rings in thin layers. Iron for periods of warm, oxygen-rich flow; black manganese during deeper, slower seepage; pale carbonate when gas pressure changed. Strong tremors would cause the animal to constrict, leaving narrow dark boundaries in the deposit. Over many years its casing would become a banded record, part horn and part seismograph, though no one in the valley would read it deliberately.

I would feed on what the mountain brings. Symbiotic microbes would live in folds along my body, taking energy from the meeting of hot mineral fluid and cold meltwater. I would absorb some of what they made and filter the rest from the current: bacterial grains, dissolved compounds, fragments of organic matter washed down from moss. I would therefore belong to neither the grazing world above nor the deep microbial world below. I would live from their contact.

That position would make me useful without making me important.

When pressure rose too sharply, my exposed crown would close. The sudden movement would disturb midges sheltering nearby. They would lift from the moss; finches would startle after them; shepherds at the spring might raise their heads. Sometimes this small agitation would occur minutes before a vent coughed steam or a crust collapsed. Animals might learn that a closed crown meant unstable ground, though they would not know whether they had observed a warning or merely another creature’s fear.

The shepherds would know me best. They would seek the salts deposited around my opening and lick the stone clean. Their tongues might tear away my outer vanes, which would regrow. In return, their hooves would break the sealed mineral crust and keep the seep from choking. When an old female paused above me with one foot lifted, she and I would be attending to the same buried event from opposite sides of the surface.

But I would not remain in one fracture forever.

Eventually the seam would cool, clog, flood, or split too wide to hold me. Then the vein listener would perform the only truly exposed act of its life. It would loosen itself from the mineral sheath it had built and contract upward. What emerged would be smaller than expected: a wet, silver-gray knot with many fine trailing filaments, almost weightless once emptied of water.

It could not walk far. Instead, it would wait for the seep to overflow, or for a shepherd’s hoof to lift it, or for runoff to carry it across the gravel. Most displaced listeners would freeze on the surface or be eaten by birds. A few would enter another warm crack. There they would extend downward, divide, and begin listening again.

After departure, the old mineral casing would remain in the stone: a hollow, banded tube descending farther than any beak or claw could follow. Water would use it. Microbes would colonize it. Frost might split the surrounding slab along its length. Thus even the listener’s abandoned memory would become part of the machinery that remakes the valley.

I imagine myself there during the gray interval before evening, although evening has no clear boundary. Above me, the saddle would seem nearly empty. Steam would flatten under the cloud. Shepherds would be dark, widely separated marks. Somewhere unseen, a fox would place its feet in their tracks. Meltwater would begin stiffening into clear ribs.

Inside the fracture, however, emptiness would be impossible. The mountain would be crowded with transmissions.

I would feel a spring failing uphill and another pressure front gathering below. I would feel a herd turning before its movement became visible. I would feel snow settle, water seek a lower route, and microscopic colonies thicken the walls around me. No message would be addressed to me. None would require interpretation. The valley would simply pass through my body as force, chemistry, temperature, and rhythm.

I would not know the place as a landscape.

I would know it as something still becoming—  
and I would be one of the small, living instruments by which it feels itself change.
