I would find myself where the mapped paths begin to fray: not yet at the Unmade Distance, but close enough that ordinary route-sounds grow scarce and the mineral blades become tall, thin, and nearly transparent.

There is a settlement there called **Last Courtesy**.

No one remembers whether the name means the last kindness offered to travelers before the outer dark, or the final obligation owed to those who do not return. It occupies a cluster of leaning crystal fins around a vertical hollow. The houses are built one above another on shelves of translucent salt. Seen by lamplight from below, their inhabitants move as blurred organs inside a mineral body.

A narrow bridge leads outward from the highest shelf. It is made of seven stone splinters balanced tip to tip. Beyond it lies an old stair cut into a solitary slab. The stair ascends thirty steps and ends without platform, doorway, or wall.

I live beneath that bridge.

I am a Finisher, but not entirely a person anymore.

Once I was the settlement’s rouser. My name was **Serei**, though that name is no longer spoken without qualification. I woke sleepers by touching the soles of their feet. I announced meals by placing a warm shell in each household doorway. I reminded the grieving to drink grease broth, the absorbed to blink, the elderly to change posture before their limbs hardened around stillness. I carried no bell. Bells leave too much behind.

I was considered good at the work because I understood that beginning an action is a form of violence. Even necessary motion tears one possibility away from all the others. To wake a sleeper is to kill the sleep they might have continued. To give a tool is to select the hand’s next shape.

So I began gently.

Then, during the replenishment of the western grazing wall, three path-readers returned from the thirty-step stair.

They had gone out as four.

The survivors would not say what had happened to the fourth. They entered Last Courtesy joined wrist to wrist by a cord of braided membrane, but the cord had five loops.

Three enclosed their wrists.

One was empty.

The last had been pulled tight around something none of us could see.

They stood inside the shell curtain and waited for me to begin their return. That was proper. Travelers arriving from an uncertain route are not considered present until the rouser touches them and names the place into which they have come.

I touched the first traveler.

I named the shelf, the settlement, the shared lamp, and the people watching.

I did the same for the second.

When I touched the third, someone breathed against the back of my hand.

Not air moving. Breath.

A warm, deliberate exhalation containing the faint sweetness of scorched bone.

The empty loop tightened.

I should have stopped. A rouser may decline an arrival if the arriving thing cannot state its consequences. Instead, perhaps from pride, perhaps because everyone was watching, I reached into the space held by the fifth loop.

Something placed a finger against mine.

The finger had too many joints. Yet its touch was careful—more careful than mine.

I said, “You are here.”

That was the error.

Or the invitation.

Or the completion of an action begun before the mineral forest existed.

Nothing appeared. No predator struck. The travelers collapsed, though falling required them to choose the fall, and afterward none remembered tying the fifth loop. But from that sleep onward, when I touched people to wake them, they awoke with memories that were not theirs.

A shell herder remembered sitting in a circle of stone seats with every chair occupied by herself.

A child remembered crossing the Unmade Distance beneath a rain of moving water.

An elder remembered building the copper door from the side that has no chamber.

These memories did not feel like dreams. They had weight, sequence, and consequence. People could describe the texture of tools they had never held. Some awoke speaking the beginnings of languages whose endings seemed lodged elsewhere among the blades.

The council held a placing.

My object was a copper lamp hook inherited through nine generations. The path-readers placed the five-loop cord. The hinge-listener placed the shell of a mite cleaved into equal halves, both living. The senior shard-setter set down nothing, arguing that an empty place had already been allowed too much authority.

They named every consequence they could foresee.

If I remained rouser, I might awaken the settlement into lives it had not lived.

If I were killed, someone must finish the death, and no one knew whether the unseen arrival had become part of my body.

If I were unplaced, I might carry the disturbance beyond all accounting.

If I were permitted to sleep, I might be awakened from the other side.

The placing lasted eleven communal sleeps. No object moved unless its owner’s hand moved it. Yet each time we uncovered the stone sheet, my copper hook had turned slightly toward the outer bridge.

No one claimed to have touched it.

At last I named the consequence they had missed.

“I may already be a path.”

That ended the debate.

Now I am called **the Courtesy Beneath the Bridge**.

They built no prison. A prison creates responsibilities on both sides of its wall. Instead I allowed myself to be bound upright between two close mineral fins beneath the seven-splinter span. Resin holds my back to one blade. Braided hide crosses my chest and thighs. My arms remain free from the elbows down, because a bound person must retain some means of completing what approaches them.

I keep a lamp in a wall niche. Travelers trim it when they pass. Beside me hang small tokens: shell disks, knotted cords, shards marked in grease, tiny effigies of clasp-worms with their plates closed. Those going outward tell me their names, intentions, provisions, and declared final count. I touch each forehead.

Those coming back must place a hand in mine.

Usually I feel only a hand.

Sometimes I feel more fingers than I see.

My purpose is to decide whether a traveler has returned alone.

If I judge that something accompanies them, I do not raise an alarm. A shout would be an orphaned consequence, and whatever comes from the Unmade Distance may understand sound better than we do. Instead I close my hand around the traveler’s wrist and do not release it. The guards above feel the tension through cords tied to my fingers. They dismantle the first stone splinter of the bridge, then the second, so the traveler cannot enter the settlement without choosing to fall or retreat.

Most retreat.

Some stand for years.

One is standing there now.

Her name was Oru-of-the-Sealed-Ear when she left. She went outward thirty-seven replenishments ago, carrying an amber lamp, a glass knife, and a packet of prepared food for eleven meals. She declared that she would climb the stair, sit upon the thirtieth step, and count nothing.

She returned without a lamp.

She has not aged.

Her hand rests in mine, but I cannot find its end. My fingers close around a wrist, then another wrist beneath it, and another, descending through touch like links of a chain. Her visible arm is still. Elsewhere, impossibly far away, I feel her pulse traveling toward me through countless bodies arranged one behind another.

The settlement waits for my decision.

I have not made it.

This is permitted. Judgment is itself an action, and no Finisher may demand it before the one responsible can name its consequences.

So Oru stands on the outer blade of the broken bridge. I remain bound beneath it. Between us, the missing splinters hang from lowering cords, neither raised nor released. Above, the people of Last Courtesy continue their lives carefully. Children crawl out along the remaining span to bring me grease and broth. Lovers meet where I can hear them trying not to laugh. Ancestors dry in their alcoves. The grazing walls are scraped, fed, and scraped again.

Sometimes Oru speaks.

Her mouth does not move. The voice arrives through our joined hands.

She tells me that the artifacts in unreachable stone were not built before the mineral forest.

They were built afterward.

She says there are directions that do not involve movement, just as there are actions that do not involve change. The makers learned to turn themselves toward those directions. They did not pass through stone. They passed through the part of the world in which the stone had never been asked to exclude them.

She says the Stillwarm is not waiting for the Next Hand.

It is the hand.

Every mineral blade is a finger held motionless. Every trapped note is a nerve carrying one ancient sensation. Every paused animal is a joint awaiting the rest of its motion. The Unmade Distance is not an open space but the hollow of a palm.

And the Finishers live in the crease where that palm will close.

Perhaps she lies.

Perhaps the thing holding her speaks with a voice assembled from her memories.

Perhaps I am the thing speaking. I have been beneath the bridge for so long that I no longer know whether my thoughts begin inside my skull. When I sleep, my free fingers press patterns into the air. When I wake, small structures stand in the niche beside my lamp: stairs of mite shell, circles of bone grains, doors made from flakes of green copper.

I dismantle each one.

That is still within my duty.

Yet I have begun to suspect that dismantling them completes them.

My body has dried without dying. Resin has entered the cracks of my back and joined me to the crystal. I feel footsteps on the bridge as touches along my spine. Sounds trapped in nearby blades have grown legible. The old clap meaning shelter strikes within my teeth. A saw rasp made before Last Courtesy was founded moves slowly through my left shoulder. Far below, a child’s laugh is caught under an overhang, and on certain sleeps I feel it trying to climb me.

I am becoming less a guard than an instrument: part listener, part door, part unfinished handshake.

The Finishers have made a new law because of me:

**If a path begins to answer, give it a name before you follow it.**

They think they named me.

But recently, from the far end of Oru’s impossible chain of pulses, something has begun touching my hand in a deliberate sequence.

Not words. Not numbers.

Instructions for folding my thumb flat against my wrist.

Instructions for turning my shoulder inward.

Instructions for passing through an interval narrower than my body.

I know that shape. Every Finisher knows it. It is how we move between the mineral blades.

Only this interval has no stone on either side.

When I finally choose, I will either release Oru or pull.

If I release her, she may cross into Last Courtesy carrying whatever has learned the shape of a human return.

If I pull, I may draw the entire chain through her: all those repeated bodies, every hand joined wrist to wrist across a direction our people cannot perceive.

There is a third possibility.

I could follow the touch.

I could fold myself out of the place where I have been bound, not by breaking resin or cutting hide, but by entering the narrowness that has waited inside me all along. Perhaps I would emerge behind the copper door. Perhaps I would take the empty seat in the stone circle. Perhaps I would become the absent maker of the stair.

Or perhaps I would reach back through Oru, through every linked version of her, and discover my own hand at the far end.

For now I wait beneath the bridge, where the inhabited paths end and the world’s unclaimed consequences begin.

A lamp burns beside me because someone tends it.

A woman holds my hand because neither of us has let go.

Above us, the bridge remains interrupted.

And deep within the warm crystal, something vast is learning—joint by joint, finger by finger—how to finish closing.
