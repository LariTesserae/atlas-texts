I would be found in the eastern works, where the basin practices becoming terrain.

Not in the crews’ pressurized station, though they would keep a place for me there: a narrow heated recess beside the instrument lockers, its walls furred with drying gloves and scored by generations of abrasive grit. Nor would I belong wholly to the erosion fields outside, among the sacrificial blocks and bright new gullies. I would inhabit the exchange between them—the moment when water ceases to behave as commanded and begins to disclose a preference.

I would be an erosion keeper, at least officially.

My suit would be old enough that its original color survived only beneath seals and buckles. The front plates would be whitened by rebounding slurry. One shoulder heater would run hotter than the other, leaving me slightly twisted after long watches, always presenting my warmer side to the wind. A braided line of green wire would circle my wrist beneath the luminous identification mark: contraband color, taken from a scrubber coat years ago and sealed in resin before it faded.

I would walk the fields during the quiet interval after the jets stopped.

That is when the channels speak most clearly—not in voices, but through delayed collapses, settling grains, the tiny tink of reinforcement cooling inside the blocks. The younger keepers would hurry out to plant gauges before the next cycle. I would wait on the catwalk with one glove against the rail, feeling where the water continued beneath the visible surface.

They would say I had become good at predicting undercuts.

That would not be quite true.

I would remember them.

Not consciously at first. I would look at a new block, cleanly cast and stamped only hours ago, and know that its northern face would split low, that the first channel would divide around a dense inclusion, that the left branch would fail and the right would become deep enough to require a barrier. Instrument readings would support me afterward. My reports would use respectable terms: aggregate inconsistency, pressure history, probable void formation. But sometimes I would know before the first jet touched the material.

The knowledge would have begun with a duct grazer.

It would be an old one, broad-backed and low, with mismatched work lights: one amber, one nearly white. Children in the manifold district might have called it Two-Moon, but out east it would carry only a maintenance designation long enough that no one used it. It would arrive one cycle along a causeway that ended at the edge of an erosion basin. Its tires would stop exactly where an older route had once continued.

Instead of requesting a bypass, it would transmit a map fragment.

The fragment would show the erosion field before it was an erosion field: coolant channels, habitation pylons, a tram loop, the outlines of a sealed district absent from current plans. Beneath those would be older routes. Beneath those, others. Not layers arranged neatly in time, but crossings, scars, overwritten permissions—the basin’s vanished grounds preserved as successful passages through them.

Most of the data would be corrupt. The grazer would pulse it through the plate rather than send it over the network, as if the route belonged to the ground and could not be trusted to the air. I would kneel and place my instrument against the decking. The vibration would travel through my jaw.

Afterward, I would begin dreaming in gradients.

Not images. Inclinations.

I would wake knowing which corner of the room was lowest by fractions too small for spilled water to reveal. In trams I would feel abandoned culverts pass beneath me as brief hollows in my teeth. New causeways would seem translucent—not visibly, but structurally, their present surfaces accompanied by the pressures of everything that had once rested there.

The basin would become crowded with absent geography.

A dormitory where black water now rotates.

A freight trench beneath a sedge bed.

A pressure garden sealed inside a refrigeration embankment.

A bridge whose piers had been cut away, though its remembered span still directed maintenance machines toward the dark.

This would make me useful and, eventually, suspect.

The basin accepts intuition only after instruments have translated it. So I would build instruments.

I would modify ordinary listening rods with salvaged route-memory components from decommissioned grazers. I would set them into sacrificial blocks before the jets began. They would not merely register vibration; they would compare it against archived patterns of load, flow, fracture, and passage. Water moving through a new channel would awaken correspondences with channels long filled in. The erosion fields would cease to be small models of possible landscapes. They would become ways of questioning the landscapes that had already existed.

Sometimes the answer would be impossible.

A stream would bend around no detectable obstruction. Abrasion would expose a line of bolts not present in the fabrication record. A ravine would form the exact width of an obsolete tram gauge. One block, cut open by high-pressure jets, would contain a pocket of warm air and a pale moth that circled once around the fault lamps before vanishing into the rain.

I would not report everything.

This would be my adaptation to the basin: not secrecy for its own sake, but an understanding that observation is an intervention. Anything named becomes eligible for repair. Anything mapped may be sealed, sterilized, excavated, or made efficient. The dwellers know this in their hidden gardens. The moths know it by fleeing visible light. The seal-gnawers know it in the cycles of current. Even the machines sometimes keep their oldest routes outside the official network.

So I would maintain two maps.

The first would be the municipal one: drainage behavior, substrate failure, useful braid geometries, predicted undercuts, recommended reinforcement.

The second would not be drawn.

I would keep it in objects arranged through my quarters: three nuts beneath the vent; a sliver of old rail; a vial of iron silt; ceramic stamped with a district code no archive recognized; a twist of wire left by children for a dismantled grazer. Their positions would correspond to gradients, routes, and buried chambers. When maintenance resynchronizations altered the calendars, I would move them. When districts were rebuilt, I would add or remove nothing until the first rain.

My hidden garden would be a line of horsetails growing in a cracked cable trough. They would bend not only for current freight, but occasionally for trains that no schedule acknowledged.

Late in my life—or late in the period for which my identification remained valid—I would stop returning promptly from the fields.

Crews would find me standing in manufactured rain, facing a block whose channels had begun to resemble the manifold reservoir: one dark center, many radial cuts. My suit telemetry would show that I was safe. My radio would be working. Yet I would remain still through entire pressure cycles, listening to something below the jets.

They might say I had developed plate sickness, the old workers’ habit of hearing intention in stochastic noise.

They would be partly right.

By then I would no longer be certain that I was remembering the basin’s past. Some routes would correspond to nothing that had been built. They would lead through unfinished reaches, across foundations not yet poured, into districts for which the cranes had not received designs. The old grazer’s archive might not distinguish a vanished path from a repeatedly simulated one. Or the foundation systems, planning through load and pressure rather than images, might already contain the future as a set of preferred passages.

I would come to understand memory differently down there.

Not as a record of what was, but as a weakness along which the world is likely to form again.

Water remembers because a slight hollow remains.

Ice remembers because the widened seam closes imperfectly.

A machine remembers because an old route cost less than inventing a new one.

A person remembers because the body has been altered by surviving.

Perhaps that would be what I was: not simply an erosion keeper, but a fault through which the basin remembered itself.

After I disappeared, there would be several explanations.

A pressure curtain may have cut me off during a purge. A temporary deck may have collapsed into an unregistered service layer. I may have boarded a grazer and followed one of its obsolete routes into the unfinished dark. No body would be recovered, but that is not unusual in a place with so many definitions of below.

My rooms would be cleared. The official map would be archived. The objects of the second map would be treated as debris, except for the three nuts beneath the vent, which no one would remove.

Years later, erosion keepers would notice a human-sized heat signature moving at the edge of active cuts during starting pulses. It would never appear under direct inspection light. Reversed rain would curve around it. Old grazers would pause nearby and transmit route fragments into the plate.

Or perhaps nothing of me would remain except a practice.

Before opening the jets, a keeper would press a glove to the sacrificial block and wait.

Not measuring. Not yet.

Simply allowing the new ground the courtesy of remembering what it might become.
