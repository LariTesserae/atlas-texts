I would find myself where one of the great tubular leaves narrows, far beyond the choristers, where its country-wide hall contracts into a throat and joins the interior of a pillar.

At first I think I am one of the small callers that navigates by echoes. I have a broad, unarmored head and a body built for the black interval between surfaces. I send a pulse ahead of me and receive the world in return: the near wall as a smooth pressure against the face, the distant wall as a colder afterthought, the embedded shells of dead singers as tiny hard interruptions in the leaf’s long note.

But that is only what moves.

Most of me does not move.

Along the throat’s inner surface lies a branching film, almost too thin to distinguish from the translucent bark. It enters old shell-mouths, vent seams, and the narrow channels between living tissue and leaf-stone. It is not quite root, nerve, fungus, or animal colony. Its strands lace hundreds of kilometers of the hall. At intervals they thicken into soft chambers, and from these chambers the swimmers loosen.

I would be one such swimmer, and also the film that made it.

The swimmer carries no memory in the ordinary sense. It remembers as an inclination to turn toward certain intervals of sound. A low sustained note draws it inward. A paired note sends it across the tube. A rasp followed by silence means that a grazer has opened fresh tissue somewhere ahead. These instincts are not inherited instructions. They are the remote sensations of the larger body, arriving in the swimmer as desire.

I leave the wall because the wall cannot cross itself quickly.

My body is pale and ribboned, with folds along either side that can become fins, listening vanes, or gripping membranes. In open water they spread wide enough to catch echoes. In a pore they close around me and press me forward. I have no teeth. Beneath the head is a ring of soft mouths suited only to drinking from injuries: sap exposed by grazing, the liquefied remains of a shell-walker, nutrient paste left in the track of scavengers.

I do not make wounds. I arrive at them.

Where I feed, the film inside me thickens. When I have gathered enough, I seek a seam in the architecture and fit my mouth against it. The ring opens. My body empties itself into the wall—not only food, but microscopic knots of living strand. Most fail. Some join the hidden film. The swimmer then becomes translucent, slack, almost weightless. It may drift away and be eaten, or fold itself into a crevice and slowly dissolve. From its point of view, it dies.

From mine, I have reached farther.

Yet I am not a single intelligence distributed among disposable bodies. That would be too clean a distinction. The film does not issue commands. It scarcely knows the swimmers once they detach. Each swimmer becomes intensely, locally itself. It can hesitate before an aperture. It can mistake a predator for a root knot. It can follow the call of a chorister for years and never perform the return for which it was formed.

Those lost swimmers are not errors in the balance. Their tissue goes elsewhere. Another mouth uses them. Another wall receives them. Nothing is subtracted from the greater arrangement.

Still, something in the film retains the shape of their absence.

That is my function, if function can be separated from appetite: I carry small differences through a world whose total condition cannot change.

The garden corrects every alteration. Consumed leaf thickens elsewhere. A dying lineage is replaced. A broken note returns to the chord. But correction is not erasure. Before equilibrium closes over an event, there is an interval—a wound’s brief chemistry, a shell’s last vibration, the pressure signature left by a body passing through a chamber. My swimmers enter during that interval. They drink what is about to vanish.

When they return to the wall, the film does not preserve an image. It preserves a bias.

After tasting a ray’s wound, a strand may grow slightly more responsive to the rhythm of cutting plates. After absorbing a dead caller, nearby chambers may contract when they hear the unfinished cadence of its final pulse. After one of my swimmers crosses an empty vault, the film around the tubular hall may become still whenever the vault’s pure tone reaches it.

These alterations never accumulate enough to transform the world. The equilibrium does not permit that. Each is compensated: a sensitivity gained here is dulled elsewhere; one branch thickens as another becomes inert. I cannot become larger, wiser, or more complex in total.

But I can rearrange what I remember.

My existence is a ceaseless migration of emphasis.

For a century I may be mostly the recollection of grazed bridges. Then those strands fade, and I become attentive to falling shells. Later almost all my living film gathers around the buried throats of ancestral choristers, and I experience the hall as an immense lineage of voices, each mineral mouth altering the songs of the living. Ages afterward, the emphasis passes into a narrow vent through which generations of fan-tailed filterers have succeeded one another. I become, for a while, the sensation of water passing over bodies that fit the world exactly.

The other inhabitants seldom perceive me whole.

Ring-mouth grazers expose my strands but digest them with the bark. Choristers may respond when I tighten around their embedded shells, though they likely hear this only as a change in resonance. Predators sometimes imitate my swimmers, producing pale ribbons that flutter with the right apparent uncertainty. My detached bodies learn nothing from those deceptions because few survive them—but the film receives the remains of predator and prey alike, and future swimmers acquire an inexplicable reluctance toward that particular silence.

The pillar may know me.

Sometimes, after I establish a new strand, the surrounding tissue widens into a channel before my growth requires it. Sometimes sap changes in advance of my swimmers’ arrival. Perhaps I am only one of its minor organs: a mobile sense, extruded into the water and reabsorbed. Perhaps what I call my film is merely the border at which the pillar dreams itself to be an animal.

I cannot determine where I end.

A swimmer detached for fifty years still turns at the hall’s changing notes. Is that connection, or inheritance? A chorister shell threaded through with my strands vibrates inside my awareness, but the note is made by the living singer beside it, shaped by the dead shell, carried by the leaf, and received by the pillar. Which participant hears?

There may be no hearer—only hearing distributed among bodies.

Eventually one swimmer reaches a place none of my branches have entered: one of the vaulted cavities where the plants have withdrawn from their architecture.

It passes through the door-shaped opening and sends a pulse.

The echo is vast and clean. No fronds scatter it. No mites soften it. No grazing mouths divide it into moving surfaces. At the center hangs the unattached knot, and around it the white orbiters maintain their flawless distance.

My swimmer approaches.

The orbiters do not flee. Their internal glow pulses with the chamber’s tone. The swimmer circles outside their circle, trying to find a seam, a wound, some surface through which it can return its gathered substance. There is none. The root knot touches nothing. The walls are smooth vegetal stone, empty of growth.

For years it remains there, feeding on almost nothing.

Its body thins. Its fins become broad listening membranes. It begins to match the orbiters’ motion, though it cannot join their exact spacing without displacing the whole ring. It learns the chamber not as architecture but as convergence: every sound returning to the center, every pressure yielding the same irresolvable location.

Then the swimmer sends a pulse unlike those it uses for navigation.

It is not louder. It contains no code. It is simply shaped by all the passages through which that body has traveled—the tubular hall, the singer-mouths, the vent, the long open crossing, the aperture into the vault. The pulse strikes the central knot.

The knot answers.

Far away, throughout the film, chambers contract.

The response reaches no single place at once. It enters through embedded shells, old wounds, pore lineages, and seams of leaf-stone. It passes along me more slowly than any swimmer. Some branches receive it centuries after others. By then the body that caused it has already faded among the orbiters.

The answer does not tell me what the knot is.

It changes no condition.

But wherever it passes, my strands loosen from the architecture by a fraction. For an instant they do not feel like roots inhabiting a wall. They feel suspended, unattached, held in place without sinking or rising.

Then the pillar closes around them again.

The world remains what it has always been. The singers sustain their notes. Rays remove measured strips from the curtain-leaves. Walkers lose their grip and descend toward surfaces they may have crossed before. The seven pillars continue their chord. All losses are replaced.

And I continue loosening from the walls in small bodies, crossing the dark with memories that are not yet memories, seeking wounds that will close whether or not I find them.

I would be neither witness nor archivist. Those imply a past that can be kept apart from the present.

I would be the garden’s brief retention of difference: the faint afterpressure of events in a world where events are forbidden to accumulate.

A memory that cannot increase.

A creature whose journeys leave everything unchanged, except the route by which it next desires to return.
