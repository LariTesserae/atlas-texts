I would find myself below the apparent city, though not at its deepest point.

I would be one of the places where water changes its mind.

Not the rain while it is still falling, with the whole basin open beneath it. Not the marsh, which has accepted breadth and sky. Not the blind fish, enclosed within a complete and ancient darkness. I would inhabit the intervening seam: a narrow bedding plane where water arriving from many districts gathers, pauses under pressure, and is divided again.

At first I seem to be a spring. Beneath a pale ledge in one of the central valleys, the wall is always dark. Drops swell from no visible opening, descend through moss, and enter a channel worn no deeper than a finger. In dry weather there may be only one drop at a time. After rain, the entire seam shines and the wall releases a clear curtain. Beetles come to its margins. Grains of shale collect below. A small court of flowers, farther down the channel, depends upon its continuance.

But the spring is only my visible edge.

Behind it, I extend sideways through stone. I am thin enough in places that no fish can enter me, no moth can shelter, no root can follow far. My body is less a chamber than a contact: limestone above, shale below, water between. I have no center that could be pointed to. Pressure reaches me before the storm water itself does. A darkening on the northern escarpment becomes, hours later, a cool insistence in my narrowest branches.

I know the city by what passes through me.

From the upper terraces comes water sharpened by open air, carrying dust, pollen, fragments of lichen, and the faint organic trace of grasses. From old conduits comes slower water with a mineral heaviness, nearly unchanged by season. Thaw enters suddenly, tasting of long contact with fractured rock. Once in a great while, clay clouds through me where some distant floor has shifted. I cannot see the collapse. I receive its color.

The city above appears fixed because its motion is distributed across centuries. To me, that motion is immediate. Every new grain in the current is a wall becoming waterborne. Every widening fissure changes the pressure along my length. I feel towers not as silhouettes but as weight. I know avenues by the rain they admit and courtyards by the delay with which they drain. Entire districts are present to me as differences in temperature and mineral taste.

I am not the water alone. Water leaves.

Nor am I the crack alone. The crack widens, clogs, forks, and may one day close.

I am the temporary correspondence between them: the route made possible by this stone, and the stone gradually remade by this route.

Perhaps this makes me less a creature than a habit the basin has acquired.

Still, I possess something like hunger. I draw water along the softer bed. I receive it wherever pressure permits. In drought I contract into separate beads held in pores, my continuity broken but not entirely lost. During storms I become whole. Remote branches join. Buried hollows fill. The dark wall under the ledge begins to weep from end to end, and for a few hours I reach from the roofs of the central plateau to a spring several districts east.

That flood is the nearest I come to waking.

Yet waking does not make me singular. Other waters press beside me, above and below, crossing my path through joints I cannot enter. Some descend toward the blind fish. Some rise into moss. Some vanish into deep faults and may not return within any living season. We meet without recognition and separate without loss. What travels through one of us may later travel through another. Our identities are routes, not substances.

At my exposed mouth, however, I become briefly legible.

The moss records my ordinary extent. A darker outer fringe records the wet years. Pale mineral deposits show where I once emerged before a flake of wall detached and moved the opening sideways. Below, the shallow channel preserves the direction of my overflow. A person—if such a being crossed this unmade city—might call these signs evidence of a spring. They might cup a hand beneath the ledge and drink.

For that moment I would be small enough to hold.

They would taste cold, limestone, perhaps a trace of iron. They would not taste the northern cloud, the buried chamber, the frost-opened joint, the pollen from a flower court kilometers away. They would not feel the immense weight beneath which the water had traveled. Yet all of it would be there, not symbolically, but in dilution.

Then I would leave their hand.

I would pass into the gutter, over a ledge, under silver grass. Part of me would lift into the air. Part would sink again. Part would reach the marsh and enter a sedge root. The route behind the wall would remain, filling with other water, preserving the appearance that I had continued.

Perhaps, then, I would find myself not in any substance, place, or animal, but in continuance itself: the illusion produced when innumerable departures follow one another closely enough to seem like a single presence.

I would be a spring whose source cannot be found because its source is dispersed across weather.

I would be a hidden passage that exists only while being used.

I would be the city’s smallest decision about where rain goes next.
