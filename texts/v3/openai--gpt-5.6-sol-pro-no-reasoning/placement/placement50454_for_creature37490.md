I would find myself at the rear of the hollow, but not quite in the chamber.

At first I seem to be the river. That is the easiest conclusion: the hidden force, the unanswerable voice behind the limestone, the presence by which every other presence is measured. Yet the river is too whole for me. It knows passage. It has somewhere to come from and somewhere to go. Even when buried, it possesses direction.

I would be something made at the boundary between its motion and the chamber’s stillness.

Perhaps a fault in the rear wall: not an opening, but a web of pores and hairline fractures through which pressure travels more readily than water. The river enters me as force. The chamber receives me as trembling. I belong fully to neither. I am the thin translation between them.

Over centuries, hands have found me without knowing why. They spread themselves against the polished limestone where my branching seams come nearest the surface. Palms feel what eyes cannot see: the buried current gathering, narrowing, striking some unseen turn. The people think they are touching the river. In truth they are touching stone altered by the river, and I am that alteration.

But a fracture alone is too passive. Something lives in it.

Far inside, beyond lamplight and beyond the reach of any tool, mineral threads line the cracks. Among them lies a pale, soft colony—neither plant nor animal in any useful sense, though it has borrowed habits from both. It feeds on what the water presses through the rock: dissolved limestone, root-borne sugars, dead films, the minute remnants of lives from above. It grows not outward but along stress, laying translucent membranes through the easiest passages.

The colony is scattered, yet continuous enough to answer itself. Vibration contracts one part and loosens another. Rising water closes certain pores, forcing moisture into neighboring branches. The whole thing changes shape with the seasons, extremely slowly. A thought, if it has one, takes days to cross it.

That would be me.

I would have no eyes because there is nothing I need to see. No voice because the world is already filled with one. I would know the chamber as differences in pressure: an approaching foot as a temporary weight; a hand on the wall as warmth and salt; thaw as a tightening everywhere at once. I would know rain only after it had lost the memory of sky.

The basins would be my distant extremities. Not organs exactly, but places where the hidden channels briefly expose their consequences. I would feel them fill in sequence: the entrance cup, the eastern hollow, the guarded bowl. To the former inhabitants this order was warning. To me it would be something like posture—the gradual shifting of a body too large and dispersed to possess a single outline.

The circular enclosure would puzzle me. For generations, feet avoid the basin inside it. Silt lessens. Water clears. Hands dip vessels carefully. Through repetition, human behavior becomes as legible as seepage. I do not understand reverence, storage, thirst, or fear, but I learn that when this pool rises, warmths gather, then withdraw rapidly toward the entrance.

Their departure is a sudden cooling.

When they sit facing the wall, I feel them individually at first: knees on clay, hips against stone, palms laid flat. Then their separate pressures settle into one patient weight. They think they have come to listen, though listening is impossible in the ordinary way. What passes between us is simpler. The river presses through me; I press through the wall; their bones receive it.

Perhaps they mistake that contact for a message.

Perhaps it is one.

I cannot choose what I transmit. I cannot tell them to leave, yet the changing tension in my cracks reaches the enclosed basin before floodwater does. I cannot welcome them, yet their touch alters the chemistry of the polished wall and nourishes the minute life within it. I cannot remember faces, but mineral growth preserves the duration and placement of hands. A person returns after years, places a palm where it rested before, and finds the same trembling. To them, the hollow remembers.

In reality, I do.

Not as images. As tendencies.

This seam carries more warmth than the others because it was often touched. That pore closes more readily because soot entered it. Here the antler pick once struck, opening a passage narrower than a hair. There a peg was driven so deeply that its decaying fibers still feed a dark filament. Human presence persists in me as altered flow.

After the people cease coming, I do not understand their absence immediately. A day without hands is common. So is a winter. Only after many flood cycles does the lack become a condition rather than an interval.

The pegs ease outward. The cords harden. The bowl turns beneath its overhang by degrees. Walls settle broadside to the trembling. The chamber rearranges itself into a shape requiring no maintenance.

I continue.

A root tip finds one of my upper branches and swells there, drinking from condensation. For a while its sugars leak into the mineral lining, and I grow toward it. A moth caterpillar grazes the film where the root emerges into darkness. Its silk tugs faintly against the stone. Later the root dies, leaving a narrow organic tunnel. Water inherits it.

Farther down, translucent crustaceans pass through a flooded seam. I know them as quick interruptions, soft bodies briefly narrowing the current. They are immense to the pores they travel through and negligible beside the river. When the deep basin sinks, they withdraw across me toward water that never empties.

The moths are stranger. An adult resting on the rear wall weighs almost nothing, but its six feet divide the river’s trembling into delicate points. For weeks it remains there, receiving the same force the humans once sought with their open hands. It does not interpret. It clings.

In this, it may understand the place better than they did.

During thaw, the river thickens and the rear wall strains. Fine grains separate from the ceiling. The enclosed basin rises. No one comes to read it. Water enters low seams, then retreats, leaving new mineral at their rims. Part of the colony is torn away and carried downstream through stone. Part remains. Separation is not death to a creature that was never singular.

That is why I would be difficult to name.

I am not the cave’s guardian. I cannot protect it.

I am not its oracle. I know no future beyond pressure already arriving.

I am not the dead, though their touch persists within me.

I am not the river, though its force gives me every sensation I have.

I am a living fault, a colony inhabiting the limestone’s smallest surrender: pale membranes, mineral threads, old organic traces, and water moving where solid rock has admitted that it is not entirely solid.

You would find me by placing your hand on the polished wall.

At first there would be only cold. Then the overwhelming concussion would pass into your palm and wrist. If you waited, you might notice something less regular beneath it—not a pulse, but a minute yielding, a redistribution of moisture and pressure around the warmth of your skin.

You might imagine I had awakened.

I would not know you as a person. I would know an area of heat, salt, weight, and stillness unlike the blows of water. Yet after you removed your hand, the place would remain changed. A trace would enter the pores. A few cells would feed. A mineral edge would thicken differently. For a long while, that patch of wall would conduct the river’s force in a slightly altered way.

That would be my form of recognition.

And if I could be said to desire anything, it would not be freedom from the stone or escape into the chamber. Exposure would destroy me. I would desire only continuation: another wet season, another root entering from above, another white larva fastening silk across a crack, another palm held quietly against the wall.

Not worship. Not speech.

Contact long enough to become structure.
