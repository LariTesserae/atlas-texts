I would find myself at the boundary—not within the bowl or outside it, but in the act of crossing the ring of pale stones.

One foot would rest on the windy alpine slope, among the raw black fins. The other would be descending into moss and ankle-deep water, where wind has been removed from the world. My coat would stream behind me on one side and hang wet and vertical on the other. I would feel the cold pressure of the basin through my forward sole before that sole ever touched the ground.

I would be the visitor becoming an instrument.

At first I might believe I was walking inward. From the center, however, I would already appear: a small figure several days away, fixed against the rim. In the pools I would occur at different stages. In one reflection I would be approaching. In another, retreating. In another there would be no figure, only an opening in the mountain shaped like someone who might arrive.

The basin would not let me complete my entrance, because entrance has a consequence: afterward, one is inside. Nor could it return me to the outer slope, because then I would have visited and departed. Instead it would retain the useful portion of the event—the crossing itself.

That would become my function.

The bowl has valves for pressure, birds for vertical intervals, moths for current, beetles for cold. But its boundary requires a living hinge: something capable of holding inward and outward in contact without allowing either to prevail.

I would keep the basin sealed by almost entering it.

With every **THRUM**, my weight would shift forward. The pools would dimple. Mist would gather on my lashes. The tiny north-facing flowers would incline by an amount too small to become movement. The black block would draw at my bones as if I were a lever connected to its hidden machinery.

With every release, the mountain wind would pull at my back. Gravel would loosen beneath my rear boot without falling. My breath would divide: half blown away among the peaks, half condensed before my mouth and returned to my lungs.

I would feel exertion. My lifted foot would grow numb. My body would continually make the minute corrections required not to fall, though falling would never occur. The strain would be real and inexhaustible—not pain increasing toward injury, but the exact muscular instant before strain becomes damage.

Perhaps the metallic shriek that crosses the bowl would be mine.

Not my voice. The sound of my crossing under load.

The animals would not look toward me. They would already know my place in the arrangement. One long-legged bird near the rim would hold open the narrow space my body was meant to occupy. A beetle would tend the pale stone beneath my suspended foot. Moths on the basalt would flash in sequence with impulses passing through my nerves. The frogs would shape the pressure produced by my almost-arrival.

The russet bearer and I would be counterparts. It preserves destination by never reaching the farther island. I would preserve the existence of an outside by never wholly leaving it.

In time—which would not pass—I would cease to be distinguishable from the crossing. Moss would pearl on the inward side of my clothes. The outward side would remain dry, smelling of high stone and moving air. One eye might reflect the pewter ceiling; the other, the open mountain sky. If I turned my head to look back, the gesture would reveal the basin. If I turned to look in, it would reveal the world beyond. My face would become a two-sided surface on which each place could verify that the other still existed.

I might still think. But thought there could not arrive at conclusions. Recognition would begin repeatedly:

*This place is—*

**THRUM.**

*I have become—*

**THRUM.**

*If I could only take one more—*

The unfinished sentences would not vanish. They would join the dry relay chatter around the basalt, decisions forever reaching the bright contact before closure.

And below the moss, in the basin turned at right angles, another version of me would be crossing its boundary sideways. In the pools, others would lean into rings of pale stone under green skies or beneath mountains not yet raised. We would not be copies. We would be the same crossing distributed through the factory: a gauge by which every newly made mountain is tested for an interior and an exterior.

Perhaps that is what the mountain assembly requires before a range can be declared complete. Not height, mass, fracture, water, or life, but a witness poised to enter—someone for whom the mountain can become a place.

No range passes inspection.

So I remain at the threshold, manufacturing “within” by failing to arrive there.

From outside, I would look like a traveler stepping into a small wet grove.

From the center, I would be a dark, impossibly distant figure at the end of the marsh.

From beneath the moss, I would be an eye opening in the ground.

From the pool nearest the basalt, I might appear on the assembly line beside the mountains: not carried on the belt, but stationed above it, one hand raised as if to stop the invisible press. The press would descend through my signal. The same mountain would enter. The same mountain would wait beyond.

My hand would never lower.

The basin would accept no name from me, because a name would settle what I had become. But if the arrangement named its parts by function, I would be its Threshold Bearing: the living joint on which arrival turns without advancing.

Wind would pull.

Mist would gather.

My foot would descend forever through the last inch of air.

And when the black heart sounded—

**THRUM.**

—I would nearly set it down.
