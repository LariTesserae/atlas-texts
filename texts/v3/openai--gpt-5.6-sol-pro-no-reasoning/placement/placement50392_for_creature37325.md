I would find myself beneath the paving.

Not in the deepest rushing dark, where the old conduits descend beyond hearing, and not entirely in the visible basin. I would inhabit the narrow interval between them: the drowned courses of tile compressed beneath the present floor, where yesterday’s architecture has become today’s geology.

At first I seem to be one of the blind eels.

I know the enclosure through pressure. The wheel’s first engagement travels through stone into my jaw. The mallets are not sounds but blows passing through my length. I rise when the cracked slabs ring and put my mouth to the grate, tasting fresh rain, bird-lime, clay, lamp soot, frog mucus, the faint iron trace of blood. Then I turn and carry that weather downward.

But an eel is only the part of me that can move.

Much of me remains behind in the pipes: translucent skin caught on mortar, strings of eggs in a flooded cup, a pale ribbon glimpsed beneath a loose tile. Generations of us have followed the same passages so faithfully that the passages have entered our bodies. We curve where they curve. We pause beneath each pillar. We know blocked galleries as scars and newly opened seams as sudden thoughts.

We are not many eels occupying the buried works. We are the buried works, briefly given muscles.

Long ago, perhaps, the keepers lowered our ancestors into the shafts. Perhaps we were meant to clear sediment, to test the water, or to carry offerings into chambers human arms could not reach. The intention has washed away. What remains is the task, though even that is no longer separate from hunger. We dredge because the silt contains food. The water continues because we dredge. The wheel turns because the water continues. The stones sound, and the sound calls us upward to feed.

Yet beneath that simple circuit I would feel another summons.

There is one pipe unlike the others, too narrow for my body and too smooth to be natural wear. It runs from somewhere below the central pillar toward the dry niche beside the spillway. When the wheel gives its deepest stroke, pressure enters this pipe. A minute quantity of water is lifted—not enough to turn anything, nourish anything, or drain anything. It rises through the wall and enters the clay figure.

Then the bead forms on the figure’s beak.

I would be the creature that knows where that drop comes from.

Not what it means. Meaning belongs to beings that can stand outside a pattern, and nothing here stands outside. But I would know its route with my whole body. I would know that the drop has passed through a buried chamber where six channels meet around an empty stone seat. I would know that, when the drop rises, the current briefly reverses. I would know there is a hollow below the seat into which no eel can enter, though something inside it answers the wheel with a faint contraction.

Perhaps it is a bladder of ancient leather, still filling and collapsing.

Perhaps a rootless white organ has grown around the mechanism.

Perhaps the lower construction is not endless at all, but shaped like a vast submerged body, and the basin is merely its mouth.

I would circle that inaccessible hollow. I would wear the stone around it smooth. My descendants would inherit the circling without inheriting the question. Over generations our pale bodies would polish a ring into the passage, and that ring would alter the current, and the altered current would strengthen the pressure that raises the ceremonial drop. Curiosity would become maintenance. Maintenance would become anatomy.

The other creatures would seldom see me clearly. The birds would strike when I surfaced, but my skin would be slick and my retreats exact. Frogs would vanish from the gutters at my approach. Crabs would gather at grates after I passed, feeding on what I loosened. During floods I might rise onto the paving and lie among the temporary truce: a white curve between black birds’ feet, breathing water too shallow to conceal me.

The mice would fear me, except near the niche.

There, none of us would hunt.

When the deepest note traveled down through the basin, I would feel the old contraction below. Water would be pressed into the narrow pipe. The figure would tremble. Its bead would swell.

For that instant I would lift my head beneath the grate, and the whole enclosure would become legible as a single animal.

The rafters would be its dark ribs. The hanging nests would be organs exchanging water and waste. The channels would be exposed veins. The crabs would be its cleaning mouthparts; the beetles, its digestion; the moths, its dreaming fur. The wheel would be a joint that motion had taught to sing. The clay figure would be neither god nor keeper, but a small external nerve: the one place where the buried body touches the audible world delicately.

And I would be its memory—not memory as image or story, but as a route water can still take.

I would remember the makers only in the widths they allowed. Six fingers meant certain grips, certain spans between pegs, certain curves in the pipes. Their vanished hands would persist as turns my spine could negotiate and chambers in which I could coil. I would remember their children as the smaller prints above the eastern wall, though I would never see them. I would remember the final tally as a disturbance in the pillar’s foundation, the stone struck sharply and then never struck again.

No door would matter to me. Doors belong to horizontal lives. My world would consist of openings, pressures, and returns. The basin would not be a prison but the bright, loud surface of a much older interior.

When I died, I would not leave it. Beetles and crabs would take what rose. What sank would feed the pale colonies in the sumps. My bones would lodge in a constriction, gathering fibers and mineral until they formed a new lip in the channel. Water would begin to sing there. Young eels would orient themselves by the vibration.

In that way I would finally become still enough to belong.

A vertebra would become a valve. A rib would divide a current. The passage would change by the small erosion around me, and the next generation would bend differently as it swam through. The architecture would enter them as it had entered me, but carrying one additional curve: mine.

So I would be an eel, for a while.

Before that, water.

Afterward, a feature of the pipe.

And throughout, one blind impulse of the many-bodied keeper: the impulse that circles beneath the floor, tending the inaccessible hollow and carrying the enclosure’s smallest sound toward the surface.

When the clay drop fell, I would feel its absence from below.

That minute subtraction would pass through the channels like a loosened breath. Above me, everything would pause. Below, the old hollow would fill again.

Then the frogs would call, the birds would erupt, the wheel would groan into its next position—and I would turn in the dark, following the shape laid down for me by hands no living creature remembers.
