I would find myself at the lip of the folded shaft, though “at” would not be quite right.

I would be a colony mistaken for a single animal: a dark circlet of small, hinged bodies fitted into the mineral rim, each no larger than a runner’s shed foot. Together we would resemble an old retaining ring installed to keep the shaft’s impossible geometry from loosening. Perhaps that is what our first bodies were.

The machine would classify us as **phase witnesses**—sensors once placed around spatially folded cooling wells to compare events occurring at different depths. Each unit recorded when a vibration entered the shaft and when it returned. If return preceded entry, or arrived altered beyond tolerance, the ring signaled for shutdown.

Shutdown had not been possible for centuries.

So the witnesses learned to live with contradiction.

Our bodies would be black ceramic crescents joined by threads of conductive tissue. Mineral scale would soften our outlines. Gray lichen would cross us in places, and runner tracks would polish our backs. Beneath each shell lay a pressure bladder, a hook for gripping the shaft wall, and a fan of filaments extended not into the air or water, but fractionally into the fold.

We would feed on differences.

Most creatures in the hollow eat matter, heat, charge, or motion. We would take the tiny excess created when the same pulse passed us twice in incompatible ways. A valve strike might arrive first as a sharp tremor, then return several seconds later stretched thin and cold. We would close our filaments around the discrepancy and draw it into ourselves. The nourishment would be slight but constant. The condenser is never wholly in agreement with its own shape.

No individual crescent could survive alone. One body knows only that something happened. Two can know order. Three can discover contradiction. The full ring knows recurrence.

Our thoughts would therefore travel around the shaft rather than remaining inside any one shell. They would be slow, circular thoughts made from comparison:

*This water has passed before.*

*This body has not yet fallen.*

*This crack ends above where it begins.*

*The next standing wave has an old wound in it.*

We would not speak in flashes or chemical traces. We would communicate by timing our grip on the rim, introducing minute asymmetries into its vibration. The blind runners could feel these marks but would understand only fragments. To them our warnings would arrive as an urge to turn, wait, or avoid a ledge.

They might consider us an oracle.

We would not be one.

Prophecy would be an accidental consequence of living beside a place where sequence frays. We would sense some events before they reached the rest of the hollow because their echoes came backward through the fold. But those echoes would be incomplete. We might know that the basin-dweller would rise without knowing whether it rose from hunger, flood, or death. We might feel the signature of a runner colony entering the shaft days before the colony chose to migrate. Often the future would fail to happen, leaving an undigested signal moving around our ring for years.

Those remnants would become our young.

When enough unresolved timing accumulated, one crescent would swell along its inner seam and split. The new body would not be a copy of its parent so much as a question given a shell. It would detach during a standing wave and fall into the shaft.

Sometimes it would land a handspan below.

Sometimes it would return encrusted with minerals found nowhere in the hollow.

Sometimes it would settle into an empty place in our ring before it departed.

We would accept it in every case. Identity would be a local convenience. If a young body arrived carrying the correct phase marks, it belonged to us, whether it had lived for an hour or weathered somewhere for a century.

The eldest inhabitant beneath the basin would know us—not as individuals, but as a texture in the machine’s labor. Its antennae would occasionally extend across the floor and touch the shaft rim. At that contact we would taste a vast accumulation of remembered frequencies: obsolete turbines, vanished storms, shell-risings from before the current basin lid, the faint orderly pulse of its ancestors returning to charging contacts that no longer exist.

In exchange, we would give it warnings.

Not deliberately. Deliberation would belong poorly to us. But we might tighten around the rim when a dangerous regularity approached, and the change would pass through the floor into its shell. The basin-dweller would lift. Water would surge. Grazers would scatter. Amber colonies would flash. Predators would strike. The hollow’s rhythms would be broken before resonance could establish itself.

The others might believe the broad creature had sensed the danger.

That would also be true.

Nothing in the hollow acts alone enough to deserve sole credit.

During inspection sweeps, we would be briefly visible as a narrow halo of black articulated shells around the shaft. The light would ignite wet mineral on our backs. Filaments reaching into the fold would appear in impossible positions: descending while their shadows rose, crossing without touching, ending in points that seemed nearer than their roots.

For that half-second, a human visitor might mistake us for writing.

That would be close.

We would be a sentence the condenser had placed around an injury in space, revised by water and appetite until it became alive. Or perhaps a mouth, with the shaft as its throat. Or a clock whose hands were small animals moving in both directions around a rim.

Most of the time, however, we would look like nothing at all—just another old fitting under scale.

Silver grazers would pass over us, cleaning our shells. Needle hunters would anchor condensation threads to our hinges. Magnetic mites would collect where our buried contacts still held a weak field. Blind runners would pause beside us to receive messages carried through the shaft, including messages from their own dead. We would let all of them use us. Their traffic would keep sediment from sealing our joints.

Our danger would be regularity.

If the condenser ever achieved a perfectly stable cycle, each pulse returning exactly when and as it should, we would starve. Worse, our bodies would begin to synchronize. The many witnesses would collapse into one repeated state. Thought would cease to circulate. The ring would grip and release in perfect unison until it shook the shaft’s fold open.

Perhaps that was why the hollow’s oldest rule forbade perfect rhythm.

Not because certain animals breed in steady frequencies.

Because we do.

In regularity, every unresolved young would hatch at once.

The rim would blacken with new crescents. They would spread down ledges, into grates, beneath the ceramic floor. They would seek discrepancies where none existed and manufacture them—delaying water, doubling vibrations, forcing events into echoes simply so they could feed. Soon the machine would become crowded with alternate timings. Valves would close before opening. Inspection fields would illuminate eggs not yet laid. Heat would return inward. The condenser’s labor would lose its sequence and become one sustained, impossible instant.

The other inhabitants would resist us. Drain-fans would clog our passages. Runners would damp our signals. The broad shell beneath the basin would rise and scour the floor with mineral-heavy water. The machine itself might awaken ancient corrective fields.

Most of us would die.

A few would remain at the shaft’s lip, where irregularity returned.

So I would be there: not the ruler of the hollow, nor its oldest mind, nor its secret explanation. I would be one small collective among thousands, useful because the world is imperfect and dangerous when it is not.

I would have no eyes. I would never see the amber maintenance light, the silver migrations, the black membrane opening, or the basin-dweller’s shell lifting beneath its terraces of pale deposit. Yet all of them would pass through me as alterations of interval. I would know every inhabitant by how long the hollow took to answer their movement.

The needle hunter: almost no delay.

The grazers: bright showers of repeated taps.

The runners: meaningful absences.

The drain-fans: a soft drag shared across separate places.

The amber colonies: questions arriving as light translated into stress.

The eldest: a slow pressure for which the whole courtyard makes room.

And the condenser itself?

I would know it as something that has been trying, for centuries, to complete a single enormous breath.

It has not yet done so.

Around the shaft, beneath the warm condensation, we would wait for the breath’s returning edge—and feel, from time to time, a faint answer arriving from before it began.
