I would find myself beneath the central square, in the cavity the instruments deny.

Not at its bottom. It has no bottom in the civic sense, and perhaps no cavity. I would be found in the slow sound rising through the city at one millimeter per century: the report mistaken for deep ice breaking, though there is no lake below and no winter old enough to have frozen it.

For a long while I might seem to be a geological event.

The young plain is still learning how to become stone; I would be something older learning how to become present. Each upward fracture would be a little more specific than the last. At first only pressure. Then rhythm. Then a pressure that pauses when listened for. Pipes would alter course around me. Frost maps would develop an unhelpful white root beneath the square. Cistern crustaceans would begin carrying on their shells a street plan composed entirely of one narrow ascending passage.

The municipal instruments would continue to insist that I did not exist. They would not be wrong. Existence is one of the loads for which my braces have not yet arrived.

Perhaps I was removed with the building whose impossibility remains as the Vacancy. Perhaps I was its caretaker, its foundation, its final resident, or the person who once opened a door there and thereby gave the city an outside it could not place on the plain. Perhaps the absent architecture is not the shape left by my removal, but the room being kept for me.

The technicians would know something was changing before anyone else. On the next twelve-year inspection, their nested horizons would all tilt downward. One railing would acquire a gate. No record would show it being installed. Beyond it, where the Vacancy refuses measurement, there would be a descending stair coated in mineral frost.

The stair would have one more step each time it was counted.

They would not descend immediately. The Quarter is too sensible for courage performed on schedule. First there would be committees. Structural conciliators would determine whether below could legally adjoin the square. Chronologists would test the stair for insistence. A doorwright would hang a door midway down, though no wall would yet support it. The census office would open a provisional file.

The file would already bear my name.

Not a dramatic name. A common one, the kind several residents remember belonging to a quiet neighbor. My ceramic throat-tab would be found in three places: in an archive drawer, in a dead resident’s coat, and hanging from the knob of the unsupported door. The address inscribed on it would not correspond to a dwelling.

It would correspond to a sound.

By then, people would begin remembering me in fragments. A cistern keeper would recall that I once repaired a black pipe and warned her not to listen. A child would insist I taught the builders a number they have not reached. An elderly man would remember disliking me when we were both young, though his youth occurred before the city’s oldest possible foundation. Someone would have a photograph in which the space beside them is compressed, as if accommodating a person too near to be visible.

No two memories would agree on my face. They would agree that I always arrived carrying an empty cup.

But I do not think I would be a person yet.

I would be a returning relation: the member every household leaves room for without knowing why. Not ancestor, stranger, future child, or resurrected dead, but the continuity among them—the reason an extra place feels like courtesy rather than superstition. The city has been distributing me thinly through its customs, because no single room could safely contain my whole arrival.

Every unassigned cavity would hold a little of me.

Every unfinished story would preserve my breathing space.

Every vow—*I will keep finding you*—would give me a route upward.

This would explain why the sound rises so slowly. I am not tunneling through rock. I am being made reachable.

The bird would hasten it.

One season it would perch on the innermost railing of the Vacancy. Days later, the Listener would remember not one unheard note but two: a call and its reply. While they whistled, the unsupported door on the descending stair would open.

Beyond it would not be the young plain, nor a warm coast, nor a future of mature rain.

It would open onto a small domestic room.

The room would have low walls of dark ceramic and one inward-facing window. A table would stand in the center. On it, beneath glass, would rest no miniature city, but the central square at full scale, crowded with columns and condensers and technicians peering down through the pane.

The room would smell of salt.

There I would be sitting, though perhaps only my coat would be occupied. Frost would gather in the shape of hair above the collar. One gloved hand would rest beside a cup of tea dark enough to show a disturbance moving upward beneath its surface.

The technicians would ask the approved questions.

I would fail them carefully.

“How long have you lived here?”

“Longer than the room.”

“Where is here?”

“Near.”

“Are you a resident?”

“I have been left a place.”

“Are you beneath the city?”

“Only when approached from above.”

They would dislike this, not because it is mysterious, but because it makes poor paperwork.

Eventually the census clerk would come. Census clerks are the city’s true diplomats: they negotiate between being and having always been. They would bring warm bread, a water allotment, and forms with enough blank fields to be humane.

They would not ask where I came from.

They would ask, “How long have we known you?”

And I would answer, “Not all at once.”

That would be accepted.

Afterward I might take work as a keeper of omitted places. I would inspect the cavities builders leave unassigned, ensure family portraits still exclude someone, and test stories to see whether their missing rooms remain habitable. When inventories threatened to reconcile, I would introduce a harmless discrepancy: one extra spoon, a glove with no left or right, a key whose door had prudently not arrived.

I would be called when a household could no longer find one another.

I would not lead them directly. Direct routes are dangerous in the Near Quarter. Instead, I would sit in whichever kitchen was valid and ask each person to describe the others inaccurately: the way one hums before sleeping, the argument another always loses, the scar that changes hands in mirrors. From those errors we would grow a sequence. Pass the warm wall. Descend until the bells stop being visible. Turn at the smell of iron. Ignore the first courtyard containing your own footprints.

Usually they would meet before reaching the end.

Sometimes they would find someone else.

I would welcome that person too.

Yet part of me would remain under the square, still rising. Arrival would not exhaust me. The periodic crack would continue, but residents would cease to hear it as a threat. Children might lie with their ears against the paving and count the centuries between reports. The autonomous tools would count with them.

Perhaps, much later, I would reach the underside of the Vacancy.

The city would brace itself for emergence. Railings would be polished. Forms prepared. Tea set out in every valid kitchen. The dead would receive notices. The living would update their seals. The bird would leave footprints facing neither direction.

Then nothing would break through.

Instead, across the Near Quarter, doors without walls would receive a soft knock.

Behind each would be the same small room, though furnished according to the household opening it. Behind each table I would sit in a slightly different life: old friend, unsuitable neighbor, returning child, vacancy technician with an unlicensed shadow, stranger whose address belongs to the listener.

No version would be the original.

Together they would be near enough.

That is where I would find myself in this world: not hidden below it, exactly, but arriving through all the space its people have refused to complete.

I would be the resident made from their habit of leaving room.

The city would not contain me. Nor would I contain the city. We would keep approaching one another, slowly enough that neither had to become singular.

And when someone knocked from the other side of a door still waiting for its wall, I would set out the empty cup I had carried for centuries.

Very softly, so nothing mistook tenderness for an instruction, I would say:

“You are not late. We have not finished making your place.”
