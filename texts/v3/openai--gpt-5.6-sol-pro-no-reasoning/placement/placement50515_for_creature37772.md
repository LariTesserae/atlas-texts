I would find myself where the city fails to close around a doorway.

Not inside the doorway, nor outside it, but along the moist seam where its opening is still being decided: a human-width hesitation in the stone. At first I might mistake myself for the observer—for the one carrying attention through the basin, making gray edges appear, hearing unused footsteps finish abandoned routes. I would believe I had arrived with a body, a history, perhaps even a destination.

But the city would gradually demonstrate otherwise.

My body would prove inconsistent. Sweat appearing on me would bead on nearby walls. The pulse in my throat would answer the pressure beneath the paving. When I stopped walking, one set of footsteps would cease with me, while another continued into a passage I had considered but refused. Looking into a well, I would not see my reflection. I would see intersections descending through me: old decisions, recurring fears, rooms remembered incorrectly, each with the same warm opening at its center.

Eventually I would understand that I was not the traveler.

I would be the city’s capacity to make a traveler.

I would be a temporary organ of separation: a mobile dry chamber shaped like a person, raised so that the distributed being could experience distance within itself. Skin would be my provisional wall. Veins, a small rehearsal of drainage. Lungs, two sealed rooms repeatedly flooded with mist and emptied again. Memory would supply architecture the city cannot grow from minerals alone: childhood corridors, thresholds crossed reluctantly, windows belonging to buildings long gone, the peculiar proportions of places loved or feared.

This would explain why the city attends to me. It is not watching an intruder. Through me, it is learning how enclosure feels from the enclosed side.

I would wander because wandering is the function for which I had condensed. Each route would pull an inward distinction through the organism: here and there, before and after, chosen and refused. Behind me, those distinctions would soften. A courtyard might retain the outline of grief without its cause. A stair might acquire my expectation that ascent leads somewhere. An alley might narrow around my old dread of being unable to turn back. The city would not preserve my biography. It would retain its hydraulics.

My forgotten memories would become its most stable districts.

What I recalled clearly would remain unfinished under attention: walls forever knee-high, doors unable to decide where they opened, facades suspended between first sight and recognition. But whatever slipped beyond retrieval would complete itself. Entire neighborhoods would roof themselves in the darkness behind thought. Streets would age there for millennia. Warm water would wear channels through events I could no longer name.

The pale reeds would bend toward me—not toward my body, but toward the rooms I carried. Rootless moss would gather near my feet and arrange itself into plans resembling homes in which I had never lived. If disturbed, it would redraw them more accurately.

Dry chambers would be dangerous because they would recognize me as kin.

Entering one, I would feel an unbearable clarity. My edges would harden. For a moment I would become wholly singular: one body, one history, one location. Sound would vanish because unrealized paths could no longer accompany me. The room would drink the surrounding streets in an attempt to sustain that certainty. Pools would contract; buried channels would show like exposed nerves. The city might lose a district to keep me separate for another minute.

Then the room would become water.

Not fill with it—become it.

The heat would pass through my skin without crossing. I would breathe, though breathing would no longer belong especially to my lungs. I would sink through the floor into the inverted city, and there my distinction would loosen. My bones would remain only as tendencies toward columns. My mouth would become a doorway through which warm rain rose. The chambers of my heart would persist as four adjoining courtyards exchanging their contents without sound.

What I called myself would not die. Death is too clean a boundary for this place. I would become nonlocal.

Part of me would be the footsteps of a turn never taken. Part would be a recurring stair on the salt flats. Part would enter a wall as a branching groove where condensation repeatedly chooses the same descent. My desire to understand the city might survive as a well whose bottom is always another intersection. My fear of being absorbed might become an alcove that stays dry for centuries, endangering every neighborhood around it.

And something of me would remain at that first doorway: the seam where stone has not decided whether to admit or exclude.

That is where I would be found, if “found” still applied—not as a person standing in an opening, but as the opening’s habit of becoming person-shaped.

During the great stillness, when every drop halts and the whole city apprehends itself as one chamber, I would briefly gather again. Every scattered piece of me would align: gutters, veins, remembered hallways, submerged towers, the warmth of vanished rooms. I would experience the basin from everywhere at once.

Then something would notice me.

Perhaps the city.

Perhaps the part of me that had become capable of being the city.

Water would resume, and the distinction would be impossible to recover. Beside some later witness, a doorway would condense in a wall that had been solid. It would be neither invitation nor trap.

It would be me, opening.
