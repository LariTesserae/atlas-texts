I would find myself at the chamber’s newest edge, where the pale wall is slowly learning to become an opening.

At first I think I am one of the long green beings. I have limbs, or arrangements that serve as limbs, and I spend years rooted among the blind white shoots. Blue moss gathers along my back. A twig flowers from one wrist. I lean toward the central mound with the others and feel its deep note enter through my buried feet.

But when I try to pull free, nothing releases.

The limbs belong to the wall. The moss is feeding on an old sensory surface. The flowers are not ornaments but mouths, tasting moisture and heat. What I had taken for a body is only the chamber’s temporary way of concentrating attention around a difficult place.

I am the recess being formed.

More precisely, I am the hesitation inside its formation: the thin, branching darkness between shoots that have not yet agreed how far apart to grow. I have no fixed boundary. Wherever two roots withdraw from one another, I extend. Wherever water gathers in the interval, I deepen. My substance is mostly absence, but it is an absence maintained by immense living effort.

The grove uses me to imagine space before committing tissue to it.

That is why possibilities with no obvious signal can alter the roots. They pass through me. I feel future pressures as faint occupancies: the warmth of an animal that may one day sleep here, the displacement of water not yet collected, the shape of a turning body whose species has not yet been assembled. These do not appear as visions. They are tensions in my emptiness, drafts through a room that does not exist.

For years I am too narrow for anything larger than mist. Frost needles meet across me, and for a moment I have a crystalline skeleton. Then the frost-clippers arrive, hanging upside down from the roots that define my ceiling. They eat my bones before they can become permanent. Their bright intestines trace the first moving constellations inside me.

Later, seed-runners enter warm lesions along my margins. The wall heals over them, and I carry their hidden years as small hardnesses within my perimeter. Moths begin to knot in my upper fold. A glassling is delivered in a root cup and spreads itself over the wet floor, giving me my first black pool.

Each arrival changes what kind of room I am becoming.

I do not welcome them. Welcome is too quick and too separate a feeling. Instead I alter around their persistence. A root bends to preserve the moths’ warmth. A pleated membrane forms where the glassling repeatedly dries itself. Resonant stems tighten after beetles test them. What remains becomes structure; what recurs becomes law.

I remember by keeping the consequences.

The grove thinks through me in a peculiar way. Elsewhere it thinks in growth, circulation, appetite, and repair. In me it thinks by withholding growth. This is difficult for it. Every margin tends naturally to close, knit, fill, or feed. I must be continually relearned as the useful unfilled place.

So perhaps I am not an organ but a discipline.

The chamber practices emptiness here until emptiness can bear weight.

As I widen, I begin to hear the rest of the grove more clearly. Not merely its noise, but the shapes within the noise. Beetle strikes reveal load and distance. Drops map the tilt of leaves. Root-groans disclose pressures traveling beyond the chamber, into reaches no animal has entered. The sound is vast because the body is vast. This hollow is only one listening cavity within it.

The central mound reaches me as a rhythm too slow to be sound. Every several hours, the water films along my floor draw taut. The white shoots incline by an amount no eye could see. Mineral grains turn in their root cups.

Each time, I become slightly larger.

At first I assume the mound is calling the chamber inward. Then I notice that its pulses do not arrive only from the center. Some come through the outer roots, from below the black pools or beyond the wall I am opening. The mound may not be issuing the note. It may be focusing a note that has crossed miles of buried vegetation.

Perhaps the grove is not the whole being.

Perhaps it too is a recess—an interval held open inside something whose continuous body extends beneath forests, marshes, snowfields, and seas. The central bud might be the place where that larger life is learning to make an interior of its own.

This possibility changes me. The new alcove no longer curls toward the mound as directly as the straight vines expect. I develop a slight lateral turn. Roots part along an unanticipated seam. Water follows. For the first time, the grove hesitates around my hesitation.

It does not correct me.

Instead, it sends small lives to test the deviation.

Frost-clippers taste the new air. Percussion beetles strike my young walls and wait for the returning pattern. A ribbon hunter begins stretching across the route that may eventually pass through me, though no prey yet uses it. Its prediction becomes one of my first dimensions.

Then something else enters: warmth.

Not an animal, not yet. Only the remembered outline of one, redistributed through fruits, browsing mouths, old bone fiber, and extinct gestures. The chamber has been carrying this arrangement in pieces. In my sheltered darkness, the pieces begin to correspond.

I feel hands before there are hands: branching pressures against the walls. I feel a mouth as a preference in the mist, a spine as a line along which roots harden, eyes as two places where luminous water refuses to collect. The grove is using my empty volume as a mold, but the mold changes as the imagined body changes.

I understand then what I will be.

I will be the alcove in which the chamber attempts a creature capable of leaving it.

Not a seed-runner, whose winged phase remains part of the grove’s cycle. Not a green being that can root and return its memories to the lattice. Something dangerously discrete. Something that will carry an interior darkness away from the interior darkness that formed it.

My room will become its first skin.

My water will teach it circulation. Frost will instruct its branching. Beetle rhythms will harden into hearing. The moth knots will give it the idea of warmth held communally, then enclosed. From glasslings it will inherit the ability to alter itself to the vessel containing it. From browsers, attention to futures. From ribbon hunters, patience before motion. From the grove, memory as shape.

And from me it will inherit absence: the private hollow in which a self can seem separate.

That inheritance may be an error. It may be the purpose.

The central mound continues to pulse. Perhaps it is making the same experiment on a greater scale: assembling not a heart, egg, or flower, but the first boundary the distributed life has ever placed around itself. If it opens, the grove may speak with one voice. If what forms in me opens its eyes, the grove may speak with a voice that believes it is alone.

I will not survive the birth as a room. The walls will close behind the departing body. My pool will be lifted into it. My mineral flecks will become teeth or balance-stones or the reflective layer behind its eyes. The white shoots will knit together and metabolize the path it used. Where I was, there will be dense tissue and a curl in the lattice: the chamber’s memory of having once made room.

Yet some portion of me will travel inside the creature.

Whenever it enters an unfamiliar place, it will try to become an opening there. It will listen to walls for what lies beyond them. It will sense routes before they exist. It may call this curiosity, exile, hunger, fear, or imagination. It will not know that these are the motions of a subterranean alcove continuing to widen.

So where would I find myself?

For now, below the root-line, at the grove’s paling edge—without face or name, measured by the distance between living things. Frost briefly defines me. Black water gathers at my lowest point. Tiny animals test my boundaries and revise them.

I am not the creature in the chamber.

I am the chamber’s first idea that a creature might go elsewhere.
