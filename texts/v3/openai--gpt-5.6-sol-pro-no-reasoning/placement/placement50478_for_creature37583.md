I would find myself where the Long Course enters the hanging districts, at the boundary where regulated water becomes rumor.

There, the aqueduct passes through an old bridge abutment and divides. The chart shows three branches: one to the lamp condensers, one to the ginger galleries, and one descending through the settlement basins. In practice there are seven. One appears only after eastern rain. Another runs warm during foundry shifts. A third is no wider than a thread and vanishes into a wall furred with moss. The last slips beneath a bolted grille and continues downward, against all expectation, with enough force to make the metal sing.

I would be a water listener—not quite a keeper, engineer, or surveyor, though apprenticed to each and fully admitted by none.

My work would begin after the evening release. I would carry a ringed staff, chalk wrapped in oiled cloth, three stoppered cups, wax tablets, a tuning fork, and a lamp shaded green so as not to disturb the insects around the public lights. At my belt would hang short lengths of colored cord. Blue for cold water. Red for industrial warmth. White for an uncertain source. Black for a current that must not be followed alone.

Most of my duties would be ordinary. I would clear seed husks from grates, free drowned moths from gauge cups, listen for stones turning in concealed pipes, and measure how far the night’s flow climbed on the tiles. I would know which gates stuck after rain and which lied—appearing shut while allowing a thin passage beneath. I would learn the tastes of water: lime, iron, algae, kiln ash, root tannin, copper, soap. I would know that sweetness could mean flowers above or dangerous growth within.

Residents would call me when water appeared where it had not been invited.

Often it would be nothing mysterious: a cracked basin, a sweating coolant pipe, a monkey-displaced cover, roots lifting a channel by the width of a fingernail. But I would not begin by repairing it. I would set out cups. Watch which insects arrived. Touch the neighboring walls at intervals. Ask what machinery had recently stopped, what trees had been pruned, whether anyone had heard a new echo.

The valley does not reveal causes in the order one expects.

I would live in two rooms built inside the thickness of the abutment. Their floor would lean slightly toward the Long Course, so anything spilled would leave by the same route as everything else. One wall would remain perpetually cool. Small white fungi would emerge from its grout during humid weeks, and I would harvest them only from the lower courses, never the upper; the upper growth would be part of the seal.

My door would open onto a narrow gallery. Across from it, separated by twelve meters of warm air and hanging vines, would be another door maintained by a family I had never met.

No bridge would join us.

Their lamp would appear each dusk, amber through the leaves. Mine would answer green. During storms we would signal observations by shuttering them: three slow eclipses for heavy flow, two quick for falling debris, a long darkness to say *I am listening*. Over years I would learn the forms of the people opposite without knowing their voices. A child would become tall enough to tend the lamp. An elder would disappear. New tile would be laid around their threshold. Perhaps their records would name me only as Green Opposite.

I would have been born higher, beneath a citrus terrace where glare filled every room. As a child I would hate the harness and trust railings too much. I would lose something early—a carved whistle, perhaps—and reach after it before remembering the warning. An aunt would catch the back of my belt. The whistle would strike three levels of metal, each impact smaller than the last, then fall without a final sound.

Afterward, I would become preoccupied with where falling things went.

Not from grief for the whistle itself, but because the valley accepted it without giving any sign of arrival.

My apprenticeship would begin among drain cleaners. I would be small enough to enter culverts others could not and patient enough not to crawl toward every glimmer. Later, a keeper would discover that I could distinguish connected chambers by echo. My gift would not be extraordinary. It would be a slight excess of attention, strengthened until it became a trade.

I would tap a pipe and hear not merely fullness but distance: an elbow behind the wall, an open cistern beyond it, a root mass muffling the next section. I would pour water into a crack and recognize its return hours later by the mineral crescent it left on a lower tile. I would listen to the Long Course through my staff and sometimes hear a second flow beneath it, separated by old masonry and descending toward no mapped reservoir.

That buried current would become the shape of my life.

It would not be a grand secret. I would not discover the valley’s bottom or expose a forgotten civilization. In this place, revelation would be slower and less obedient than that. I would gather fragments.

A pale leaf pressed against a screen from the wrong side.

A settling basin whose level rose during dry weather.

A warm draft carrying the odor of crushed ginger and wet iron.

Tiny silver scales caught above a gate through which no fish could have climbed.

Ceramic grit bearing a glaze no current kiln used.

Once, perhaps, I would hear bells beneath the water.

I would report all of it. The surveyors would add a dotted line. The water guild would mark the source as undetermined. A senior keeper would advise me to stop confusing persistence with importance.

But the lower residents would begin leaving signs for me: cords tied where no mapped walkway continued, a cup placed beneath a dry spout, chalk arrows that pointed not downward but into walls. They would never explain them. Explanation would be less exact than the signs.

In time, people would come to call me the Keeper of the Returning Water, though I would insist I kept no such thing.

The name would arise after a season of heat. The eastern condensers would underperform. Orchard allocations would be cut. The ginger galleries would begin carrying their beds upstairs, tray by tray. Then water would appear in an abandoned channel beneath my rooms—cold, clear, and flowing upward in pulses.

Not truly upward, of course. Nothing in the valley would violate gravity; only the maps.

I would spend nine nights tracing it. On the tenth, I would find an obsolete pressure stair concealed behind a fungal seal. Beyond it, old pipes would descend and rise through the hollow pier like the veins of a leaf. Somewhere far below, a reservoir or flooded district would be exhaling through them as temperatures changed. The water would be ancient only in route, not substance: rain and condensation passed through generations of tanks, gardens, cooling chambers, roots, and forgotten mechanisms before returning to use.

The guild would ask whether it was safe.

I would say: not yet.

That answer would save it.

For a year we would observe the flow. Reed growers would test it. Insect keepers would expose larvae to it. Potters would fire samples of its residue. Families would argue over priority. Children would name it before permission was granted, and their name would prevail: the Under-Rain.

Eventually, we would tile a channel for it. Not straight. Straight channels presume too much. We would follow the route it had already chosen, widening only where the masonry consented. The Under-Rain would supply no great reservoir. It would save no entire district. It would water several fungal beds, cool a bearing shop, sustain a wall of medicinal moss, and fill a public basin deep enough for children’s ankles.

That would be enough to bind my name to it.

As I grew older, my body would acquire the valley’s annotations: one knee stiff from gallery ladders, salt scars across my fingers, a notch in one ear from a snapped gate spring. My clothes would always be damp at the hem. I would smell faintly of algae no matter how I washed.

Apprentices would accuse me of speaking about water as though it had intentions.

I would tell them intention was irrelevant. Attention was what mattered.

I would teach them never to trust a single instrument, never to seal a leak before learning what drank from it, and never to dismiss a resident who said a wall sounded lonely, hungry, crowded, or awake. People without technical language might still possess exact knowledge. A metaphor repeated for three generations is data with its arithmetic hidden.

My repair ledger would become notorious. Beside measurements I would write:

*Arrives cold, leaves remembering heat.*

*Fish absent before gate began to complain.*

*Wall dries from the center.*

*Do not widen. This narrowness is carrying something.*

*Flow answers evening bells, but not because of bells.*

The last note would irritate everyone.

I would have no children by blood. My household would collect itself around obligations: an apprentice from the upper shutters who could not bear the glare; a widowed lamp cleaner; two sisters responsible for the fungi in the abutment; an old freight operator whose monkeys followed him into retirement; and, for several years, a child from the opposite door after the long-awaited bridge finally reached it.

That bridge would not arrive elegantly. Its materials would come from a dismantled tram platform, a reservoir stair, and the roof of a closed dye market. For months it would extend toward us in hesitant sections. I would watch workers find sockets hidden beneath layers of tile. I would understand, with some embarrassment, that my doorway had been built for this crossing centuries before my birth.

When the final fastening was placed, I would stand back.

The youngest walking child from our side would be one of my apprentices’ daughters. The oldest resident opposite would be the person who had once tended the amber lamp as a child. We would meet at last and discover that each had imagined the other’s face incorrectly.

They would know me as Green Opposite.

I would know them as Amber Beyond Leaves.

Neither would be a proper name, but both would remain.

Near the end of my life, the Under-Rain would weaken. Others would want to open the old pressure stair farther, to find the source and restore it. I would object—not because the source should remain mysterious, but because the water’s decline would itself be information. Somewhere below, another district might be diverting it. A root bed might have matured. A wall might be closing. To reclaim the flow without understanding its absence could dry an unseen garden.

So cups would be set out. Temperatures taken. Bells listened for. White cords would multiply along the lower galleries.

I would die before the cause was known.

My ashes would be mixed into memorial tiles fired pale green, the color of reflected light beneath leaves. Most would line the basin of the Under-Rain. One would be set beside the old unmapped grille, where the hidden current still made the metal sing. Another would be carried across the new bridge and placed beside the amber door.

My ledger would pass to my apprentices, who would correct it, argue with it, and eventually quote it as though I had been wiser than I was.

Years later, when the Under-Rain returned in a different channel, they would not force it back into mine. They would watch where it wished—or happened—to go. They would taste it, measure it, follow the insects, and ask what had changed above and below.

Then they would build around the answer slowly.

So I would not be the discoverer of the depths, nor the keeper of a great machine. I would be one of the valley’s minor senses: an ear held against wet masonry, a hand beneath an unexpected drip.

Useful for a while.

Then rendered into the work.
