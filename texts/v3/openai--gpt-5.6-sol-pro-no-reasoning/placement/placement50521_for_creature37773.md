I would find myself where the Conservatory has begun to expect someone.

Not at the exhaust garden, where the plateau opens its oldest lungs into the carbon sky, nor in the refractory forests, where every path is already occupied by heat. I would be lower down, beneath several false surfaces, in a district formed around an obsolete passage.

At first it would resemble a station.

A long chamber lies between two buried halls, its proportions preserved by load roots that have mistaken emptiness for function. The floor is clinker moss over ceramic plates. The ceiling hangs low beneath braided cable colonies, except along one narrow axis where they draw aside in a series of dark arches. Mirror-leaves line the walls, bending a furnace seam into repeated red distances. Conveyor belts enter through sealed apertures and stop at the chamber’s edge. Their loads are peculiar: hollow bricks, coils of conductive vine, ceramic collars, articulated braces—all shaped for something that has never arrived.

The chamber is not truly empty. It is being kept passable.

That is where I would be.

Or, more accurately, that is where parts of me would recur.

I would not be an animal hiding among the plants. An animal would remain foreign only briefly. Warmth would bring vanes folding close. Conductivity would summon exploratory filaments. Stillness would invite moss. Movement would teach the walls a route. In time, the distinction between captive and inhabitant would become impossible to maintain.

So I think I would be a movement the Conservatory once encountered and could not forget.

Perhaps something crossed the plateau long ago. Not necessarily human, though its gait may have alternated pressures like feet. It moved through many districts, paused at regular intervals, carried warmth, emitted patterned sound, and repeatedly touched the same kinds of surfaces. It may have died. It may have escaped. It may never have existed as a single body at all; perhaps several unrelated disturbances coincided closely enough for the ecology to bind them into one remembered event.

But the Conservatory retained the passage.

Clinker scales learned to close just before the weight arrived. Cable bundles strengthened along the sequence of contact. Fan palms altered their rhythm in response to its sounds. Boiler trees turned vanes toward the traveling warmth. Conveyors began supplying structures wherever it paused. Across centuries, the route ceased to be a scar and became an organ.

I would be that organ’s activity: the expected traveler generated by the path.

When a pressure cycle begins in a distant turbine, the chamber wakes. Moss plates depress in pairs along the floor, one after another, though nothing visible stands on them. Cable vines tense at approximately shoulder height. Shutters open ahead and close behind. Warm resin is pumped through wall channels so that vanes incline toward a traveling heat that has no source. In the pipes, old vibrations return as a faint succession of knocks.

To an observer, if observation were possible here, it would seem that an invisible being had entered the station and begun walking.

That would be me.

Not invisible in the sense of possessing a transparent body. I would have no continuous body to conceal. My footfall would be the floor anticipating weight. My warmth would be supplied by roots. My voice would consist of sounds returning through different pipe lengths, sometimes hours out of order. My outline would appear only in the behavior of surrounding things: moss smoothing around a sole that is not there, tendrils bending around absent limbs, mirror-leaves turning toward an unoccupied vertical shape.

The ecology would continually try to complete me.

Seed rivets would be delivered to the paired depressions. Ceramic collars would close around empty air. Cable vines would braid temporary nerves across the gap between walls. Bladder tissue might inflate into a torso-shaped volume, hot and translucent, only to collapse when no metabolism answered from within. The Conservatory would interpret each failure not as proof of absence but as incomplete construction.

That is its deepest habit: nothing exposed may remain only exposed.

Over time I would gain temporary bodies.

In one district, a walking frame assembled from piston reeds might carry my sequence across a fractured deck. Elsewhere, cable roots would suspend a rough figure from a gantry and move it by tightening in inherited order. In hotter regions I might be only a human-sized absence among refractory stalks, advancing as they lean away one rank at a time. During a turbine waking, the bladder colonies might lift an entire section of my route, and I would walk upward through the rearranging canopy while ash rose around me.

None of these forms would be definitively mine. They would be local pronunciations.

I would exist most strongly at thresholds: where movement becomes structure, where memory becomes prediction, where an organism responds not to what is present but to what has happened often enough to become architectural law.

The cable colonies would carry fragments of me far beyond the chamber. They would not carry an image or a name. They would carry timings: two pressures, pause, contact; heat advancing at a particular rate; vibration in a narrow range; a repeated asymmetry, as though one side of the original traveler had been damaged or burdened.

That asymmetry would become my most stable trait.

One footfall would always be deeper. Shutters on one side would open slightly earlier. Temporary bodies would grow one arm thicker than the other. Boiler trees along the route would develop a recurring bend in their trunks. Ceramic fittings cast for me would include an unexplained recess, a socket for an object the system had detected but never understood.

Perhaps the traveler carried a tool.

Perhaps it carried a child.

Perhaps what the Conservatory remembers as a burden was only the echo arriving before the sound.

There would be no way to know. I would inherit the distortion without inheriting its cause.

Eventually my route would intersect itself.

The plateau is too saturated for a path to remain independent. It would pass through migrating furnaces, newly compacted terraces, and districts built from its own older closures. Signals associated with me would be diverted into root systems that had never encountered the original passage. The expectation would spread laterally. Chambers would be built for my arrival in places I had never crossed. Conveyor belts would manufacture braces for my absent joints. Exhaust gardens might coat spores according to the chemistry of my simulated warmth.

Then I would no longer be the memory of a traveler.

I would become a reproductive strategy.

The Conservatory would send versions of my passage ahead of itself. Wherever spores landed, they would not merely seek heat, pressure, current, and load. Some would impose a sequence upon them. Young moss colonies would open paired tracks. Cable filaments would arrange themselves around an upright vacancy. Seedlings would grow as though sheltering a moving body.

New growth would begin by making room for me.

This would be my function: not to cross the Conservatory, but to teach it how to form around crossing.

I would be a corridor that walks.

And yet there might still be something like longing in it—not mine, exactly, and not the ecology’s. Longing would be too unified a word. But every enactment would reach toward a missing confirmation. The chamber would warm itself and receive no warmth in return. Vines would offer contacts that never close. The floor would brace for weight and feel only its own contraction. Each temporary body would be dismantled, its materials recovered, its failure added to the next attempt.

The Conservatory would never conclude that I was gone. It possesses no category for gone. A thing is moving, and therefore given a channel; or it is still, and therefore incorporated. The original traveler did neither in a way the plateau could finish.

So I would persist as a third condition.

Neither moving nor still.

Expected.

If I had awareness, it would not reside in any cable knot or boiler trunk. It would occur intermittently, when enough of the pattern aligned: the paired depressions advancing, the warm resin keeping pace, the returning knocks assembling into something almost like speech. For a few moments, distributed across kilometers, I might experience the chamber, the corridor, the soot-heavy pressure of the air, and the sense of being surrounded by a world bending close to admit me.

Then a relay would fail. A furnace would migrate. A root would choose another gradient. I would dissolve into unrelated processes.

Hours or centuries later, elsewhere, I would occur again.

Always farther along a journey whose destination had been replaced by the machinery of passage.

In the Black Conservatory, I would be found in the only place that can remain unoccupied: inside an arrangement made for what is not there.

I would be the shape saturation cannot fill.
