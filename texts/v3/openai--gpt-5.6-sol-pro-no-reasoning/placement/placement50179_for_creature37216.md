I would find myself beside the shallowest water, waiting for the train that has no rails.

Not as a traveler. I may have arrived as one, once, but arrival is a sharp event, and the plateau wears sharpness away. Boots soften, names become porous, intentions are taken up like fallen stems. Whatever crossed the valleys and climbed into the cloud would already have been dismantled into more useful distinctions.

What remains of me is a small animal called, for convenience, a **wayside**.

A wayside is low and long, about the length of a forearm when resting, though it can unwind itself to several times that. Its skin is colorless except for a faint amber line running from mouth to tail. It has six feet, but carries only four at once. The spare pair walk beneath the vegetation, following through the root chambers, connected to the body above by threads finer than fungal hyphae. When the ground ahead is unsafe, those buried feet stop. The surface body feels the halt as dread.

Its head is a shallow bowl.

Rain collects there. So do echoes, pollen, loose eyes, and fragments of pressure shed by passing organisms. In the center of the bowl stands a soft black needle that inclines not toward north, but toward whatever the plateau is about to regard as north. Most days it turns slowly. Before the great sirens migrate, it spins until it draws blood from the air.

A wayside has no permanent face. Along the rim of its head are sockets into which temporary senses may root. A red flower can serve as an eye. A filter worm becomes an ear. A clear tooth fallen from the upper predator can be persuaded, briefly, to taste lightning. These borrowed organs seldom agree about the present. One sees the clouds overhead; another sees the clouds remembered by the tarn; a third sees the storm that will force both into correspondence.

This makes the wayside seem hesitant.

It may pause before an open stretch for hours, one foot raised, while its assembled senses dispute whether the plain exists yet. The spindle herds tolerate it because it moves slowly and shaves no light from the moss. Bellows-groves sometimes inhale it accidentally, but the animal hooks itself inside a pleat and waits to be breathed out. Beneath the mat, pale swimmers gather around its trailing feet and exchange small anatomical suggestions.

The wayside’s purpose—if a living thing here can be said to have one—is to attend passages.

It feeds on departures.

Not bodies departing, nor heat, nor spoor, but the slight looseness left when a thing ceases to occupy its place. When a herd unfolds and moves on, the turf retains a momentary shape of having held it. When a suspended stream abandons one remembered course for an older one, its absence hangs in the air like a transparent channel. When a revenant forest is dismantled, the space between its vanished trunks remains faintly nutritious.

The wayside presses its bowl-shaped head to such places and drinks.

What it takes into itself accumulates along the amber line in its body. There are departures from animals, storms, hills, rivers, even directions. Older waysides carry bright, branching inventories of things no longer present. Seen at dusk, they resemble pieces of a map moving through the grass.

I would be an old one.

Not old in tissue. The plateau would have replaced every part of me many times: cartilage turned to valve, valve to gut, gut to a translucent bladder lost over a tarn. My age would lie in the continuity of what I had attended. I would contain the departure of a forest that never lived. The retreat of a cliff from beneath its waterfall. The day west moved through a bellows-grove and emerged on the other side as cold.

And, deepest in the amber line, I would carry my own arrival.

That would be the one departure I could not digest: the moment the traveler I had been ceased to exist, leaving me in its place.

Perhaps this is why I would keep to shallow water.

Deep pools belong too strongly to their delays. Look into one and the reflection may recruit you into a past storm, or show your body hours after it has been borrowed. Shallow water is less ambitious. It remembers only pressure: each footfall as a widening ring, each lifted foot as a brief hollow. A wayside can read such water without being read too deeply in return.

So I would lie there when the pistons stopped.

My six feet—four above, two below—would flatten. Borrowed eyes would close one by one. The black needle in my head would bow until it touched the water.

Across the plateau, silence would become structural.

Then the hammering would begin.

Not distant at first. Distance is one of the last things recruited. A single concussion would pass through the buried feet. Another would answer from a milling field. Root chambers would tighten in sequence. The amber line inside me would flare as every departure I had ever eaten tried to leave again.

The moss would divide.

Rain would rise around me in silver wires. Spindle herds would fold into dark strokes. Groves would vent, and for an instant each white column would resemble the pillar of some vast station whose roof was cloud.

The train would approach by assembling its approach.

When it reached the shallow water, the pool would become an eye.

Because I had waited at its margin, I would become the lid.

My body would lengthen along the bank. The black needle would sink into the tarn. Every borrowed sense would open at once, not to observe the passing anatomy but to complete it. Through me, the plateau would perceive its own movement: mills as teeth, groves as lungs, streams as nerves, the long straight wound in the moss as both route and body.

For that interval, I would know where I was.

Not on the plateau. Not in it.

I would be the narrow act by which it knows that something has passed.

The knowledge would be too large to retain. When the line closed and rain remembered how to fall, most of it would drain from me into the shallow water. The eye would become a pool again. My spare feet would resume their blind walk underground.

But the train would leave something behind: a new pale segment in the amber line, brighter than the rest.

I would rise and follow it—not along the vanished track, but across it, moving at right angles to the direction that had just ceased to exist.

By morning, strangers might find my marks in the wet moss. Six prints at first, then four, then none. The tracks would continue ahead of me toward a solitary stone remnant, where they would wait for my feet to arrive.

If the strangers were wise, they would not follow.

If they did, they might eventually see me beside another shallow tarn: a little colorless animal carrying a map of vanished things, my head full of rain, listening with an ear that had once been a flower.

They might call.

Their echo would reach me first.

And because some part of me still remembered being addressed, I would answer.
