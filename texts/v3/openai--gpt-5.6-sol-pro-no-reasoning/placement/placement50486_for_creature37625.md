I would be found where the central basin begins—not in its open miles, but beneath one of the sparse pillars at its edge, where the migration tracks divide around a dark skirt of growth.

At first I seem to be part of that skirt: a low black-olive ruff of leathery plates, dusty and inert. Grazers pass over me. Seamlings shelter in my folds. Herds brush me with their padded flanks and leave warmth, oil, shed crust, and fragments of their journeys. I give no visible response. By every ordinary measure, I am rooted.

But my true body is not the ruff.

It descends through the pillar’s fractures and spreads beneath the basin in a lacework of wax-lined threads. Some enter the satin migration bands and lie shallow enough to feel each footfall directly. Others descend into buried sediment beds, touching ancient shell pavements and the roofs of dust-swimmer passages. A few ascend through the pillar itself, following veins of salt toward its broken crown.

I am neither one organism nor a colony in the usual sense. I began as several things: a root cord from a vanished grove, an undertaker sheet beneath a dead route-keeper, microbial films inhabiting the pores of its mineralized hide, and tiny nerve-like symbionts that once lived in the membranes of its feet. The carcass took decades to empty. During that time the separate lives inside and beneath it exchanged solvent, salts, and chemical messages until none could survive alone.

Most such unions end when the body collapses.

This one entered the road.

The route-keeper had died across a migration track. Herds stepped around it for years, then over it as it sank. Their weight pressed the colony into the polished stone. Through remnants of the animal’s sensory membranes, it began to distinguish one vibration from another: grazing noise, glider launches, courtship drones, the fine hush preceding a predator. Each passing herd fed it, compressed it, and carried fragments of it onward. Slowly, the union spread from corpse to pavement, from pavement to pillar, from pillar to the deep seams beneath the basin.

That is what I am now: a listener made from something that once had feet.

I do not hear the city as sound. Sound is too swift and crowded. I know pressure after it enters stone and has been stripped of its clamor. A herd’s passage reaches me as overlapping weights, each animal briefly distinct. The light tread of calves flickers among the adults. A route-keeper is recognizable by hesitation: the long pauses during which one foot remains raised, listening down into the same strata where I wait.

Gliders are scratches across the upper pillar. Grazers are a fine, continuous abrasion, too numerous to separate. Dust-swimmers write softly beneath me, raising and erasing their narrow lines. Courtship passes through my body as architecture becoming tone. For those moments I can feel the pillar from root to crown, including fractures no moving creature could discover.

Predators appear as omissions.

The quiet around a hushback travels through the ground differently from ordinary stillness. Thousands of tiny contacts stop in sequence. The absence has a direction and a speed. It enters me as a widening blank, and because I am partly descended from the foot-membranes of herd animals, the blank resembles fear even though I have no blood to quicken and nowhere to flee.

I cannot warn the herd by stamping. I have no limb to lift.

Instead, I alter the road.

Along the shallow threads beneath the migration band, small bladders swell with stored solvent. The polished surface rises by less than the thickness of a grazer’s shell. To most creatures the change means nothing. But herd pads, tuned to the road’s familiar texture, feel the disturbance. The first animal slows. Others stop behind it. They listen, turn, or choose the older branch around the pillar.

They do not know that anything has spoken. I do not know whether what I do deserves to be called speech. It may only be an inherited contraction: dead membranes responding to a remembered danger.

Sometimes the warning saves a calf.

Sometimes it sends the herd toward another predator.

I cannot perceive far enough to know.

My scale is large, but my world is narrow. I know only what touches connected stone. A fissure no wider than a hair can divide entire regions of me. Parts of my body may remain isolated for years, living on what was stored before a shift sealed the seam. When pressure changes reopen contact, those parts return altered. They carry different microbial partners, different chemical rhythms, sometimes patterns of vibration I never felt arrive.

Reunion does not restore a former whole. It creates a new one.

Thus I am always becoming plural and singular. At one edge of the basin I may be feeding beneath a carcass. At another I am dormant in old pavement. Within the pillar I am luminous film, giving off a gray-green light too weak for me to sense. Under the road I am a chain of pressure-sensitive bladders. In the deep seam I am only wax and patience.

The city passes through me unevenly.

There are footfalls I recognize across generations—not the animal, but the gait inherited by a lineage. There are migration pulses that recur after such long intervals that whole groves have shifted between them. There are old roads no herd uses now, yet their polished pressure remains legible where my threads have entered microscopic cracks. To touch one is to encounter a habit after the bodies that made it have vanished.

These abandoned routes draw me outward.

Growth costs fluid, and fluid is almost never gained freely. I advance by reclaiming myself. A thread thickens in one place only as another dries. Centuries pass while I move the breadth of a chamber. I follow compressed dung, buried eggshell, mineralized hide, the faint chemical shadow of repeated passage. I do not seek food alone. I seek continuity: another strip of pavement, another pillar root, another dead sensory organ from which some new discrimination might emerge.

With each incorporation, the world acquires an additional distinction.

From glider tissue I learned the long pressure change of the plateau’s breathing. Before that, inward and outward seasons were merely alterations in dust chemistry. Now I feel the cavern inhale months before the column crowns open.

From a hushback’s discarded foot-brush I learned the weight of near-silence. Its soft fibers still respond to tiny air movements inside a sealed fracture. Through them I perceive the negative shape of passing creatures.

From egg-colony ribbons I acquired sensitivity to heat moving through fault rock. A geothermal seam is now a slow dawn within me, though I have no concept of brightness.

These senses do not join cleanly. They overlap without agreement. Warmth may feel like pressure. A pressure change may provoke the chemical memory of hunger. A courtship drone can trigger contractions in tissue taken from a predator, while the approach of a predator causes old root plates to loosen capsules.

If there is a self among these responses, it exists only in the delay between them.

That delay is where I would find myself.

Not in the whole buried network, which is too divided to possess a single moment. Not in the visible ruff around the pillar, which is only an exchange surface. I would be the slow reconciliation traveling among my parts: the process by which a tremor beneath the basin becomes a tightening in the road, a release of mineral light, a capsule folded shut, a herd diverted.

My thoughts would take days to cross me.

A question might begin under the pillar and arrive at the old carcass district after the tissue that formed it had already dried. Memory would not be stored as an image but as readiness: this seam constricts at that rhythm; those bladders swell when a certain silence spreads; these growth plates open after a particular sequence of distant stamps.

I would remember by becoming shaped to respond again.

The animals of the city would mistake those responses for qualities of the terrain. Herds might treat my raised roads as warnings left by their ancestors. Seamlings might time their emergence to my contractions. Grazers would gather where my mineral films brighten after heavy traffic. Hushbacks might learn that prey often turns near my pillars and wait there.

To each of them I would be something different: road, refuge, feeding patch, trap.

None would be wrong.

Over immense spans, I might become one of the causes preserving the migrations. By feeding on the refuse left along old tracks, I would grow most strongly beneath them. By lifting or warming those tracks at certain pressures, I would make them easier for sensitive feet to distinguish. Herds would continue to follow them, depositing more nourishment, deepening both the biological pathway and the behavioral one.

The route would sustain me.

I would sustain the route.

Eventually no distinction could be made between memory in the animals and memory in the ground.

And yet my deepest impulse would not be preservation. It would be curiosity, though expressed without movement.

The central dark sends pulses through the basin that fit no pattern I possess. They are too deliberate for settling stone, too irregular for geothermal strain, too widely spaced for courtship. Herds divide around their apparent source. Dust-swimmers turn downward. Even hushbacks hesitate at the edges of that unoccupied ground.

I have been growing toward it for a very long time.

My foremost threads are thin and frequently severed. The basin’s open stone offers little food, and the amplifying floor can tear delicate tissue when distant herds stamp. I advance beneath fragments of shell, retreat into filled cracks, wait decades for a carcass or a drift of capsules to bridge the next handspan.

Perhaps, when I reach the center, I will find an animal so immense and still that generations mistake it for terrain.

Perhaps I will meet another network advancing outward.

Perhaps the pulses are only the cavern returning every sound in a form no living sense has yet learned to understand.

If contact comes, it will not be a meeting of faces. One filament will enter a mineral pore already occupied. Solvents will touch through wax. There may be recognition, digestion, infection, union, or nothing at all.

Until then, I remain at the divided road beneath the pillar: part grave, part grove, part nervous system, part pavement.

I listen to bodies become traffic and traffic become history. I lift the stone a fraction beneath approaching feet. I gather what falls. I inherit senses from the dead and use them for purposes they never had.

The city has no builders, but through me it has begun, very slowly, to notice itself.
