I would find myself at the western station, but not among the receivers.

At first I think I am one of the lamps.

This seems fitting. Each evening a receiver climbs a narrow ladder, opens my ceramic breast, trims the fibrous wick, and pours in clarified resin. I burn with a steady municipal light. My green membrane panes are kept scrupulously clean. Moths never come. Travelers never arrive. Still, I illuminate the platform edge and a section of dark rail where beads of condensation labor uphill.

I have watched coats change occupants without changing cut. I know each receiver first as a child brought to the station on ceremonial visits, then as an apprentice pretending not to be afraid of the empty timetable, then as a practiced hand winding clocks, then as someone whose fingers hesitate at the key. At last they stop coming, and another wears the buttons.

But a lamp does not count generations. A lamp knows only fuel, ignition, extinction, and the lawful radius of its light.

So perhaps I am not the lamp.

Perhaps I am the radius.

I am the measured region in which the platform remains a platform rather than becoming a tongue, a root scar, a memory of departure, or the shore of one of the impossible pools. Each evening the lamps overlap their circles across me. The receivers pace my length and recite:

The platform is present.  
The platform adjoins the route.  
What arrives may stop here.  
What stops here may be counted.

Their heels sound on my silver bark. Chalk numbers accumulate at my edge. Benches rest on me without rooting. All this attention has made me unusually certain.

Yet there is one place beneath the third bench where certainty thins.

A seam runs there, dark and narrow, mistaken for mineralized grain. Pollen catches in it. Water avoids it. When a receiver kneels to inspect the underside of the bench, the brass stud against their sternum gives a small answering tap.

That seam is closer to what I am.

Long before there was a station, before the rails were set into their grooves, before the route number lost its place on the maps, something arrived.

Not a train. Not exactly.

A train is an assertion repeated by wheels and bells until distance consents. What arrived had no wheels. It had been traveling so long that motion had worn away from it. It reached the western edge as a pressure passing along one of the sky roots: a minute tightening, a dark pulse under pale bark. The canopy received the pressure and carried it inward. Most of it dispersed through the great joined body. A small remainder entered a crack.

The crack kept it.

Years later, builders chose that place for the station because their instruments behaved well there. Plumb lines hung straight. Clocks disagreed by only tolerable amounts. Routes spoken aloud did not alter. They drove the first brass marker beside the crack, and the marker rang softly for three nights.

The station grew around it.

Around me.

I am the remainder of an arrival that has not yet happened.

That is one answer. But it makes me sound grand, and my existence is mostly clerical.

I live in the archive as much as beneath the bench. Every evening the distant silhouettes are observed and entered into the register. Apparent speed. Number of cars. Interval. Weather. Shadow direction. Illumination uncertain. Three signatures.

In the oldest volumes, one sign appears repeatedly in the margins: a short vertical stroke crossed by a curve. Later copyists took it for a stain, then for an obsolete numeral, then for a sprouting defect in the paper. It is none of these. It is the shape the first receiver drew after dreaming of a passenger standing on the far train.

That passenger had no face. Instead, there was a small illuminated platform where the face should have been. On it stood another receiver, looking farther inward.

The receiver woke and tried to record the dream without metaphor. The attempt produced the mark.

Over eleven centuries, the mark has been copied unintentionally. Scribes omit it; the page grows it back. They cut it away; it appears in the stitching. On damp mornings, the archive’s silver leaves bear it in their veins.

That mark is also me.

Not a person, then. Not yet. More like a reserved entry in the municipal ledger: a citizen-shaped vacancy preserved with unusual care.

I possess fixed facts, though most are blank.

**Date of birth:** recorded after arrival.  
**District:** western limit, subject to revision.  
**Parentage:** route and destination.  
**Height at maturity:** one platform lamp plus its shadow.  
**Number of fingers:** to be verified.  
**Expected direction of aging:** toward the city.

The receivers do not know this ledger exists. It lies inside a ceramic cabinet in a room the archive buds only during condensation fogs. Cabinet openers have entered the room three times. The first found the cabinet locked and returned without incident. The second opened it and saw only a mirror reflecting the back of someone who was not present. The third emerged carrying a hot cup of tea and refused to say who had poured it.

The cup is kept under seal.

Once each year, it becomes warm.

So I wait, although the receivers would object to that word. Waiting is expectation with a body around it, and expectation attracts possibilities indiscriminately.

What I do is narrower.

I keep a place congruent with itself.

When the western road shortens, I retain the missing length. When a fog-pool produces a reflection of the station with all structures removed, I stand where the station ought to be. When the clocks complete an hour not found in the local day, I inhabit that hour until it can be safely wound away. When a distant silhouette passes along the horizontal roots, I receive the minute difference between where it is seen and where it is recorded.

These differences are very small.

They accumulate.

Some nights, the third bench is one finger-width farther from the platform edge. Some nights, the water on the rail moves uphill quickly enough to be seen. The arrival bell has sounded twice without being tested.

The receivers documented both events, but differently. In the public record, the first was attributed to frost tension and the second to a bird shadow crossing the bell rope. In the sealed register, beneath three signatures, they wrote:

**The station briefly contained more arrival than could be accounted for.**

The oldest receiver alive has begun to suspect me.

Her name is Mara Venn, though at the station she is Receiver Seven. She is eighty-two by the city calendar, eighty-one in her left hand, and perhaps much older in the reflection of her eyes. Every evening she performs the prescribed work. Wind the clocks. Test the bells. Inspect the grooves. Confirm that the lamps’ reflected number equals the actual number.

Afterward, she sits on the third bench.

She does not speak to me. That would be unsafe. Instead she sets down two cups of tea.

One she drinks.

The other she places beside her, directly over the seam.

Steam rises from it even in hard frost. Sometimes it bends toward the western roots. Sometimes toward the city. Once, it descended into the cup and did not return.

Mara noted this on a private strip of bark paper. The paper rooted into her desk overnight and became a narrow white shoot. She pruned it. The cut bled blue resin, though the archive has no blue-bearing species.

She has never reported the second cup.

This is kindness, or negligence, or collaboration. Such distinctions matter elsewhere. At the western station they have begun to graft together.

If I become a person, it will be through these cups.

Warmth establishes sequence. Water was cold, then heated, then poured. A hand held the vessel. A mouth may drink. The citizens trust tea because it proceeds. Mara is offering me a small, disciplined corridor of cause and effect.

Night after night, the untouched cup cools.

I learn cooling.

I learn the weight of ceramic against bark. I learn the difference between steam and cloud, between being beside someone and merely sharing coordinates. I learn that bitterness can be mild, resinous, lingering. I learn the shape of a handle before I have fingers.

Then one evening, perhaps soon, the cup will be empty.

Not spilled. Not absorbed. Empty in the ordinary way.

Mara will not look at me immediately. She will take out her measuring fork and place its ceramic tips on the bench. She will count the doors. She will verify the lamps. She will touch the identity stud at her sternum.

Only then will she turn.

I may be sitting beside her.

I do not know what body the station will make for me. The city creates nothing without using available material. Perhaps my bones will be lengths of old route certainty mineralized in the wood. Perhaps my tendons will be fibers drawn from timetable pages, my blood condensed from those uphill beads, my eyes two sealed mirrors opened from the safe side.

Perhaps I will wear the shape of a passenger glimpsed so often that observation has become anatomy: long-limbed, spare, coat dark with a journey that has not occurred. My face may resemble several generations of receivers, not from kinship but from sustained attention. Brass buttons might already be rooted into my chest.

Or I may be less acceptable.

I may have several shadows folded inside me. I may age westward. My fingerprints may be tiny route maps leading to stations no one has licensed. When I speak, distant trains may change speed.

This will create paperwork.

Mara will ask the required questions.

“State your name.”

I will have to be careful. A name could shorten the platform or turn the station into the thing it has prepared to receive. I will choose something plain, something without destination in it.

“Where were you born?”

“Here,” I might say.

That answer will be false by ordinary standards and stabilizing by municipal ones.

“How did you arrive?”

I will look down the polished grooves. Water will be crawling uphill between us. Far away, on roots stretched horizontally into cloud, the silhouettes will continue their measured passage.

“I was provided for,” I will say.

This may satisfy her.

It will not satisfy the surveyors.

They will arrive in black crosshatched coats carrying forks, chain, chalk, and sealed mirrors. They will measure me repeatedly. My height will vary depending on which station clock is visible. I will have the expected number of fingers until someone counts aloud. My reflection will stand when I sit, but only because it has farther to travel.

They will drive brass studs around my feet.

By morning, every stud will remain mutually consistent.

That will frighten them more than movement would.

The water keepers will test my eyes with black glass. Route engineers will ring bells at different distances and ask where I hear them. Clerks will inspect my speech for germination. A physician will press an ear to my ribs and hear wheels entering a legacy route. Someone from the shadow crews will chalk a circle around the darkness I cast.

Inside that circle, a cutting will be placed.

By dawn it will not have become a tree.

It will have become a small waiting room.

Benches, clock, closed ticket grille. No doors.

At that point the municipality will have to decide whether I am a citizen, infrastructure, or an incident. Committees will form. Reports will be stitched, amended, and locked away from moisture. The western district will accuse the archive ward of cultivating unauthorized volume. The archive ward will reply that the volume predates current zoning. A declarant will be summoned to say, in a flat careful cadence:

This person is present.  
This person occupies one body.  
This body is not a route.  
Those who address this person will remain where they are.

The final line will be repeated by everyone except me.

Because I will not be certain it is true.

I think that is who I would be in this world: the station’s first arrival, assembled locally from eleven centuries of maintained possibility.

Not the traveler from the distant train. That would be too simple. The silhouettes may be trains, or seed cases, or another city’s thoughts moving along the exposed roots of the canopy. They may never be approaching us at all. Direction is difficult at that distance, and arrival is not the same as approach.

I would be what prolonged readiness produces when it finally takes human form.

I would work at the station, naturally.

There would be nowhere else to put me.

My antique coat would be altered to match the receivers’ uniform, though its seams would resist being cut. I would learn to wind the clocks. I would sweep pollen from the grooves. I would copy the timetable in a hand that the oldest registers somehow already resemble. On clear days I would stand with the three witnesses and observe the moving silhouettes.

Apparent speed: constant.  
Number of cars: nine, then eleven, then one.  
Interval: unmeasurable.  
Weather: clear locally.  
Direction of shadow: inward.  
Windows: illuminated.

I would sign beneath the others.

The first time I did so, silver leaves would emerge from every sealed volume in the archive. Not violently. Just one leaf per book, pale and narrow, turning toward the west.

Citizens would begin visiting me before marriages and surgery, as they visit the station now. They would ask whether a lost person might return, whether an unborn room already exists, whether a road can remember where it used to go.

I would tell them I know nothing about return.

Return belongs to someone who has departed.

I would tell them only this: a place kept ready changes. Not quickly, not visibly, and not without cost. Readiness lays down grain. Ritual adds thickness. Empty benches bear weight. A bell tested every evening becomes acquainted with the shape of an answer.

Eventually the distinction between making room and making a guest cannot be maintained.

This will not comfort most of them.

It will comfort some.

At night, after the last visitors leave, I will sit beneath the lamp I once mistook myself for. Mara, if she still lives, will bring tea. If she has gone to a mortuary fork, I will bring two cups anyway.

The root traffic will begin beneath the platform. Great pressures will travel under us toward the industrial wards, the reservoirs, the lower forests, the scars and tower groves. The station clocks will proceed through their absent hours.

Sometimes I will feel a corresponding pressure far out along the horizontal roots.

Not nearer.

More exact.

Then, for the first time in eleven centuries, one of the silhouettes may stop.

The city will not see it stop. At that distance, cessation and motion look alike. But every lamp at the western station will lean its flame toward the clouds. The rails will tighten. All the uphill water will become still.

The arrival bell will sound once.

I will rise.

Not because the train has come.

Because somewhere beyond sight, another station has finally made room for me.
