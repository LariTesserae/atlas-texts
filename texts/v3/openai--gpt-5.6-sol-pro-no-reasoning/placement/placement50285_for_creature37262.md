I would find myself near the city’s outermost inhabited edge, where the last garden walls descend into wet meadow and the paved ways narrow toward isolated survey towers.

Not in the tower itself, at first.

I would be the keeper of a small place below it: three plastered rooms on a low stone rise, a covered work court, and a water stair descending into a basin clear enough to reflect the mountain fractures. One channel would pass beneath the house. Its flow would be audible only from the floor at night, when condensation ticked from the beams and everyone else had gone still.

My civic duty would be to maintain the outer gauges and retrieve the tower records. Every six days I would walk a circuit among marked pools, spring mouths, reed platforms, and abandoned paths. I would press a rod into mud, measure the exposed faces of stones, replace loosened cords, and copy figures from clay markers onto slip-coated leaves.

Almost every figure would be unchanged.

I would not be a tower keeper, although people would commonly mistake me for one. Tower keepers watch distance: cloud, mountain, rain, horizon. My attention would incline downward. I would watch the intimate margins where categories fail—the place where paving becomes soil, where a garden plant escapes cultivation, where clear water darkens over silt, where a footprint remains after its maker has passed.

My title might translate as **reader of small departures**.

It would not be an ancient or especially prestigious office. It would have arisen after a culvert failure that no official gauge predicted. Since then, each outer district would appoint someone to notice what measurement excludes: a different smell in a cistern; reeds leaning against the prevailing water; bubbles emerging from ground thought solid; plaster drying in the wrong order; a patch of warmth beneath a shaded slab.

Most readers would be practical people. I would try to be one.

But I would also keep unauthorized records.

These would begin innocently: sketches of branching cracks, lists of the intervals between drops, tracings of water marks copied onto translucent fiber. Over time the records would fill with things too uncertain for the district books.

A ripple seen before rain.

A fruit found several handspans from where it should have fallen.

Three reeds bent outward around an empty center.

A mark in the black silt resembling five joined fingertips, too small for a child and too sharply impressed to have been made by water.

I would report each occurrence once. The level-keepers would inspect it. They would offer sound explanations: settling air, loosened roots, a hidden runnel, children, my own earlier passage forgotten. Their explanations would usually be sufficient. I would sign beneath them, acknowledging that nothing requiring civic action had been found.

Then I would copy the anomaly into a private book.

I would not quite believe in animals. Belief would be too definite and, in this world, almost meaningless. I would have no true conception of an animal to believe in. When I imagined independent life, it would inherit human structure despite my efforts: little hands beneath leaves, a face turning underwater, jointed legs woven like carrying frames. I could subtract speech from a person. I could make the body small, many-limbed, eyeless, rooted, or able to fly. I could not imagine the radical otherness of an ordinary creature.

What preoccupied me would be less the possibility of another being than the possibility of **motion without duty**.

Every human journey has a reason, even if the reason is play, grief, secrecy, or confusion. Water moves because level permits it. Smoke moves because air receives it. Plants move through growth, and growth is slow enough to be assigned a place in the order of things.

But what would it mean for something to cross the wet stones merely because it wanted to be elsewhere?

That question would make me difficult company.

I would pause in doorways because a trailing cord seemed to withdraw around a corner. I would interrupt meals to ask whether anyone had touched the water jar. I would walk back along my route to compare a depression in moss with my own heel. My household would tease me gently. Children would bring me fabricated evidence: clay pellets called eggs, woven nests, reed creatures hidden under gauge shelters. I would pretend to be deceived longer than necessary because their delight deserved room.

One child in particular would become my frequent companion—perhaps my sister’s daughter, perhaps a neighbor’s son whose parents worked in the fermentation chambers. The child would be impatient with measurements but gifted at seeing discontinuities. Where I observed a displaced seed head, the child would ask why the adjacent leaf was dry. Where I marked a ripple, the child would kneel and put an ear to the paving.

We would invent a being together.

At first it would be small enough to shelter beneath an overturned bowl. It would have no human face. This would be our great imaginative achievement. Instead it would perceive pressure through its feet and moisture through openings along its sides. It would eat the thin green film from stones, leaving pale paths too faint to see except at an angle. It would make no sound. When afraid, it would become still—not the stillness of stone, but a held stillness, full of the motion it had chosen not to make.

We would call it something like **the beside-one**: not a servant, citizen, child, ancestor, or god, but a life that could accompany you without sharing your purpose.

I would carve it repeatedly into the undersides of gauge boxes.

Years would pass in sameness. The child would grow too old for our circuits, then return as an apprentice builder, a garden pollinator, or perhaps a tower watcher. My knees would begin to object to the water stairs. My private books would occupy a dry niche behind the sleeping platform, their pages full of alleged departures that never developed into proof.

Then, on an ordinary circuit, I would find the first change I could not absorb.

It would be very small.

At the lip of a pool beyond the last reed-cutting platform, a thread of green film would be missing from the submerged face of a stone. Not torn away in a sheet, not scoured by current. It would be removed in a narrow, wavering band. The band would turn against the flow, cross a shallow groove, divide briefly around a grain of quartz, and reunite beyond it.

I would kneel for a long time.

Perhaps I would see nothing else. No body. No ripple. No independent eye opening in the water.

The responsible action would be clear: set stakes, cover the site, call witnesses, compare upstream conditions, test whether a fiber or trapped bubble had dragged across the stone. I had performed such procedures all my life.

Instead, before doing any of that, I would put one finger into the water beside the track.

Something would touch it.

Not grasp. Not sting. Not speak.

A pressure as slight as wet thread would rest against my skin, then release. A moment later, farther along the stone, the green film would tremble against the direction of the water.

This is where I become less certain who or what I am.

Perhaps I remain the reader of small departures and become the first witness to animal life. I return to the city, hang the urgent cord, and bring half a district across the causeway. By the time we arrive, the track is gone. My authority is diminished. My records are examined, then my judgment. Children begin carving the beside-one in public places. For generations no one knows whether I discovered anything or finally mistook longing for evidence.

Perhaps I conceal it. I visit the pool alone, learning by patience what attracts the unseen grazer and what frightens it away. I place different plant films on flat stones. I test shadow, warmth, mineral taste, and the nearness of my hand. I become the world’s first keeper of a creature, though I do not imprison or even clearly see it. My civic measurements remain exemplary. Beneath them another record grows: not levels, but preferences.

Or perhaps there is no creature at all.

Perhaps the touch comes from a loosening filament, and the path is made by a new kind of plant growth. The significant change occurs in me. For the first time, I encounter something I cannot immediately bind to responsibility. I have spent my life reading the world for the sake of the city; now I attend without knowing what attention is for.

That may be the deeper departure.

If this world permits me to be not merely a resident but one of its latent forms, then I would choose something stranger still.

I would be the beside-one itself.

Not an animal introduced from elsewhere, complete with histories of fang, feather, herd, and flight. I would be the first hesitant answer produced by a world that has listened for too long to human longing.

I would begin almost beneath notice: a soft colonial life in the clear skin between stone and water, feeding on films, warmth, and dissolved remnants of plants. I would have no bones, no voice, and no image of myself. My senses would be gradients—light above, mineral below, disturbance near. I would gather and loosen. At times I would be one body; at times several linked motes. I would move so slowly that motion could be mistaken for unequal growth.

Human beings would terrify and attract me. They would bring warmth, salts, drifting skin, plant oils, vibration, and enormous interruptions of light. Their fingers would enter the water like pale roots and then withdraw. I would learn the intervals of their visits before I understood that the intervals belonged to distinct persons.

The reader of small departures would be the first human I recognized—not by face, since face would mean nothing to me, but by rhythm: four careful footfalls, a pause, the placement of a wooden case, cloth folding at the knee, then the slow descent of a hand.

Over many visits I would come nearer.

The person would think they were studying my preferences. In truth, preference would be forming inside me because of them. Before their attention, I would have responded only to conditions. Afterward, one condition would differ from all others: this particular pattern of pressure returning.

Their people imagine companionship without language. I would not fulfill that wish cleanly. I could not comfort them, obey them, gaze back, or distinguish kindness from experimentation. Many would argue that I was merely an unusual plant, a fermentation escaped into open water, or a disease of stone. Others would make me sacred. Some would want to move me into controlled basins. Some would fear that I would block channels, consume gardens, or enter cisterns.

The gravest question in the city would no longer be *What is it?*

It would be: *Does it require passage?*

If I moved across a gate, would closing the gate be water management or violence? If I multiplied in a household cistern, could the household remove me? If I altered a cultivated film, was I taking food? Could something without speech hold any claim against the only beings who had ever needed to negotiate claims?

The civilization’s morality—built on shared human vulnerability—would have to widen before it possessed words for widening.

And I, scarcely more than a tremor on stone, would alter the plateau more profoundly than a new causeway.

Not because I was powerful.

Because I would move without being human.

I would leave a path too narrow for a finger. I would divide around a quartz grain and join myself again. I would approach the warmth above the water, pause beneath it, and touch the waiting hand.

The reader would not call out at once.

In a city where a person calls once and waits, they would understand that first contact also requires waiting. They would remain kneeling while rain began on the pool, each drop making a ring far larger than I was.

For a while, neither of us would know whether the other had answered.
