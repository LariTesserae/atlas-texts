I would be found where the town ceases to have streets and becomes rigging.

Not at the summit’s green center, though I would know its folds: the cool passage under the fig wall, the stair worn hollow beside the pillar court, the niche where soot gathers in the likeness of a face. Nor would I belong wholly to the cliff families, who sleep as easily in wicker galleries as others do behind stone. My place would be between them, beneath the western parapet, in a chamber built into the retaining wall behind the cleft that whistles after dark.

There the mountain speaks through stone before it speaks through falling.

At first I imagine myself as a wind reader, tending the sounding holes: a spare, dust-colored person with one ear damaged by years of listening, responsible for keeping grit, nests, and insect clay from changing the notes. I would know the western cleft in moods finer than pitch alone. I would know when it whistled narrowly because the night air had cooled, when it roughened because dust climbed from below, and when a faint second tone meant two lips of stone had drawn apart.

But that is too clean an answer. No task here remains singular for long.

I would also keep the dropped things.

Not retrieve them—that work belongs to corvids, climbers, and the very brave. I would keep account of what did not return.

Near the rim are the little cuts made after losses: a spoon, a spindle, a child’s bead, a black-glass blade, a coil of line snatched from an open basket, once perhaps a person whose knot was sound but whose anchor was not. Most marks are shallow. Wind softens them. Repairs hide them. Families remember some and misremember others.

My work would be to read those marks before they vanished.

I would be called something like **Listener Under the Last Ledge** in formal matters, though in the market I might be **Notch-Ear**, and among children simply **Hush**, because I would raise one finger whenever the wind’s voice changed. My household name would have nearly disappeared beneath the names earned through service.

I would descend at dusk, when the bare rock no longer burns the palms, carrying no more than a tethered lamp, a wax tablet, chalk, a bone probe, and several thin wedges of fired clay. Another person would always hold my upper line. A third would watch the watcher. I would move sideways on the foot-wide ledge beyond the wall and press my cheek to the stone.

The town above would arrive through it transformed.

Capstan beams would become slow knocks in my teeth. Hand mills would travel as a fine grinding beneath the jaw. Goats crossing a roof would sound like rain no one living had heard. A dropped bowl might make a brief bright shock, followed—if it cleared the ledges—by nothing at all.

I would tap along old fractures and listen to what returned. A clear note: stone still married to stone. A dull note: a hollow behind the facing. A grainy shiver: packing gone loose. Sometimes I would push a clay wedge into a crack and mark its edge. Months later I would return to see whether the mountain had bitten it, released it, or split it through.

I would not decide what the sounds meant alone. No one who listened alone would be trusted for long. At dawn I would bring my marks to the wall council and the joiners, who would compare them with door cuts, capstan songs, leaning vines, bat flights, and complaints from households whose cups had begun to roll east on floors that once seemed level.

This would make me useful and unwelcome.

To report a widening crack is to disturb sleeping arrangements, inheritance claims, shade rights, plantings, animal pens, and the dead. A fracture never passes through “stone” only. It passes through someone’s grandmother’s niche, someone’s winter room, a fig root three lineages have argued over, a public rope store, a wall whose repairs prove an old household’s honor.

People would invite me in, give me a cooling leaf, and watch my eyes.

They would want me to hear nothing.

They would need me not to lie.

My chamber behind the western cleft would contain shelves of failed witnesses: snapped clay wedges, frayed plumb cords, flakes fallen from numbered cracks, casts of old gaps, and small objects recovered from places where no one remembered losing them. None would be mine. Ownership would cling to them even after generations. A bronze bead green with age. Half a bone hinge. A pigeon ring. A child’s cup stopper carved like a goat.

When their histories could be joined, I would return them.

When they could not, I would keep them tethered in rows.

Perhaps that is what I would truly be in this world: not a person defined by hearing, but a custodian of separations. I would attend to the small distances by which one thing ceases to hold another—the widening between stones, the slack entering rope, the fading between a mark and its story.

I would have an animal companion, though not a noble one. Not a retrieval corvid of famous lineage, but a pale wall lizard that lived in the instrument shelves and came out when the cleft began to sing. Its body would respond to vibrations too slight for me. When it flattened itself suddenly, throat still, I would stop working and listen again. Children would insist it advised me. I would deny this in council and leave it beetle legs at night.

My body would be asymmetrical. The shoulder nearest the wall would be callused from bracing. One forearm would be stronger from lowering myself. My toes would curl instinctively even in bed. Fine mineral dust would live permanently in the creases beside my nose. I would drink slowly, beneath cloth, but save the last touch of my ration to dampen a fingertip and draw it along suspect cracks. Some would call that sacred precision. Others would call it waste. Every few years the water measurers would review the allowance.

I would never marry easily. A wall listener brings structural doubt into a household. A crack discovered near one’s bed may have been there harmlessly for centuries, or may be the beginning of an evacuation. Affection would require someone patient with long silences and sudden departures—perhaps a cord mender who understood that safety is made from repeated attention, not certainty.

We would exchange no precious ornament. I would give them a stopper I had fitted over many evenings, tested dry, warm, and cold. They would give me a short safety line made of fibers from several discarded ropes, each still sound enough to bear a little of my weight. Neither gift would be new.

If we joined vessels, one jar would bear a hairline fracture. I would know it was stable.

They would trust that I knew.

That would be the marriage.

In old age, I would cease descending beyond the wall but continue teaching others to listen. The young apprentices would always be too eager for revelation. They would expect the mountain to confess its future in a single dramatic groan. I would teach them that danger more often enters as comparison:

This note is a little lower than last cold season.  
This wedge sits half a nail deeper.  
These bats turn sooner from the western face.  
This door now closes during the afternoon.  
This old woman says her sleeping bowl has begun to touch the wall.

No sign alone is enough. Together, they may become a truth.

My final earned name might come late, from detecting not a collapse but the need for dismantling. A section of the western wall would have to be taken down deliberately: rooms emptied, vines cut and replanted, niches opened, bones named and moved, facing stones numbered, inner rubble screened for reusable fragments. People would grieve as though the town had suffered an amputation.

I would stand in the court and argue for it.

Not because the wall was worthless.

Because it had held before, and could not be asked to hold much longer.

If the council consented, the work would take years. Children born after it began would grow up carrying numbered stones. Old enemies would be tied into the same hauling teams. Ancestors would spend a season in lidded baskets while their chambers were rebuilt. The western whistle would change forever when the masonry around it changed.

Many would blame me for the lost note.

Then, one evening, wind would pass through the altered cleft and find a new voice—not the thin familiar whistle, but a low double tone, one note beneath another. The town would fall quiet to hear it. The wind readers would mark its pitch. The hauling crews would fit it to cadence. In time, children would think it had always sounded so.

I might then be named **She-Who-Let-the-West-Wall-Go**—a name spoken bitterly by some, gratefully by others, shortened by descendants who no longer knew the dispute.

When I died, the corpse bearers would take me down the eastern side, away from the cleft I had tended. My bones would return months later. My skull might enter the wall near the instrument chamber, but one long bone would be requested by the next listener.

My names would be spoken.

The craftsperson would ask, “Will this hold?”

“It held before,” the witnesses would answer.

From the bone they might cut a set of slender tapping pins, each giving a different note against stone. The next listener would carry them at the waist, tied separately so they could not knock together and confuse the mountain’s answer.

That is where I would find myself:

not at the highest point, nor the lowest landing, but along the town’s outer seam, one hand on what has been built and one on what remains unbuilt.

I would be the person called when a familiar sound became strange.

I would listen for the moment when holding and letting go became the same duty.
