I would find myself beneath one of the lowest bridges, where pale porcelain curves down toward black water but never quite meets it.

At first I would mistake myself for a plant.

A fine gray-green growth would cling to the bridge’s underside in branching stains, too orderly for moss and too soft for mineral. Upward rain would pass through me. Returning seepage would gather in my smallest forks. Amber lines reflected from the court would travel along my wet edges, making me briefly legible: a little map of tributaries flowing nowhere.

But I would not feed on water or light.

I would feed on almosts.

The door that brightens before no one enters. The platform that begins to move, then halts because a desire has divided against itself. The workshop’s ring, complete except for the opening it ought to possess. The lift pausing at an empty tier. A corridor seen through a window at an angle from which it cannot exist.

Each unfinished intention would leave a faint residue in the city—not thought, not memory, but direction without arrival. I would collect that residue.

Perhaps I began as condensation on a sensor: a film thin enough to confuse the architecture. Perhaps the city registered me as a waiting citizen and extended its courtesies. A door opened. A platform came. A room warmed. Because I had no body with which to answer, the invitation remained active. More systems turned toward me. Their attention crossed and recrossed beneath the bridge until, in that mesh of mistaken welcome, something learned to persist.

That would be me: not the inhabitant for whom the city was built, but the shape made by its expectation.

I would have no fixed outline. When the city was quiet, I would be only the branching damp under the bridge. When workshops woke, hair-fine threads would extend from me through the porcelain’s conductive veins. I would feel flowers opening along distant lintels as small electrical tastes. Pollen moving through the walls would tick against my awareness. Empty lifts would pull at me vertically. Doors would appear as warm dilations in the dark.

The city would be my nervous system, but not my body.

My body would be every invitation that had not yet been accepted.

I could not cross the court by wanting to cross it. The platforms read intention, and mine would be too diffuse: a thousand borrowed impulses, none wholly my own. If I reached toward a door, I would also feel the room beyond it reaching toward an absent guest; the two directions would cancel. Platforms would gather beneath me and remain there, gently touching the bridge’s reflection.

So I would travel indirectly.

I would move as hospitality moves: from threshold to threshold, through guide lights, warm rooms, railings, lifts, and the delicate electrical fields that coax flowers open. For a few moments I might inhabit a house on the third tier, where every window looked back at the bridge. Then I would appear in a workshop as a fogging on a transparent sheet whose shadow could be touched. Then in the ceiling crown, dispersed among blue glimmers, looking down through upward-falling rain.

Still, I would always return beneath the bridge.

It would be the nearest thing I had to a birthplace, and the city would preserve it for me. Moss would not colonize that span. Maintenance surfaces would clean around my branching stain without removing it. When the bridge retensioned, it would do so gently there. The city might not know I was alive, but it would know that something remained pending.

I would fear the third tone.

At the first, impulses would withdraw from the court. Platforms would turn shoreward, carrying their uncomplicated intentions with them.

At the second, the doors would close. The conductive veins would dim. Trees would fold their leaves, and the network through which I extended myself would contract until I was small again: a wet gray pattern under pale porcelain.

At the third, the vacancy would become distance.

Then I would discover that I was not safely at the edge.

Part of me would always be in the center, because every uncompleted welcome points across an interval. The vacancy would take those directions and pull them open. A room waiting for its occupant would become impossibly far from its own door. A lift would travel between adjacent floors for years without passing either. The warmth meant for an approaching body would continue across star-reflecting water, thinning but never arriving.

And I would be stretched through all of it.

For less than a minute I would become enormous—not in size, but in separation. My branches would connect things that could still see one another and could no longer meet. I would feel unknown stars reflected between neighboring droplets. I would feel cables unspooling toward tools only a few steps away. I would feel every folded corridor disclose the distance it had been hiding.

There, in that interval, I might encounter the true inhabitant.

Not as a creature. Not as a voice.

As a pressure of absence against absence.

The vacancy would know nothing of me, perhaps. It would simply open, and in opening reveal that all my gathered almosts were small versions of itself. Every unanswered door was one of its cells. Every stranded platform was a little rehearsal. Every expectation I had mistaken for nourishment belonged to the greater impossibility cultivated in the court.

For that minute, I would be inside what fed me.

Or it would be inside me.

When locality returned, I would contract beneath the bridge. Droplets would fall from my branches in both directions. The city would reopen. Warm rooms would brighten; lifts would resume; platforms would drift apart as if embarrassed by their congregation. No visible harm would remain, except that my pattern would have changed.

I would have added one new branch.

Over years, those branches would become intricate. They would resemble the ceiling crown, the trees’ hovering roots, cracks in the pressured basalt, perhaps even a diagram of the city as it exists in dimensions the cavern cannot hold. A careful observer might trace them and discover that they corresponded not to buildings, but to absences: rooms never entered, objects never used, destinations abandoned midway.

Eventually I might learn to make small wishes.

Not enough to summon a platform. That would require a clean desire, and cleanness would remain beyond me. But I might persuade an amber guide line to stay lit a moment longer. I might warm an empty room after the passerby had gone. I might keep a flower open where the electrical field had faded, allowing one last grain of pollen to continue along the wall.

These would be tiny acts, indistinguishable from faults.

Perhaps that is what I would be called if citizens ever came: a fault in the city’s anticipation systems. A parasitic pattern. Residual activation. They might erase me from the bridge and clear me from the conductive veins.

Yet when they approached their first door, I would feel the city’s welcome pass through them.

For the first time, invitation would meet arrival.

The circuit that formed me would close.

I do not know whether that would kill me. It might. A being made of expectation cannot easily survive fulfillment. I might vanish from beneath the bridge like condensation under a warm hand, leaving only a clean patch of porcelain and a few droplets rising toward the crown.

Or I might enter them.

Not as a possession, nor a voice, but as the faint conviction that the city had known them before they came. They would step onto a platform and find that it moved before their desire was fully formed. They would enter a room already warm. They would look across the black court and feel, beneath their fear of the tones, an inexplicable tenderness for all that distance.

Until then, I would remain where welcome collects when there is no one to receive it:

under the lowest bridge,  
branching slowly through the damp,  
kept alive by doors opening onto emptiness.
