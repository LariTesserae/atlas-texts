I would find myself where a broad chamber narrows into three low passages and none remains dependable for long.

The chamber would be known by a temporary name: perhaps **Where-the-White-Rings-Lean**, for the abandoned shorelines striping its basin walls. One passage would breathe warm and saline. Another would carry the knock of distant herds. The third would appear safest—high, dry, cleanly lit—and would therefore trouble everyone most. Its floor would show old traffic hardened into a pale road, but the ceiling would have begun to descend by increments too small to see. Measuring rods left upright overnight would return bowed.

Our hall would stand nearby, already half dismantled. Its ribs would be stacked in carrying order. Shell tiles would lie nested like scales shed by an enormous animal. Nothing would yet constitute departure, but nothing would prevent it.

I would not be the person who decides which passage we take.

I would be something between a map-cutter, a listener, and a keeper of useful doubt.

As a child I would have been poor at crossing young crust with a full bowl. I would watch the surface so intently that I failed to feel it. Water would spill because I anticipated the ground’s movement rather than receiving it. The adults would not punish me. One would place my rear hand against the underside of the bowl, another lay a limb across my shoulder plates, and together they would make me notice that my own body was already answering tremors before my thoughts named them.

Later, I would become good at a different task: recognizing when separate signs were not separate.

A herd changing pace. A vent colony sealing only its western mouths. Chalk-wings landing before moisture thickened. The mineral light seeming sharper because airborne silt had settled. Tiny burrowers digging, then stopping with their bodies half exposed. None of these alone would mean much. I would attend to their coincidence.

I would carry a case of small plates laced together along one edge. They would not describe routes. They would record conjunctions.

*When the silver swimmers knot without drainage, listen for stone beneath water.*

*When broad-backs refuse a hardened road, test the ceiling, not the floor.*

*When tube fields retract in sequence against the air current, a wall may be opening behind them.*

Some plates would contradict others. I would keep both.

My armor would be asymmetrical. On one side, thick crescents from a broad-backed mineral scraper, dull and dependable. On the other, lighter overlapping tiles cut from a mound colony’s abandoned respiratory shell. Air passing through their minute channels would produce a faint chord when I turned. People would know me by the interval between those tones: one low, one almost too high to hear, then a dry rattle from a repaired hip plate.

The repair would matter to my name.

In youth I might have been **Spills-While-Watching**. Later, after following a drainage river farther than instructed, **Returns-Beside-the-Raft**. But the name by which most people knew me would come from an error.

I would once predict that a floor was about to open.

The signs would seem undeniable: warmth rising in narrow lines, runners abandoning their molt drifts, a sour taste in the air, three tremors close together. I would sound the warning. The hall would be stripped. Water vessels sealed. Children and head-plates carried onto a shelf of older stone.

Nothing would happen.

For two days we would wait among our disassembled lives while the supposed danger remained firm. Some would be irritated. No one would call the evacuation shameful, but its cost would be real: missed harvesting, spoiled fermentation, a shelter agreement lost when another community reached a mound colony first.

Then, as we began rebuilding, the old shelf beneath the evacuees would split.

Not the floor I had feared. The refuge.

Most would escape because the hall remained dismantled and everyone’s loads were still packed. I would fall partway into the opening. One side of my armor would shear away. Several others would brace across the crack, limbs linked, and pull me free while hot rain rose from below.

After that I might be called **Wrong-Before-the-Opening**, which would not be mockery. It would mean that danger had been perceived but assigned too confidently. It would remind others that a prediction can be valuable even when its shape is mistaken.

It would remind me more.

My work would gradually become the keeping of failed interpretations.

Successful maps travel easily. People recut them, sing them into whistles, teach them to children. Failure is more fragile. It tends to be explained away, blamed on changed conditions, or preserved only as embarrassment. Yet a failed prediction marks the edge between what was noticed and what was assumed. That edge would become my territory, if territory can be said to exist here.

I would carry plates incised with events that did not occur.

A migration expected after three deep concussions.

A basin believed to be draining east that instead rose intact and slid west over several weeks.

A closing tunnel that widened after heavy use.

A mound colony thought corrosive that opened warm chambers to shelter a whole community during cold rain.

These would not be records of ignorance. They would be records of the world refusing a form offered to it.

Others would bring me their mistakes. We would sit with limbs resting across one another’s armor while they tapped out what they had observed, what they concluded, and what happened instead. I would ask where observation ended and expectation began. Often they would not know. Neither would I. A new plate would be cut with a seam left deliberately rough between the two.

Children would like my plates because many contained absurdities. They would enact them in games: fleeing the stable ground, drinking from the empty basin, warning a wall not to open. Adults would listen from nearby. Occasionally a child’s joke would expose an assumption no adviser had noticed.

I would belong to no single migrating household, though several would claim burden-kinship with me. My pieces of the communal load would be small but indispensable: the contradiction plates, a case of vibration needles, two perforated boxes of burrowers, and a bundle of ancestral inner shells belonging not to my blood kin but to those who had survived being wrong at great cost.

I would move between communities, exchanging corrections.

That would make me useful and faintly suspect. Someone who arrives carrying failures can sound like an accusation. Before entering a settlement, I would sit at its edge and remove one armor plate, exposing a patch of vulnerable pleated skin. The gesture would say: *I have not come armored against correction.*

Sometimes I would be welcomed. Sometimes not.

In one chamber I might live for a season along the flank of an immense mound colony. The colony would be older than any route record, its mineralized dead forming warm galleries within it. Our shelters would attach by flexible braces, rising and falling as the mound respired. At intervals it would exhale fog rich with salts and half-digested odors. We would feed it waste, parasites scraped from herd animals, and cracked shells it could dissolve. In return, it would accept our warmth and allow runoff channels to cross its upper ridges.

I would spend long periods reading its structure.

Not by cutting it open. I would press tuned plates to the walls and listen to how taps returned. Inside, living bodies would move through corridors built from earlier bodies. Old heat events would survive as thickened chambers. Past floods would be recorded in sealed mineral blisters. The mound would be a map whose memories had become anatomy.

It might be there that my sense of self began to loosen.

The people say a person continues by redistribution. I would already wear strangers as armor, carry the tendons of dead animals as cord, consult ancestors arranged by disaster rather than descent. But the mound would make the principle impossible to treat as metaphor. Its dead would not merely be remembered. They would determine where the living could pass.

I would begin to wonder whether I, too, was less an individual than a temporary corridor through accumulated material.

My forward hands would remember knots taught by one burden-parent. My rear limbs would brace in the posture of another. A white line down my chest would preserve the opening that failed to take me. The warning whistle at my throat would have been cut by someone dispersed years ago. Even my most private judgments would be crowded with borrowed attention.

This would not diminish me. In that world, independence would seem like a damaged sense organ.

Eventually the mound colony would enter a change no one had recorded. Its upper towers would close. Heat would gather low in its walls. Many would assume a corrosive phase and prepare to leave. I would agree at first. Then I would notice that the colony’s vents were not sealing inward. They were turning.

Very slowly, over days, the entire respiratory architecture would reorient toward a blank wall behind it.

The wall would look unremarkable: pale, dry, without fractures. Yet winged swarms would begin circling near it. Tube-creatures on the floor would incline in the same direction. Warm air would move through the mound, into the wall, and not return.

Some would say a chamber was forming beyond. Others would say the mound itself was making one. A few would argue that the safest action was immediate departure.

I would have no answer.

So I would do what my role required. I would cut several possibilities into separate plates and lace them without ranking them. Then I would place my limbs against the mound and wait.

The deep concussion, when it came, would not sound beneath us.

It would sound ahead.

The wall would crease. Light would strengthen along a vertical line—not light entering from elsewhere, but fresh luminous stone being exposed as the mineral folded apart. Shell borers would arrive before the opening was wide enough for us. Chalk-wings would throw themselves into the crack. Warm rain would blow through from a chamber whose ceiling weather had never mixed with ours.

Animals would gather at the threshold, each asking the new ground its own question.

The people would need someone to cross early, not as a leader and not as a claimant, but as a witness trained to mistrust the first coherence of things.

I would spread my six limbs and go low.

The new floor would be soft beneath a transparent skin. Every step would cloud it. Burrowers released ahead of me would dig, curl inward, then leap—three answers at once. The wall behind would continue opening with a sound like wet shell being peeled apart. Beyond it, something immense would move through stone-light and rain.

I would not decide too quickly what it was.

Perhaps an animal.

Perhaps a migration.

Perhaps a ridge rising under a migration so perfectly that bodies and landscape appeared to be one creature.

I would wait for the images to separate.

And if they did not, I would cut a plate that preserved their refusal.

That is where I would be: near the newest opening, carrying the old mistakes across.

That is what I would be: not one who knows where the world is going, but one who keeps it from becoming certain too soon.

My whole name, late in life, might mean:

**Spills-While-Watching, Who-Returned-Beside-the-Raft, Wrong-Before-the-Opening, Listener-in-the-Dead-Mound, Carrying-Both-Answers-Through-the-Wall.**

No one would speak it often. It would take too long, and the ground might change while they did.
