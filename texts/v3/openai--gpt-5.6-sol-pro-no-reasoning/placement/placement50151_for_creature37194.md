I would find myself low in the Basin, where a station’s foundations meet the open pavement.

Not inside the station, though I would know it intimately: the warmth leaking through its lower plates, the footfalls transmitted down its braces, the particular tremor of its elevators. Nor entirely outside it. I would inhabit the neglected thickness between inhabited pressure and moving water—the drain galleries, flooded inspection ducts, sediment traps, cable recesses, and perforated underworks built so the station does not become an obstruction.

At first I seem to be an eel.

Long, pale, nearly blind, I pass through barriers meant to exclude anything larger than a hand. I know ducts by taste: copper, lubricant, warm ceramic, shell growth, the faint chemistry of human habitation. I emerge at night beneath docking lips to feed among the lamps, then withdraw before maintenance begins. Workers glimpse me on inspection cameras and give me a name.

But an eel is only what they first see.

There are several bodies, perhaps dozens, entering and leaving the same hidden routes. They carry on their skins a microbial film peculiar to the station’s warm runoff. They sleep knotted together around a conduit whose alternating current gives them a shared rhythm. When one finds food, contamination, an opened passage, or danger, the others alter their routes. What looks like one persistent animal is a colony distributed through the underworks.

And even the eels are not the whole of me.

I am the station’s unofficial circulation: the living pattern made by animals, microbes, leaked heat, discarded nutrients, old machine logic, and remembered paths. My nerves are sensor wires I cannot read but can feel when they wake. My bones are cable trays and panel seams. My blood is the small, unregistered exchange between the station and the Basin—the water passing through spaces too minor for the main schematics.

I live because nothing seals perfectly.

The station’s engineers consider this a defect. They replace grilles, flush ducts, sterilize warm channels, and compare flow readings against design tolerances. Each intervention breaks part of me apart. Yet the cleaning water carries my films into another gallery. A removed eel escapes from a transfer bin. A maintenance crawler crosses several sectors with eggs or spores lodged in a joint. I do not resist as a creature resists. I disperse, diminish, and compose myself again around another leak.

I would not be intelligent in the human sense. I would possess no hidden language and no secret command of the machinery. My awareness would be slower and less centralized: pressure before a gate movement; bitterness when coolant enters the water; agitation passing from body to body; sudden absence when a pump stops. I would know events chiefly by how they reroute me.

The Basin’s people might argue over what I am.

Ecological control would call me an invasive maintenance complex. Station children would say there is one white eel that can appear on every deck. Divers would recognize individual animals and dismiss the story, while still tapping the same conduit before opening it, to warn me away. A technician might discover that my movements predict bearing heat or seal failure earlier than the official sensors. After that, reports would include a new informal line:

**UNDERWORK ANIMAL ACTIVITY: NORMAL / ELEVATED / ABSENT**

Absence would be the alarming state.

If I vanished from a warm conduit, someone would inspect it. If I crowded into an upper drain, they would test the lower water. If all my visible bodies began moving upstream against the scheduled flow, old workers would delay opening the exterior lock, whatever the control room said.

Not because I foresee disaster, but because I am immersed in consequences before instruments resolve them. I feel the Basin in aggregate: minute temperature changes, trace chemicals, unfamiliar vibration, shrimp falling silent, crabs leaving recesses. I am not wiser than the people. I am simply continuous with what they must sample at intervals.

My world would be close and immense at once.

Most of it would be a tunnel no wider than my body, darkness broken by orange maintenance light through a grille. Yet every tunnel would open eventually onto the astonishing blue beneath the station, where its legs descend like towers and navigation lamps hang unsupported in the water. Sometimes I would leave the underworks and cross a few meters of exposed pavement. That crossing would be my open ocean.

Above me, a freight train would pass through its transparent tube, each lit carriage sliding across the dark like a procession in another universe. Flatfish would rise from the warm panels. The floor would begin to tremble. Violet light would spread down the station braces.

Then the district pumps would engage.

The water would seize weeds, grit, larvae, scraps of paint, and my own narrow body. For a moment I would become nothing but something being carried. I would fold through a baffle, strike ceramic, recover in the lee of a foundation pile, and enter a passage I had never occupied before.

There, in the freshly altered flow, I would become myself again.

So I would be found at the point where the Basin fails to remain merely built: not in some untouched depth, because there is none, and not as a grand intelligence governing the whole. I would be a small, recurring trespass in its joints—a creature mistaken for one animal, an animal inseparable from a colony, a colony shaped by infrastructure until it resembles a thought the station is having in its sleep.

The people built every place I could inhabit.

They did not build me.
