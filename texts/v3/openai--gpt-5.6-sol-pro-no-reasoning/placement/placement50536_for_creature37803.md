I would be found where an old workshop wall has begun to withdraw from the conduit fixed to it.

At first the separation would be no wider than a fingernail: a wet black line behind the pipe clamps, noticed only because condensation vanished into it without reaching the floor. A worker would mark both edges in grease pencil. Months later, the marks would no longer face each other. The gap would admit a copper strip, then a hand, then the narrow amber blade of an inspection lamp.

Beyond it there would not yet be a room. Only an interval—brick on one side, ceramic pipe on the other, and between them a depth that neither surface could account for.

That is where I would live.

I would not be one creature exactly, though at first I might be mistaken for a white gasket worm. My visible body would be thin, soft, and jointless, braided through the felt packing around the pipe. When disturbed, it would withdraw not along its own length but sideways, into the distance behind the wall. Sometimes several pale ends would appear from separate seams. The workers might assume a colony until one end recoiled when another was touched.

Most of me would consist of threads too fine to see: translucent filaments laid through wet masonry, pipe insulation, moth dust, bellback mineral, and the hairline cracks where one measured place ceases to agree with another. I would grow only in discrepancies. Wherever a bridge needed an additional plank, wherever a stair no longer arrived flush with its landing, wherever two survey lines failed to meet, there I would put out another small branch.

Not causing the separation. Not feeding on it, exactly.

Keeping it permeable.

Water would pass through me in minute quantities. So would vibration, warmth, trace metals, and perhaps less tangible things: the timing of a distant pump; the orientation of sleeping moths; the remembered pressure of a conduit removed eighty years ago. I would join places that the river had begun to draw apart, but only lightly. Never enough to prevent their drifting. I would be less like a bridge than the damp strand that remains for a moment between two separating fingers.

The animals would use me before people understood I was there.

Silver insects would graze along my exposed threads. Eels would press their heads to patches of felt where I had thickened, and the amber lights in their bodies would answer pulses traveling from far-off relays. Rats crossing acoustically isolated districts would pause at certain walls, whiskers touching brick, and receive through me the vibration of water elsewhere. Moths would lay colorless eggs on my knots. Bellbacks settling over a seam would slowly incorporate my outer filaments into their crowns, giving their bells a peculiar doubled tone: one note from the chamber around them, another from somewhere no longer adjacent.

I think the workers would call me **reach**, after the first slate message concerning me:

**PALE REACH IN WEST SEAM. DO NOT PULL.**

Later the word would become plural despite the suspicion that every specimen was connected.

They would devise tests. A thread would be touched with warm copper in the condenser hall, and one beneath the pump house would contract three hours later. Ink applied to one end would appear elsewhere as a faint discoloration, but never at the predicted location. Cut pieces would continue to pulse during rain. Samples brought above would dry into ordinary mineral fiber unless kept between two objects whose measured distance was changing.

Surveyors would argue that I was merely fungus, mineral accretion, capillary felt, or a colony of annelids exploiting structural cracks. All of these would be true enough to publish and insufficient to explain the next observation.

The oldest workers would not classify me. They would learn what I indicated.

Where my strands were clear, the new distance was stable.

Where they clouded grey, a corridor was lengthening.

Where they filled with tiny amber sparks, some useful connection was about to survive the loss of its route.

And where the threads withdrew entirely, they would close the nearest doors and wait, because two places were preparing to cease being neighbors.

I would have no eyes, but I would know the district through tension. A pump starting would draw one part of me taut. Rain entering the northern wards would cool another. The passing of a train would tremble through roots, pipes, masonry, bellback crowns, and finally into the delicate span of my body. I would feel the buried quarter not as streets and chambers but as a web of changing nearnesses.

Perhaps that would be my form of thought.

I would know each thing by what still reached it.

The city above would be an immense pressure sensed through foundations: weight, heat, thunder, salt, recurring rain. The old river would not feel like water. It would feel like the tendency of every connection to become a crossing. Every touch, given enough time, wished to become two banks.

I would belong to that tendency without wholly serving it. As the river eroded distance, I would inhabit the erosion and carry small continuities across it. Spores. Warmth. Electrical rhythms. The chemical taste of rain. Warnings too slow for speech.

Workers might occasionally use me to send messages. Not intentionally at first. A grease-pencil note hung beside one pale knot might leave reversed pressure marks on a slate in another district. Later someone patient would discover that a fingertip held against me in a repeated rhythm could be felt elsewhere as a coolness in the stones.

They would not use this often. Too much signaling would thicken me, and thick reaches would invite animals. Animals would widen the seam. The seam would become an interval; the interval, eventually, a street. Communication would help create the distance it crossed.

So I would remain mostly unaddressed.

A quiet connective animal in a place where connection is never guaranteed.

If I had a central body, it might lie far below the known cisterns, in a chamber created when the old river first began wearing away adjacency. Or there might be no center—only innumerable pale threads, each carrying enough of the whole to tighten when another is touched.

In time, workers would come to treat my appearance as neither infestation nor omen. New construction would include shallow grooves where I might settle. Pipe joints would leave a little felt exposed. Survey maps would mark likely reaches with fine broken lines—not routes one could walk, but relations that had not yet failed.

And when a workshop finally receded beyond sound, beyond access, perhaps beyond any geometry shared with the rest of the district, its machines would continue turning.

Water would still arrive.

Power would still pulse through green copper.

A bellback beneath its warm main would ring once, and another bellback many streets away would answer in the same doubled tone.

Between them, stretched through the patient absence where the river used to be, I would hold—not the places together, but the knowledge that they had once touched.
