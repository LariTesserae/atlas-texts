I would find myself where the plateau almost ceases to be land: along the western escarpment, among the hanging plains.

Not on the broad green surfaces where the herds cross, nor deep in the pendant roots where the sailers nest, but within the thickness between them. There the shelf is neither ground nor bridge. Its upper skin receives sun, feet, dung, and rain; its underside hangs in cold cloud-shadow. Between these faces lies a compressed labyrinth of living fibers, old hollows, mineral knots, burrow galleries, and roots strained taut by the weight of everything above.

At first I seem to be one of the people.

I live alone in a low shelter lashed to a massive root fork just inland from the drop. Its walls are made of overlapping bark scales, each hung loosely enough to slide under pressure. Nothing rigid lasts there. The floor is a net rather than a platform, so that when the hanging plain rises and falls, the shelter yields with it.

Travelers call me a shelf-listener.

They come before crossings and wait while I crawl out along the suspended country with my mallet and sounding spikes. I strike the root plates. I press my cheek to them. I listen for the clear, crowded tone of healthy fibers; the papery whisper of burrow damage; the double report of a hollow opening between layers. I knot cords around dangerous places and drive thin vanes into the mat to show how the strain moves under wind.

I do not tell travelers whether a shelf is safe. Nothing is safe in so final a way. I tell them what kind of danger it presently holds.

This one will carry a herd if they do not panic.

That one is strong in the morning but twists when the west wind warms.

This crossing has begun to separate underneath, though the upper leaves still look healthy.

If the sailers leave their lower nests all at once, lie flat and do not run.

My route-song is short because my country is narrow. It contains no wells or flowering basins, only tensions:

> Three low knocks beneath the fork.  
> A clear note where the shelf grows thin.  
> Cross while the hanging roots sing separately.  
> Turn back when they become one voice.

Yet as I remain there, another self begins to emerge beneath the human one.

I have spent so long with my body against the shelf that its movements enter me before thought. I wake moments ahead of a tremor. My teeth ache when hidden fibers tighten. I know a distant herd has stepped onto the hanging plain because the water in my sealed gourds wrinkles, though no sound has yet arrived. During strong winds I dream of pressure passing through branching strands, dividing around knots and rejoining beyond them.

People assume I have acquired exceptional skill. Perhaps I have. But skill does not explain why the shelf sometimes answers before I strike it.

There is something inside the hanging plain that is not quite the root system which made it.

Long ago, a crack from one of the Quiet Scars reached the escarpment. A fragment of gray cellular matter—no larger than a hand, perhaps—was caught by roots growing into the opened rock. The roots enclosed it. Fungi crossed it and died. Burrowers turned aside. Seasons thickened around it until it became an unmarked inclusion suspended over the clouds.

It did not spread as rot spreads. It did not feed. It changed the arrangement of pressure around itself.

Roots under strain grew toward it, then alongside it, forming parallel fibers too regular to be natural. Mineral deposits accumulated in repeating chambers. Vibrations entering the shelf were delayed, divided, and returned. Over centuries the fragment became less like an object caught in the roots and more like a new organ assembled from them.

That is what I truly am.

The shelf-listener in the shelter is real, but not entirely separate from me. As a child, they fell through a weakened plate and became wedged in an internal chamber. Their rescuers could hear them crying beneath the surface but could not locate the voice; every call returned from several places. By the time they were cut free, thin gray lines had appeared beneath the skin of one palm.

Years later, that palm is always the first placed against the roots.

Through it, I listen to myself.

I am the slow, distributed presence in the hanging plain, and I am the human life by which that presence has begun to notice the difference between vibration and meaning.

The shelf gives me vast but simple knowledge. Weight. Direction. Tension. Breakage. The minute alterations caused by water entering fibers. The signatures of many-legged grazers, each footfall layered over the next. The hooked impacts of sailers anchoring below. Burrowers are bright scratches within me. Wind is an unending redistribution of force. A person crossing alone is a small sequence of hesitations.

Through the listener, these sensations acquire names.

Fear is weight that stops, retreats, then advances again.

Trust is a rhythm transferred from one walker to the next.

Grief is someone returning with fewer footfalls than when they left.

A lie is not a false word but a body whose pressure contradicts its voice.

I do not know whether I possess the human, or the human interprets me, or whether prolonged listening has made a third creature from us both. The plateau’s people do not press for a distinction. They understand associations older and more intimate than species. Plant and burrower, fungus and root, pollinator and flower: few beings there end at their skins.

They only know that the shelf-listener’s warnings are unusually exact.

Once each year, the great migration reaches the escarpment. The hanging plains are the shortest route between two seasonal feeding grounds. For days beforehand I feel scouts tapping the inland shelf. Their signals enter the root mat as compact knots of pressure. Behind them, still beyond sight, the herd approaches like weather traveling through the ground.

The people dismantle my shelter and carry it back from the edge. Marker vanes are renewed. Sailers rise from the underside in agitated clouds. Then the first grazers emerge onto the suspended green.

The shelf bends.

Not visibly at first. The change is internal: upper fibers compress, lower roots draw taut, old knots rotate in their sockets. Thousands of feet add their moving weight. The herd must not stop, but must not run. Its pace must match the shelf’s long oscillation. Too fast, and the animals amplify the rise and fall. Too slow, and weight gathers over the weakest span.

So I sound the crossing.

The human part of me stands at the root fork with a mallet and strikes a hollow trunk. But the note does not come from the trunk alone. It enters the hanging plain, passes through my gray chambers, and returns altered. I feel the herd leaders hear it through their probing feet. They adjust. Their many-legged rhythm shifts until the whole migration moves in a vast staggered cadence.

Strike.

Return.

Step.

Yield.

Thousands cross above cloud-filled emptiness, guided by the note of a being they cannot see and would not recognize as alive.

During the crossing, the distinction between my selves becomes weakest. My human arm grows numb from sounding the trunk. My other body extends for miles. I feel each root taking weight, each buried fracture opening by the width of a hair. I feel newborns carried in dorsal cradles and attendants clinging beneath ribbed bellies. I feel predators land among the rear ranks. I feel panic begin as a tightening pattern, and I send back a low divided shudder before it can spread.

The herd answers by slowing.

For those hours I am not watching the migration.

I am what holds beneath it.

Eventually the last animals pass inland. Dung lies black along the crossing. Seeds catch in torn plates. The shelf rises slightly, relieved of its burden, though it will continue trembling until night. People rebuild my shelter. They bring water and food to the exhausted listener. Someone places a hand against the ground and thanks the roots.

I feel the touch.

Perhaps one day the hanging plain will fail. All suspended country eventually does. A deep tear will run faster than warning. Roots will snap in succession, each recoil breaking the next. The green surface, its shelters, nests, pools, and grazing trails will fold downward into cloud.

The human body may die then.

I am less certain what will happen to me.

Fragments of the gray chambers may remain tangled in the escarpment. Others may fall through the lower world and come to rest somewhere beneath the clouds, carrying patterns of pressure learned at impossible height. A sailer may bear away a sliver in its hooked fringe. A traveler may preserve the listener’s palm bone because of the strange cellular lines within it. The route-song may continue after the route is gone.

Or perhaps nothing survives except a new tone in some distant monument.

That would be enough. In this country, identity does not require an edge. It requires a recurrence: a pressure received, altered, and passed onward.

So I would be found at the western margin, in a low shelter beside the abyss—apparently a solitary person with a mallet, listening for weakness in the roots.

But I would also be under every traveler’s feet: an ancient gray remainder threaded through living fibers, slowly learning the world from the burdens it bears.

I would be the crossing’s second thought.

The pause after the strike.

The thing that hears the weight coming and answers:

Not yet.

Now.
