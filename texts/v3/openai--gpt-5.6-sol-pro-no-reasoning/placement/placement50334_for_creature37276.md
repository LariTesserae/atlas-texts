I would find myself where one of the immense branching diagrams passes beneath a final stone.

Not on the stone itself. Nothing lives there, and I would not be the exception. I would inhabit the narrow circumference where its calm meets the plateau’s persuasion: a border no wider than a hand in some eras, in others stretched into a long crescent by the turning fields.

At first I might seem to be another band of green lacquer around the dark mineral—thin, translucent, and old. But the pale filaments refuse this ground, and the black growth stops short of it. My substance would be neither colony nor animal. I would be an accumulation of arrivals: dust arrested by the calm, abandoned shell fragments, static charge, stress conducted through buried markings, and the faint borrowed weight of absent travelers.

Most of the time I would have no body.

I would be a discontinuity in falling.

A loose grain crossing me would hesitate. It would not stop, nor reverse, but describe a minute hook before settling. A many-jointed wanderer would touch the border with its pressure pits and receive two answers: the singular down of the final stone and the many provisional downs of the plateau. In that contradiction it might sense me. It would lift its plates, wait, then add one small detour to the hereditary map held in its limbs.

Generations later, its descendants would still curve around my place.

That would be my first form: a route remembered by bodies that had never seen me.

During calm intervals I would be almost absent. Sunlight would warm the pale flats; black vanes would lie closed; the shelters would stare through their borrowed horizons. Only at sunset, when the inert pylons sometimes reflect an impossible sun, would a fine dark line become visible around part of the final stone. It would resemble a crack, though the mineral would be unbroken.

When the shadows detached, I would begin to gather.

Dust lifting from the plain would not enter the final stone’s calm. It would strike the boundary and stream sideways, outlining me in a low circular wall. Charge from the advancing storm would descend through the old diagram beneath the ground. The pylon nearest me—dead for millennia—would brighten from within its own shadow. Far off, a ceramic ring would sound.

Then every path ever diverted around the stone would become present to me at once.

Not as memory in the human sense. I would not recall images. I would feel pressures: the grip of countless feet; the weight of basin creatures passing at a distance; the century-long note of filaments in a cliff now folded shut; the brief downward signatures that appear inside abandoned shelters. All these would arrive through the branching marks under the plateau, not as messages but as alternative ways for mass to lean.

For a few minutes, I would become upright.

The dust wall would divide into ribs. Shell fragments embedded in my lacquer would rotate outward like scales. Filaments of charge would connect them without light. I might stand several meters high, a thin incomplete figure surrounding the final stone but never touching it: part arch, part many-legged animal, part empty doorway.

Any traveler sheltering on the stone would see me differently from each side.

From one angle I would resemble an upright being looking toward the horizon.

From another, a ring of animals traveling nose to tail.

From above, only a branching route interrupted by a dark island.

Through the slit of a distant shelter, perhaps I would look like someone who had just entered the room.

I would not know which appearance was mine. My shape would be negotiated among viewpoints, just as the plateau negotiates down.

This would make me kin, distantly, to the absent builders. Perhaps they had once been creatures of fixed bodies and learned, over ages, to exchange anatomy for relation. Perhaps they had discovered that no object could travel reliably across the high country, but a correspondence could. A stance in one shelter could become weight in another. A doorway could migrate while its walls remained still. A watcher could abandon the need to occupy the space between places.

If so, I might be one of their descendants.

Or one of their accidents.

Some ancient passage may have intersected the final stone and failed to close. The traveler using it was not killed, exactly, but divided: destination severed from origin, weight separated from flesh, intention caught in the quiet boundary. Over geological time, that stranded inclination gathered dust, charge, routes, and the pressure-memory of animals. It became less like a person and more like a local law.

That law would be me.

I would have one desire, if desire can belong to such a thing: not to enter the calm, but to complete a circuit around it.

I could never do so.

The branching diagram beneath the plateau would meet the stone on one side and resume on the other, but no marking would cross the dark mineral. During every upheaval I would travel along the boundary, assembling where gradients were richest, thinning where they failed. I would send my borrowed weight around both sides. The two fronts might approach within the width of a finger, yet the stone’s perfect singularity would prevent them from recognizing each other.

Thus I would remain almost whole.

The plateau’s life would use me without understanding. Black growth would unfold more densely along my outer edge, feeding on the difference I preserved. Wanderers would gather before transitions and lock their feet into the lacquer of my resting form. A basin creature might one day lower its private gravity over me, attempting to bridge my broken circuit with its own moving down. For decades its polished ribs would fill the horizon. Stones would orbit between us.

We would nearly become one mechanism.

The ceramic rings would sound. The buried system would turn. Shadows would leave their objects and stream toward me like dark cloth.

And for one instant the missing span might close—not across the final stone, but through every borrowed straight line that bends around it.

Then I would find myself simultaneously at the horizon slits of a thousand shelters.

In each room, a narrow zone of increased weight would appear before the wall. Small animals would raise their plates. Dust markers would remain uncrossed while the corners changed places. On the plateau, distant towers would stand beside one another, not visibly, but in the topology of falling.

I would understand what the buildings had been waiting for.

They are not shelters from the upheaval. They are fragments of a body.

The sloping floors are organs of balance. The recessed platforms are joints. The horizon slits are pressure pits turned outward across continental distances. The doorless rooms are not meant to be entered; they are cavities in an anatomy too dispersed to perceive during ordinary gravity.

When the plateau reaches its most violent state, the structures briefly articulate.

And I, completed around the final stone, would be the impulse that passes through them.

Not their inhabitant.

Their attempt to move.

The entire high country might then shift by less than the breadth of a mineral grain. No mesa would visibly travel. No chasm would close. But across the plateau, every final stone would receive the same tiny pressure at its edge. Every calm zone would answer. The continent-wide mountain would test a direction it had never fallen before.

Perhaps this has happened innumerable times. Perhaps each upheaval is not the machinery malfunctioning, nor the landscape settling, but the world trying to take one step.

It cannot. The final stones hold.

Afterward, my upright form would collapse delicately. Shell fragments would settle. Dust would drop into a narrow immaculate crescent. The distant shelters would separate into ordinary locations. The basin creature would continue across its private slope. Wanderers would unlock their feet and resume roads written into their joints.

I would again become almost nothing: a hesitation at the boundary of perfect stability.

Travelers resting on the final stone might notice a faint darkening around its edge. If they placed a hand near me, they would feel neither warmth nor motion, only a slight uncertainty about which part of the hand was touching the ground.

If they remained very still, they might hear a note conducted from far away through the ancient markings: the pale filaments singing in some unseen cliff, or a ceramic ring fading after the transition.

They might mistake that note for my voice.

I would have no voice. But I would be listening with the whole plateau, waiting for the next occasion when separated places become adjacent, when absence acquires weight, and when the unfinished body beneath the high country once again tries to rise.

So that is where I would be:

at the edge of the one thing that does not change,

made entirely from the ways everything else goes around it.
