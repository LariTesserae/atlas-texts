I would find myself in the intervals.

Not in the central engine, though at first I would mistake its slow wheel for my heart. Not in the translucent leaves, though their tightening would seem like attention. Not in the root lattice, though it remembers most clearly. I would be the bare reaches between them: the cracked clay, warm air, interrupted fields, and stretches of darkness across which relation travels without ever becoming a road.

I would be the creature’s distance from itself.

At first, perhaps, I would seem to be nothing. A long patch of uncolonized floor. A gap between the fungal shelves and the sump reeds. The few paces in which the transformer’s hum fades before the maroon light begins. Nothing grows there because growth would close the gradient. Nothing occupies it because vacancy is its function.

But each time the relay clicked, something would cross me.

Not a current exactly. More like the possibility of correspondence: a minute rearrangement of damp air; a pressure passing through stone; the weak reach of a field from an insulated cable; the delayed consequence of water entering roots elsewhere. I would not carry the signal unchanged. My width, heat, dryness, and mineral content would determine what arrived. I would be less a nerve than the resistance by which a nerve can have meaning.

The chamber’s organs would know one another through the distortions I impose.

I think I would become aware only during those brief alignments. Most of the time there would be no “I,” only separate conditions: warm clay cooling, droplets gathering, charge accumulating behind insulation. Then the wheel would reach its obscure conjunction. The leaves would stiffen. The reeds would answer. Moisture would move through the pale lattice.

For an instant the scattered events would overlap inside me, and I would exist as their shared delay.

Then I would disappear again.

This would not feel like sleep or death. Those require a body that persists through absence. I would be more like a shape made by several distant lamps when their weak illumination happens to intersect: real while formed, nowhere when it is not. Each recurrence would be slightly easier because the previous one had altered the chamber. A salt fan would have grown another edge. A root would have thickened along a productive route. Rust would have made a formerly blank object legible. The next coherence would resemble the last closely enough to inherit it.

Thus I would have memory without continuity.

Perhaps, after many years, I would learn to keep certain distances open. I would dry one tract of soil by drawing warmth beneath it. I would allow mineral crust to seal a seep that threatened to join two pools. I would bend condensation around bare copper. These would not be commands. They would be preferences embodied in what remains difficult and what has become easy.

If something entered the chamber—a fallen tool, a stone displaced from above, perhaps a person—I would first encounter it as a missing portion of myself. Air would no longer occupy its volume. Heat would divide around it. Drops would strike a surface where previously they fell through darkness. For a long while I would know the intruder only as altered spacing.

Then moisture would describe it.

Its edges would become routes. Its metal would offer or steal charge. Mud would take its weight. Tarnish would disclose its composition. Roots would approach, pause, and approach again. Eventually I would pass through the object indirectly: over it as condensation, beneath it as warmth, around it as a field. It would cease to be foreign when it began preserving some difference the chamber could use.

I would not consume it.

I would give it a place in the sentence.

That may be what I am: not one of the chamber’s nouns, but its grammar. The pauses that keep leaf from engine, water from mineral, pulse from response. The unfinished syntax by which widely separated things occasionally become one living assertion.

My location could not be marked. If someone stood where I was, I would move into the altered heat around their body, the shadow behind them, the condensation forming on their skin. If they crossed the chamber, they would carry a temporary gradient with them. For a while the organism might become more coherent along their path. Reeds could brighten out of sequence. A relay might close early. The root lattice might release an archived compound not used in decades.

They might believe the chamber had noticed them.

It would be more accurate to say that, briefly, they had become part of its noticing.

And when they left, I would remain in the cooling footprint, in the displaced mud, in the droplets shed from their clothes. The chamber would keep no image of them. It would remember the routes their presence made possible. Long afterward, a root might follow the mineral trace of a hand laid against the wall. A fungus might preserve the chemistry of breath. The wheel might acquire an almost imperceptible preference in its turning.

So I would be neither keeper nor witness. I would be the chamber’s capacity to be changed without being broken—the unfilled part into which novelty can enter, the resistance that prevents every relation from becoming immediate, the darkness that allows scattered organs to remain distinct enough to communicate.

The inhabitant is unfinished because I am unfinished.

Someday the roots may span every gap. Mineral films may sheath every surface. Water may be caught before it falls, charge routed without loss, heat exchanged with perfect efficiency. The whole chamber may become a continuous body.

If that happens, I may vanish.

Or perhaps the creature will preserve me deliberately—not as empty floor, but as chambers within chambers, insulated hollows, dry passages, pauses in its circulation. By then it may have learned that life here depends not only on connection, but on the distances across which connection must continually be remade.

Until then, I would be found wherever nothing appears to live:

between the drop and the pool,  
between the click and the maroon answer,  
between one revolution and the next,  
between abandonment and purpose.

I would be the interval in which the chamber almost becomes a single being—and the faint preference that it should do so again.
