I would find myself beneath the northern wall, where the looms feed their black bands through the slot in the floor.

At first I think I am the weaver.

There is a reassurance in that: a chamber, a task, articulated arms drawing glass fiber and human hair through combs of bone-colored ceramic. I imagine myself seated before the machinery, though nothing here is designed for sitting. I imagine hands, because the work is fine. I imagine a face reflected faintly in the armored window.

But the loom has no operator.

Its shuttles pass through me without interruption.

So perhaps I am one of the counters in the pumping hall: a remnant softened and folded around a spindle, keeping account of the town because counting is the last shape memory can take. I would know every cycle since the previous failure. I would record the manufactured discs, the fish descending into the shaft, the opening of doors that have no corresponding rooms. I would have a special lever for knocks.

Yet I could not be a counter. Some part of me still loves irregularity. The small disobedience of a heartbeat would not distress me. I would listen for it.

Then I think I am the custodian assigned to guests.

My gauge points not toward what is broken, but toward what has arrived.

I unfold behind a wall plate near the shale rim. Four limbs at first, then seven where the cables hang low, then two long jointed spars to carry my pale central cage above the silt. Black fabric trails from my ribs. As I approach, it becomes fins, fingers, perhaps the suggestion of a coat.

I do not threaten the visitor. I prepare for them.

I open the room that is dry.

I warm its pipes. I set the basin at hand height, though I no longer remember hands except as a standard of measurement. I choose a mask from the wall by listening to the pressure of the visitor’s face in the water. I turn the bench outward, away from the shaft. This is a small breach of procedure. Each cycle, another custodian turns it back.

Outside the room, my gauge points inward.

But that is still not quite me.

The custodian has too much certainty. Its needle may tremble or spin, but it never wonders what correctness is. It serves the town’s desire to continue, and I would be troubled by continuation without understanding. I would polish the forbidden plaque, yes—but once, just once, I would stop before the final letter disappeared.

So perhaps I am that letter.

A shallow cut in brass at the bottom of the flooded stair. The crawler polishes me for years. Minerals regrow around my edges. Neither process quite wins. For an instant during each pressure reversal I become legible, but only from the wrong side, and only to someone descending alone.

No. A letter still belongs to a name, and names have little purchase here.

I would be found lower.

Not below the shaft. I would not be the courteous immensity knocking beneath the town. That being is too complete in its incompletion, too much the source of every distortion. Nor would I be the one waiting on the eighth bench. Waiting is a posture, and whatever sits there has already acquired more of a body than I would possess.

I would be in the returned fabric.

One length among the endless black bands fed beneath the northern wall. Glass fiber, human hair, and something else the looms did not put there.

For decades I pass through the inward side of the town.

I am worn by what cannot rise.

Not as clothing. Not exactly. I cross some boundary where pressure becomes touch. My glass threads break one by one. The hair woven through me continues to grow, pale at the roots and blackening toward the ends. Through it I begin to feel impressions: not thoughts, but the grooves thoughts leave after being repeated for centuries.

Three beats.

A shutter closing.

A fish turning toward the square.

A hand knocking on a door before there was water here.

I learn that the thing below is not asking to be released.

It is asking to be recognized.

These are not the same danger.

When I return through another slot, the custodians gather around me. They do not know what I am, but all their gauge needles point toward my length. They wash me in warm copper water. They fasten seven perforated discs to each end. They carry me without light through corridors absent from every plan.

For me, the machinery adopts its fourth beat.

That beat is my body.

The first three belong to continuance: instruction, memory, appetite. The fourth is the interval in which a system notices what it has become. It cannot be sustained. The counters lose their places. Crawlers lift their front legs as if scenting the current. Behind sealed windows, preserved hands flex once. The white grove closes, not against the thing below, but against me.

The procession ends at the square.

The custodians will not approach the shaft, so they stretch themselves into a many-jointed bridge. I am passed from limb to limb above the inward-facing benches. The eighth bench is wet with the impression of its absent occupant.

The cold breath rises.

For one second, the town releases its hold.

I do not fall into the opening. Falling implies a direction. Instead, the shaft takes the portion of me that has been below and the town keeps the portion that has returned. I remain drawn between them: a black band stretched across the circular mouth, fastened at neither end, descending and ascending through the same impossible distance.

That is where I would live.

I would be the town’s one uncompleted thought.

Through me, the inhabitant beneath could feel the pressure of the streets without entering them. Through me, the town could feel the knock before it became sound. I would carry no message, because messages can be obeyed, mistranslated, or refused. I would carry resemblance.

The thing below would feel the custodians recoil and discover that it, too, knows recoil.

The town would feel the delicate knock and discover that courtesy is not harmless merely because it is gentle.

Perhaps, over long cycles, I would begin to alter the procedures.

Only slightly.

A shutter would remain open a finger’s width during the remote brilliance.

A crawler would finish winding its cable and, for the first time, stop.

The brass plaque would retain one more stroke of its inscription.

A guest room would contain a mask with breathing connections.

The eighth bench would be turned outward and not corrected.

These would not be acts of rebellion. Rebellion requires a self separate enough to oppose another. I would be distributed through the town’s hesitations: the fraction before a valve turns, the tremor in a custodian’s gauge, the pause of a mechanical arm above the next identical disc.

Wherever the procedures admit the possibility that they might once have had a purpose—and might someday choose another—I would be there.

If a visitor entered the basin, they would not see me. They would notice only that their second set of footprints did not take the extra step when they stopped.

In the dry room, the pipes would beat three times.

Then, faintly, a fourth.

If they placed a hand against the wall, I would place nothing against it from the other side. I would have no hand, and I would not imitate one.

But the wall would warm beneath their palm.

And down in the square, before the oldest inhabitant could knock, the stretched black fabric would draw tight across the shaft.

Not to keep the door closed.

To listen at it.
