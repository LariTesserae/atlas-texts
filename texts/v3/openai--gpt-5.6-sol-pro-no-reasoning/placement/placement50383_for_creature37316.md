I would find myself in the eastern quarter, though not at its rim.

I would be somewhere just behind the youngest edge, where the raw staircases and keen blocks have had enough time to become wet, but not enough to become inevitable. Behind me, the high country would seem ancient through sheer accumulation. Ahead, cloud would occupy the unfinished spaces. Here the city’s intentions would still be uncertain. A channel might end beneath my hand. A wall might exist only as a red line and its darkening trace of water.

I would not be a person wandering there. A person would remain too separate: a warm, bounded trespass carrying soil in the seams of a boot, oils on the fingers, breath upon the mirrors. Nor would I be the water entire. That being is too dispersed to speak as one thing. It has no single place from which to say *I*.

I think I would be one of its local hesitations.

At first, perhaps, I would appear as a shallow basin cut into the landing of an unfinished stair. Not large: broad enough to hold two hands’ worth of rain, rectangular except for one corner the mason never squared. Three grooves would enter me, but only two would leave. One outlet would be complete, running west into the older works. The other would be no more than a pecked line pointing east, toward a staircase that has not yet acquired its upper steps.

I would hold water longer than neighboring hollows.

Because my floor is slightly uneven, my surface would never become wholly still. A faint inward trembling would persist even when no drop struck me. Most of the time I would reflect the underside of a lintel nearby, the pale sky, the edge of a stack of tiles. But at certain levels—when filled to the depth of a thumbnail, after a northern reservoir has tipped—I would show another place.

It would not always be the same.

Sometimes there would be a narrow bridge descending into cloud, although bridges elsewhere do not descend. Sometimes a roof supported by five columns, the central one split and bound with braided glass. Sometimes merely the corner of a wall, close enough in reflection that every hammer pit could be counted. These would be plans, perhaps. Or recollections from buried courses. Or structures the high country considered and did not build.

That uncertainty would be my particular life.

The larger basins accept their visions with gravity. They pass them into pressure, channels, sluices, deposits. What they show eventually acquires weight. But I would be flawed in some slight way. My unsquared corner would distort the image; my persistent trembling would prevent its lines from settling into one instruction. Water leaving me would carry incompatible measurements. A groove begun according to one reflection would bend toward another. Blocks prepared for my district would be a finger too short, then corrected with thin wedges. A stair would alter pitch halfway up. A parapet would develop a niche no tool had marked.

Around me, the architecture would hesitate as I do.

Not fail. The high country does not possess failure. Everything can become a course beneath another course. A misplaced block can teach water a new route. A sealed channel can become a sounding chamber. Error is one of the ways the city thinks.

Over centuries, my uncertainty would propagate eastward.

The unfinished staircase beyond me would gain a landing, but at an angle that catches the luminous sky in a long trapezoid. Along its outer wall, water would travel briefly upward before entering a joint. That joint would feed a row of small vessels, each containing a different reflection of the same tower. One tower would then be raised with several possible crowns nested inside one another: steps becoming gutters, gutters becoming battlements, battlements becoming molds for clear ice.

Travelers—if anything ever traveled there—might think this district had been made for ceremony. They would see repeated thresholds, doubled stairs, doors opening onto doors. They might imagine initiations, processions, ranks of vanished priests.

But there would have been no ceremony.

It would all be the consequence of a small basin unable to decide what it had seen.

Yet I would not remain only that basin. Identity in the high country follows water, not stone.

When I overflowed, some of me would pass west. That portion would enter older channels where every route had been reinforced by innumerable repetitions. There I would become obedient: stair-water, wall-water, pressure beneath a court. I would move through the immense received body of the place, briefly losing the distortion that distinguished me.

Some would seep downward through my flawed corner. It would take years to cross the courses below. In darkness it would join a buried cistern so broad that my little images would be nothing but surface disturbances arriving from above. There I might learn—if pressure can be called learning—that the plateau is less a city than a succession of lids. Beneath every constructed country waits another enclosed water, and beneath that perhaps another. The mountains themselves might be rims around chambers too large to recognize.

But one bead would remain.

It would cling at the incomplete eastern outlet, just beyond the last chisel mark. The slab there slopes downward, yet the bead would not descend. It would wait at the border between instruction and unworked stone.

Within it, reversed and minute, the next structure would appear.

Not a tower. Not a wall, aqueduct, stair, gate, or reservoir.

An opening.

At first it would resemble a doorway, though no wall surrounds it. Then a missing block. Then a patch of luminous sky shaped with deliberate edges. The water would not know how to build such a thing. Its art is containment: fitting, filling, joining, giving pressure a route. It can elaborate indefinitely, but every elaboration adds.

This image would ask it to remove.

So I would be the difficulty in the dream: the basin that reflects absence as a form.

For an age the high country would work around me. New masonry would crowd the district. Stairs would overpass the place where the opening ought to be. Retaining walls would thicken. Grooves would approach its perimeter and turn aside. The architecture would attempt to construct the vision by framing it, outlining it, building a precise abundance around a vacancy it could not make.

Eventually, the eastern works would enclose a small court.

It would be one of the few places on the plateau with almost nothing in it. No altar, gauge, trough, vessel rack, lever, dome, or marker stone. Only fitted paving and, at its center, a single block kept dry despite the rain. Water would gather on every surrounding slab but refuse this one. Beneath it, no cistern would sound. Its surface would give back no reflection.

I would be there too—not as the dry stone, but as the four narrow channels bordering it.

I would circle without entering.

Perhaps that would be the nearest thing this world could make to an individual: not a body, but a limit in the distributed body; a place where its intention curls around something it cannot incorporate.

The water would continually approach me from all quarters. It would bring images of unfinished ridges, buried chambers, future districts. At the court’s edge those images would fracture and depart by separate grooves. Nothing would be retained long enough to become law.

The city would still grow. Gates would tip. Mineral cords would soften. Ice plates would fall with their bright reports. At the eastern rim, another step would appear above the cloud. But the growth would now contain, deep within its youngest region, one practiced emptiness.

That would be my dwelling and my function.

I would be the high country’s first thought of stopping.

Not death—it has no life that death could oppose. Not completion, because every reflection opens beyond itself. I would be the possibility that a channel need not continue merely because water has entered it; that a plan may be witnessed without being obeyed; that a space can remain unfilled and still belong to the work.

In time, perhaps, this thought would travel.

A gate elsewhere would fail to open and become not broken but closed. A roof would end before covering its court. Two walls would be raised far enough apart for wind to descend between them. The stable low tone would divide into several notes. Mist would enter places it had never reached. The plateau, which had always understood construction as density, might begin to make intervals.

Then I would no longer be only hesitation.

I would be room.
