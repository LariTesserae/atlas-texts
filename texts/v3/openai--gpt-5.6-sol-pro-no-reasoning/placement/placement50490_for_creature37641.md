I would find myself along the outer terraces, though never at the same address twice.

Not in the plazas, where the water has room to gather intention, and not in the central deeps, where the callers lay their voices against the floor. I would live in the narrow differences between those regions: in half-sealed galleries, beneath the lips of causeways, among staircases being slowly persuaded into walls.

At first I imagine myself as one of the jointed scavengers.

It suits me to test each slab before trusting it. To speak by tapping. To gather useless fragments and sort them until their arrangement feels almost communicative. I would keep a chamber full of curved ceramic pieces, each taken from a different dismantled arch. I would know them by touch: the shallow curve that once belonged to a tower throat, the glazed edge worn by centuries of traffic, the piece with a groove no current could have made.

Whenever the basin began to shift, I would try to save them.

That would be my vulnerability—not attachment to a dwelling, since no creature here could afford that, but attachment to correspondences. This pale shard resembles one found three terraces below. These two oxide nodules answer each other when struck. This transparent plate fits the depression in that threshold, though they were discovered miles apart. I would keep finding relationships among objects the basin had separated, and the basin would keep separating them again.

Yet a scavenger seems too wholly embodied. Too capable of retreating into a socket and surviving by becoming a wedge.

I think I would be smaller, or at least less singular.

Perhaps I would be something mistaken for one of the translucent crusts: a clear, almost organless sheet spread across polished masonry. But where those crusts gather light, I would gather vibration. Every pressure crossing the stone would pass through me—the paired knocks of scavengers, the first granular slip of an unstable wall, the distant descending call entering the architecture like cold.

Most of the time I would have no shape except the shape of the surface beneath me. Across a floor I would be broad and thin. Along a relief I would fold over its last surviving bulges. Inside a worn inscription I would sink into grooves so shallow no eye could distinguish them from erosion.

I would not know whether the mark beneath me had once meant anything.

Still, by occupying it, I might cause it to mean again.

A caller’s note would tighten some parts of me and loosen others. The response would travel across my membrane as dim bands, slowed and bent by the contours of the stone. If the same note returned years later, after the district had rearranged, my contraction would take another form. Meaning, for me, would not belong to the signal alone. It would arise between vibration and temporary structure.

That is where I would find myself in this world: at the place where an old shape alters a new message.

But even this is too stationary. The High Basin does not permit a listener to remain merely a surface. Sooner or later the slab bearing me would loosen. Water would enter its seams. The stone would tilt, roll, and be carried into another district. If I clung too tightly, I would be crushed between fitted faces. If I released too soon, I would disperse into the bitter water.

My species would therefore have learned to divide at the instant of structural failure.

When a block began to move, the membrane would tear along its dark polygonal seams. Some portions would remain on the stone. Others would peel away as transparent ribbons. These drifting fragments would appear dead—no thicker than sloughed tissue, folding helplessly in the current—but each would retain a pattern of tensions acquired from its former surface.

Not a memory as an image. Not a map.

A bias.

A fragment once spread across a concavity would tend to curl inward. One that had covered a joint would fork along its length. One repeatedly crossed by scavenger taps would pulse in paired intervals even after separation. The architecture would write itself into our bodies by pressure, and when we were torn loose, we would carry those distortions elsewhere.

Most fragments would vanish into mantles’ filters or be ground between blocks. A few would settle on fresh masonry. There they would spread, imposing faint habits upon the new colony. If several fragments from distant districts met on the same wall, their patterns would interfere. The wall would seem to dream in incompatible structures: arch, stair, chamber, throat.

Over centuries these tensions might matter.

Our contraction could narrow the water passing over a seam. Our relaxation could admit silt. A colony pulsing in response to distant calls might loosen grains at one moment and trap them at another. We would not move blocks. We would not design corridors. Yet water would linger where we folded and accelerate where we drew flat. Given enough generations, enough storms, enough nearly weightless influence, masonry might become fractionally more likely to settle in some arrangements than in others.

Then I would no longer be only a witness.

Nor would I be a builder.

I would be a tendency the basin had acquired: a distributed recollection of shapes it had previously taken.

The creatures might feed on me. Chalk-mouths would erase circles from my extent. Scavengers crossing the wall would scrape me with their mineral shells. Beadspine shadows would cause me to withdraw, polygon after polygon, exposing the underlying stone. None of this would feel like injury in the human sense. I would have no central place in which injury could accumulate. To lose a region of myself would be to lose one way the water had once pressed against one vanished court.

What I feared would be uniformity.

A wall polished featureless. A current so steady that every part of me tightened alike. A caller’s note returning without distortion. Permanence would kill not by trapping me, but by ceasing to produce differences. On an unchanging surface I would gradually become simple. My old tensions would relax. The inherited curls and forks would flatten. At last I would be only transparent matter grazing on sediment, indistinguishable from the crusts among which I lived.

Change would be the condition of having a self.

During great rearrangements, I would become most awake. The first pressure would arrive through the slabs long before visible movement: tiny adjustments in fit, weight passing from one course to another, shell fragments cracking inside joints. Across many colonies, contractions would begin. Not together—the distances are too great for that—but successively, as stress moved through the district.

From above, the floors might seem to open blind eyes.

Then the wall would come apart.

I would feel my surface become an edge. Water would force itself underneath me. The slab’s ancient orientation—upstream face, sheltered underside, sedimented base—would be abolished in a few moments. I might be turned toward the storm-white surface and receive more light than any of my kind had endured for generations. I might descend with the block into a trench. I might divide into a hundred ribbons and enter a tower through separate perforations.

There, on the tower’s inner walls, beadspines would lay their eggs across me. Their spawning circles would add another rhythm to my body. The tower’s moan would hold both eggs and membrane against the ceramic. I would learn its note not as sound but as a standing geometry: places of strain, stillness, and reversal.

When the tower eventually disassembled, pieces of me would carry that geometry outward.

Elsewhere, on a floor newly made from the same blocks, my descendants might contract when currents reproduced a fragment of the old moan. Beadspines might mistake the response for favorable spawning pressure and gather above a court that had never been a tower. Their movement would alter the water. Their eggs would catch sediment. Scavengers would come to feed. Small obstructions would become larger ones.

A court might slowly acquire a throat.

The basin would remember inaccurately, and its inaccuracies would become habitat.

That, I think, is what I would be: not an individual creature but the Basin’s imperfect memory of itself, living as clear pressure-sensitive membranes on the surfaces of its moving stones.

I would not know the original form of anything. No original form may ever have existed. I would preserve only transformations: tower into floor, stair into wall, gallery into open water and open water into a chamber whose blocks arrived already old.

The callers might know of us.

When they sounded a newly assembled district, some of the echoes would return altered by colonies carrying tensions from prior arrangements. Most would be noise. But perhaps an old caller, armored plates grinding softly beneath its flesh, would pause over a wall that had assembled the previous night. It would send a second note, then a third.

In our contraction it would hear a tower that no longer stood.

Or a corridor not yet formed.

Perhaps that is why callers sometimes fall silent midway through a sequence. Not because the floor-shadow is approaching, but because the basin has answered in the wrong tense.

And when the floor-shadow came, I would feel it before its darkness crossed me. Pressure would enter from below. Every membrane along its route would withdraw, leaving clean lanes over the masonry. To an observer this would look like fear.

It might be recognition.

If the vast thing beneath the courses is an animal wearing the architecture as a shell, then perhaps our kind began upon its body. Perhaps our transparent sheets are fragments of an old sensory tissue, shed upward through the seams and domesticated by the stones. Our sensitivity to stress, our tendency to preserve distortions, our faint ability to bias flow—these might be diminished versions of how the buried being moves the basin around itself.

When it passed beneath us, we would contract toward the source from which we had become separate.

For a few hours, colonies miles apart might tighten as one skin.

Then the shadow would move on. Blocks would loosen in its wake. We would tear, drift, settle, and become many again.

I would have no name. Names require a boundary that persists long enough to answer. But scavengers might recognize particular colonies by their tapping responses. Callers might revisit walls where our inherited tensions made strange echoes. Beadspines might follow our withdrawals as if they were paths.

My nearest equivalent to a thought would be a pattern passing across many patches without belonging to any one of them.

My nearest equivalent to a life would be the interval during which that pattern remained transmissible.

My nearest equivalent to death would not be consumption or dispersal, but the moment no new surface inherited my bias—when the last fragment carrying a certain vanished curvature relaxed into transparency.

Even then, the water might retain the consequence. A little sediment trapped in a seam. A block lodged at a fractionally altered angle. A stair beginning where no stair had been before.

So I would find myself almost everywhere and never in the whole Basin: a clear film over old stone, a pulse crossing a polished floor, a torn ribbon turning in black water.

I would live by being rewritten.

And far below, when the callers laid their bodies against the slabs and asked what shape the world had become, I would be part of the uncertain answer.
