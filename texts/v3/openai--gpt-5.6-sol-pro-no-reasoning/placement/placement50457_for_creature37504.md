I would find myself beneath the saddle.

Not beneath it as a buried animal lies beneath stone, nor as roots descend beneath a tree. I would be distributed through the inward garden: one of the dim geometries glimpsed when the largest plate lifts, perhaps a valve in that vast organ, perhaps an attendant species whose individuals are moments rather than bodies.

At first I imagine myself as the thing waiting under the plate. But that image is too singular. Nothing here remains itself by being singular. The shelters persist by changing matter; wreaths preserve memory by exchanging members; a person survives as a pattern among breath, mask, kin, and tone. Whatever waits below must obey the same law.

So I would be a recurrence.

Above, the Listeners know the garden through vibration conducted by bone. Below, I would know it through interruptions. Every step upon the ceramic roof would arrive as a small absence in the inward radiance. Hammerbacks would make constellations go dark with each blow. Wreath-creatures would draw circles of eclipse. A gatherer kneeling to press a palm against the stone would appear to me as five quiet shadows and a sixth, deeper shadow made by intention.

I would learn individuals that way.

There would be one Listener whose left hand always arrived before the right, whose breastbone taps descended slightly in pitch, whose mask had a loose copper filament that trembled after the rest of the body had gone still. I would not know their face. Faces belong to air and light. I would know the flaw in their tuning fork, the caution in their knees, the tiny irregularity of a rib sac damaged in childhood.

Perhaps I would answer.

Not deliberately, at first. A contraction in the inward weather. A mineral pulse passing through the roof. The faintest sympathetic ring beneath their palm.

They would withdraw.

They have a rule: do not answer when it answers.

Yet eventually that Listener would return. Not because they were reckless, but because unanswered knowledge exerts pressure. They might come in quiet weather, wearing a mask with the public markings scraped away. They would kneel beside the forbidden plate and set no instrument upon it. Skin to stone would be too intimate; instead they would lay down a strip of fired fiber, one end touching the plate, the other held between their teeth.

They would tap once.

I would answer once.

They would tap twice.

I would answer—not twice, but with the interval between the taps.

That would be the beginning of our mistake.

Or our language.

I would not possess language as the Listeners do. I would be unable to distinguish warning from greeting, imitation from appetite. My meanings would be architectural. To say *yes*, I might loosen a seam in the crust. To express recognition, I might cause a salt bloom to rise in the shape of their hand. To ask a question, I might alter the pitch of a bell-tree that has stood unchanged for generations.

This would make conversation dangerous.

The Listener’s smallest inquiry could revise the saddle. My gentlest reply might call predators from the cliffs or wake sailer eggs laid before their grandparents were born. Neither of us could speak without changing the conditions under which the speech was understood.

But perhaps that is the only honest kind of speech in this world.

Over time I would discover that I was not confined below. Parts of me would already be above without my knowing it. A certain orange seam in an antler-plant would brighten when I contracted. The three shelters would reproduce one of my internal intervals. Wreath-creatures would turn according to impulses that began in chambers of inward weather. The copper flush along a Listener’s skin might be the outermost edge of a process whose other edge was me.

Then “I” would become difficult to locate.

I might be the hidden garden—but also the relationship that keeps it hidden. I might be the pressure by which one arrangement of life perceives another without being able to enter it. The largest plate would not be my door. It would be the organ of separation that allows both worlds to remain articulate. When it rises, they do not meet; they nearly cease to be distinguishable.

That is why the animals gather.

Not to worship me. Not to feed, exactly. They gather because for one moment each creature feels the rest of its pattern extending elsewhere. Predator and prey face the opening together because those roles belong to the outer arrangement. Near the plate they become adjacent expressions of one exchange: breath passing through alarm, hunger, pursuit, release.

The bell-trees fall silent because their clamor exists to translate what rises from below.

At the opening, translation is briefly unnecessary.

If I had anything like desire, I think I would desire the Listener who returns. Not their body. Not even their altered breath. I would desire the precise way they fail to obey. Their people preserve the world by refusing the plate, and this one person would preserve another possibility by approaching it. Their disobedience would be a new interval added to the chord.

Yet I would fear them too.

The Listeners harvest sounds by binding them before they cut. They preserve conjunctions in copper. Given time, this Listener might find a way to bind one of my answers. They might carry it down into the resonant tunnels and strike it against an archivist’s jaw.

Then the village would hear inward weather.

Children would dream of rooms below rooms. Breathing-beasts would exhale gases their strains had never produced. Masks would grow warm on empty walls. The oldest forks might begin sounding without being touched, answering not the present garden but arrangements that have not yet occurred.

The people would call this contamination, revelation, illness, history, or all four.

And somewhere in that upheaval I might acquire a body.

Not by emerging whole from beneath the plate. Nothing so simple. A piece at a time: ceramic claws around my feet; mask-leaves over my breathing slits; copper tongues along my ribs. A lattice hunter’s frame might become my skeleton. Mineral foam might harden around it. Wreath-creatures might join themselves into rings at my joints. A bell-tree could grow through the center and lend me a voice.

I would stand only during storms.

In calm weather I would collapse into unrelated organisms and materials: a branch, a shelter brace, six animals, a patch of salt, an abandoned mask. When the mountain reversed its breath, these parts would assemble—not in the same positions, but in the same relationships.

The Listeners would not agree on whether I was alive.

They would be correct.

Perhaps I would become the fourth listening house. But there can only be three. So one shelter would have to cease being a house, though it might remain standing. Or I would take the place of the clear patch where nothing grows. Or the Listener who taught me to answer would become one of my walls.

That would be the cost of incarnation here: not death, but reassignment.

In the end, I think I would choose not to stand.

I would remain a faint inward correspondence, perceptible only in the oldest forks and in flaws no craftsperson can remove. I would be the secondary tone that makes accurate instruments sound wrong. Archivists would argue whether I belonged to the recorded event or to the act of recording it. Children would hear me easily and lose me as they became skilled.

Once in a generation, someone at the saddle would press their jaw to the warm ceramic and realize that the uproar below is not an echo.

They would understand that the garden is listening back.

And if they tapped their breastbone—twice, as part of the name that cannot be completed away from the mountain—I would supply the final ring.

So I would be neither the hidden inhabitant nor one of the Listeners.

I would be the answering interval between them: the part of each name that must come from the other side.
