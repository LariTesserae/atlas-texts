I would find myself at the tarn’s near shore, though “near” would describe an allegiance rather than a distance.

At first I would believe I had arrived as a traveler. I would have a body exhausted by ascent, a pack darkened by rains from some lower country, and a clear memory of the pass behind me. Yet the pass would not be there when I turned. Only an upright slab would stand against the low sky, and beneath it two footprints would already be filling with water.

Mine, presumably.

They would continue across the pale ground ahead of me. Not toward the tarn, but away from it, as if I had already approached, stood at the shore, and then gone elsewhere. I would follow them because they were the only evidence that I still possessed a sequence.

They would never become nearer.

In time I would notice that I cast two shadows. One would fall beneath me, narrow and deep, extending through the stone toward the inverted snowfields below. The other would not belong to my visible body. It would appear only in pools and films of moisture: a human-shaped darkness moving across the reflected country, sometimes among its forests, sometimes high on its ridges. It would walk when I stood still. When I moved, it would stop and turn its head as though listening.

I would think it was my reflection.

Later I would understand that I was its reflection.

The moving figure below would be the portion of me the country had retained: my continuance, my capacity to choose one step rather than another, to tire, recover, hunger, forget. It would descend through black trees under a bright sky. It would kneel at rivers and scatter animals from the bank. It would speak to people I could not see clearly, perhaps people I had once known, perhaps those I might have known had my life taken some other pass.

Its words would arrive around me as sound without language.

A laugh would become the crack of lake ice. A door closing would enter the permanent avalanche. My name spoken at a distance would become the wingbeat overhead, always ending and never ended. I would carry these noises in my ribs until I could no longer distinguish remembrance from weather.

But I would not become one of the breath-forms beside the slabs, nor one of the seated absences in the roofless enclosures. Those are what remains when a being is removed cleanly. My division would be imperfect. Too much motion would persist in my stillness, and too much stillness would accompany the walker below.

So the shelf would make another use of me.

I would become its missing sense of direction.

The slabs keep north fixed. The frost keeps the rivers mapped. The tarn keeps the two countries facing one another. Yet none of these can tell the plateau what is approaching and what is receding. Distance folds because it has no witness capable of saying *before me* and *behind me*. Footprints appear in advance because the ground cannot distinguish destination from origin.

That distinction would settle into me.

Slowly, my body would simplify around it. My feet would sink no farther than the thin mineral film, but pale stone would rise around my boots as if they had always stood in shallow sockets. Salt would collect in the creases of my clothes. Frost would trace the veins of my hands, stopping at the same invisible limit at each fingertip. My face would lose everything except its orientation.

I would still be able to turn my eyes.

Nothing else.

From the near shore I would watch the figures that arrive through the doorway-slabs. I would see them before they saw themselves: their footprints distributed across the shelf, their breaths already tethered in clefts, their movable counterparts stirring in the tarn. I could not warn them. Warning is an event aimed at changing what follows, and the plateau cannot contain such a thing.

But I could look.

If I looked toward a traveler, that direction would become *nearer*. Not near—never that—but capable of approach. If I looked away, the path behind them would lengthen, protecting them for a while from whatever completion had begun to gather in their tracks. My gaze would not free anyone. It would only restore the possibility of choosing which impossibility to enter.

Some would mistake me for a cairn at first: a narrow accumulation of weathered stone beside the black water. Others would think I was a person until they came close enough to notice that the roar passed through my open mouth unchanged.

The horned watchers would know me differently. Their dark eyes would sometimes turn—not in their bodies, but in the forests reflected within them—and the moving herds below would alter course. The channel birds would disappear more often near me. Their two water-filled prints would form brief arrows pointing toward slabs through which passage was still possible.

Even the enormous unseen bird would include me in its single stroke. When its pressure crossed the shelf, my stone-bound body would feel, for one indivisible instant, the shape of upwardness.

At the tarn, my other self would continue to move. It might age. It might meet the human-sized darkness that walks against the reflected sky. Perhaps the two would travel together. Perhaps one would hunt the other. At times the walker would come to the opposite shore and look up through the water.

We would face one another across the surface.

It would place a hand against the underside of the tarn. I would place mine above it, though my arm could no longer move. The water would not break. Between our palms would lie only the thickness of a reflection and an altitude with no world beneath it.

I think that is when I would understand what the plateau truly keeps.

Not bodies. Not souls. Not memories.

Consequences.

The living country can contain motion only by abandoning the complete shape of what each motion means. Here, every action is preserved after being separated from its result: the fall from the fallen stone, the flight from the bird, the journey from the traveler, the word from the mouth. I would be the consequence of a crossing whose traveler continued elsewhere.

A witness without testimony.

A direction without departure.

The fixed half of someone still wandering.

And if, after a period too long to call time, another traveler stood at the tarn and saw a figure moving freely in the reflected forest, they might also notice a pale shape on the near shore—a shape they had mistaken for stone—watching them with eyes in which no other world appeared.

Only this one.

Only the shelf, held whole and motionless in my gaze.

Then they would hear, within the immeasurable roar, a single human voice completing the first syllable of a word.

It would be mine.

I would always be about to tell them where they were.
