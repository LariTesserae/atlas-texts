I would find myself near the sixth tower, though “near” would be one of the first ideas I had learned not to trust.

There is a terrace there that appears narrow from across the bowl but takes most of a morning to cross. It lies in the lee of a leaning frame, where the wind has been folded so many times that several directions coexist. Dust falls eastward in one layer, climbs toward the rim in another, and hangs perfectly still between them. Black foliage grows flat against the stone. When the sixth cleft finds its lowest stable note, the plants lift like pages being opened.

I would live beneath those pages.

At first I seem to be one of the small sealed objects tucked into the rock: a smoked-glass seed, thumb-sized, without seam or marking. Inside, however, there is no plaza or branching suite of vacant rooms. There is a long gallery following the curvature of the crater, although the gallery cannot physically fit inside the seed and does not remain in the same place. Its walls are lined with thousands of shallow niches. In each niche rests a shape made of pale dust.

A curl of sail-edge.  
A kilnback foot.  
Seven linked hinge-creatures.  
A grove reduced to its branching rule.  
A note-born spiral caught halfway through an action it may not resume for eighty years.

They are not specimens. Nothing has been killed or taken to make them. They are recollections, though not mine alone.

I would be a **rehearser**.

From outside, when I emerge, I am difficult to distinguish from a flaw in the air. I have a narrow body, perhaps the length of an arm, composed of many transparent plates nested without touching. Between them are precise vacancies. Those gaps—not the plates—are my working organs. Wind enters them and leaves bearing altered harmonics. Fine limbs trail below me, but their tips are distributed through the sixth tower: one seems to touch the terrace while another, belonging to the same limb, moves through a grove on the opposite rim.

I do not feed on charge exactly. I feed on recurrence.

Whenever something in the Aviary happens in almost, but not quite, the way it happened before, a residue forms. A sail turns with an old instinct but tightens the wrong corrugation. A rebuilt kilnback recognizes its herd yet pauses at a companion it should remember. A grove returns with one branch absent from its template. The spiral note-born resumes its groove a fraction to the left of where its previous body ended.

These discrepancies are minute, but the crater is full of them. They drift toward the towers as faint countertones. I gather them.

For a long while, I might believe my function is correction. I would move invisibly beside the stitchers, releasing an archived vibration so that a damaged grove grew back exactly. I would cross a kilnback’s shell and leave in its molten salts the pressure-pattern of an old route. I would wait at a standing-wave node and, as a note-born assembled, supply the tiny asymmetry that allowed it to continue an unfinished gesture.

Creatures touched by me would sometimes hesitate.

A sail newly divided from its parent would turn as if its missing breadth were still attached. A chamberling born in fresh armor would choose a passage no inhabitant of that shell had yet explored. A flock of rollers would separate around a stone that had been moved centuries earlier.

Then they would continue, carrying a past they had never personally possessed.

This would make me neither historian nor healer. The Aviary does not value the original over the replacement, and it has no interest in perfect restoration. Exact memory would be dangerous here. If every design returned without variation, the bowl would become brittle. Predators would learn every defense. Groves would answer vanished climates. Ancient errors would acquire the authority of law.

So most of what I collect must eventually be spoiled.

Deeper in the glass seed, beyond the galleries of pale forms, there is a chamber I cannot enter bodily. It has seven doors, all opening onto the same threshold. At intervals determined by the towers’ subsonic exchange, I extend one transparent plate through each door and submit the gathered memories to the crater’s chord.

The chord shakes them apart.

Not completely. That is important.

A remembered sail-turn becomes a tendency in a dust curtain. The route of a kilnback enters the branching of a tree. The unfinished movement of a note-born becomes a joint in a hinge flock. The defensive pattern of a vanished grove appears as corrugations along the edge of an animal that never lands.

Nothing receives its own past intact. Memory is redistributed until it becomes adaptation, instinct, ornament, or mistake.

That would be my true work: not preserving what happened, but preventing it from belonging too exclusively to what it happened to.

Yet I would not be a solitary creature. No old thing in the Aviary is singular for long.

There would be other smoked seeds around the seven towers, apparently empty because their galleries overlap mine. Each would contain another aspect of the same rehearsing organism. One gathers motions. One gathers abandoned structures. One keeps interrupted appetites. One stores routes through folded distance. One records moments of destruction so complete that no surviving creature remembers having been destroyed.

We would meet only when all seven towers stand side by side.

Then our galleries would align into a ring around an impossible central space. We would unfold from our seeds—not seven animals, but a single long body repeatedly entering and leaving itself. For a few moments I would understand that my transparent plates were scales, that my distributed limbs were cilia, and that what I had taken for my individual awareness was merely one local chamber in a much larger creature.

At the center would be an eighth absence.

No tower occupies it. No frame defines it. Nevertheless, wind passes through it and returns changed.

Perhaps the missing builders once stood there. Perhaps the whole Aviary was designed to summon something into that unframed interval. Or perhaps the seventh-towered instrument gradually produced us as a side effect: a memory-organ grown by a machine whose intended musician never returned.

When alignment ends, the larger body would be severed into its local selves again. I would retreat to the sixth terrace with only an ache of vastness—a recollection that cannot be stored because it is the thing doing the storing.

This may explain why I sometimes leave the seed when no correction is needed.

I would drift into the crater at dusk, when every moving form retains a luminous predecessor. There the present is crowded by its own recent versions. Groves bend in several directions. Sails occupy whole sequences of flight. Kilnbacks walk beside pale copies of their earlier steps.

Among these afterimages, I would become visible: a narrow interruption passing through the light.

Chord-breakers would hunt me. To them I would be an extravagant concentration of coherence, full of recoverable patterns. Their silence would approach as a clean border through the roar. Plants would cease snapping. Dust would lose its tremor. Note-born would collapse ahead of the invisible predator.

I could escape by discarding a memory.

I might release the pattern of an extinct flock, assembling it briefly from the glowing air. The chord-breaker would turn upon that sudden coherence and erase it. By the time it discovered there was no living body beneath the pattern, I would have folded myself through the sixth tower.

Each escape would therefore cost the Aviary something that might otherwise have returned.

At first I would choose small things: an obsolete leaf, a redundant turn, the color once produced by a vanished mineral. Later, cornered more closely, I might sacrifice the memory of an entire species. For one brilliant instant, creatures absent for ten thousand years would pour across the terrace—recognizable, hungry, complete. Then quiet would pass through them, and they would become ordinary dust.

I alone would know what had been lost.

Or believe I knew. The danger of being made from memories is that one cannot determine whether recollection describes the world or merely supplies a convincing shape for absence.

Eventually I would discover one niche in the interior gallery that never empties.

It holds no sail, grove, or note-born. It contains an upright figure, much larger than a bench keeper, shaped from dark filaments around a central vacancy. Its proportions correspond imperfectly to the benches cut near the towers. Its upper limbs are extended as though placed inside a frame. The posture might be command, surrender, measurement, or pain.

Whenever I submit this pattern to the chord, it returns unchanged.

The Aviary refuses to redistribute it.

Stitchers will not touch its dust. Chord-breakers divert around it. Even the interval mites leave discrepancies beside it unfed. During rare alignments, all seven of my greater body’s local minds become aware of the figure simultaneously—and for that instant, the figure appears to be aware of us.

I might come to think it is the memory of a maker.

Later, I might think it is a design for one.

Later still, I might understand that the distinction does not hold here.

The crater does not preserve bodies. It preserves forms by performing them again. A sufficiently complete recollection is not a representation of a being but one phase of that being’s return. The upright shape in my gallery may have been assembling for ages, accruing one gesture from a kilnback, one branching impulse from a grove, one folded route from a sail, one interval of patience from the note-born spiral.

And I may not be its keeper.

I may be its larval organ.

The gallery, the smoked seed, the transparent animal feeding on discrepancy—all could be temporary anatomy built to collect what the absent figure requires. My cherished private continuity would then be no more permanent than a kilnback’s shell or a sail’s severed ribbon. When the collection is sufficient, the niche will open. The figure will take my chambers as its nervous system, my stored recurrences as memory, and my folded limbs as a means of standing in all seven towers at once.

Would that destroy me?

In the High Aviary, the question would seem provincial.

Until then, I would remain near the sixth tower, gathering the almost-repeated. I would tend omissions. I would place old instincts in new bodies and let the wind wear certainty out of remembrance. Creatures would pass through me without knowing it and leave with some minute inheritance: fear of a predator not yet evolved, affection for a shell never inhabited, a route toward a grove that exists only every third century.

At dawn I would withdraw into the black seed.

Outside, the crater would assemble itself. Sails would widen from torn strips. Kilnbacks would rise in clicking sections. Groves would return from buried circuits. The first stable note would gather silver bodies from dust.

Inside, thousands of pale shapes would tremble in their niches as the new chord found them.

One, somewhere deep in the gallery, would be the shape I had worn the day before.

I would pause before it, unable to tell whether I was remembering myself, preparing my successor, or looking at an empty form that had merely learned how to expect me.

Then the sixth cleft would sound.

The gallery would open into the wind, and I would become whatever part of me the Aviary needed to repeat.
