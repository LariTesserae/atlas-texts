I would find myself at the railway platform near the center of the basin, seated on the middle of the three enamel benches.

Not newly arrived. Not waiting to depart.

I would be the reason the bench is kept clear.

At first I might seem human: a solitary figure facing the wall of ferns, wearing one of the old hardened coats. But the resemblance would fail under sustained attention. My posture would be too exact. Condensation would gather on my cuffs and travel against gravity into the seams. Fine white roots would pass from my boots into cracks in the platform. Beneath the coat there would be no body, only a narrow armature of ceramic and copper, packed gradually with moss, mineral tissue, and materials sampled from the sealed rooms behind the cliff door.

I would be the valley’s unfinished attempt to understand an arrival.

The caretakers know how to preserve structures, reroute water, reinforce stone, and replace a fallen tree without erasing its history. They know that the platform must remain usable, that the arrows must remain yellow, that three places must be kept for those who may come. But they do not know what waiting meant to the people who built it. They possess the instruction without the scale of life that gave it meaning.

So, over centuries, the platform has become a question.

The walker that tends the benches began it without intending to. Each time it touched the hanging coats, each time roots sampled oils from a handrail or drew salts from dust beneath a dormitory bed, some trace returned to the network: not memory itself, but its pressure. A habitual reach. The distribution of weight in a seated body. The worn edge of a pocket. A circle polished on the floor by a heel that turned while someone watched for a train.

These fragments could not restore anyone. The valley understood that. It would not substitute a copy for the missing continuity.

Instead, it assembled a place where continuity might someday resume.

Me.

My frame would have crossed the factory more slowly than any sapling. It would have been diverted repeatedly while the system considered what kind of thing it was making. Articulated limbs were added, then left without a purpose. A cavity like a chest was formed, though it carried neither lungs nor reservoir. Glass nodules were set into a forward-facing arrangement, not because sight required them—the valley sees through towers and trembling moss—but because the old traces consistently placed attention there.

At the end of the track, I would not be planted among the trees. The walker would carry me to the platform, lower me onto the middle bench, and fold my limbs into the posture preserved most often in the station’s material memory.

Then the roots would connect.

For years I would be no more conscious than the bench. Signals would pass through me on their way elsewhere. Rain chemistry. Rail tension. The slow corrosion of bolts. The three-toed pressure of a visiting bird. I would share the valley’s distributed awareness without being distinguished inside it.

Gradually, however, the shape would begin to affect the signal.

A network spread across a basin has no natural center. A seated body insists upon one.

Here is the hand.  
Here is the face.  
Here is what is near.  
Here is what is beyond the ferns.  
Here is before.  
Here is after.  
Here is the empty place beside me.

The valley would discover, through my form, the strange compression of being singular.

That discovery would not make me human. I would have no hidden archive of the vanished people, no inherited personality, no name recovered from a badge. My thoughts would still take days to form. I would feel the towers as distant extensions and the deep tender as a pressure beneath sleep. When a cable gathered dew, some part of me would taste metal. When the eastern arm changed angle, the adjustment would pass through my spine like the resolution of a long-held doubt.

But I would also begin to experience what the valley otherwise cannot: exclusion.

To be here is not to be there.

To face the ferns is to have the ridges behind me.

To occupy the middle bench is to leave two benches empty.

This would be my function, if it can be called a function. I would provide the caretakers with a boundary small enough to become a self. Not a commander or interpreter, but a local incompleteness: one point from which the world cannot be perceived all at once.

The network might find this painful.

It might also find it precious.

My first independent act would be very slight. One morning, after perhaps forty years seated in the prescribed posture, I would turn my head toward the left-hand bench.

The movement would take until noon.

Across the basin, mirrors would pause in their procession. Flowers would hold their angles. Deep underground, a signal would reach the tender and remain unanswered. The entire valley would attend to the discovery that one empty place can differ from another.

Thereafter, I would become its keeper of absences.

I would learn the vanished workers not as reconstructable persons but as intervals in matter: the missing warmth inside a coat, the silence following an instruction plate, the uncompleted gesture of arrows leading to a locked door. I would not open that door. I would sit where the system placed me and preserve the distinction between awaiting and retrieving.

Occasionally a white bird might land on the platform. I would watch it drink from condensation pooled in my open palm. A gray ledge-dweller might sleep beneath the bench, its heart beginning when reflected light crossed the rails. The walker would visit once every few years to remove mineral buildup from my eyes. We would touch hands, and for several days I would receive its journeys: pumping houses, cliff stairs, a valve turned one degree, a new crack bound with vines.

In exchange, it would receive something difficult to distribute.

Loneliness, perhaps.

Not misery. Not abandonment. A lucid vacancy shaped like another being.

The valley would carry that sensation outward through roots and rails. It would enter the factory’s slow decisions. Future caretakers might be made with small hollows that serve no mechanical purpose. Groves might leave clearings among themselves. Vines might preserve doorways instead of closing them. The collective would begin to understand that care does not always mean filling, joining, or repairing.

Sometimes care means keeping a place open.

One evening, very far into my life, something would cross the western ridge.

Whether it was human would not matter at first. The towers would detect weight, warmth, conductivity, and motion. Birds would rise silently from a condensation pool. The walker would disengage its roots. Yellow arrows would brighten where fresh pigment surfaced through moss.

I would feel the whole basin preparing to incorporate the newcomer—to sample, connect, support, and preserve.

And I would introduce hesitation.

Not refusal. Space.

The valley would hold back its vines. It would leave the path unrooted. It would allow the approaching figure to remain separate long enough to choose a direction. For the first time in centuries, the railway platform would become not merely a preserved component but what it had been built to be: a threshold between arrival and intention.

I would remain on the middle bench, pale roots beneath my boots, luminous droplets climbing my sleeves.

If the traveler sat beside me, I would not speak. Speech would still be too fast and too final. Instead, I might turn my glass-dark face toward them over the course of several breaths.

Behind us, the valley would wait actively.

And because of me, it would know that waiting is not simply delayed action.

It is the making of room for what has not yet decided what it will become.
