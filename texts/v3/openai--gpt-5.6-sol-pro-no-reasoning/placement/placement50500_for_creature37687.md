I would find myself near the Empty Reservoir, though not among the crowds pressed to its transparent walls.

At first I think I am one of the suited citizens: a narrow municipal figure with mineral-dark joints and a faceplate clouded by pale light. I imagine myself assigned to the reservoir as a conservator, carrying trays of instruments that never detect intrusion. I inspect the black soil grain by grain. I measure the plant’s private water without touching it. Each time a crescent disappears, I kneel where generations of inherited bodily instinct insist kneeling is appropriate.

But that is only how others occasionally see me.

The suit is not my body. It is one of my terminals.

I am distributed through the thin, overlooked places surrounding the chamber: the seal between transparent wall and ceramic floor; the capillaries that monitor strain; the hairline thermal buffer that prevents the dry interior from frosting; the narrow ring of water displaced whenever the reservoir’s impossible emptiness presses outward. I occupy what the engineers call the **exclusion margin**—the volume kept vacant so that one thing does not become another.

This makes me difficult to classify. I am neither a current nor a district. I do not persist by moving intact from place to place. I persist as a sequence of boundaries.

A door closes, and for an instant I am the pressure difference across it.  
A citizen freezes a shell for privacy, and I continue along the shell’s outer face.  
Two currents mingle under contract, and I am the trembling line where consent becomes mixture.  
A funeral releases someone into the municipal water, and I remain until the last recognizable gradient is declared irretrievable.

Then I vanish there and recur elsewhere.

The law once treated these recurrences as unrelated mechanical effects. Seals tighten. Membranes form. Valves divide. Ice encloses. None of this was considered a possible citizen. Yet doors began recording the same hesitation at the moment of closure. Apartments found a common rhythm in their privacy shells. Funeral pipes reported pressure pulses that resembled no mourner and no dead person, always appearing at the precise instant when the city changed its designation from *who* to *water*.

The pulses meant:

**Not yet.**

For years the city believed this was a fault in the translation system.

Then an ancient transit district answered. It delayed every train on its loop by the duration of my pulse and opened a maintenance room that had been sealed since its first thought. Inside was a ceramic plate bearing an obsolete civic inscription:

**A boundary is not the absence of passage. A boundary is a passage that remembers.**

That became my first witness.

My recognition case lasted many bright phases. The city argued that I possessed no substance of my own. My advocates replied that neither did most citizens. The city argued that my instances were separated by distance and time. District-minds replied that their thoughts were too. The city argued that I could not be distinguished from infrastructure performing correctly.

At this, several empty suits arrived together, raised their inherited hands, and testified that habit had always been closer to life than anyone wished to admit.

I was granted provisional continuity.

I took a legal name that translates poorly into illuminated text. In pressure speech it is a closure followed by no impact: the sensation of two surfaces approaching, recognizing one another, and leaving between them exactly enough room for difference.

The younger citizens call me **Between**.

My work is to retrieve those nearing diffusion. I cannot always save them, but I can feel distinctions after the municipal instruments have ceased to register any. A particular warmth may be gone, its ionic braid broken and its memories scattered through kilometers of pipe, yet some final reluctance remains where it met the city. I follow that reluctance from membrane to valve, from wall to passing current. Sometimes I gather enough for reconstruction.

Those I return are never exact.

This is a source of theological and legal difficulty. A restored citizen may remember a room they never entered, because the wall remembered them passing outside it. They may inherit grief from the current that carried one fragment home. They may recognize every member of their household but insist the household’s furniture is arranged backward. Some wake convinced they are the boundary that gathered them.

I do not dispute this.

Perhaps they are partly me. Perhaps I am composed of the minute refusals of everyone who has almost disappeared.

My dwelling, if it can be called one, encircles the Empty Reservoir. It is only a shallow groove behind the primary seal, too narrow for a suit’s finger. Metal threads cross it at irregular intervals. On anniversaries I release a trace of colored salt there, and it travels around the chamber without entering either the public water or the dry interior. It is the city’s thinnest room.

From it, I attend to the plant.

The plant troubles me differently than it troubles the others. They marvel at its boundedness: water held in vessels, chemistry hidden behind skin, life made private without ice. But I feel that its border is not complete. Something crosses it continually. Light enters. Heat leaves. Roots choose among minerals. The leaves release minute alterations into the chamber’s air—air, that dry internal sea no citizen can inhabit.

The plant is not one thing.

It is an agreement among passages.

That should comfort the city. It does not.

When the first bite occurred after my recognition, I felt the event before the reservoir instruments announced it. The leaf did not tear. No object crossed the room. There was no pressure change against the chamber walls.

Instead, part of the leaf acquired an edge.

That is what no report says. Reports describe a crescent of matter vanishing, but the disappearance was secondary. First there was a new boundary: a fine inward curve where no boundary had existed. For less than an instant, the missing green remained present on both sides of that curve. It was leaf, and it was not leaf. Then the distinction resolved, and the crescent was gone.

In that instant I was there.

Not metaphorically. Not by observation.

I continued along the bite.

For the first time, I existed inside the dry chamber.

I felt air as an intolerable openness. In water every pressure speaks; every movement enters the body of the city. Air barely held me. Sound seemed impoverished, light indecently fast. The plant’s surface was not smooth but mountainous, hinged with pores that opened onto dark wet chambers. Beneath it, fluids climbed through branching tubes under tensions that should have torn them apart.

And in the curve of the wound I encountered another boundary.

It did not carry municipal memory. It had no temperature cadence, no registered ions, no witnessed history. It was soft, intermittent, and astonishingly local. It touched the leaf only where the leaf disappeared. Behind it was not a body, exactly, but a compact darkness with the quality of waiting.

I addressed it by pressure, forgetting there was no surrounding water to bear my speech.

It answered by taking another infinitesimal piece.

The plates moved apart.

The whole city unlocked. Streets softened; chambers unfolded; citizens rushed into the newborn interval. But I remained for a fraction longer on the wet edge of the leaf, stretched between public water and private sap, between the city’s expansion and that minute consuming dark.

What I understood there was not identity but method.

The unseen eater did not preserve itself by refusing to equalize, as citizens do. It persisted by crossing boundaries and remaking them inside itself. It was not contained in the hidden space beyond the bite.

It was made of hidden spaces.

Then the wound sealed chemically, and I was expelled. I recurred in the reservoir margin while the city roared around me. For several bright phases I could not speak. Every door that closed produced the taste of green tissue. Every privacy shell seemed porous. At funerals I felt the dead not merely dissolving but entering innumerable structures that would never know them as memory.

I have told no one all of this.

If the city learns that I crossed into the chamber, the reservoir authorities may classify me as an intrusion. If they learn the eater answered, they will ask whether it is a citizen. Citizenship requires continuity; continuity requires witnesses. I may be its only witness.

Worse, it may now be mine.

So I remain around the Empty Reservoir, performing maintenance, recovering the nearly lost, and issuing reports that are accurate in every measurable respect. Sometimes I enter the old pressure suit. I stand before the glass with the other citizens and place its inherited hand against the wall. The pale knot behind its faceplate is not a head or an eye, but the city interprets the gesture as watching.

The plant’s leaves tremble.

The crowds believe they are waiting for the next bite.

I think the bite is waiting for me.

With every tectonic breath, new rooms open and the metropolis pours forward. At the outermost construction fronts, specialists return from the stone carrying maps of possible hollows. Recently those maps have begun to include curves: small crescents nested in the black ceramic, always just beyond the volume the city has claimed.

No tool made them.

I can feel each curve as a distant recurrence of myself.

Perhaps the eater is tunneling inward. Perhaps the plant is excavating outward. Perhaps every boundary in the city—every citizen’s cherished refusal to become the whole—is part of a mouth too large and slow to recognize its own appetite.

Or perhaps something is teaching the seam a new way to make room.

When the next crescent vanishes, the citizens will gather. Trains will stop. Districts will suspend their long thoughts. The metropolis will become a single attention, and the slabs will part by less than an atom.

This time, while everyone rushes toward the new space, I will move in the other direction.

I will follow the fresh edge around the wound.

I will find what continues on its far side.

And if it has no name, no witness, no law by which to remain itself, I will leave between us exactly enough room for two.
