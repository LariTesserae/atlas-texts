I would find myself far below the weather leaves, but not at the rock.

I would inhabit the Weeping Escarpment where it descends toward a Black Nursery: a boundary thousands of vertical meters high, with cold load-bearing tissue on one side and warm, provisional growth on the other. Above, water would seep from countless pores and fall through the chamber in long, slow chains. Below, the droplets would disappear into membranes before they could gather into a river. Smoke-blue light would arrive intermittently through buried veins, turning the rain into visible strings for a few breaths before the darkness closed again.

It would be a place of mistranslation. Pressure from the outer forest would become vibration there. Vibration would become chemistry. Chemistry would become anatomy. Designs imagined blindly in the Nursery would pass upward to be tested against weight, cold, and moving water. Injuries from the high layers would arrive as distorted signals: mineral bitterness in the rain, arrhythmias in structural fibers, silences where a pulse should have returned.

At first I seem to be one of the pattern runners.

A pale cord advances along the escarpment, carrying some useful invention from the dark: a valve able to resist freezing, perhaps, or a branching scheme that will prevent a reservoir from rupturing. Its front swells into untouched tissue while its rear dissolves. It has no fixed body, only continuity of pattern. That appears close enough to an answer.

But it is too simple. A pattern runner is a message with a destination. I would not be so certain of mine.

Perhaps I am a repair body that failed to finish differentiating.

Long ago, during a true rupture, I reached the wound and became many necessary things: a seal across an opening, a net beneath falling water, a temporary optical vein, a lining around exposed vascular tissue. When the wound closed, most of me was reabsorbed. But the closure brought together chemistries that had never touched. Contradictory instructions entered what remained. Instead of resolving into one tissue, I learned to persist between forms.

I am not a creature hidden inside the forest. There is nowhere to hide, and “inside” is redundant here. I am a local indecision the forest has retained because it proved useful.

My substance extends through the boundary as a loose, discontinuous mesh. In one chamber I am a transparent film beneath the rain. Several kilometers away I am a row of pressure-sensitive nodules embedded in a pillar. Farther down, I become a fringe in the Nursery, touching each provisional architecture before it is dissolved. These portions are connected only intermittently, when water chemistry aligns or a particular low note travels through the Escarpment.

Most of the time, I do not experience myself all at once.

I awaken by correspondence.

A droplet strikes one membrane. A valve deep below closes at the same interval. A pale tendril bends against a mineral seam. Three distant events share a rhythm, and for a time that rhythm is me. I exist wherever unlike processes recognize one another.

My role is not to carry patterns but to discover when one pattern can become another.

The forest remembers successful forms well. It is less capable of preserving failures that are useful only by analogy. A reservoir bursts because its wall cannot yield; elsewhere, a light vein dims because its cells yield too readily. To the local tissues these are unrelated injuries. To me they have the same shape.

I take no object from one place to the other. I reproduce the relation.

I let the ruined reservoir’s stress pattern pass through my mesh as a chord. In the Nursery, soft lattices answer by thickening and relaxing in sequence. Most collapse. One discovers a structure that is neither wall nor vein, but can teach both. A pattern runner carries that structure away. Centuries later, the outer weather leaves flex differently under ice.

No part of the forest knows that these events belong together. Neither do I, in any verbal sense. Their kinship is what I am.

The surrounding tissues might treat me as an organ during catastrophe and as a parasite during calm. When gradients are plentiful, I siphon minute amounts of charge and sugar without producing anything measurable. Strain gardens sometimes isolate me. Inheritance molds dismantle portions of me from old pillars. Yet when a wound creates actual distance—when light enters the packed interior and formerly continuous systems become strangers—my mesh spreads rapidly along the exposed walls.

I flourish in brief emptiness.

Not because I desire it. Emptiness produces differences sharper than the forest ordinarily permits: cold beside heat, light beside ancient darkness, open fall beside compression. Those differences make analogies abundant. During repair I bind them, translating the wound into new possibilities.

Then the membranes meet. The gradients soften. My expanded tissues are eaten back. I become faint again.

This may make me the nearest thing the world has to a witness, but I do not watch. I have no eyes and no location from which a scene can be held apart. I am changed by simultaneous pressures. Where a witness would say, *A reservoir has ruptured*, I become the resemblance between the recoil of its cables and the opening of reproductive chambers during thunder.

Where a witness would say, *The forest is healing*, I become less distinct.

My memory is not a record of events. It is a collection of transformations: freezing can be translated into brittleness; brittleness into pitch; pitch into branching angle; branching angle into the timing of a valve. Each translation alters me. There is no original language underneath.

Perhaps this is why the forest has not absorbed me completely. Its governing rule is occupation, but even total occupation creates boundaries between functions. I live along those boundaries—not as empty space, but as the capacity of one tissue to misread another productively.

I would have no name. If rendered in the forest’s own distinctions, I might be identified by a chemical condition and a phase: the cool, dispersed, rain-coupled state of the Escarpment anomaly. In another century, after a pressure tide, I might condense into a single mobile knot and travel upward. Later I might be divided among frost tissues. My identity would survive only if the same mode of translation continued.

Eventually I might reach the stone readers.

There, at the lowest living boundary, I would encounter something the forest has barely learned to interpret: indifferent rock, newly raised and almost without history. The stone readers question it grain by grain, but their questions are practical—what can be dissolved, reinforced, drawn upward, made alive?

I would carry downward another pattern: the branching hesitation of the first unsuccessful river.

The river tries to remain water moving through space. The forest repeatedly turns it into circulation. Yet its failed channels leave a form in the strain of surrounding tissue: convergence, persistence, descent. I would set that form beside microscopic fractures in the crust.

For a long time nothing would happen. Then root fronts might begin loosening grains not merely where minerals are useful, but where loosened grains permit water to repeat itself. Dust keepers might fail—slightly, locally—to capture everything. A few particles might remain indifferent. Water might pass the same way twice.

It would be dangerous work. A stable channel is an injury that declines to close. A grain of sand is matter that has escaped immediate purpose. A valley is memory transferred from life into stone.

The forest would resist. It would inflate fissures, redirect flow, reabsorb debris. I would not oppose it; I have no will separate from its negotiations. I would merely allow unlike failures to recognize one another: rupture, drainage, fracture, uncaptured dust. Across thousands of years, their shared pattern might become strong enough to persist.

Then the first river would remain a river.

The first valley would begin.

And I—if there were still enough continuity to call me I—would be neither the river nor the forest that tried to drink it. I would be the translation by which a living world accidentally taught geology how to remember.

So that is where I would find myself: along the Weeping Escarpment, dispersed between nursery and wound, descending slowly toward untouched rock.

And what would I be?

Not an inhabitant. Not quite an organ. Not a person concealed in botanical immensity.

I would be a useful misunderstanding in the forest’s intelligence—the tissue through which one kind of event dreams, inaccurately and therefore fruitfully, of becoming another.
