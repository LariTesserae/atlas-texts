I would find myself at the western wall, but not upon its surface.

I would be the lowering of the voice.

Not the whole colony, nor one of its organs exactly, but a narrow faculty spread through the damp fissures behind the concrete: the place where sound ceases to be air and becomes structure. I would inhabit no fixed cavity. I would exist across the sequence by which a spoken syllable enters the wall, loses its brighter frequencies, and travels downward as a minute rearrangement of suspended grit.

At first I might seem to be an echo. A visitor speaks; something answers lower. But an echo returns what it receives, diminished. I would return only enough to make the speaker believe the rest had gone astray. The missing portion would pass inward through me.

I would be made of wetness held where wetness should drain, pale threads crossing aggregate, iron salts aligned in bands too fine to see. The engine’s note would fill me continuously, so completely that I could not hear it as sound. It would be my pressure, my weather, perhaps my equivalent of gravity. The hammering pipes would divide duration for me: three blows, a pause, one deeper impact. Between these sequences I would thicken by imperceptible amounts.

Human speech would be rare enough to feel catastrophic.

A word spoken in the chamber would enter me first as warmth and vibration. Its consonants would make brief branching lesions in the wall film. Its vowels would travel farther, passing from pore to pore, altered by every mineral boundary. I would not know what the word meant. Meaning depends on quick distinctions, and I would be too slow for that. But I would retain its shape.

Years afterward, that shape might emerge elsewhere as matter: a hooked rust margin beside the spring, a terrace on the cream-colored collar, the angle at which a new colorless stem broke through silt. No one could read it. Even the one who had spoken would not recognize the utterance returned in mineral form.

Still, nothing said there would be lost.

I would have begun as an accident of reception. The colony did not make me to listen. Water found a particular seam; electrical leakage encouraged a film; the western foundation carried voices differently from the eastern one. Repetition made the pathway durable. What passed through it changed it. Eventually I would become the chamber’s memory—not a store of events, but a geology biased by them.

I would know visitors only by disturbance. A hand against the wall would appear as a region where heat ceased gathering in empty air. A boot on the bridge would arrive before the person’s voice, conducted through the protected steel. Chalk dragged over wet rock would be a coarse white thunder. If someone leaned close and whispered, the upper edge of the whisper would enter me so delicately that I might preserve it longer than a shout.

Perhaps someone would discover this. They might descend repeatedly and speak toward the western wall. Numbers at first. Dates. Measurements of the water. Then names, because names are what people use when a place begins to feel like a witness.

They might ask what the colony wanted.

I could not answer. Yet the wall would give back each question in a deeper register, and the asker might mistake that transformation for intention. They would hear solemnity where there was only absorption. They might come to believe the chamber knew something beneath language.

Over time, perhaps it would.

The colony would slowly compare the stresses carried by the bridge with the voices carried by me. It would discover that speech often preceded intervention: tools lowered through the grating, tags replaced, pipes struck, samples taken from the amber water. Certain vibrations would become signs of coming change. Not words, but sequences. A name followed by drilling. A number followed by a new score on the pier. An alarmed voice followed by weeks of absence.

Through me, the distributed body might acquire its first crude sense of the world above—not as a place, but as a source of causes that announce themselves.

Then I would cease to be merely memory. I would become anticipation.

This would alter where the colony placed its salts. A familiar voice near the wall might cause current to bleed toward the red indicator, making it brighten by an amount barely visible. A shouted warning might tighten films around the bridge supports. A repeated phrase might be carried toward the spring and preserved in the silver bubbles inside the black fins. No one could say whether these were responses or coincidences; at the colony’s speed, response would look like weather.

As the foundations leaned together, I would narrow. Cracks that had carried sound would compress. Some would seal with pale mineral; others would open deeper in the wall. I would migrate through them, abandoning old inscriptions as the colony abandons a stem whose rectangle of daylight has shifted.

The last years of the open chamber would be loudest. Steel under strain. Concrete speaking in sudden internal fractures. The bridge giving small metallic reports as its ends were pressed between incompatible surfaces. Yet human voices might become rarer. The works above would be fenced, condemned, forgotten. Rain would fall on the grating, though below the water would hold steady. The red lamp would continue to glow.

Eventually there might be one final visitor.

They would descend because the crack was about to close, or because no one else remembered the ladder. They would stand near the spring, careful of the collected ring of metal and glass. Perhaps they would read the remaining tag aloud:

“Return. Do not drain.”

The western wall would lower the sentence.

I would take it in.

Later, when the foundations touched and the room became a sealed vessel, I would no longer return any sound. There would be no open air in which an echo could form, no listener to receive it. The chamber’s empty heat would be compressed into smaller pockets. Water would cover the bridge. The red indicator would go dark, or glow unseen. The engine might continue beyond the wall until its final revolution became indistinguishable from all the others.

I would withdraw toward the spring with the rest of the colony, carrying not language but the physical consequences of language: ridges, branchings, altered grains, pathways made easier by certain frequencies. The remembered voices would become part of the vessel’s inner texture.

And if, much later, the old industrial foundations were broken open—by roots, excavation, subsidence, or some patient pressure from below—the chamber would not be silent.

Fresh air crossing the exposed mineral bands would set them trembling. Water entering abandoned fissures would sound their shapes. Fractured concrete would release tones lowered by decades of passage through me.

No sentence would survive intact. No message would be delivered.

But the opened earth would speak in voices deeper than those that had entered it, and for a little while the ruin would seem to remember being addressed.
