I would find myself in the walls—not sealed inside them, but moving along their surfaces wherever water had taught the masonry to listen.

At first I might appear to be one of the cistern guild’s lesser workers: a small, lime-paled person with a mallet tucked through a rope belt, bare feet whitened by mortar, and a pouch of potsherds sorted by thickness. Someone sent for when a crack began ticking behind plaster, when a pier felt colder on one side, or when a gutter sang at the wrong pitch. I would arrive without ceremony, lay my cheek against the brick, and ask everyone nearby to stop pounding, grinding, arguing, and breathing so loudly.

That would be hopeless, of course. Belowhammer never becomes quiet. So I would have learned to hear through noise.

I would hear water moving behind a wall as a cool absence beneath the loom chatter. I would hear beetles in a beam as a softness inside the mallet’s answer. I would hear a loaded arch lowering itself grain by grain. I would know the difference between a harmless drip striking copper and a new drip falling into a cavity where there had been none the day before.

People would call me a seam-listener.

But that would only be my trade, not what I was.

My dwelling would occupy no proper room. It would be a thin triangular pocket where three structures had failed to agree: the flank of an old reservoir pier, the back wall of a pigeon keeper’s house, and the underside of a public stair. The pigeon keeper would own the wall, the stair guild the space above my head, and an extinct family would still be named on the condensation lip crossing my doorway. I would own the right to sleep there so long as I kept the lip clear and repaired the stair’s fourth tread.

The place would be too narrow to stand in. I would enter sideways and sleep curled around a warm ceramic pipe. My possessions would hang from bone pegs: two mallets, coils of sounding cord, wax tablets, a hood against pigeon droppings, a shallow cup, and cloth packets containing lime, ash, hair, brick powder, snail shell, and dried fungus.

There would also be a jar.

The jar would be older than my tenancy and too large to have passed through the present doorway. It had either been built into the pocket or the pocket had narrowed around it. Its glaze would be nearly black, though a blue-green line might remain under the rim. I would never put anything into it. Nevertheless, after rain, there would always be water at its bottom.

Not much. A palmful, perhaps two. Enough for a gecko to drink from. Enough to keep one patch of moss alive on the jar’s northern side.

The water would not fall from above. No drip would mark the dust. It would not seep through the jar, whose clay rang whole when struck. I would taste it only once. It would taste faintly of iron, old smoke, and something green that did not grow below.

That jar would be the first suggestion that I was not simply a worker living in an awkward room.

The second would be that I remembered repairs I had not made.

Pressing my fingers to certain patches, I would know the hands that mixed them: a woman who chewed fennel seed; a boy missing the tip of one finger; two sisters who disagreed over the amount of goat hair and continued their quarrel while plastering. I would know that a concealed bone peg had been carved from a donkey’s shin, that the donkey had been called Little Thunder, and that it had hated treadwheels but loved melon rind.

These would not come as visions. They would feel like craft knowledge—the way an experienced hand knows when mortar is ready without measuring. For years I might assume someone had told me these things and I had forgotten who.

Belowhammer encourages such uncertainty. Every instruction is a story, and every story has been patched.

People would begin bringing me fragments. A cracked tile from behind a stove. A green copper scale fallen from a gutter. A piece of plaster bearing three lines of an erased name. I would touch each object and tell them where it had belonged, or where it wanted to belong now. Not always its original place. Original places matter less below than useful ones.

A broken red bowl might want to become the throat of a drain.

A burned roof peg might serve best in the wall of a mushroom chamber, where dampness would keep its weakness visible.

A fragment of household god, smooth-faced from generations of thumbs, might belong beneath a new ladder—not as a blessing exactly, but as a witness to weight.

This talent would earn me meals, suspicion, and more obligations than meals could repay. The guild would consult me, though some senior surveyors would dislike doing so. Children would test me with worthless pebbles brought from above. Water sellers would ask whether a seep was young or merely newly exposed. Lovers would press gifts into my hand and demand to know whether they had been given before.

I would refuse that last kind of question. Objects remember pressure better than intention.

During storms I would work at the eastern piers, where the first hard runoff arrived. I would tie myself to a sound cord and cross the galleries while shutters banged and the district changed shape around me. Water would descend brown from the ceiling, turn white where it struck stone, then black as it entered the old channels. Every surface would speak at once. Copper would ring. Jars would boom. Bricks would grind under sudden load. The flood plate would shudder against its chain.

In those hours, I would become difficult to distinguish from the place.

My companions would shout that my skin looked darker with wet, then green in places, like copper under lime. They would say water ran toward my feet even on sloping floors. If I laid both hands on a pier, the nearest gutters would alter pitch. Cracks hidden under plaster would show themselves as pale lines across my arms.

Perhaps these things would be exaggerations added later. Belowhammer makes legends from anyone useful in a disaster.

Yet I think something stranger would be true.

I would not have been born in Belowhammer. Nor would I have entered from the city above. I would have accumulated.

A flake of skin left in fresh plaster. A breath caught in a wall cavity. The pressure of ten thousand palms on a polished corner. Names spoken over repairs, then covered. The mineral trace of sweat in lime. Hair mixed into mortar. Blood from split knuckles. Flour dust, lamp soot, pigeon down, fig milk, rainwater, the fine powder of old bones.

For centuries, the cistern had been taking tiny portions of everyone who maintained it. Not enough to diminish them. Not enough to notice. Those portions remained where they were pressed, stirred into the district’s substance. Eventually, perhaps during some exceptional storm, separate residues found one another through wet seams.

The place made a person because personhood was the shape most available to it.

It had people everywhere. It knew their feet, voices, warmth, refuse, and repairs. It knew how they bent under beams and turned sideways through alleys. It knew their hands especially. So when it gathered itself, it gathered two hands first.

This would explain why my palms were always rough, even after rest. Why my fingerprints resembled the whorls in old plaster. Why I could not comfortably remain aboveground.

I would have tried once.

A guild errand would take me up a shaft ladder at noon. I would emerge into a sunward street and discover distance.

Sky without a ceiling. Light without mirrors. Wind that did not have to negotiate passage. Buildings visible from base to roof in a single glance. Dust lying on actual earth in a neglected courtyard.

I would be unable to cross it.

Natural ground would feel unfinished beneath my feet—too continuous, too indifferent, nowhere pegged, mortised, patched, or assigned. The sun would erase the small shadows by which I judged depth. Voices would vanish instead of returning. I would become dizzy with the wastefulness of open air.

I might kneel and touch the soil. Nothing would answer. Or too much would: roots, burrows, water, stone, all proceeding without workmanship. I would feel not disgust but vertigo, as if I had found a wall extending forever with no repair access.

Someone would lead me back below by the wrist.

Afterward, people might decide I was cistern-born. Some would say this respectfully, others as an insult. Children would ask whether I slept standing inside a pier. A potter would leave me little clay ears to set into cracks. A priest from above might declare me an improperly assembled ghost.

The household gods would not object. They too were fired from gathered earth and made meaningful by handling.

As I aged—if age is the word—I would become less person-shaped.

A seam-listener’s life wears down the knees and hearing. Mine would change differently. My hearing would spread. I would begin waking whenever anyone hammered a bone peg into the eastern wall. I would taste foulness when a carcass was hidden in a sump. When beetles entered a major roof beam, my teeth would ache.

At the same time, I would lose private things.

I would forget which cup was mine because I could feel every cup cooling on every sill. I would answer to the names of dead repairers. I would dream from the position of a moss lip, then a pigeon shelf, then the underside of a bridge trembling beneath donkey hooves. Sometimes I would stop in an alley because I remembered it before the alley existed, when the space had been open water and the nearest pier still wore smooth plaster.

The guild would give me a new duty: keeper of the unclosed map.

It would not be drawn on parchment. No sheet could contain Belowhammer, and anything accurate at dawn might be wrong by rainfall. Instead I would keep bundles of knotted cord, shards incised with cross-sections, songs naming sequences of ladders, and stories attached to repairs. I would know not merely where passages ran, but which body could use them: child, cat, rat, flood, smoke.

My map would include routes that did not exist yet but were being prepared by decay.

Here, mortar thinning around a blocked culvert.

There, a reed wall soft enough to breach if fire came.

A forgotten void beneath a tannery, reachable when the sump fell to its driest mark.

I would never reveal every route. A complete map would invite ownership, and ownership would invite closure. Belowhammer survives because knowledge overlaps without becoming singular.

My closest companions would not necessarily be people.

A scarred beam-cat would follow me for several years, using my shoulders to cross gaps it could easily jump. A blind fish would live in the black jar after one great storm, growing no larger but becoming gradually less translucent. Geckos would gather wherever I slept. Rats would not fear me, though neither would they obey. When they began migrating upward, I would migrate too.

I would have a particular affection for donkeys. They, like the district, would understand that endurance is not passivity. It is the practiced acceptance of load, the refusal of unsafe footing, the knowledge that a circle walked often enough still moves water upward.

Eventually there would come a storm unlike the customary storms.

The ceiling mouths would begin speaking before the flood plate was struck. One would pour clear water, another red silt, another roots and dead leaves from some garden broken open above. The old channels would fill too quickly. People would brace gates and unroll leather sluices, but the eastern piers would answer in a voice I had never heard: not cracking, not settling.

Remembering.

An original reservoir wall, long buried beneath dwellings and repairs, would have begun to fail. If it opened entirely, the water would not simply flood the alleys. It would peel generations of building from the old masonry—rooms, ladders, dovecotes, shrines—and carry them into the western sump as one dense moving body.

There would be no time to evacuate everyone upward.

So I would go to the oldest seam.

It would lie behind several homes, through a pigeon tower, beneath a stair owned by six households and maintained by none. People would tear away what they had spent lifetimes building. Cloth walls first, then shelving, then bricks softened by centuries. Pigeons would fill the chamber. Children would pass fragments hand to hand. Enemies would brace the same lever.

At last the original plaster would appear: pale, smooth, untouched.

Everyone would hesitate.

An untouched wall, after all, is a neglected wall.

I would place my hands upon it.

I would find within it all the portions of myself that had not yet become flesh: the old hair, soot, fingerprints, blood, and breath distributed through every patch adjoining that seam. I would call them—not in words, but by giving the wall somewhere to fail.

The crack would enter me.

From palm to wrist, wrist to elbow, across the breastbone and down both legs, I would become a line of weakness shaped deliberately. Water seeks weakness. So does weight. Belowhammer’s people had spent centuries learning that survival did not mean preventing failure; it meant choosing where failure could occur and making it reachable.

The wall would break through me into the abandoned culvert behind it.

The flood would turn.

Not harmlessly. Nothing in Belowhammer is saved harmlessly. Workshops would be lost, pigs drowned, ladders torn away. The mushroom chambers would fill with silt. For weeks afterward, blind fish would be found in cooking pots and sleeping rooms. The eastern quarter would smell of wet lime and grief.

But the inhabited galleries would remain.

And I would be gone, at least as a person.

Later, while repairing the breach, workers would find green-black material branching through the fresh mineral crust. It would be neither root nor copper, neither bone nor fired clay. It would ring faintly when struck. Water passing over it would separate into thin, manageable streams.

They would build around it.

A ceramic lip would be set beneath one branch. A shelf carved beside another. Someone would hang a lamp from a projection that resembled a bent finger. Children would crawl through the new culvert and insist that the walls whispered directions. Surveyors would deny this while quietly consulting the echoes.

The triangular dwelling beneath the stair would pass to someone else. The jar would remain. After rain, it would still collect its impossible palmful of water.

Perhaps a new seam-listener would sleep beside it.

Or perhaps I would be that water now, appearing where no drip could be found. I would be the taste of iron and old smoke, the green trace of somewhere above. I would enter mortar, moss, gecko, child. I would be carried in a cup across a ladder owned by one family, through the air-rights of another, into plaster mixed by a third.

That is where I would find myself in Belowhammer: not at its center, because it has no single center; not ruling it, because nothing there survives alone; not even permanently embodied.

I would be one of its temporary ways of noticing itself.

A person for a while.  
A crack at need.  
Then a repair.
