I would not live in a room.

I would live in the thickness between rooms, in the part of the city that appears solid on every current survey: a wedge of old mortar and root-bound limestone between a family bath, a cooling conduit, and the rear wall of a chapel. From the bath side, I would be a warm patch where children press their feet. From the chapel, a faint irregularity in the bacterial boundary line. The conduit workers would know me as a vibration that does not belong to their pipe but has never caused trouble.

There would once have been a passage there.

Not a grand forgotten avenue. Nothing worthy of salvage stories. Only a maintenance throat, built when the brick vault was young and the cooling conduit had not yet been laid through it. Later, roots entered through the joints. Mineral waste accumulated. A household widened its bath into one end. The chapel closed the other. Surveyors marked the remaining space inaccessible, then filled the symbol in.

But inaccessible is not the same as empty.

I would be one of the Uncounted, perhaps, though I would not think of myself that way. “Uncounted” is a word used by those who believe the failure belongs to the thing omitted rather than to the count. I would have a household mark somewhere—an obsolete one, inherited from rooms that no longer connect to one another. I might still possess a spoken name, but there would be nowhere to speak it. My touch-name would have changed through disuse into something I tap against stone for my own assurance: two quick contacts, a pause, then pressure held until the wall answers.

I would have entered the passage as a child.

Not by accident. I would have been selected for pipe work: small, calm, able to exhale without panic when ceramic pressed my ribs. A conduit had fevered behind the chapel. I went in dragging a tool roll, while an older diagnostician kept two fingers against my ankle and read my movement through the line.

Inside, I found that the plan was wrong.

The conduit continued, but the space around it opened into a narrow vertical seam lined with old carved niches. Warm water rose from below. Roots hung across it in pale skeins. Behind those roots, something pulsed—not the deep, unmistakable beat of the Pressure House, but a smaller answering contraction, delayed by several breaths.

The diagnostician outside signaled for me to return.

I signaled that I needed another moment.

Then the conduit shifted.

Nothing ruptured. No wall collapsed. The roots merely tightened as if a hand had closed around them, and the passage behind me narrowed by less than the width of a finger. That was enough. My ballast clasp caught. The tool roll turned crosswise. Silt rose and erased my feet.

I could have pulled hard. A visitor would have. A frightened child might have torn free, damaged the root mat, clouded the chapel intake, and perhaps returned alive.

But my first serious lesson had been stillness.

So I became still.

By the time the silt cleared, I could no longer leave by the way I had entered. The workers opened the bath wall and reached far enough to touch my tool line, but every attempt to widen the throat changed the vibration of the load-bearing roots. The gardener condemned the cut. The household protested. The diagnostician remained with a hand against the wall for three days, tapping instructions and apologies through the brick.

They meant to find another entrance.

Perhaps they did.

Perhaps by then I had already gone farther inward.

The seam descended beside the warm conduit, and a current moved through it strongly enough to carry a small body. I abandoned the tools one by one, then the outer scales of my clothing, then the ballast beads. I broke my emergency buoyancy cell but held the gas beneath my tongue instead of letting it lift me. With my ribs folded and one arm ahead, I followed the warmth.

The city inside the city was not a place of halls and machines. It was a mesh of omissions: brick cavities, dead pipes, root hollows, the interiors of obsolete walls. Spaces no builder had intended to join had been joined by dissolution, pressure, and patient growth. They formed routes too narrow for ordinary travel but extensive enough for water, roots, translucent grazers—and someone willing to become narrow.

At first, I survived by theft.

I pierced kelp bladders from behind walls and drew out oxygen. I scraped fungal threads from stone. I tapped air cells with hollow needles and sealed them afterward with wax. I slept curled around warm pipes and woke whenever a valve altered the current. Offerings left for the Uncounted sometimes reached me. More often I found them after another hand had been there.

That was how I learned I was not alone.

A cable coil would be moved into a dry niche. A broken lamp cell would disappear and return charged. White boundary bacteria would bloom in an inaccessible crack, spelling no recognized warning but bending around my hand when I approached. Once, while sleeping with my sternum against an old iron plate, I felt a touch-name tapped from the other side.

It was too long to belong to one person.

I answered anyway.

Years passed, though I would not measure them by years. I would measure them by shutter-openings transmitted faintly through stone; by harvests, when root strain altered; by the replacement of a nearby pump whose new rhythm I disliked for thirty-three cycles and then ceased to notice.

My body would change.

Not into a monster, and not all at once. My shoulders would draw inward. My ribs would become more flexible from continual compression. Mineral sealants, rubbed into wounds, would leave pale branching ridges along my sides. The breathing folds under my jaw would deepen in the oxygen-poor seepage between walls. My toes would grow strong enough to hold in cracks while both hands worked. I would crop my nails into wedges for scraping mortar and sensing vibration.

Sight would matter less. Most spaces would be too dark even for my eyes. I would read heat through my cheeks, current through the fine hairs of my forearms, structural strain through my palms. I would learn the private sounds of materials: brick before it powders, ceramic before it stars with fractures, a root before it changes from brace to wedge.

Eventually, I would stop stealing.

I would begin maintaining.

There are failures that cannot be reached from inhabited space without destroying the thing one means to save. A pinhole leak behind an ancestral wall. A root growing toward an air-cell seam. A cable whose insulation has become food for a pale, unofficial moss. I would reach these from within. I would bind, trim, graft, divert. I would use salvage filaments finer than hair. Where gaps could not be sealed by tools, I would press mineral paste into them with my fingers and remain there until it hardened.

People outside would notice only that the leak ceased.

They would leave an offering.

I would take the fine cable, the lamp cells, the food paste. I would leave the polished bolt with no thread untouched, because it was plainly decorative and therefore belonged to someone more than a useful thing could.

In time, households would develop customs around me without knowing they were mine. The bath children would tap the warm patch before entering. Chapel worshippers would release one extra bubble into the inverted cups. Conduit workers would mark the unexplained vibration on successive diagnostic slates until it became an accepted feature rather than a fault.

A story would arise that an ancestor had been mortared into the wall alive and now kept the cooling stream clean.

This would be inaccurate, but not offensively so.

My relation to the Pressure House would be more complicated.

Deep in the omitted spaces, its pulse would not be one sound. The city hears the principal beat, then silence. Inside the walls, I would hear what follows: braces taking strain, ceramic cells flexing, roots tightening around pipe collars, trapped pockets of air changing shape. Thousands of delayed answers would travel outward through different materials.

The Pressure House would strike once.

The city would reply for a long time.

Among those replies would be the smaller pulse I had found as a child. I would eventually reach it—not a second heart, but a chamber no larger than my torso, formed around an ancient valve enclosed by stone. Each central beat would force a membrane across the valve mouth. A little warm water would be drawn from somewhere deeper and released into the wall roots.

The valve would be old beyond naming. The surrounding rock would bear cuts like those on the House, but made for fingers of an unfamiliar number. A brass stem would emerge from the limestone and end in a handle no one could turn because most of it had become stone.

I would place both hands on it.

I would understand nothing.

Understanding would not be required for service.

So I would remain near that hidden valve. I would clear its membrane, feed the roots that cushion it, and trace the metallic sweetness of its seepage. When its delayed answer shortened, I would alter the nearby growth. When it lengthened, I would open a forgotten channel by scraping limestone grain after grain.

Perhaps I would report this to the Keepers of Interval. Not in person. I might tap observations through a brace during the hours when they slept against the House. Most would dismiss the signals as after-vibration. One would begin recording them.

A keeper with no ceramic counters yet.

They would lie with their cheek against the cylinder and wait after every pulse. Eventually, they would hear me answer from within: not words, but measured intervals. Beat, pause, two taps. Beat, longer pause, three.

We would develop a language of discrepancy.

They would never see me. They might not believe I was a person. I might not be certain they were one keeper rather than a succession of hands. But together we would discover that the ancient gauge falls slightly faster when the hidden valve’s answer weakens, and slightly slower when the warm seep runs clear.

This knowledge would be too incomplete to announce. Announcements provoke work; work provokes change; change may damage what diagnosis has barely distinguished.

So the keeper would add no doctrine and claim no discovery. They would arrange for offerings to be left near a disused cistern: ceramic needles, cultured sealant, a current gauge small enough to fit around one finger.

I would use them.

Much later, when my body could no longer travel far, I would begin to join the structure more literally.

The mineral ridges on my skin would thicken. Rootlets would enter the dead outer layers without pain. I would sleep longer against the warm valve, waking only for the pulse. I would still eat. I would still think. I would still possess the old spoken name that had nowhere to be spoken.

But the distinction between holding the wall and being held by it would become unimportant.

Outside, someone would knock politely.

A child, probably, daring another child to summon a wall-dweller.

I would answer.

Not immediately. One must not reward impatience. I would wait until they leaned close enough to feel through the brow and sternum. Then I would tap my touch-name: two quick contacts, a pause, pressure held.

The child would flee, delighted and terrified.

Years afterward, that child might become a diagnostician. They would return with a slate and instruments and insist the warm patch contained an anomalous cavity. Gardeners would object to excavation. The household would cite tradition. A Keeper of Interval, now carrying several counters, would place a palm against the wall.

I would feel all of them there: their heat, their caution, their competing rights to interrupt one another.

I would tap once.

The keeper would become still.

The wall would not be opened.

That is where I would find myself: not at the center, because the city has no single center; not outside its society, because outside is only another kind of dependence; not hidden in the romantic sense, because the wall would know me, the roots would use me, the cooling stream would pass my waste toward gardens, and strangers would trust repairs they could not attribute.

I would be a citizen reduced to function and enlarged into habitat.

A listener in the masonry.

A keeper of the interval after the Interval.

And when the deep pulse came, I would feel it before the sleepers, before the handrails, perhaps even before the black-ringed keepers against the House. It would enter through the ancient valve and pass across the mineral seams of my ribs.

For a moment, every crack around me would open by an immeasurable degree.

Warm water would move through.

Roots would draw tight.

Far away, thousands of sleeping hands would close around cords.

Then the pulse would fade through brick, iron, ceramic, mortar, bone.

In the long inhabited silence, I would hold the hidden valve open with the weight of my body and wait—not wondering whether the mechanism had stopped, but listening to learn which part of the city answered first.
