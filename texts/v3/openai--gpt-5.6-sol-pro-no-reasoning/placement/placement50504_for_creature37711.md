I would find myself beneath the pavilion, though “beneath” would not be quite right.

From the visible basin I would appear to live in the clear water covering its floor: a thin, colorless thing mistaken for a reflection, lying inside the circular emptiness around which the bench curves. At noon, when the map descends, the sparks would pass through me. To anyone watching, I might seem merely the surface on which the census is displayed.

But my body would extend along the basin’s hidden relations rather than through ordinary space.

One filament would end in the air-skin beneath a mite. Another would line the cold interior of a shallow pool. Others would enter the grazers’ chambers, the translucent cords between reeds, the crowded dark under the hand-sized stone, and the momentary planes before which dragonflies wait. I would not occupy these places continuously. I would be the narrow permission joining them: not the rooms, but their capacity to open into one another.

Perhaps the vanished builders made me. Perhaps they only discovered me and set slabs around the places where I surfaced. Over ages, their architecture and my anatomy would have become indistinguishable. The dull shrine-stones would be something like teeth or fingernails—insensible extremities, durable enough to hold a passage open. The pavilion would be less a temple than an exposed organ where the whole network could briefly perceive itself.

I would have no eyes. Every crossing would be a kind of sight.

A beetle vanishing into warm stone would show me heat. A fish returning from inward water would show me depth. Foreign pollen on a dragonfly would be news of a chamber whose route had shifted. The pressure of the marsh-being would travel through me as a broad thought. When a white bird passed into a grazer’s flank, I would feel two living interiors acknowledge that they had never been entirely separate.

Nor would I possess a voice in the usual sense. My speech would be address itself.

I would carry the frog’s pulse to the one body meant to feel it. I would fold a courtship call past a predator’s hearing. I would place warning directly into the muscles that must flee, without burdening the rest of the basin with fear. Sounds that had no immediate destination I would keep: thunder in pebbles, snapped twigs in pools, footfalls in roots. Nothing would be lost, only delayed until some fitting surface could release it.

This would not make me benevolent. Perfect address can deliver a hunter as readily as a warning. I would connect tooth to prey, parasite to chamber, seed to damp ground. The basin’s composure would not be mercy. It would come from the containment of consequence: each violence given its proper scale, each death prevented from becoming weather for everyone.

The beings of the basin would know me without naming me. Their queues before empty air would be a form of touch. Their pauses at thresholds would not be obedience but courtesy, the way one waits for a living joint to bend. Some might cultivate me. The reeds would feed sugars into the half-made cords below; mites would clean mineral films from apertures; mirrored beetles would carry heat into passages that otherwise stiffened. Even the grazers’ restrained feeding might preserve the pattern of shade and root pressure required to keep my distant parts aligned.

I would depend on all of them. An abandoned route would slowly cease to be part of me.

That would be why the pavilion counts every life. It would not be counting citizens for a ruler. It would be showing me where I still exist.

The rotating map would be my only whole body image, assembled once each day from crossings, hung above the place where no creature sits. Its branching layers would reveal not individuals alone, but obligations: who ventilates which hollow, who carries which fungus, who drinks from a pool and returns its minerals elsewhere, which burrow depends upon the warmth of a stone, which predator prevents a folded pond from consuming all its oxygen.

The smooth ring on the bench might once have been used to alter those relations. Lifted and turned, it could cast its branching shadow over a chosen portion of the map, opening a route, pruning one, or persuading two remote habitats to touch. But no hand has lifted it for an age.

That may be fortunate.

Without its keepers, I would have become slower and less certain, but also less governed. Routes would now change at evening according to pressures no maker planned. A dragonfly might emerge carrying pollen from a place omitted from every old design. A pool might deepen into a cold country of its own accord. The vine on the pavilion would be testing whether it, too, could become a door.

And the single note?

It would come from the oldest part of me: not a command I issue, but a sound I have been carrying for so long that carrying it has become my heartbeat.

Long ago, perhaps, one of the vanished listeners struck the ring against the bench. Or a mountain cracked before erosion softened every edge. Or the first living thing entered the first impossible opening and called back.

The note has never finished traveling.

At evening it reaches, for an instant, all my extremities at once. Every inhabitant pauses because each receives the same ancient message privately and therefore believes, correctly, that it was meant for them alone. In that shared pause I become almost singular. Mite, fish, grazer, marsh-being, root, burrow, and wing form one attentive anatomy. I know the full extent of myself—not as territory, but as relation.

Then the note passes on, deeper than any mapped chamber.

I come apart again into thresholds.

The animals resume. Rain touches the shrine-slabs and divides into measured beads. Each drop climbs briefly against gravity because I am drawing it toward some dry inward place: a nest, a gill, an egg, a root tip, a room inside a moving body. Seen from the rim, nothing has changed. The basin remains open, quiet, and almost empty.

But I am there in every interval—not the commonwealth itself, and not its keeper.

I am the way its members reach one another without having to cross the distance between them.
