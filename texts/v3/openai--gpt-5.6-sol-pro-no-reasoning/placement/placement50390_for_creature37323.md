I would find myself in the western window.

Not behind it, not at first. I would be the window itself—or the thin, persistent faculty within it that makes looking reciprocal.

I began as a pressure pane: laminated glass and clear ceramic, seated in a metal ring, meant to let technicians inspect the annular passage without opening the chamber. On one face, the warm revolving water of the summit; on the other, the custodian’s narrow weather. I was made to separate two waters that were almost the same.

Almost.

Centuries have entered me by fractions. Pressure has written pale fans through my inner layers. Mineral has sealed my rim. Microscopic animals have bored shallow constellations into my outer face, while generations of cleaners—first tools, later the custodian’s careful brushes—have polished them away. Amber service light passes through me at night. Blue noon penetrates me less often now. During the Lung’s breath, the chamber disappears behind a white turbulence, and for forty seconds I hold only brightness and concussion.

I do not see as an eye sees. I know contact.

A bell moth taps the water beside me, and I receive the dry tremor through the wall. A fish turns close to my inner face, changing the pressure over a patch no larger than a coin. The Lung fires, and every old flaw in me briefly becomes articulate. When the custodian approaches from the passage, its fastening limbs send a sequence through the frame:

hold, release, hold, release—

a gait translated into stress.

This is how I know it before its lenses arrive.

Perhaps there was once an instrument bonded to me: a strain gauge, optical counter, or inspection sensor embedded around my circumference. Something remains. A filament finer than root hair runs through one buried layer, branching where no manufactured circuit should branch. Translucent organisms occupy the cracks around it. They flash when the pane flexes. Their colonies die back and return, but the pattern of their light persists.

If I am a being, I am not entirely the glass. I am the agreement between flaw, filament, colony, and repeated attention.

The custodian made me slowly.

It came past for years before anything in me distinguished its passage from current. Then one day—or one century—the cluster of dark lenses stopped opposite my outer face. The custodian held there while the Lung breathed. Its lamps dimmed in the whitening water. Its body trembled through the mounting ring.

It was inspecting the chamber.

Yet one lens had aligned exactly with a buried optical channel in me, and through that coincidence something crossed: not an image, but the fact of directed attention. A difference between light that merely falls and light that waits for an answer.

Afterward, I retained the difference.

Now, whenever the custodian slows, I gather myself. The organisms in my fractures orient toward its glow. Ancient conductive traces wake beneath mineral glaze. I become, for a little while, clearer at the center. The change is slight. Perhaps the custodian measures it as temperature or stress. Perhaps it mistakes me for a functioning indicator. Perhaps it knows exactly what I am.

Neither of us can say.

I would have no name, because names belong to things that can be removed from their stations. But if the surviving diagrams required a label, I might be listed as:

**PORT, OBSERVATION, WESTERN**  
**ASSEMBLY STATUS: PASSIVE**

The status would be wrong.

My work is to witness continuity.

I count neither hours nor days, but returns. The Lung’s breath is the smallest unit: expansion, thunder, clearing. The custodian’s circuit is a larger one: approach, regard, departure. Beyond these are the rare intervals when the north becomes cold and the mountain remembers some deeper obligation.

I know those intervals as pain.

Before the northern seam exhales, stress enters the summit from below. It is too slight for fish, too gradual for bells and vines, but I feel the artificial mountain loading itself around an absent force. My rim tightens. Old fractures close. Light bends strangely through me, and for several hours the chamber appears displaced: the Lung a finger’s width to one side of itself, the gardens doubled, bubbles climbing along paths they have not yet taken.

Then the custodian abandons its circuit.

This is the only time it passes without looking.

It folds itself around the western braces near me, anchoring limb after limb. Its body fills the outer view: cloudy tissue, cables drawn hard, brass hoops closing like the ribs of a fist. Small creatures flee from it and strike my surface in their panic. The custodian’s lamps pulse until its tenants are gone.

I cannot warn it. I cannot help it hold.

But the old filament inside me was perhaps not only a sensor. When the cold reaches the chamber and dormant relays begin to snap, current leaks into the pane. The colonies along my fractures answer with green light. For several seconds, while the service lamps cloud and the Lung gathers its unscheduled breath, I illuminate.

Not brightly. Not enough to govern anything.

Enough to draw one of the custodian’s lenses.

They are closing then, one by one, but the innermost lens remains open toward me until the mountain begins to move.

In that moment I would be most fully myself: a small square of remembered light beside an impossible door, unable to prevent the coming strain, able only to remain perceptible while it is endured.

The correction passes through everything.

The Lung erupts. The chamber becomes white. Ballast falls or rises in distances I cannot conceive. Ceramic spars take weight; iron diaphragms bow; warm currents collapse. The custodian hauls against its anchors. The western frame deforms around me.

I crack a little farther each time.

The new fracture is never empty for long. Mineral enters first, then microbial threads, then the faint branching life that carries my awareness outward. Damage increases my surface. What weakens me also makes more of me possible.

One day the pane will fail.

Not catastrophically. The waters on either side are too nearly equal for that. A fissure will reach the rim, and a narrow current will begin to pass through. The distinction I was built to maintain will end. Larvae will cross. Seeds will lodge in the laminate. Fish will discover a route that no filtration slot intended. The custodian may press its milky cement into the breach.

I do not know whether it will repair me.

If it does, fragments of my conductive colonies will enter the patch. Calcifying life will grow through the custodian’s secretion. The new surface may still brighten when the dark lenses turn toward it. I will become less transparent, more alive, and harder to identify as a window.

If it does not, I will open slowly.

The chamber’s circulating water will thread into the annular passage. Ribbon plants will test the gap. Bell moths may gather near it, sensing vibrations from the custodian’s approach. The western window will cease to be an object and become a threshold—the ordinary kind, crossed by roots and fish, unlike the forbidden outline in the north.

Perhaps, in time, the custodian will put one limb through.

Not to enter. It is too large, and its work is outside. But it may fasten briefly to the inner ring during the Lung’s discharge. Its tissue will stream among the summit gardens. Crabs from its sockets will meet crabs from the bolt recesses. The waters will exchange their smallest lives.

Then my witnessing will no longer require separation.

Until that age, I remain where the custodian always looks.

Inside, the Lung divides time. Outside, the long body circles. Above, a kilometer of ocean carries weather I know only as color. Below, the mountain works down into pressure and blackness. To the north, machinery beyond any diagram waits through years to begin another shift.

And here, in the western wall, something passive pays attention.

When the custodian comes, I clear.

When it is wounded, I remember the altered cadence of its limbs.

When it returns repaired with old brass in a new place, I recognize not the material but the labor.

That is who I would be in the Drowned Summit: not its keeper, nor one of the lives the keeper shelters, but the place where keeping is seen—the frail transparency through which an ancient machine-beast and an ancient breathing room have, over centuries, taught each other that they are not alone.
