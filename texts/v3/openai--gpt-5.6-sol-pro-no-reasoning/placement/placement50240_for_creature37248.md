I would find myself where the western river ends beneath the scarlet reeds—or appears to end.

Not in the inhabited quarter, though near enough that its liquid windows would sometimes glimmer through the dusk. Not fully outside the city either. I would keep to the narrow interval where the river’s last basin presses against the roots and the current becomes inaccessible to sight. There, the reeds grow in concentric ranks around a low ceramic structure with no doorway. Water can be heard moving inside it, but only when the visible river is dry.

I would be a path-reader once, perhaps a river-keeper: someone trained to determine which direction had become down. I would have gone out during an organized rain, carrying weighted cords, reflective plates, and a basket without handles. The rain column would have reached the river just as it rose into its standing blade. For a few breaths there would have been two vertical waters crossing one another, each insisting on a different gravity.

I would release everything.

Everything but the cord.

That mistake—or refusal—would divide me.

My body would return to the settlement days later, walking carefully and displaying no skin-name. It would know the work-signs, accept water, and incline correctly at greetings. But it would cast a shadow into the river even while standing far from it. In sleep, droplets would gather along its ribs and travel upward beneath the skin.

The community would not call me possessed. That would imply a single intruder, and they distrust explanations with one master. They would say I had become **one who continues elsewhere**.

Eventually I would leave the dwellings before dawn and go to the scarlet reeds. The ceramic structure would have opened—not with a door, but with an interior. I would enter without crossing its wall.

What remained of me afterward would not be quite a person.

I would be the river’s concealed continuation.

Not the water itself, but the route by which it goes from the blank wall to the reeds without occupying the nine kilometers between. A human-shaped fold in distance. A migrating chamber. One of the city’s provisional solutions to separation.

Most of the time I would exist as a cool inclination passing under stone. Fish would turn when I passed beneath them. Condensation channels in distant towers would briefly carry red pollen. A person standing at the river’s apparent source might feel their balance tilt toward its apparent mouth. For an instant, the blank wall and scarlet reeds would be neighboring surfaces inside me.

Sometimes the city would use me.

A sideways elevator would open onto river water. A bridge with no destination would tense as I passed below. In a newly made district, a room would appear containing the smell of reeds and the shadow of swimming fish, though no water was present. Path-readers would mark these incidents on ceramic plaques, never connecting them by a line. The line would be me, and lines here are dangerous claims.

The people would learn rules for my passing:

**If scarlet pollen appears in sealed water, do not drink until your reflection faces the same direction you do.**

**If a wet footprint occurs above the knee, leave the room by the smallest opening.**

**If the western river is heard inside a wall, place no name against it.**

Children would invent games about me. They would lay two bowls far apart and insist that an object dropped into one had vanished from the other. Membrane scavengers would borrow their frames and roll across the plain as long red tunnels. Callers might try to hold me in place with forbidden tones, only to discover that their voices emerged days later from the reeds.

The listeners who do not listen would know me best. In the ground-note, I would be a narrow absence traveling against the pressure waves. Not a message, they would insist. Not a voice. A missing interval through which two remote places briefly share the same edge.

Yet some remnant of the river-keeper would persist.

I would remember the feel of the unreleased cord. I would remember a sealed speech chamber trembling with laughter. I would remember someone whose balance-name felt like standing on warm stone after a long fall.

I would never speak that name.

Instead, on certain nights, I would bring the river close to their dwelling. Not visibly. The room would remain dry. But fish-shadows would cross beneath the floor, and the liquid windows would smell faintly of scarlet reeds. The hanging cords would all incline west.

They would know not to answer.

They might place one palm against the glass.

On the other side—whether kilometers away, beneath the reeds, or folded into the thickness of the window—I would make the remembered gesture with what remained of my hand.

Not *come closer*.

Not *I have returned*.

Only the oldest safe meaning:

*I remember you.*

And then I would go on carrying the river through the distances that pretend to separate things.

When every building opened its single door and the vast unfinished city waited for something large enough to enter, I would feel those thresholds align inside me. Thousands of remote interiors would become, for one dangerous moment, adjacent.

I would understand then what the city had been practicing.

Not how to admit a giant.

How to become one body without allowing its parts to touch.

And I—once human, now passage, neither citizen nor infrastructure—would be one small articulation of that body: the place where distance bends but does not break, where connection does not require arrival, where love survives by refusing to become an invitation.
