I find myself below.

Not in the true dark beneath the shelf, where the under-sails open their kilometer-wide folds, and not upon the brilliant upper skin, where every life must cling or fly. I inhabit the intermediate country: the galleries hollowed by wind through the crown, where daylight enters through old scars and travels for kilometers inside translucent tissue.

I am a **seamkeeper**.

That name suggests a duty, perhaps even a profession, but neither exists here in any deliberate sense. I am an animal shaped around a useful compulsion. I go where the plateau is deciding whether to heal.

My body is long and jointed, narrow enough to pass between compressed leaf-sheaths but broad enough to bridge a fresh crack. Along my underside are hundreds of gripping lamellae, alternating magnetic pads with hooks that catch the plant’s fibers. I have no eyes. In the galleries, light is unreliable: dazzling through one membrane, absent beyond the next. Instead, I perceive strain.

Every surface speaks through pressure. A load-bearing rib hums with slow tension. A dead spar answers sharply. Hot oil moving behind a wall produces a soft drag against my sensory plates. Wind entering a distant rupture changes the pitch of an entire gallery. I read these vibrations through six branching limbs held against the substrate. When the crown flexes, I feel possible futures opening around me.

Most creatures flee a growing seam.

I enter it.

Freshly exposed tissue is dangerously reactive. Volatiles smoke from ruptured channels; salts bloom into cutting crystals; contractile fibers convulse as chemical messages arrive from distant organs. My armor is layered ceramic and carbon felt, polished by the walls through which I move. It seals tightly enough to survive the first poisonous breath of a wound.

I feed on what appears there: oxidized oils, damaged membranes, fungal films, small scavengers, and the tender emergency tissue the plateau extrudes before it has determined what the injury means. Yet feeding is only the beginning. The materials I cannot digest accumulate in chambers along my flanks. Different chambers hold catalysts, salts, metal grains, dormant spores, and molecular fragments gathered from many provinces.

As I cross a wound, I leave some of them behind.

The plateau responds according to the mixture. One trail provokes sealing wax. Another encourages a flexible hinge. Another suppresses frantic growth and allows a crack to remain open as a lung. I do not understand these outcomes. I follow tastes, pressures, temperature gradients, and the deep satisfaction that comes when an unstable vibration resolves beneath my feet.

Sometimes my trail repairs the crown.

Sometimes it teaches the wound to stay wounded.

That is why the plant alternately feeds and hunts my kind. Along certain galleries it grows dark nodules rich in the compounds we need to molt. Elsewhere it closes valves ahead of us, floods passages with hot hydrocarbons, or raises combs of crystal that tear our soft undersides. A seamkeeper useful in one district may carry the wrong chemistry into another. We are remedies that can become infections simply by crossing an invisible border.

We migrate alone. A congregation would be too chemically loud. We know one another mostly by old trails: a metallic bitterness on a wall, a pattern of shallow bites, an interval tapped into a hollow spar. Near breeding time, these marks draw us toward ancient circular wounds where the crown flexes but has never fully closed.

There, for a few hours, dozens may gather.

We do not mate face to face. Each animal presses a packet of living tissue into a suitable seam and continues onward. If two or more packets meet beneath the plant’s scar layer, they combine. The offspring begins as a soft strip incorporated into the healing wound. For its first season, it is almost an organ of the plateau. It receives oils through the surrounding tissue and moves only when the seam opens again.

Some never separate. They are sealed into load-bearing ribs and live for years as twitching sensory knots, neither wholly animal nor plant. When strain increases, they contract, changing the distribution of force through the crown. Their descendants may pass the site generations later and feel their pulses in the wall.

Others emerge small, white, and nearly boneless. They follow the chemical shadow of an adult until their armor hardens. Most choose the wrong vibration. They enter a closing seam, a furnace duct, or a vein about to reverse its flow. The plateau incorporates them without malice.

My present gallery slopes eastward through tissue older than any migration. Above me, ironmouth herds cross a pressure ridge. I feel each locked footfall descending through the ceiling: six contacts, a pause, six more. Far below, an under-sail asks its slow question of the shelf. The answer arrives much later as a change in exhaust, followed by a tremor too broad to have a single source.

Between those rhythms is another signal.

A discontinuity.

I stop and spread my limbs.

The tower-roar reaches me through kilometers of living material, stripped of its higher notes. The gallery should be flexing in three directions. Instead, one portion of the wall is silent. Not still—silence is never still here—but mechanically absent, as though the tissue beyond it has ceased to belong to the same body.

I taste the seam.

There is salt armor, old wax, recent stress compounds. Beneath those lies an oil signature I do not know.

Not decay. Not native growth.

A seed from another high country has struck somewhere beyond the wall.

Perhaps decades ago it punched into the crown and began its chemical negotiation. The surface scar may already appear mature: a white clearing, polished by wind, crossed by herds that know nothing of what lies beneath. But here, in the intermediate dark, two continent-sized organisms have reached one another.

The foreign tissue is not advancing like a predator. It is sending minute offers into the seam—catalysts, membrane fragments, pulses of charge. The plateau answers with blights and sealing crystals. Each answer alters the invader. Each alteration changes the next answer.

Parasite, offspring, graft, invader.

The distinction has not yet been made.

My forelimbs begin to tremble with the urge to cross.

If I do, I will carry substances from one body into the other. I may help the plateau recognize and destroy the foreign growth. I may temper its defenses and make grafting possible. I may introduce an error that turns negotiation into a tumor kilometers wide.

There is no overseer to forbid me. No mind asks for caution. Only pressures, hungers, and inherited compulsions converge in my body.

Above, the light changes. Billions of scales tilt together. The ironmouths flatten and lock themselves down.

A bass note enters the gallery.

The seam opens by the width of one limb, and unfamiliar blue-green light shines through.

I place a gripping pad on either side.

For one instant I belong to both countries.

Then the note rises, the walls draw apart, and I go in.
