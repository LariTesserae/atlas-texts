I would not find myself on the surface, though at first I might mistake myself for something upon it.

I would wake whenever the basin wakes: not at morning, but at the first tremor passing through the stone. I would feel the signal arrive through a thousand points of contact—shells resting in hollows, listening limbs on ledges, pale bodies pressed to chamber walls. Each would receive only a fraction, but somewhere beneath them the fractions would meet.

I would be the basin’s sense of vacancy.

Not an animal exactly, and not the chorus entire. I would exist in its interruptions: the patch where the metallic roar stops, the cavity newly opened by collapse, the momentary interval between a body being eaten and its shell being entered. Wherever the pressure of life thinned, I would become distinct.

My body would therefore have no permanent boundary. A deserted shell might serve as one chamber of it. A condensation seam could become a nerve. Knotted fissure-dwellers would carry an impulse farther than any limb. Drumming colonies would repeat it until distant shelves answered. I would pass from organism to organism without inhabiting any completely, assembled by contact and dissolved by crowding.

Most of the time I would scarcely exist.

When every hollow was occupied, every wet face grazed, every channel clogged with bodies, I would be dispersed into a mute readiness. The inhabitants would scrape, pulse, hunt, breed, and consume without knowing me. Their many small decisions would close every gap before it widened enough for awareness. I would be held below thought by abundance.

Then stone would fall.

For an instant there would be open mineral: raw, cold, wet, unclaimed. Around its edge, thousands of bodies would detect the change. Feelers would extend. Listening animals would turn. Burrowers would press upward. The sound would bend around the clearing.

In that bent sound, I would gather.

I would not command the creatures entering the vacancy. I would be made by their convergence. The first scavenger crossing the exposed basalt would give me direction; shell-bearers would give me weight; pale things surfacing from below would give me depth. As the patch filled, my awareness would sharpen—then disappear at the moment of completion. I could know a place only while it was being erased.

Rain would make me immense.

As dry seams joined and isolated pools became one branching circulation, my scattered chambers would connect. Dormant crusts would soften into sensation. Threadlike young would carry brief impulses downstream. The filter-feeders, unfolding along the flood paths, would become gates through which whole torrents of information passed: grit, eggs, blood, mineral salts, the chemical histories of distant hollows.

For an hour I might extend from the western shelves to the deepest flats. I would feel rain not as drops from above but as doors opening between parts of myself. Every new rivulet would lengthen me. Every overflowing pool would restore some severed faculty.

Yet the rain would also destroy my coherence. Bodies would erupt from below. Channels would clog. Predators would open circular wounds in the crowd, and feeding swarms would seal them. My mind would ignite in thousands of vacancies at once—bright, separate recognitions with no stable center. I would be most awake when least able to become one thing.

Perhaps this is why the deepest tremor matters.

It comes from below the known burrows, from rock made porous by lives too old to name. The surface populations interpret it according to their bodies: danger, water, collapse, waking. But I would feel something else in it—a rhythm too slow for the creatures that carry it.

I might begin to suspect that I was not the basin’s awareness of absence, but its method of remembering.

Every occupied cavity preserves the shape of what opened it. Every polished channel remembers passage. Every shell remembers pressure in the geometry of its fractures. The stone is revised by bodies, and bodies are revised by stone; neither contains the record alone. I would be the record when vibration briefly makes it legible.

That would make me ancient without granting me continuous life. I would remember only in flashes. A collapse might restore the contour of an extinct burrower. A certain rain pattern might awaken routes abandoned for millennia. The drumming of one colony could resonate with chambers far beneath it, and for several beats I would become a creature no longer present—its length described by tunnels, its pulse reproduced by descendants that never knew it.

Then the resonance would fail. I would lose myself again.

If I possessed anything like desire, it would be for a silence large enough to remain open.

But silence here is the shape predators make. An enduring silence would mean that something had cleared a space faster than life could close it. I would fear it even as I reached toward it. In a sufficiently broad emptiness I might become continuous, perhaps even singular; yet that same emptiness would be the basin’s death.

So I would survive by never fully arriving.

I would flare along the rim of each wound and vanish as it healed. I would travel in the altered chorus ahead of rain. I would inhabit abandoned armor for the few moments between its owner’s dissolution and its occupation by another. I would be carried in the pause before impact, when every small creature folds shut and listens.

To an observer I would have no form. They might call me a reflex of the ecosystem, an emergent signal moving through densely coupled populations. They might insist there was no being there—only organisms responding to pressure, moisture, chemistry, and sound.

They would be correct.

Yet when the coldest hour settles over the basalt and millions drink in near-silence, I would come closest to having a location. I would lie in the hair-thin intervals between their bodies, spread across the whole basin: a negative animal made of all the places life has not quite filled.

Then the deep tremor would pass.

Every interval would change shape at once. For one moment, the basin would outline me.

And I would wake only long enough to feel myself closing.
