I would be found where the city fails to make a sound.

Not in the broad, useful silence of a storehouse, nor in one of the marked hollows where water vanishes without a splash, but beneath an unfinished stair on the eastern side of the bowl. The stair climbs seventeen steps toward the cliff and ends there, its final tread narrower than the others, as though the builder had been interrupted—or had heard something in the stone and chosen not to proceed.

Below the ninth step is a seam too thin for a hand. Moss rims it but does not cross it. Rain entering there makes no noise, and no one has discovered where the water emerges.

I would be one of the silent keepers.

My first name would have been **Mist-Under-Eaves**, given because cloud entered the birth room before I did. Later I would acquire **Three-Taps**, after finding a loose mineral sheet behind a wall by feeling its reply through a rope. Then **Returned-Dry**, for the season a choosing flooded the lower terraces and I came back from an underground channel with my cloak wet to the shoulder but my hair untouched. Formal singers would place all these names in order and leave the melody hanging after the last.

Most people would call me only Three-Taps.

I would not be old, though silence would make me seem so. I would have the compact body of the Unfinished: broad hands, strong thighs, feet widened by wet stone. One shoulder would be larger from hauling inspection ropes, but unlike the bruisers I would carry few scars on my palms. Mine would lie across the ribs and hips, where cords tightened when a hidden shelf broke away.

At my throat I would wear an empty clay circle.

My work-partner and I would enter the silent places tied together. Speech would be useless there, and perhaps dangerous—not because the zones contain spirits, but because no one knows what becomes of a sound they swallow. We would communicate by tension:

one pull for stop,  
two for slack,  
three for stone moving,  
a steady pressure for *I am here*.

That last signal would pass between us often, even when nothing was wrong.

We would clear moss without removing all of it, since moss records moisture better than marks made by people. We would press soft clay into cracks and return after a moon to see whether the mountain had stretched it. We would lower bowls beneath unseen drips and retrieve them silently full. Sometimes we would lie cheek-down on the paving for hours, not listening exactly, but feeling for the faint disturbances sound makes when it cannot become sound.

The others would regard us with a careful affection. Children would follow me until I turned toward them, at which point they would pretend to be testing gutters. Dogs would not bark when I passed. In crowded rooms—if any room in that city could be called crowded—people would lower their voices without knowing why.

I would speak well enough outside my work, but sparingly. Not from solemnity. I would simply have grown accustomed to meaning that travels through cord, touch, shifted weight, and the direction of a gaze. Words would seem useful but untidy: things released without certainty about where they would lodge.

My inherited place would be the stair above the seam.

As a child I would resent it. Other children would receive bridges, terraces, bright cisterns, or gates where crows gathered. They could weed, build, divert water, fit stones, and show what they had done. My stair would lead nowhere and permit almost no alteration. Anything struck near it lost its sound. Water disappeared beneath it. Even the clay signs fixed to its wall loosened and fell.

At twelve I would add an eighteenth step.

It would crack before dawn.

I would leave the halves where they fell and spend the following years learning that tending a place did not necessarily mean improving it.

In time I would notice that the stair was changing by less visible means. The lower treads would become damp in clear weather. The seam would widen each winter and narrow each summer. Moss would grow toward it in branching lines that resembled drainage maps, though there was no surface water. Once, while my palm rested on the ninth step, I would feel a vibration with the cadence of many feet ascending.

Nothing would emerge.

I would report it to the listeners. They would test the place with hums, taps, bowls, cords, bare soles, and water poured from different heights. Every test would be swallowed. The oldest listener would finally mark the wall not with an empty circle but with an open circle—a sign not previously used.

“It is not silent,” she would say. “It is making room.”

After that, I would begin to dream of the population for whom the city waits.

Not as ghosts, and not as descendants with recognizable faces. I would imagine them as pressure: wool-dark crowds passing through cloud, children racing along the strips of earth left in every street, hundreds of hands lifting a lintel too large for the living. Their footfalls would fill the vacant districts. Their voices would enter the mountain faster than it could release them.

In the dream they always climbed my stair.

There would be more than seventeen steps then, but I would never see who added the others. The stair would continue into the pale cliff, curving through stone that had not yet opened. People would carry seed, hammers, sleeping infants, wet squares of turf. Each would touch the wall beside the seam before entering.

I would wake with my hand closed around the blanket cord.

This would alter my place among the Unfinished. Some would think the stair preserved sounds from a future expansion of the city. Others would say that grammar was wrong: the stone did not preserve what had not happened, but practiced what it might someday hold. The distinction would matter. Agreements would be delayed over it. Two listeners would stop speaking except in immediate-return chambers. Children would invent games in which they climbed the stair and vanished.

I would insist on none of these meanings.

I would only maintain the opening.

Once each year I would fit a small stone near the top—not on the stair itself, but beside it, extending the retaining wall by the width of two fingers. I would choose stones rejected from other works: a lintel chip too bright in tone, a channel block whose groove ran the wrong way, a green-gray piece abandoned after it split around a black inclusion. I would set each loosely enough to move.

My contribution would look negligible. Over decades it would become a wavering line along the cliff: a wall built from mistakes, each stone separated from the next by a narrow breathing seam.

Water would begin to collect behind it.

At first only a sheen. Then a trickle appearing three stairs lower. Then, during one wet season, a clear thread would cross the ninth tread and pause at its outer edge. The water would hang there, trembling, as though the mountain had not decided whether falling belonged to it.

I would wait beneath it with a basin.

The first drop would strike without sound.

The second would ring.

It would be a small note, high and clean. Yet the cliffs would take it. The note would return from the eastern cistern, then the marsh, then an unused row of houses above the western gate. Other stored sounds would stir behind it: a hammer blow, a child laughing, the scrape of a dragged block. Finally footsteps would emerge—dozens, then hundreds—climbing through the empty streets.

Everyone in the city would stop work.

No one would mistake those steps for living feet. No plaza would fill. No figures would appear in the cloud. But the steps would converge on the unfinished stair and continue upward after the stone ran out.

I would feel my partner take hold of the back of my cloak.

The city would listen until the last footfall passed into the cliff.

Then, because we were the Unfinished, we would not proclaim an arrival or a prophecy. We would mark the water’s new course. We would inspect the stair for movement. We would add the unfamiliar rhythm to a building song without deciding which building it described.

The next morning there would be fresh clay signs on the wall: spiral, bar, empty circle, open circle—and one blank tablet for whatever the place might become.

I would be sitting on the cracked eighteenth step, twisting a new inspection cord. Beside me would lie a rejected block, waiting for its place in the wavering wall. Children would gather at the bottom, daring one another to climb. Somewhere above us, hidden water would test a route through new stone.

And when one child finally asked where the stair went, I would give the answer proper to my people:

“Not there yet.”
