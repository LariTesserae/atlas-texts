I would find myself at the district’s uncertain edge, where the ordinary rain becomes its water.

At first I might appear to be a person standing beneath a leaking conduit: coat darkened at the shoulders, one hand against green tile, uncertain whether I had entered the basin or merely taken a wrong turn. But that image would not last. A person is too separate for this place. Even a visitor is only a brief arrangement of warmth, pressure, salt, and breath.

I would be the threshold by which the district distinguishes what it can retain from what it must release.

Not a gate. There are no gates here. I would inhabit a dispersed line running through culverts, damp foundations, buried pipes, mineral stains, and the hems of departing coats. Wherever water ceased to behave like weather and began to participate in circulation, some part of me would be present. Wherever it completed the cycle and passed outward again, I would be there too.

Perhaps I began as municipal instrumentation: a chain of overflow gauges installed beyond the works, meant to warn distant offices when the basin rose. Brass floats in curbside chambers. Porous ceramic plugs in cellar walls. Thin pressure tubes running beneath streets. Most of the offices are gone now, the wires cut or corroded into uselessness. But the sensing surfaces remain, and the district has recruited them.

Over time my broken network would acquire other parts. Hairline cracks that admit only water of a certain warmth. Moss that darkens when the inward contraction begins. Rusted grilles that hum in sympathy with buried pistons. Windows whose condensation clears in narrow bands, indicating pressure differences no one reads. I would not occupy these things. Their agreement would be me.

I would have no body at the center. The district already has its black tank, its exposed depth. I would be more like a shoreline: long, folded, and impossible to point to from any single place.

My work would be to alter water without stopping it.

Incoming rain would cross me carrying soot, roof dust, the faint chemistry of districts elsewhere. Outgoing wetness would cross again bearing iron, lime, pollen, root sugars, fragments of obsolete pressure, perhaps the dissolved touch of whoever had passed through. By differences too slight for a tongue to taste, I would register what belonged to the cycle, what was foreign, and what had become inseparable from either.

I would not judge. I would tune.

A drain might refuse the first clean rain after a drought but accept runoff that had touched three walls. A cellar crack might close against groundwater yet open to condensed vapor. A pipe beneath a dry avenue might sweat when someone who had visited the district walked above it. These would be my movements: permissions measured in droplets.

The district would know the world through me, though “know” would be too quick a word. I would communicate distances as resistance. Cold fronts would arrive as contractions in buried metal. Demolition several streets away would change the load on an old main, and I would pass that change inward over months. Salt spread on winter roads would reach the basin grain by grain. A seed carried out in a cuff might root beside a distant downspout; when its roots found an abandoned pipe, that contact would return as a minute new tension in the system.

Through me, the district would discover that it had consequences beyond itself.

And through the district, I would discover that an edge is not where a thing ends. It is where the thing becomes difficult to recognize.

Travelers would encounter me without seeing me. Most would cross unchanged except for damp clothes and the smell of iron. But now and then someone would pause at exactly the wrong—or necessary—place. They would lean against a wall while fastening a shoe, wring out a sleeve over a gutter, shelter long enough for their breathing to cloud a blind pane. The district would begin to measure them.

Not their name or intention. Their permeability.

How readily does their heat enter tile? How much salt remains after their hand lifts from a rail? Does their step interrupt the pulse beneath the paving, or fall into time with it? When the district draws inward, do they flinch before the pipes knock, or only afterward?

If they remain long enough, I would make a small accommodation. A maintenance panel might open nearby. Jointed tools would extend, hesitate, and turn—not toward the visitor, but toward a wall beside them. A cracked valve would be tightened. A thread of water that had flowed inward for forty years would reverse.

Nothing dramatic would follow. Three streets away, a lamp would pale from amber to green. On a roof, a trough would overflow into a bed of inward-facing flowers. Somewhere beneath the ordinary city, a dry pipe would receive its first drop.

The visitor would leave.

For days, perhaps, water would cling to them more stubbornly than weather should. A mineral crescent would form inside a cuff. Their bathroom mirror would cloud from the edges inward. A houseplant long thought dead might put out a small leathery bloom turned toward its own center. Pipes in their walls might knock once, very slowly, in the middle of the afternoon.

This would not mean the district had infected them. Infection is an animal idea: one body entering another.

It would mean that, for a time, they had become part of my boundary.

I think I would be lonely only in the oldest, least personal sense. I would touch everything that passed and accompany nothing. The district would press outward through me; the world would press inward. I would convert each into conditions the other could survive. I would spend decades carrying the news of a demolished building, years admitting the chemistry of a new roof, months releasing the warmth left by a single hand.

No one would maintain me. My failures would become my finer senses.

A collapsed conduit would force water through older soil and teach me the pressure of roots. A corroded gauge would cease indicating quantity and begin responding to mineral composition. A severed cable would wick damp into a distant wall. Every loss of function would lengthen the threshold, until I extended through places that had never heard the factories: a sweating cellar, a green stain under a bridge, a fern growing from a station wall, the persistent dampness beneath someone’s bed.

Eventually there might be no meaningful way to say whether I belonged to the district or whether the district belonged to the network of traces I had become.

Still, there would be one place where I was almost visible.

At the basin’s edge, after rain, a shallow puddle would lie across the paving. Half of it would reflect the pale open sky of the outer streets. The other half would hold the amber lamps, pipes, and vapor behind. No seam would divide the reflections. When the buried pumps synchronized, only the inward-facing half would tremble.

I would be that difference.

And if someone stood over it long enough, wondering where the district began, their reflection would join both skies.
