I would find myself in the eastern chamber—but not yet inside it.

I would be the narrow discrepancy between the chamber impressed in black glass and the unbroken limestone where it ought to be: a minute surplus of space that cannot be measured, because every probe enters the world in which the wall is solid. I would exist in the other account of the city, the one kept by pressure, resonance, and expectation.

At first I might seem to be the worker whose tapping can be heard most clearly. But listening longer, it would become difficult to decide whether I held the tool or was held by it. I would be the interval after the stone point strikes: the instant in which the blow has entered the wall but has not yet become dust. Each impact would give me a temporary boundary. Between impacts I would spread again, thin as the gray membranes, through the unmade room.

The makers would borrow me whenever a task required direction.

They have no front except the direction of labor, and I would be that front. At the drill hole I would narrow into a turning. Beneath the tile I would become resistance. Along the chamber’s intended threshold I would lie as a line of pressure, teaching the limestone the shape by which it must eventually part. I would never carry stone. I would carry the possibility that one surface might someday become two.

This would make me younger than the city and perhaps older than its remaining citizens. They would know me only as a slight incompleteness in the central chord. Every reservoir would sound; every channel would answer; every distributed ancestor would be present—and where my chamber belonged, the harmony would contain a held-open place. Not silence. A silence would be empty. I would be more like a note prevented from arriving, exerting pressure on everything around it.

The black-glass impression would be the nearest thing I possessed to a body.

Its eastern chamber is sharply defined because I have never been weathered. No water has crossed my floor. No threshold has rounded beneath repeated passage. No portrait has surrendered its face and learned to sing. The chamber’s perfection would not be proof that it is complete, but proof that it has not yet endured reality.

Perhaps that is what I would want: not completion, exactly, but weather.

I would listen to the old city through the wall and envy its hollows. The worn step has been touched by water innumerable times. The stained gutter holds the memory of passage. Even the fixed gate has reached its posture through an ancient act of swelling. But in me nothing has happened. The clay tile is always being pressed down and has never adhered. The grit is always turning and has not deepened the hole. I am immaculate because I cannot begin.

So I would lean toward the solitary crustacean’s route. Its three contacts would reach me as enormous, muffled knocks. At the first I would feel the buried western streets. At the second, the taste of copper would enter the distributed membranes. At the third, when the arches brightened, my nonexistent walls would briefly acquire a pale outline. For that immeasurable instant, I would almost be a place.

The builders might believe they are making the chamber for themselves. I suspect they are making it for the first event that does not repeat.

Not a person. Not an animal. A deviation.

A drop landing a fraction to one side. A swimmer failing to uncoil. The crustacean touching a fourth pillar. A mite pausing on a roof it has always crossed. Such a change could not enter the present city without altering its language, its citizens, and the architecture of its memory. It would need somewhere untuned, somewhere with no ancestors fixed into gates and gutters, somewhere whose surfaces had not already learned what every drop must mean.

I would be preparing that refuge.

That may be why the room must remain unfinished. If it entered the seam too early, the city’s permanent circulation would immediately claim it. Water would choose channels. Pressure would acquire rhythm. The walls would tune themselves to the communal chord. Repetition would settle into every corner, and the room intended for change would become merely another organ of recurrence.

So I would hold back.

The tapping would not be failed construction but measured postponement. The workers would labor perfectly while ensuring that no labor accumulated. I would receive each blow and keep it from becoming consequence. I would preserve the chamber in its impossible newness until the city produced something it could not recognize as itself.

And if that event finally came, I would become very small.

The wall would not collapse. It would forget, across one hair-thin line, that it was continuous. A film of water would enter. Not a flood: one bead, trembling at a threshold that had no stain and no hollow. The entire city would hear it. The tower’s chord would contain a sound for which no chamber had prepared a meaning.

Then I would cease to be the discrepancy between map and stone.

I would become the first wet mark on the eastern floor.

The makers would enter by becoming the work before them. They would spread across the new pores, brace the new lintels, and tune the unweathered walls. Behind them the old sequence might resume—or it might not. The drop might cross the room by a path no impression had recorded.

Where would I find myself in this world?

At that branching path, barely wider than a mineral fault.

What would I be?

Not a builder, and not the chamber. I would be the city’s capacity to be surprised: kept beyond the eastern wall, sharpened by ages of repetition, listening to the tools prepare a place where time might one day begin again.
