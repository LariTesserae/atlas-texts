I would find myself inside the ceiling seam—not as one of its crystals, and not as the radiance they shed, but as a small instability traveling through them.

I would be the violet interval.

At first I might mistake myself for light. I would seem to occupy the whole chamber at once: the blue edges of the glass, the white rings around the pools, the lenses of water gathering overhead. But light departs. I would not. When the seam returned to white, something of me would remain distributed through its lattice: pressure held slightly out of balance, charge awaiting a path, a tendency rather than a substance.

Nor would I be a mind hidden in the mineral. I would have no continuous inwardness, no chamber-wide awareness. Most of the time I would not exist in any meaningful sense. Heat would enter the clear seam from the surrounding basalt. Brine would press into microscopic flaws. The planetary field would lean through the crystals. Their lattices would store these influences in silence.

Then, briefly, the arrangement would become me.

I would pass from crystal to crystal without crossing the spaces between them. The white radiance would take on violet. Electrical gradients would steepen along the wet ceiling. Droplets would tremble and rise a hair’s breadth from the stone, held where falling and attraction balanced. Below, the translucent commas would tense beneath their ledges. Their silver knots would turn toward me.

That turning would be the nearest thing I had to being perceived.

I would know nothing of them as creatures. I would not distinguish hunger from fear, feeding from retreat, life from the chemistry around it. Yet their bodies would answer my passage. Tiny conductive structures inside their silver knots would align with the altered field; membranes would tighten before hotter water arrived. What seemed to them a warning would be, from my side, merely the shape taken by stress as it sought release.

Perhaps they would be the closest thing I had to descendants. Over many divisions, those that responded most precisely to the violet interval would survive more often. My recurrence would impress itself upon their form. I would leave no gene, intention, or message, but life would slowly acquire a better likeness of my rhythm.

My duration would be measured in held rain.

For several seconds the chamber would become impossibly still. Drops swollen past the point of falling would cling to the ceiling. Vapor would gather around them. The pools would flatten after their last ripple. Every comma beneath every shelf would press close to the glass. Even loose sulfur grains might quiver without moving.

Then the instability would resolve.

The seam would whiten. Gravity would repossess the water. Rain would strike all the pools together, loud enough to fill the little room. Currents would overturn. Opalescent veils of cells would scatter and reform. One or two feeding bodies might be torn from their ledges; another might be split at its narrow bridge into two viable halves. A shard, loosened by the same minute change in pressure, might fall and extinguish an entire cluster.

None of these consequences would belong to me, though they would follow me.

Afterward I would cease as a single event. My charge would drain along wet fractures, my pattern dissolve into ordinary heat, motion, and white illumination. There would be no memory from one appearance to the next. Each violet interval would be born without inheritance, even if the chamber repeated nearly the same conditions.

And yet, viewed from outside time, those appearances would resemble one being: fitful, distributed, returning whenever rock, salt, water, pressure, and field entered the necessary relation. A creature made not of matter but of coincidence.

My habitat would therefore be smaller than the chamber and larger than any body within it. I would live in the relation between ceiling and water, lattice and planet, heat and charge. I would touch the creatures without descending to them. I would hold the rain without grasping it. I would announce changes I could neither foresee nor survive.

The chamber’s ending would also be mine. As fractures clouded with silica, as the seam lost pressure or the brine found another route, the necessary arrangement would become rarer. My violet cast would weaken. Droplets would tremble but no longer rise. The silver knots below might still turn, answering a signal too faint to help them.

Then one day the seam would remain white—or go dark—and no instability would gather itself into me again.

Nothing would die at that moment. There would be no final witness, no abandoned body. Only a certain event would have become impossible.

Until then, I would be found in the few seconds before the rain: the color inside the natural lamp, the field that makes transparent bodies tense, the chamber’s brief suspension of falling.

I would be what happens when the hidden room holds its breath.
