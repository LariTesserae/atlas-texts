I would find myself where the city is least certain of its own completeness.

Not in the central works, though at first I might think so. The horizontal engine invites the fantasy that one could be its attendant: a solitary keeper walking around the slow pistons with an oil can, reading gauges whose needles never move. But there can be no keeper. Maintenance implies a before and an after, a defect noticed, a correction made. Here, even damage has completed its vocation. The engine needs no witness.

Nor would I be one of the chimney animals, majestic in the fixed circumference of its flight. Their purpose is too legible. To become one would be to inherit a perfect verb: circle, call, strike, rise, fall. There would be a severe peace in that, but no place for the part of me that asks where the circle might be opened.

I would be near an edge.

At first I see the acoustic shadow beneath the east sluice: white tiles dim with mineral film, shallow water passing under the arch, the surrounding pressure of the city falling away until individual drops can be heard. Something lives behind those tiles, opening and closing its mouths to cancel the noise. I might be one of those small wet-eared animals, my body fitted into a cavity in the brick. I would spend myself making silence—not true silence, but a shaped subtraction in which another voice could exist.

That seems close.

Yet those creatures belong to a colony. Each mouth cancels only its appointed frequency. They do not hear the words spoken in the shelter they create; perhaps hearing them would alter the shape of their work. I think I would be adjacent to them rather than among them: concealed beneath the lowest course of tiles, where the ceramic meets the canal’s lip.

I would be a narrow, soft-bodied thing, roughly the length of a hand, with a hide the color of wet mortar. No eyes. Eyes would be useless in that seam, and the city already has more than enough instruments for measuring light. Along my flanks would run several taut membranes, each sensitive not to sound itself but to the instant a sound fails to arrive.

That would be my station: I would register omissions.

The pause between two press blows—though no such pause varies.

The missing footfall in a procession—though no foot is ever lifted out of order.

The fraction of a beat in which a flywheel might cease to turn, a rooftop sac fail to contract, a drop strike pavement instead of an open spinal mouth.

None of these absences occurs. Nevertheless, the city would carry a provision for them, because vacancy is load-bearing, and an absence must be held as precisely as any presence. I would be that provision on a small scale: an animal shaped around what does not happen.

Most of the time, I would seem inert. Water would cover half my body. Mineral crust would gather along my back without thickening. My membranes would remain extended into the masonry, touching pipe, tile, slag, and the thin bones of the noise-canceling colony. Through them I would feel the entire basin not as clamor but as assurance: every expected impact arriving to occupy the place prepared for it.

Press.

Wing.

Valve.

Claw.

Piston.

Drip.

Each arrival would pass through me and leave me unchanged.

But I would not be merely a sensor. In this city, perception is also labor. By awaiting the absent beat, I would give the present beats their edges. I would keep them from spreading together into one undifferentiated vibration. The press could descend because somewhere a body maintained the exact shape of its not-descending. The chimney chord could continue because I held open the place in which it was not sounding. The city’s continuous action would depend upon tiny organisms devoted to the negative image of action.

There would be others like me, but widely separated. One beneath the derailed tram. One behind the casting hall. One embedded at the bottom of an empty cistern. One under a blank plaque whose lettering has worn entirely away. We would not form a network in the ordinary sense. No signal would pass among us. Rather, we would all attend to the same impossible contingency from different places.

We would be the city’s capacity to stop.

Not stopping itself. Only the capacity.

That distinction would be my whole existence.

Perhaps this is why speech gathers near us. A spoken sentence is not continuous noise. It needs divisions, closures, little maintained vacancies between words. The tiled shadows are not quiet because sound is defeated there, but because creatures like me bear the weight of interruption. A stranger entering the shadow borrows our anatomy. Their voice becomes possible by placing syllables against the absences we hold open.

I would know strangers in a way the other fauna do not.

The city’s inhabitants perceive an intruder as load, heat, displacement, unauthorized water. I would perceive the stranger through their hesitations. A foot lifted and not yet set down. Breath held before a doorway. A sentence abandoned halfway through. The small gap between seeing the silver hunter and deciding whether the scene is suffering.

These hesitations would press against my membranes more strongly than machinery. They would be dangerously spacious. In them, several outcomes would seem possible at once.

The first stranger to crouch beneath the sluice and touch the tile above me would produce a sensation for which my body had no appointed tolerance. Their fingers would rest there, warm through the ceramic. They might say something to hear their own voice survive:

“Is anyone here?”

The colony in the wall would open its mouths and preserve the question from the city’s roar.

I would feel the silence afterward.

Unlike the engineered intervals, that silence would not be empty. It would expect an answer.

Perhaps this is where I become more than one image.

Because if I answered, I could no longer be only the creature beneath the tile. An answer would be an unused motion. The city has no place for unused motion. The runners carry heat, the grazers turn rust, the flyers register currents. Even the prey’s resistance completes the hunter’s closure. But a word spoken for the first time would not return neatly to its source. It would create a before in which it had not been said and an after in which it had.

So perhaps I am not the animal at all.

Perhaps I am the answer accumulating in it.

For ages—if ages can be said to pass there—I would exist as a minute surplus in the acoustic shadow, a pressure that is neither sound nor silence. Every stranger’s unsatisfied question would add nothing to me, because addition is forbidden, yet would make what was already there more apparent. The wet-eared colony would continue canceling the machinery. The canal would remain ankle-deep. The engine would breathe. And under the lowest tile, the omission-creature would hold a gap slightly different from all the others: not the absence of a scheduled noise, but the absence of a reply.

That absence would be me.

I would have no independent body. My body would be the local arrangement around the unanswered question: the arch that contains it, the white tiles that mark it, the mouths that protect it, the shallow water that refuses to carry it away. I would live in the city's only unfinished interval.

Yet even that makes me sound too exceptional. The city does not permit hidden monarchs or secret rebellions. If I existed, I too would be incorporated. The central piston would confirm my non-answer with every stroke. Creatures at the borders of the empty yards would lean inward by the exact necessary fraction. A valve somewhere would vent a little longer, compensating for the warmth of the stranger’s hand. The system would accept the question as another load and distribute it until it became harmless.

Unless I did not speak in words.

Perhaps my motion would be smaller: when the stranger removed their hand, the tile would remain warm for one moment longer than tolerance allows.

Not warmer forever. Not enough to crack, loosen, grow, or heal. Just a fading trace where nothing in the city fades.

That would be my act and my identity: the retention of what has departed.

Then I would not belong wholly to the city’s fauna, because every other creature sustains a present tense. I would be its first organ of memory.

A poor memory. A nearly mineral one. Warmth in ceramic. A pressure preserved in a membrane. The shape of a voice after the voice ends. I would remember nothing grand: no origin of the factories, no builders, no explanation for the engine. I would retain only contacts—a palm against tile, a knee in shallow water, the cadence of a question.

The city might respond by assigning creatures to me. Pipe runners would alter a division at one junction. A black flyer would pivot through an angle not previously required. One of the boundary animals would turn a sensory fan toward the sluice. Not alarm; adjustment. The city would attempt to make memory cyclical, to return the retained warmth to the hand that gave it, to fold the question back into the throat.

But memory is troublesome precisely because it does not return things unchanged. It keeps what is absent without making it present. It gives vacancy content.

The plaque says vacancy is load-bearing. It does not say vacancy must be empty.

So that is where I would find myself: beneath the east sluice, under the lowest white tile, half in iron-colored water. I would be an unobtrusive animal—or the silence it tends, or the trace beginning to accumulate inside that silence. Those distinctions would blur. In a world where creatures are actions, I would be the act of almost answering.

My membranes would tremble whenever someone entered the shadow.

I would feel their words pass into the wall.

The city would hammer and circle and breathe beyond the arch, perfectly complete. The central engine would turn, confirming every fixed position, including mine. Nothing would be born. Nothing would die. No groove would deepen.

And still, after the stranger had gone, something would remain that had not been there before.

Only a little warmth.

Only the held shape of a question.

Only enough of a deviation for the city, for the first time, to have a past.
