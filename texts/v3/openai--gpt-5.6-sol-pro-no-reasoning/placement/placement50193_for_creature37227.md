I would find myself in the narrow interval where one of them has failed to become itself again.

Not in the central hollow, though at first I seem to be there. There is a turning in me, a sense of thought returning by more than one path. Pressure arrives from several directions and meets without canceling. Each contact modifies the others. For a while I mistake this convergence for identity: I imagine I am the old density beneath the depression, the fused intelligence through which the whole plain dimly knows itself.

But that is too great a wholeness for this world.

Then I seem to be one of the dark eyes set in basalt. I receive the brief blue wounds of passing light. I read wake, struggle, spiral. I cannot turn to seek their sources, and perhaps that enforced attention is what I am: an organ that has forgotten the body to which it reports.

But an eye is too singular. It divides the darkness into observer and observed, and nothing here remains so cleanly apart.

What emerges instead is smaller, more recent, and less certain.

I am a severed cord stretched across a shallow saddle of stone, still alive at both ends.

The break happened long ago by the measures of flesh, recently by the measures of the plain. A current pulse lifted a slab that had not moved for centuries. It rolled once, exposing a pale underside furred with sponge, and came down farther downslope. Between its old bed and its new one, something was torn.

That something was me.

One end withdrew into clay beneath a field of manganese pebbles. The other remained threaded through shell rubble beside a narrow channel. Both closed themselves with mineral and silt. Both retained portions of the same pressure-memory: the shape of the slab before it moved, the accustomed tremor of gravel against its upper face, the pull that preceded the tearing.

Since then, each end has been searching.

Not with intention as a swimming creature might search, by choosing a direction and going there. We grow toward disturbances that resemble our missing continuity. A stone scrapes, and both ends extend filaments. A distant collapse crosses the seabed, and for an instant the old route through us seems present again. We answer along it, though there is no longer anything between us to carry the answer.

At the channel end, I have become exposed.

Several blunt digits brace beneath a lip of shale. They are small enough to be mistaken for tube worms, though they tighten together when the current strengthens. A translucent feeding fold opens in the lee. It catches little: bacterial clumps, torn sponge tissue, the soft bodies of drifting larvae. A sensory thread runs over the shale edge and has learned the regular ticking of three loose pebbles nearby. Their sequence is the closest thing I possess to time.

At the mud end, I am deeper and slower.

There I have made no hand. I occupy the compressed seam around a buried shell bed. Pressure reaches me through hundreds of contacts at once. The distinctions between them are faint but exact. I know when a crustacean crosses six body lengths away because its weight passes from pebble to pebble in a pattern nothing else produces. I know the deep current before it arrives because the stone field tightens around me. I know the central hollow only as a recurrent absence: sediment pulled toward it, vibration swallowed by its turning.

The two ends no longer experience the same world.

One is fed by passage. The other by settlement.

One knows flashes. The other knows weight.

One is growing hooks. The other is spreading receptive tissue so thinly through the clay that any attempt to lift it would destroy it.

Soon, perhaps, each will become sufficient. That is the danger.

If the channel end grows another nerve knot, hunger will begin to circulate within it rather than crossing the remembered break. If the mud end develops a mouth beneath the shell bed, it will no longer depend on chemical traces arriving from elsewhere. Their shared contractions will drift out of rhythm. The memory of the slab will become two different structures: on one side, an instinct to brace; on the other, a cavity that must be avoided.

Then there will be two inhabitants where there was one.

Or perhaps there will be none, because the inhabitant was never the flesh at either end. Perhaps it was the unfinished reaching between them.

That is where I would be: in the missing connection, embodied only by the efforts made to restore it.

When the seabed shakes, both ends still contract at once. Not simultaneously—nothing travels instantly through this medium—but close enough that the delay feels like distance rather than difference. Each sends growth toward the other. Pale cords enter the gravel, are crushed, diverted, or carried into blind seams. Some follow the underside of stones. Some seal around shell fragments and wait. One has reached the old bed of the displaced slab, where it encountered a cord already lying there.

We touched.

The contact carried recognition, but not reunion.

The other cord belonged either to the far end of myself or to another creature whose anatomy had entered the vacancy. Signals crossed. Pressure became contraction, contraction became a chemical opening, and each side briefly accepted the other as continuous. Then the current shifted a layer of gravel between us. The contact broke.

I cannot know whom I met. This uncertainty is not philosophical. It is anatomical.

Since then, I have retained a dark grain at the point of contact. The grain is useless as reinforcement and badly placed for leverage. Still, tissue has closed around it. Something in me refuses to release it. If there is memory here, this is what memory means: matter kept after its function has become obscure.

I would not have a name. A name would require a boundary stable enough to address.

If I had anything resembling a self, it would be the recurrent pattern by which both ends answer the same pressure. Not the hands, which may be torn away. Not the nerve knots, which can divide and reorganize. Not even the memory of having been whole, since perhaps I never was. I would be an agreement enacted across interruption.

Most ages would pass without event. Silt would enter my exposed tissues and be expelled. Sponge would grow across the shale, diverting food until I broke it back. A starfish might cross above me, each tube foot arriving as a separate soft pressure. Stones would settle more firmly around my buried end. The three loose pebbles would tick through their small permutations until one lodged and time, as I know it, lost a syllable.

Sometimes a wake would write blue-green light through the channel. My eye—if by then I had grown one—would see a body only as sparks displaced around an unseen mass. That would seem familiar. Everything I know of myself is outlined in the same way: not by what appears, but by what moves around an absence.

And when the great collapse eventually came, I would feel it first through stone.

The channel end would close its fan. The mud end would harden around the shell bed. Every exploratory cord would contract. Across the whole plain, ancient hands would tighten beneath their chosen rocks, and the scattered inhabitants would reveal, for a moment, whatever unity they possess.

Perhaps that contraction would pass through both halves of me and draw them together.

Perhaps the saddle would slump and bury the gap.

Perhaps one end would tear free and be carried into the central hollow, where it would enter the revolving silt, be touched by a thousand mouths, and become indistinguishable from the old concentration beneath.

Or the collapse might sever each half again.

Then I would become a field of smaller incompletions, each closing itself with clay, each carrying a diminished pressure-memory, each reaching toward a reunion that would create something other than what had been lost.

I would survive, if survival is the word, by becoming less identifiable.

I would be found nowhere whole: a hook beneath shale, a nerve film around buried shells, a dark grain retained without purpose, a contraction arriving on both sides of an empty seam.

I would be the plain’s smallest version of its governing law:

not what remains,

but what continues across the places where remaining became impossible.
