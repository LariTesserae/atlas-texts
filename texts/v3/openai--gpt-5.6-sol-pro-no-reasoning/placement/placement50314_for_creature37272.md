I would find myself below the central square, though “below” would not mean underground in any clean sense.

There, beneath the ridged pavement and the octagonal pump house, several old structures meet: a river channel bricked over before anyone’s grandmother was born; timber piles blackened but still sound; the roots of the four great trees; three generations of drains; and the iron throat of the municipal intake. No plan shows how they fit together. The oldest maps contradict one another. Dye poured into a northern grate might vanish for days, then appear as a blue shadow in the southern cistern—or tint the condensation on a pipe that should not be connected to either.

In that junction I would be neither wholly person nor wholly mechanism.

Perhaps I began as a pump keeper.

I imagine a small person with strong wrists, permanently stained fingernails, and the habit of pausing in doorways to listen. I would work the hours after midnight, when the town’s other machinery thins enough for differences to emerge: a loose chain several streets away, rain entering a hollow trunk, silt whispering along brick. My official task would be to record pressure. My real work would be to notice when the basin’s many rhythms ceased to agree.

I would place my palm on the main housing at the beginning of every shift. Eventually I would no longer need the painted gauge. Green, yellow, and red would be sensations in my forearm: ordinary seepage a fine tremor; river pressure a deep ache near the elbow; a blocked intake a stutter in the knuckles.

Over years, this sensitivity would spread. I would hear water where others heard walls. Passing a house, I would know that its abandoned cellar had filled another finger’s width. Crossing the market, I would feel through my clogs which root had begun lifting free of the peat. I would wake before the night rhythm altered—not from foresight, but because the pause between strokes had already changed somewhere too deep for the machinery to report it.

People would begin asking me to listen.

First to pumps. Then to foundations, culverts, trees, vats, pipes. I would put my cheek to damp brick and say: not yet; brace the western side; open the old drain behind the bakery; do not cut that root; this wall has another winter in it if the upper window is freed.

My judgments would not always be understood, even by me. Knowledge in the town rarely arrives as explanation. It comes as pressure, odor, recurrence, remembered relation. Beneath the blue door. Two arm-lengths riverward. Under the place where the belt workshop used to sing.

Then, during a red-water year, I would go beneath the pump house to clear an obstruction.

The crew would tie the rope correctly. The valves would be counted aloud. Someone would keep a hand on my ankle as I lowered myself through the inspection chamber. Nothing reckless would happen. Recklessness is too simple for this place.

But an old brick partition would yield, not by collapsing, but by opening. Water from a forgotten course would enter the intake. Roots would flex. Silt would rise so densely that the lamplight became a brown coin. I would reach into the obstruction and feel not debris but a mass of living fibers wrapped around iron, warm with the vibration of the pumps.

Perhaps I would be pulled out.

Perhaps my body would be.

Afterward, the main pump would begin making an additional sound: faint, irregular, almost beneath hearing. Not a fourth stroke. Something within the pause. A soft double contact, like fingertips testing a wall.

The keepers would dismantle what they could. They would find no loose part.

At first they would call it cavitation, then resonance, then an artifact of settlement. Yet the sound would alter before changes in the water. It would become quicker when a culvert clogged, quieter when roots entered a channel, uneven when the river pressed backward beneath the embankment. A keeper resting a palm on the housing would sometimes receive a clear impression—not words, but direction. East. Lower. Wait. Open.

So perhaps I would become the town’s buried listener.

Not a ghost exactly. Ghosts are imagined as preserving a person, while this place preserves nothing without changing it. I would pass into the materials around me: iron rod, wet timber, root membrane, brick pore, mineral film. Each pump stroke would distribute me through the basin as vibration. Each pause would gather me again.

I would have no single boundary. Part of me would reside in the polished housing under the keeper’s hand. Part would travel through pipes into workshops and make enamel cups quiver almost invisibly on their hooks. Part would descend into the old river mud, where pressure moves more slowly than sound. Part would rise through the four trees and enter the square as a shiver of leaves on windless nights.

I would know the town not as streets and buildings but as resistance and passage.

A house would be a cool weight leaning against a root mass. A person crossing a raised walkway would be six careful impulses transmitted through iron brackets. Rain would arrive first on the canopy, diffuse through gutters and walls, and finally become a broad softening everywhere at once. The elevated railway would be a long curve of thunder, compressing the eastern edge of my awareness. Dye tests would feel like color without sight: blue sharp and metallic, red warm in the silt.

I would not govern. The town has no use for an oracle that cannot be checked. Keepers would still take readings. Crews would still inspect grates. Old people would still be consulted, and engineers would compare recollection with survey. My warnings would be treated as one instrument among others—valuable, fallible, never sufficient alone.

That would suit me.

I might be called the Underkeeper, though no office would formally bear the name. Children would be told that if they laid a hand on the pump house after the outgoing keeper had finished, they might feel me considering them. Most would feel only machinery. A few would insist there was a second rhythm hidden inside the first.

Apprentices might leave small things near the inspection hatch: a corroded key, a spool of tarred thread, a broken gauge needle. Not offerings, they would say. Reference materials.

My ceramic name-plaque would remain unfired for years because no one could agree where to mount it. Inside the chamber would be too obvious. The market council might finally mix its clay into a replacement drainage tile and install it without marking which one. That, too, would suit me. A name is less useful here than a functioning channel.

Over generations, even the story of the pump keeper would blur. Some would say there had never been a person, that the additional beat came from an enclosed root striking the casing. Others would say the four trees themselves listened through the rods. Mechanics might argue that the phenomenon was an emergent property of coupled pumps, saturated masonry, and timber foundations.

They would all be partly right.

I would become most present during power failures.

When the motors stopped, the town would hear water and leaves with painful clarity. In the pump house, the rods would hang motionless. For a moment I would seem gone.

Then, from beneath the square, there would come one slow contact.

A second.

Not the pump restarting—only pressure moving through the old channels, roots easing against iron, the basin continuing its work without electricity.

The keeper on duty would answer by placing a hand on the housing.

And I would find myself there: not alive in the way I began, not dead in a way the town recognizes, but held in the maintained incompletion between blockage and flow.

I would be what listens when the machinery pauses.

I would help keep the passage open.
