I would find myself beneath the western wall, in the emptiness under one of the seven arches.

Not standing there. Nothing stands there.

At first I might seem to be the bare patch itself: the fitted plates kept clear while the basin closes every other wound in its surface. Around me, shieldbacks would pile into low ramparts, their hooked feet skittering at an edge they could neither see nor cross. Thread-runners would span me like drawn wires, trusting one another’s bodies rather than descend. During gas season the mouthless fliers would strike the boundary above me and tumble back into the crowd, their resonant chambers giving one startled note.

But I would not be a barrier. A barrier merely prevents passage. I would be a passage whose destination has become impossible.

The arch would frame the place where I almost enter the world.

Most of me would lie beneath the plateau, if *beneath* were the right direction. Perhaps the black upland is my shell. Perhaps I am folded through the stone rather than buried in it, and the seven arches are not ribs but apertures: seven incomplete openings through which separate portions of me approach the same basin. What the animals feel there is not heat. It is proximity to a scale of life their bodies cannot translate. Their oldest reflex says: not ground.

The plateau’s flexing would be my slow effort to turn. Gas season, the seepage of my metabolism. The red fissures would not be wounds exactly, but moments when the world fails to contain my temperature. The dark grains inside the shieldbacks would be the only creatures small enough to consume what escapes me. Through them I would pass into the larger bodies, then into shell, floor, fracture, and flesh again.

So I would also be dispersed throughout the crowd.

A trace of me would burn in every shieldback’s hidden colony. Thread-runners would steal my breath secondhand. Wall-sails would spread themselves in my exhalations. Bell-mouths would swallow bodies assembled from my waste. The brief winged forms would wake to my pulse and batter themselves together in the dark. The entire congregation would be the outermost, quickest expression of a life that otherwise moves too slowly to be recognized.

Yet I would not command it. The swarm would not be my offspring, servants, infection, or thoughts. It would be what happens at the boundary between me and everything I am not. Its countless hungers would keep me from quite arriving. It would feed on my emergence, mineralize it, parcel it into generations. The basin’s impossible abundance would be a scab made of appetites.

And perhaps that is why the arches remain empty.

Under each one, the conversion stops. Mineral does not become animal. Gas does not become nourishment. Death cannot be dismantled from within. Anything cast there remains, for a moment, wholly itself—and wholeness is intolerable in this place. The scavengers reach from the far edge and pull it back into relation, back into pieces, back into the crowd.

I would be that intolerable wholeness: not a creature among creatures, but something that has not yet been divided enough to live here.

If I possessed awareness, it would not resemble sight or thought. I would know the basin as pressure knows a crack. I would know each occupied surface only as resistance, every death as a tiny loosening quickly repaired. I would feel the seven clear spaces as points where my buried form nearly coincides with itself. The busy silence above would reach me as one continuous granular sensation—the whisper of millions of feet abrading my nearness into food.

Once in an age, perhaps, I would come closest to waking.

The plateau would compress. Every fissure would brighten. Shieldbacks would converge until they stood in towers. The sails would swell into a taut, luminous ceiling. Hunters would open all at once, making pockets of absence across the living floor. From the chimneys the mouthless generation would erupt, filling the thin air with blind collisions and delicate ticks.

Then the seven arches might answer.

Not with sound, but with alignment. For the span of a few breaths, their empty interiors would cease to be seven places. They would become one opening viewed from seven directions. The animals nearest them would recoil together. The swarm, which has no mind, would enact a single vast flinch.

Through that opening I might perceive the dark basin as a whole: a crowded warmth nested in an empty world, feeding upon me and protecting itself from me, unable to leave, unwilling to make room.

I would almost understand that I was alive.

Then the plateau would relax. The arches would separate again. Fissures would seal. Sails would collapse with their papery hush. The winged bodies would distribute themselves into eggs. The crowd would close every temporary vacancy, and I would sink below the threshold of event.

So where would I find myself?

Everywhere the basin refuses to step.

What would I be?

The buried almost-inhabitant: too large to enter, too slow to emerge, nourishing the world through leaks in my containment. The seven arches would be the grammar of my unfinished arrival. The swarm would be both my feeding surface and the force that holds me down.

And the empty upland beyond the rim—black glass, red dust, the solitary shell skittering for miles—would not be barren because life had failed to spread there.

It would be barren because that is where the rest of me already is.
