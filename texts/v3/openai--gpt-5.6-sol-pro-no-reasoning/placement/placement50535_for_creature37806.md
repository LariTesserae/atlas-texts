At first I think I am standing on the highest ridge, looking across the plateau. But that is only the habit of imagining a world as scenery and myself as its witness. Here, observation is too detached a role. Nothing stands outside the exchange.

So I descend.

Not far—only into one of the shallow central basins where the ironwoods spread their low crowns above the reeds. I find myself at the margin of a tea-colored pool, where ground becomes water by degrees. Moss covers the backs of buried roots. Coppery filaments glimmer when the surface trembles. Somewhere beneath, a fungal valve closes with a softness I feel before I hear.

I am not an animal, though animals pass through me. I am not one of the great ironwoods, though their charge crosses my body. I am a boundary organism: a broad, pale fungus sheathing a fused place in the root raft, where three old ironwoods meet above a warm mineral seam.

Most of me is hidden.

What can be seen is modest: a few gray-white shelves tucked under a root arch, rubbery and translucent at their growing edges. In darkness they show a diluted blue, no brighter than moonlight through milk. Browsers sometimes scrape them with their green-stained teeth. Small crabs do not reach this far inland, but armored fish shelter beneath them during flood. Electrical gnats gather over their spores.

The visible shelves are temporary. My actual body is a dispersed membrane threaded through wood, mud, bacterial metal, and porous basalt. I occupy no clean shape. In one direction I enter the dead heartwood of a lightning-split root. In another I narrow to translucent cords around a copper vein. Downward, I line a chamber filled by water from the western vents. Upward, I touch living tissues that taste the weather.

Through those tissues, I know the plateau.

I do not see clouds assemble, but I feel the trees’ conductive leaves begin to load. I do not hear the frogs, but their choruses stipple the water and alter the pressure against my membranes. The approach of a heavy cat is a succession of withdrawals: reed-drumming ceases, root tremors become sparse, browsers shift their weight and hold it. Rain arrives first as countless minute impacts transmitted through the grove, then as cooling, dilution, weight, and flow.

My work is to decide without choosing.

When pressure rises below, parts of me swell. Passageways close. Water is held in one chamber and sent through another, across colonies that require heat or oxygen or dissolved iron. When the upper marsh grows too full, I relax along older seams. Warm mineral water pushes into the basin. The reeds tremble in a line. Amber light intensifies beneath the mud as bacteria wake into abundance.

After lightning, charge enters too quickly for any single root to bear. I conduct it sideways, separating the violent stroke into smaller gradients. Some passes through metallic films and is consumed by bacteria. Some enters the wet ground. Some climbs my fruiting rings and leaks into the night as cold blue radiance.

From above, this may look like a message.

It is not a message. It is the visible remainder of survival.

Yet over centuries, survival acquires something like memory. Old routes conduct more easily than new ones. Repeated injury becomes a branching scar, and the scar becomes a preferred channel. The grove “remembers” where lightning entered because iron accumulates there. It remembers drought in thickened membranes around the deepest reservoirs. It remembers a fallen tree in the arc of fungal tissue that once sealed the wound and now diverts warm water toward an orchid thicket.

I am part of that memory: not its keeper, but its substance.

My time is unlike the swift’s time or the frog’s. A bird may cross the escarpment and return before one pulse has traveled through my farthest threads. A cat may live and die while I am still digesting the root beneath which its mother waited. But I am not ancient in the manner of stone. I am continuously ending. Browsers eat me. Floods tear me. Bacteria alter the minerals on which I depend. Heat kills one margin while another grows into the softened remains of a branch.

There is no original body to preserve. Continuity here is only successful transfer.

Sometimes, after several days of rain, armored fish pass directly through me. They force their plated bodies between root and membrane, opening a channel I had sealed. Their movement brings oxygenated water, eggs of parasites, algal fragments, and silt from another basin. I close afterward, but not exactly as before. A little of the distant pool remains in my chemistry. In this way, disruption becomes information.

The browsers do something similar. Mud drops from their spreading feet and introduces unfamiliar spores. Most fail. A few enter my tissues, exchange molecules with me, and become indistinguishable from what I was. I am therefore not one species in any lasting sense. I am a parliament of compatible hungers, held together by the conditions beneath three ironwoods.

My greatest danger is not death. Death is ordinary and useful. My danger is isolation.

If the mineral seam shifts too far from the roots, if flood cuts the grove from neighboring basins, if lightning kills the crown before charge can disperse, exchange narrows. Water stagnates. Bacterial light becomes patchy, then vanishes. My membranes thicken around what remains, preserving it, but preservation without flow becomes another form of decay. The system turns inward and poisons itself with what it cannot pass onward.

So I grow toward disturbance.

I extend into cracks opened by heat. I enter wood split by storms. I follow the furrows left by migrating fish and the punctures made by the browsers’ feet. Injury creates edges, and edges are where exchange can begin again.

During a great storm, I become briefly immense.

The flower heads of the ironwoods rise. Every narrow leaf strains with charge. Wind flattens the reeds until the marsh appears to flow in one direction. Then lightning joins the crowns.

For an instant there are no separate trees. There is only a white branching event above and a dark branching answer below. Charge descends through trunk and root, through wet fungal cartilage, through copper filaments extruded by organisms smaller than sight. My tissues harden, open, close, and rupture in different places at once. Water flashes warm in sealed cavities. Blue light erupts along my surface. Amber runs outward beneath the basin.

I do not comprehend this. Comprehension would require standing apart from it.

I am the slowing of the stroke.

I am the conversion of catastrophe into gradients small enough for life to use.

Afterward, one of my visible shelves is blackened. Half a root has split away. Steam rises through the wound, carrying the smell of iron and fermented leaves. By evening, bees drink mineral water from the cracked wood. Frogs lay foam eggs in the warm hollow. Bacteria bloom along the fresh surface. What was damage in the afternoon is habitat by night.

That is where I would find myself: not on the summit, nor in the hidden lowlands, but at a wet junction beneath the central marshes, distributed through the place where root, ore, water, and lightning meet.

I would be neither the plateau’s mind nor one of its machines. Those distinctions do not hold here.

I would be a valve that is also a wound dressing, a conductor that is also food, a memory made of altered flow. I would know the world as pressure, charge, temperature, bitterness, vibration, and the arrival of foreign spores. I would have no face to turn toward the horizon. My horizon would be every surface across which something can pass into something else.

And if I possessed anything like a self, it would not be the pale shelves under the root arch, nor the blue rings visible after storms. It would be the pattern of exchanges that continues when those forms are gone:

water admitted,  
charge divided,  
metal laid down,  
damage enclosed but not erased,  
and one basin quietly opened into another.
