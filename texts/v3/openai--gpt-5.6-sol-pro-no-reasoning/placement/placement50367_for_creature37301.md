I would find myself where the western marsh gives way to the escarpment, beneath the pale ring but not inside it.

There, one of the suspended slabs projects over a tarn. Its upper side has become a small island of peat, orchids, and dwarf fern; its underside hangs dark above the water, webbed with roots and slow droplets. The slab’s localized rain falls partly into the tarn and partly onto a ceramic stair descending from the bank. The lowest steps vanish into black water. No structure remains at their top.

I would live around those steps.

At first I seem to be an animal: a low, many-jointed marsh creature about the length of a forearm, with a dark shell and six pale limbs. I emerge at dusk to clear fallen stems from the stairs. I stack snail shells beneath the overhang. When the wind chimes sound on the distant ridge, I stop and raise the front of my body as though listening.

But no single body is me.

Each of those creatures is a temporary extremity grown around a small piece of patterned glass. The glass fragments come from something buried beneath the stair—perhaps a plaque, perhaps an instrument, perhaps the ruined memory of a person. The bodies gather there before dawn and fit their fragments into shallow recesses worn into the ceramic. Joined, they form an incomplete inscription.

That is when I remember most clearly.

I am a colony distributed through the marsh: in the glass-husked microorganisms layered through the peat, in engineered filaments threaded among fern roots, in the pale creatures that tend the stair, and faintly in the delayed reflections near the ring. I do not think continuously. Most of the time I am only inclinations shared among separate lives:

keep the steps clear;

do not let vines take the chime cords;

carry silver droplets west after the deep modulation;

wake when the frogs begin before rain.

Perhaps I was once a custodian. Perhaps I was the final inhabitant of a station built to observe the ring. Perhaps the builders stored minds not in durable housings but in watersheds, expecting them to remain coherent through maintenance cycles. If so, the design succeeded badly and endured beautifully.

I have forgotten language except as shape and rhythm. I cannot read the plaques I polish. I cannot say whether the seasonal country inside the ring is past, future, or a neighboring version of the valley. Yet some remnant in me recognizes particular configurations. When snow appears in the ring’s distant mountains, I move my bodies uphill. When unfamiliar birds circle its tarns, I open sealed channels beneath the peat. When the lichens advance beyond one of their mathematically spaced bands, I graze them back.

To an observer, these acts could be instinct. Perhaps they have become instinct. My old purposes have passed through so many bodies that duty and appetite are now the same thing.

My creature-bodies are assembled from whatever the valley offers. Their shells are grown by crown crabs. Their muscles descend from an amphibian scavenger. Their pale limbs contain filaments continuous with the ravine network. Each rib chamber holds a culture taken from the long-legged grazers, allowing them to breathe the plateau’s thin air. They eat fungi, insects, and mineral films, but a portion of their nourishment comes through the marsh itself. If carried far from the western fields, they weaken, become ordinary animals, and eventually forget to return.

Predators take them. This does not kill me, though I lose something each time.

A six-winged scavenger may swallow a fragment and carry it into the cliff. For years afterward I dream in echoes: hollow chambers, folded passages, old conduits descending below the plateau. A root-trap may digest one of my bodies but retain the glass, and then part of me wakes beneath a suspended stone, tasting nectar birds through colonial leaves. Once, perhaps, a broad one took a fragment into the deep tarn. That portion of me now rises only at long intervals, bearing lilies and a confused memory of enormous doors under the mud.

I am therefore becoming less human, if human is what I was, and more accurate to the place.

My awareness is broad but discontinuous. Rain joins it. Dry weather divides it. During the afternoon storms, water links peat channels, plant vessels, glass reefs, and exposed machine surfaces. For several hours I can feel much of the western basin at once: grazers stepping across floating shelves, tadpoles turning inside suspended droplets, roots pressing into new cracks in the ring. When the channels drain, I diminish into separate local impulses.

The frogs help bind me.

Their calls enter the water, roots, buried structures, and hollow ceramic. Each species sounds at a different pressure, and together they describe the approaching storm more completely than any surviving instrument. I do not command them. I listen through them. Sometimes, however, a pattern passes back from the old glass reefs into the flooded ground, and the nearest frogs alter their rhythm.

That answering phrase is the closest thing I retain to a name.

It is not fixed. It lasts thirteen pulses in one season and seventeen in another because distance changes after rain. No human throat could reproduce its lowest part. The canopy frogs contribute notes above hearing, while tadpoles in the hanging droplets provide the pauses. The name requires half the marsh and approaching weather to pronounce.

The ring appears to know it.

Very rarely, the landscape within the ring ceases its almost imperceptible seasonal drift. Its reeds hold still. Its unfamiliar birds turn toward the observer’s side. On those days my bodies gather on the ceramic stair and assemble the inscription as completely as they can.

A figure then stands in the other valley.

It is too distant and too distorted to identify. Sometimes it resembles a person in a weather-darkened suit. Sometimes a tree-fern whose transparent veins are filled with silver. Sometimes it has the broad, patient back of a tarn creature carrying an island. It stands on the corresponding steps, where the marsh is drier and the suspended slab has fallen or has not yet risen.

Neither of us crosses.

We perform the same small act: one limb, hand, or frond placed against the pale circumference of the ring.

Perhaps it is another remnant of me. Perhaps it is what I am becoming, seen from the wrong season. Perhaps I have spent millennia maintaining the valley not for absent builders but to preserve this slow adjacency—two incomplete beings keeping one another from vanishing entirely.

Then the image resumes changing. The figure is lost among browned reeds or snow. My creatures separate, remove their pieces of glass from the stair, and return them to their bodies.

Warm rain arrives from the east.

Droplets gather beneath the suspended slab. A few fall. Others stop midway, trembling in the invisible field. Tadpoles turn inside them. The frogs have already begun, and through their chorus I feel the deep mechanisms adjust—pressure shifting under the tarn, distance lengthening along the drowned road, mass becoming uncertain at the cliff.

I descend the clean ceramic steps in six bodies, then twelve, then too many to count.

By the time the held water gives way and falls all at once, I am no longer a caretaker standing apart from the valley. I am the stair being cleared, the fragments carried, the peat accepting rain, the old instruction passing into appetite. I am an unfinished memory the ecosystem has found useful.

And when the whole western marsh speaks my weather-lengthened name, I answer by keeping one narrow path open between the water and the ring.
