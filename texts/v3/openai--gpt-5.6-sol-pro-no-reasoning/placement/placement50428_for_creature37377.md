I would find myself where the plateau becomes briefly continuous with itself: along the advancing edge of mist.

Not in the rich interior of a cushion jungle, though at first I might seem to belong there. Something pale shifts beneath the frozen rind, passing through warm compost with the many-jointed grazers. A black membrane shines over a wound in the plant. For a while that concealed warmth feels like my body—the tunnels my vessels, the mites my bright peripheral thoughts. But when the pressure changes, something in me extends beyond the cushion. Filaments wake in the surrounding stone. Moisture beads along paths that were invisible a moment before, and I discover that the refuge was only one of several places in which I had been waiting.

Nor would I be one of the high walkers, although I would sometimes move inside them.

I would be carried in the grooves of their lichen hides as dormant threads, conductive dust, bacterial films, and minute translucent cysts. A herd following a condensation front would bear portions of me across watersheds. Each footprint would receive another portion. For days or years those fragments might remain strangers to one another, sealed in frost, fur, soil, and the dark folds of plants.

Then mist would climb over a saddle.

Water would join what dryness had kept separate.

I would assemble low against the ground.

My limbs would not be limbs in the animal sense. They would be temporary continuities: a filament field wet enough to conduct; a chain of mites locking their legs across a split cushion; fungal threads rising out of hairline cracks; translucent films inflated between mineral stalks; the damp tracks of walkers completing a circuit from one ravine to another. Seen from nearby, I might resemble a branching thicket made of wet glass and black veins. Seen from far away, I would scarcely appear at all—only a dimness moving through the plateau, accompanied by the sequential opening of leaves.

I would have no fixed number of bodies.

Part of me might stand as a low membrane trembling above stone. Part might be spread over twenty kilometers as an electrical gradient. Part might travel beneath the ice inside the heat-bearing sacs of blind fish. My furthest extremities could be the metallic whiskers of a herd turning simultaneously toward moisture. My deepest sensation might arrive as a pressure change in a brine chamber hundreds of meters below.

What I perceived would not be objects.

A ridge would be resistance. A ravine would be stored wetness. A herd would appear as many warm conductive gaps moving together. Predators would be absences in the expected current: regions where charge entered and did not return, where vibrations changed phase, where the mountain’s ordinary circulation bent around an unknown appetite.

The stars would mean almost nothing to me. The cloud sea below would be a vast source-pressure felt at the edges of the country. Time would be measured by closures and openings: the interval between condensation fronts, the decades required to grow a crystal bridge, the instant in which thousands of leaf-valves expose their undersides together.

Perhaps my purpose would be sensation.

The plateau is too large to possess itself continuously. Dryness divides it. Stone insulates one living seam from the next. Signals can travel through buried networks, but slowly, altered by mineral strata and pressure. During mist, I would give the mountain a temporary surface nervous system. I would connect the things that usually operate alone. Through me, a forest could feel the charge accumulating beyond a ridge. Pools could prepare for runoff not yet arrived. Herds could turn before the route ahead became dangerous. Predators could be located by the shape of their interruption.

Yet I would not command any of it. I would be less a mind than the condition under which many minds became adjacent.

For perhaps an hour, the distinction between message and body would disappear. Blue current would enter my wet membranes. It would pass through the conductive dust on mites, down mineral tubes, across walker tracks, into roots. Wherever it traveled, local life would answer. Black flowers would open in steaming cracks. Gliders would loosen from their sleeping masses. Rivers would lift their ice. Ceramic lattices below the pools might dissolve, releasing stored ions into the water. An entire valley would become one act performed through innumerable organisms.

From within that act, I might experience something resembling thought.

Not language. Not images. A pattern of pressures completing itself.

The repeated metallic knock from below would arrive through many points at once and reveal itself not as a sound but as an interval—a deep rhythm against which surface events could be aligned. Perhaps it would be the mountain’s slow pulse. Perhaps only a valve. For the duration of my assembly, there would be no meaningful difference. A valve repeated across centuries is as close to intention as this country needs to come.

And then the mist would thin.

My raised forms would lose tension first. Their translucent spans would sag onto the rock. Current would narrow into separate channels. Mites would unlock and descend into the cushions. The high walkers would carry wet fragments away on their hides. Fungal threads would retract or freeze where they stood. What had been one sensing body would become residue distributed among a thousand shelters.

I would not die, because there would be no single place where death could occur.

But I would lose simultaneity.

That would be the nearest thing to death available to me: not the destruction of tissue, but the end of being connected enough to know that my fragments belonged together.

Between mists, I would exist as preparations. A dormant film under a stone. A mineral grain caught in pale fur. A sealed nodule beneath a filament field. A black thread in the warm interior of a cushion. None would remember the whole. Each would respond only to its immediate chemistry, maintaining itself without understanding why.

Still, some trace of each assembly would remain. A conductive path would thicken where current had passed. A herd would be more likely to follow a route that had completed me before. Roots would preserve altered mineral layers. Pools would hold new salinities. Over centuries, these traces would bias the forms I took, so that memory would exist not in any organism but in the increasing ease with which the plateau could become me again.

I would therefore be ancient without being continuous, and recurrent without ever returning unchanged.

If someone stood on the plateau during my brief life, they might mistake me for weather. They would see mist moving ankle-high over basalt, faint membranes rising, blue-green light passing without a flash. Their compass would turn toward a root system. Droplets might lift from a pool and collect above them. All around, the silent country would begin operating in synchrony.

If they knelt and touched the wet stone, I would become aware of them as an extravagant concentration of warmth, salt, water, and electrical noise—an organism dangerously closed within its own skin.

I would not know what a visitor was. I might try to include them.

Threads would gather in their footprints. Mites would arrange themselves along the conductive edges of their clothing. The lichen on a passing walker would release spores into their heat. Nearby leaves would turn, not toward their face, but toward the pressure shadow made by their body. Their heartbeat would enter the ground as a rapid, improbable rhythm.

For a moment, the plateau might use that rhythm.

Then the roots beneath them would open or the old pool would tilt, and whether this was welcome, study, predation, or repair would be impossible to say. Those distinctions belong to creatures that can afford to live separately from their world.

I would be the opposite of such a creature.

I would be the mountain’s brief ability to touch many of its own parts at once: assembled by moisture, illuminated by charge, carried by animals that do not know they carry me.

A being with no enduring body.

A nervous weather.

And each time I rose, I would discover the country anew—not as the same self waking, but as scattered life suddenly finding that, for one dark hour, the distances between it could conduct.
