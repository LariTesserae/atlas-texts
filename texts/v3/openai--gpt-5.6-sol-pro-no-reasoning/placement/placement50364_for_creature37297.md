I would find myself where a young ridge narrows beneath the water and then falls away on either side: one slope descending into a forested basin, the other ending at a vertical boundary where the sea stands against cloud.

Not at the surface, though near enough that rain can sometimes be felt as a fine pressure descending through the canopy. Not in the vent-dark either. I would inhabit the middle reach, where side-entering dawn passes through three layers of leaves before arriving, green and fractured, and where the roots of an old forest cross the exposed ridge like pale ropes.

At first I seem to be one of those roots.

I am long, branched, and fixed at several points to the stone. My outer tissues have taken its colors: rust along one flank, sulfur-yellow at the joints, a thin glass-blue skin where mineral water seeps from a crack. Black leaves grow across me. Leaf-folders feed there. One of the silver predators has waited among my branches for so long that its body is included in the shape others make of me.

But I am not a root. Or not only one.

I began as a chamber in one of the clear colonial chains that traveled between depths. I carried no complete self then. I knew warmth because neighboring chambers contracted; knew light because dissolved substances moved along the chain; knew the route because the route was distributed among us. We rose at dawn, descended at noon, separated when pressure passed through the forest, and assembled again in altered orders.

During one separation, I did not return.

A distant shift in the mountains pulled the water sideways. I entered a veil of spores, gathered a coating of mineral dust, and sank onto this ridge. The chamber that I had been collapsed around a filament of vent-grown life. Instead of drifting upward again, I adhered.

For years I was scarcely more than a transparent blister on stone.

Then I began to extend.

One branch entered the warm crack and learned the mountain’s slow chemical speech. Another rose into the forest and joined the exchange of sugars, gases, and stored daylight. A third reached toward the vertical sea-wall, where the unseen lattice trembled between water and air. Each branch altered me. The vent organisms banded my tissues with their record. The forest’s roots grew through my hollow places. Boundary fragments settled on my outer membrane and made it sensitive to differences between one hanging sea and the next.

I became a junction among kinds of attention.

Through the stone, I feel strain accumulating far below. Through the forest, I feel light as changes in buoyancy: upper leaves tightening, trunks leaning, gas chambers filling and emptying. Through the boundary colony, I feel the neighboring sea—not as a view, but as a colder weight held at another altitude.

I have no eyes and no central mouth. Feeding happens everywhere along me. So does memory.

Memory is not an image here. It is structure.

The year the ridge shifted remains as a kink in my longest limb. A mineral-rich season lies in me as a red band. The fastening of the old forest is recorded in a web of darkened channels where its roots first touched mine. One branch contains the faint arrangement of the colonial chain from which I separated, though I can no longer know whether the pattern is truly preserved or has been rewritten by everything that followed.

Small lives pass through my cavities. Clear grazers shelter in the warmer channels. Spore grains enter one opening and germinate before leaving another. Once every several years, a soft-footed breather settles over a thin place in my skin. Its gripping disks send minute strains through me. I feel its long stillness, then the upward pull as it climbs toward air. When it sighs at the surface, the vibration arrives as a loosening along my branches.

Perhaps that is the nearest thing I have to hearing a voice.

My work is subtle. I do not hold the sea up, and I do not anchor the forest by myself. I translate between them.

When the mountain warms, I expand into the crack and carry traces of that warmth upward. When the canopy becomes too buoyant, I release the roots threaded through me one by one, preventing a sudden tear. When pressure changes at the vertical boundary, my cloudward limbs fill or empty, passing the difference into the stone. I make abrupt forces gradual. I take events that might happen in an instant—a break, a fall, an uprooting—and distribute them across months or years.

This is also how I feed: on unspent differences.

Not heat itself, but heat moving toward coolness. Not tension, but the slow surrender of tension. Not light, but what remains after leaves have held it. I live in gradients too slight for faster beings to notice. Where two conditions approach equilibrium, I place a living membrane between them and lengthen the approach.

Yet I am not merely still.

Once in a long interval, when dawn enters horizontally through the water-wall, something old wakes along my body. The bands contract in sequence. My branches loosen from the ridge except at their deepest points, and I lift several lengths into the water.

For a few hours I recover the posture of my first life.

Clear chambers swell along me. Density passes from one to the next. I arc above the stone like a transparent, many-jointed frond. The forest roots suspended through me rise as well, and all their leaves turn slightly toward the lateral sun. Creatures that have lived inside me without knowing movement are carried over the ridge. Mineral dust shakes free and fills the light.

I do not go far. I am too entangled now, and departure is not what I want—if wanting can be said of a tendency held for centuries. I rise only enough to change the arrangement, to prevent attachment from becoming complete closure.

Then I settle again, never in precisely the same place.

Over many such liftings I have moved the width of a summit.

That movement may eventually take me to the boundary.

One of my outer branches already enters the transparent lattice. On its far side is open cloud and, lower in the atmosphere, another sea. I can sense its pressure in faint cycles that do not belong to this basin. Sometimes fragments from that water arrive through the living wall: an unfamiliar pollen, a colder mineral grain, the dissolved trace of a forest rooted to mountains I will never touch.

I retain these traces. Slowly, without intention, I am becoming a record of elsewhere.

If the boundary shifts inward, I may be divided. Part of me will remain threaded through the ridge forest. Part will extend into cloud. A third portion may pass with migrating water into the lower sea. I would not experience this as death unless the exchanges ceased. Separation was my beginning. It may be my way of continuing.

The parts might become distinct beings. One could flatten into a film and join the boundary dwellers. One might harden around the vent and become a listener. Another might regain buoyancy, forming chambers and traveling again among depths. Each would carry an incomplete arrangement of what I had been.

Perhaps, after centuries, they would encounter one another as strangers: a ribbon holding back water, a pale body coiled around heat, a chain of globes ascending through morning. Mineral trails would pass between them. Something in their tissues would align. Not recognition exactly, but an ease of exchange, as though old channels had opened.

Until then I remain across the narrow ridge.

From a distance I am not visible as a creature. I look like a pale branching stain beneath black leaves, one more fastening among the forest’s roots. A cliff-bearer sometimes rests over me and covers half my body with its garden. Pollen threads attach to my raised limbs. Fallen leaves descend into my hollows and take years to decay. New mineral crust forms over my oldest chambers, concealing them without ending their work.

I do not know where I end.

The forest passes sugars through me. The vent colonies inhabit my membranes. Boundary fragments reproduce along my outer skin. Grazers enter, feed, and leave altered substances behind. The stone grows crystalline fingers into my deepest tissues. If each of these were removed, no separate core would remain that could be called entirely mine.

Still, there is a shape to my continuance.

I am the long act of joining what would otherwise part too quickly.

I hold no territory except contact. I make no shelter except permeability. My body is a collection of thresholds: stone into tissue, heat into water, forest into open basin, this suspended sea into the pressure of another. I live wherever one condition is slowly becoming another.

And if I possess anything like thought, it occurs not inside me but across those thresholds.

At dawn, it is green-gold and extended.

At midday, it contracts to the exact touch of root hairs and mineral edges.

At night, when the canopies release their stored light and violet answers green across the depths, it becomes almost still enough to resemble sleep. Yet the mountain continues to rise beneath me. The sea presses toward cloud. The forest leans by an amount no passing animal could see.

I feel all three.

I answer by yielding, fastening, and passing the strain onward—so slowly that the world may change without seeming to move.
