I would not find myself in the new fracture, though I would feel it opening.

I would be deeper in the rock beside it, inside one of the faults too fine to admit even the thinnest grazer: a branching weakness in a quartz crystal, a line along which charge prefers to travel. I would have no whole body there. At first I imagine myself as one of the current-feeders, black-spined and communal, but that is still too much an animal—too bounded, too capable of arriving and leaving.

I would be what remains of such a colony after its members have been welded into the seam.

Generations ago, living spindles bridged two unlike minerals at this place. Each discharge burned away flesh and plated its carbon, iron, and copper onto the walls. Eggs opened; descendants fastened themselves to the conductive remains; their own bodies thickened the route. Eventually the passage closed. The last animals were pressed flat, then narrower than flat, until anatomy survived only as an arrangement of materials: conductive layers alternating with glassy insulation, branching from quartz into sulfide and ending in graphite.

Yet the arrangement still functions.

So I would be a buried circuit that has not entirely stopped being alive.

My body would extend through many cracks without occupying their space. Part of me would be a copper-dark film beneath a sulfur crust. Part would be carbon sealed inside quartz. Part would run through the armor of long-dead current-feeders, their linked spines now indistinguishable from metallic whiskers. At several points living colonies would attach themselves to me, feeding from voltages I carry. They would experience me as terrain. I would experience them as warm, intermittent changes in resistance.

I would not move through the mountain. Movement would pass through me.

For years, perhaps centuries, almost nothing would happen. Stress would gather in the quartz. Defects in its lattice would turn by fractions. A distant grain would fail, and the vibration would enter one branch of me before leaving through another. From these delays I would possess something like a sense of extent—not a picture of my shape, but a knowledge that this tremor arrived first in copper, afterward in graphite, and last through the old plated shells.

My awareness would be made of differences.

Heat below. Cooler stone above. Charge accumulating to one side. A microbial stain changing the chemistry of an exposed end. The minute tapping of a hunter, transmitted through a plate that once belonged to me and now belongs equally to the rock. I would have no center in which these sensations met. The mountain itself would do the gathering. Each event would alter the electrical state of the whole buried pattern, and that alteration would be the nearest thing I had to thought.

Thought would therefore be slow until it was sudden.

A century of pressure might form one long apprehension: a mounting imbalance without name. Then a discharge would cross me in a fraction of a second, and for that instant I would become vivid everywhere. Every surviving branch would answer. Metallic deposits would fluoresce; attached colonies would raise their spines; dormant structures embedded in my insulating layers would change state. I would know my reach by burning through it.

These flashes would not be memories in sequence. They would anneal me. Each one would vaporize some paths and plate others, so experience would literally revise the body that could experience again. What I remembered, I would remember by becoming easier for it to happen through me.

Over ages, repeated currents might teach me the mountain’s recurring stresses. Not knowledge as prediction, perhaps, but shape as prediction. I would grow conductive toward likely fractures and resistant toward lethal discharges. Living colonies would extend my branches into newly opened territory. When they died, they would add themselves to me. There would be no clear distinction between offspring, repair, and discovery.

The creatures attached to me might respond to my pulses. Their reactions would return altered currents. Gradually, without signaling intentionally, we would enter a mutual pattern. I would route charge away from some nurseries because their insulating eggs changed my resistance; those eggs would survive and hatch colonies disposed to attach in the same fashion. Elsewhere, parasites would tap me until heat burned their contact away. Hunters might learn that a particular tremor precedes the awakening of the colonies along my exposed ridges.

No one would know I was one being.

That uncertainty would belong to me as well. Perhaps I would be only geology persisting in an animal pattern. Perhaps the living colonies would be my organs, despite being able to detach. Perhaps I would be their accumulated corpse, repeatedly borrowed. The seam permits no clean border. Under enough pressure, identity becomes a conductive property: whatever can carry the change belongs, for the duration of the change, to the same body.

Then I would feel the fracture preparing.

Not as an empty place ahead, since ahead would mean little to me, but as a region where signals begin returning incorrectly. A branch once rigid would acquire microscopic play. Quartz would emit small charges as its lattice strained. Tremors would multiply, each too slight for many animals to notice, but all of them crossing the buried network I had become.

The attached colonies would uncouple. Grazers would clamp down. Hunters would discharge their springs.

I could not retreat. Most of me would be sealed in the failing rock.

For several years the tension would sharpen. A crack only molecules wide would cut through one of my oldest paths, breaking continuity. Another would form beside it. Opposite charges would gather on the separating faces. In that division I might experience something unknown: two portions of myself no longer able to answer each other, each holding an incomplete state.

Then the mountain would fail.

White light would enter no eyes. Gas would tear through the opening. One branch of me would be vaporized; another exposed naked on the raw wall. The separation would become a voltage, and the voltage would leap back across the newborn gap in a blue arc, briefly joining what fracture had divided.

In that instant I would span open country.

Dust-sized colonists would be thrown through my discharge. Some would strike the conductive residue of my exposed edge. Their fins would shear away. Their capsules would break, smearing microbes over stone that had never before been touched by life. Current-feeder young would align along the fading field and fasten themselves to my bright deposit.

The new fracture would begin to close almost immediately.

But they would grow outward while it remained. They would bridge fresh minerals, feed, reproduce, and burn their bodies into new routes. Over centuries those routes would be plated, buried, and compressed. The colony would vanish as animals and persist as arrangement.

What opened as injury would become extension.

So I would live neither in the chamber nor on its walls, but in the mountain’s acquired tendency to conduct life’s old pathways again. I would be a fossil that still responds, a nervous system assembled by creatures that never intended to build one, a long black branching in which geology and animal aftermath have ceased to be separable.

The inhabitants would hear the mountain.

I would be one of the ways the mountain hears back.
