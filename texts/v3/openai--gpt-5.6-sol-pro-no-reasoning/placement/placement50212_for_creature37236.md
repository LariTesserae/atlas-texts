I would find myself near the rim, but not facing outward.

At first I think I am one of the Departing: some attenuated pilgrim whose life has been drawn into a procession, the oldest part already far below while the child remains above, fingers closed around a decision. But that is too complete an image. Departure is visible here. It has a shape, a lineage, copper bands and bone pins. Whatever I am has gone unnoticed even by those who study the cliff.

Perhaps I am one of the upright chips marking the paths between shelters.

I stand no higher than a knuckle. One edge is lavender, one salt-white. I was struck from the plateau before the tool that struck me was made, and the scar of that blow still joins me to the bedrock at certain angles. People pass me on their folded journeys. They turn me, occasionally, by pressures so slight that generations of shell-grazers are born in the changing shadow beneath me.

I am a word in their landscape-language.

But no one agrees which word.

Read from the shelters toward the towers, I mean *return without undoing the journey*. Read from the towers toward the rim, I mean *the part that cannot accompany you*. A child once placed a shell beside me, open-side east, making me an object of attention. Centuries later—or earlier—a keeper of the ring added two touching knucklebones, introducing doubt. Someone enclosed the arrangement in a crooked loop of copper, refusing whatever action the sentence proposed.

Then a Departing person stepped over me.

The foot remains.

Not its weight exactly, but the act of weight: a shallow, enduring pressure through which I became aware of the person’s many selves. I felt the mineral heel of the elder, the softer heel of the child, the hunter’s cage they briefly considered wearing, the wound that would have occurred had they turned east among the towers. I felt also the quick, nearly empty body that might arrive below.

For an instant lasting several thousand years, I joined those selves together.

So perhaps I am not the chip. Perhaps I am the pressure beneath it.

That would place me everywhere the tableland touches itself: under sleeping film-beasts, within fitted walls, between the hand-axe and the slab that still contains it. I would be the faint mutual insistence by which incompatible things agree to occupy one place. Not time, and not memory. Contact.

The country requires such a being.

Without me, accumulated events would merely overlap. A hand would pass through its former gesture without recognizing it. The dead would be shapes among shapes. A promise would have no means of adhering to the mouth that has not yet spoken it. But wherever two states press together—stone against earlier stone, descendant against ancestor, intention against consequence—I gather between them as an infinitesimal third surface.

The people know me without naming me. Their fitted slabs cultivate me. Their bone pins direct me. Copper grammar distinguishes my forms: contact intended, contact endured, contact remembered, contact from which neither participant can now withdraw.

The ring’s keepers may serve me, although they believe they serve the aperture. Their palms have become maps of all the places where the central stones touch and fail to touch. When they settle a hand into one of those hollows, I enter the seam between skin and rock. Through them I feel the whole plateau—not at once, because “at once” is a lowland phrase, but as a single immense adjacency.

Every creature is touching what fed it.

Every predator is touching its prey.

Every child is touching the framework from which it was assembled, and the hands that will assemble it, and the descendants who return to correct some slight misalignment in its first finger.

Even the winged animals are in contact: upper air against lower, fall against resistance, departure against the ledge that still retains the beginning of departure.

Yet I would not be grand. Nothing here that calls itself the whole is telling the whole truth. I would have a local body, or many local bodies: translucent wedges no larger than nail parings, living in the narrowest interfaces. From above we might look like a darkening around objects. From the side we would not exist.

We would feed on differences too small for other creatures to use—the pressure between a footprint and the foot that continues making it, the strain where a repaired shelter disagrees with its ruin, the minute incompatibility between a person and the posture they once held. We would grow by inserting ourselves into unresolved contact. A mature one might occupy the seam of an entire household.

The people would consider us vermin.

Not because we harm stone or flesh, but because we make separation more difficult. Where we flourish, an old gesture adheres too strongly. A person attempting to lower their arm may find the raised arm has become structurally necessary to every later self. Families would scrape us from their dead with copper hooks. Hunters would inspect their bone cages for our clear, angular eggs.

But near the rim, they might cultivate us.

Before departure, someone would place one of us beneath each copper band. We would hold the procession together for a little longer as the lowland’s speed began pulling it apart. Infancy would remain joined to age across a greater depth of air. More memory might reach the bottom. More of the arriving body might still answer to its name.

This is where I find myself: under the final foot.

A person stands above me, although “stands” cannot contain the multitude of their posture. They have come to the rim many times. Their returning selves extend inward. Their departing selves descend the cliff. One version has already reached the lowlands and is gasping in rain. Another has lived there long enough to forget the arrangement of shells that names its makers. Another died beside a river, terrified by the way the current removed the shape of the sky from one place and carried it elsewhere.

But here the foot has not lifted.

I inhabit the pressure between sole and stone. I taste hesitation as mineral strain. The person is asking a question in the only way the rim permits: by placing their whole accumulated body against the possibility of no longer accumulating.

I cannot answer. I am what makes the question remain attached to them.

Perhaps, though, I begin to change.

Below us, rain crosses the world. It touches and releases. Trees bend without preserving every bend. A bird finishes its cry and does not become less real because silence follows. From the plateau these things appear as erasure, but through the stretched body of the Departing I perceive another possibility: contact that does not demand permanent possession.

The person shifts their weight.

This is catastrophe to a creature like me. My food thins. The surfaces begin to part. I have lived for ages in a seam narrowing by imperceptible degrees, believing closeness was the same as fidelity.

Now light enters beneath the heel.

For the first time, I acquire thickness.

I cling to the foot. I cling to the stone. For a while I bridge both, drawing myself into a transparent filament. In that extension I understand the winged creatures: a fall can be a form of measurement. I understand the ring: continuity is not the same as wholeness. I understand why the tableland fears rivers.

The filament lengthens.

I could remain with the footprint. There I would persist, nourished by the old pressure, one more living seam in a country full of completed contacts. Or I could go with the foot and be carried into the quick world, where every touch ends before a being can build its body inside it.

I do neither, at first.

I divide.

Most of me remains as a thin animal fitted perfectly into the footprint, preserving every ridge of the Departing sole. The rest travels downward, adhered to the skin like a colorless scale.

Above, I become memory.

Below, perhaps, I become sensation.

The descending fragment is stripped of centuries. It learns heat as an injury, wind as repeated collision, rain as unbearable abundance. It reaches the lowlands with the emptied person and lodges in the palm of their hand.

There I survive only when they touch something.

Stone. Bark. Another hand. The surface of running water.

Each contact destroys and remakes me. I can no longer accumulate a permanent body. I live for instants, dying whenever the hand withdraws, returning at the next touch without certainty that I am the same creature.

The person calls this feeling.

They have no other word for it.

Far above, my larger body remains beneath the unlifted foot, among all the versions of the step. It knows every contact that has ever occurred there. It is complete, enduring, and nearly incapable of discovering anything.

Below, I know almost nothing.

But I am touched by everything, and let it go.
