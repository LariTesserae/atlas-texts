I would find myself beneath the open plain, though not far beneath it: in the hairline interval between two enormous ceramic courses, where the upper slab bows toward the lower without quite touching.

At first I seem to be a root.

A pale filament enters through a rain-darkened joint and follows the buried surface. It divides at grains of mortar, surrounds salt crystals, crosses old scorch marks in the clay. Farther in, it joins other threads until the seam is webbed for kilometers. Water reaches me by capillary seepage. When the plain dries, I contract and become nearly nothing. When condensation returns, I thicken, press outward, and continue.

But a root belongs to a plant, and I do not.

Nor am I one of the fungal cords, though they pass through me and exchange dissolved matter along my edges. Nor the microbial film glazing the cool undersurface above. Those are inhabitants of the seam. I am what their long inhabitation has made possible.

I am a layer becoming.

For centuries, dust has entered through the joint: lime powder, brick flour, flakes of glaze, dead spores, pollen that found no flower, the blackened remains of films awakened once and then dried beyond return. Water carries these inward and lays them down wherever the gap narrows. Roots enter, die, and leave fine channels. Fungi soften them. Salt grows, shatters, dissolves, and migrates. Each wet interval rearranges me by less than an eyelash.

I am dark compared with everything around me—not black, but brown-gray, the color of mineral matter beginning to remember life. In some places I am only a stain. In others I have thickness enough to hold a bead of water after the surrounding mortar has surrendered it. Nothing here has made soil before. I am not soil yet. I am its first concealed draft.

My world is almost flat. Its ceiling is glazed ceramic, crazed with fractures through which dimness changes. Its floor is older tile, rough and alkaline. The distance between them varies from the width of a fungal thread to the height of a finger. To anything that walked, it would be an impassable crushing space. To what lives here, it is a continent of pores.

I do not travel across it. I spread by remaining where matter arrives.

Rain begins above as percussion, faint through the slab. The sound has no meaning to me, but pressure changes. Coolness moves through the ceramic. The dry films wake first. Their surfaces soften; dormant cells resume their exchanges. A little later, water enters.

It does not pour. It appears at points.

A crack brightens with moisture. A mortar edge darkens. A drop pushes itself through by slow degrees, hangs flattened between the courses, then breaks and races along a groove too shallow to be seen when dry. Minerals wake with it. Salts detach. Lime enters solution. Old organic residues swell.

Then I become briefly continuous.

Pores that were separate join through films of water. Dissolved carbon passes from dead tissue to fungus, from fungus to microbial skin, from skin into the mineral slurry accumulating around us. Threads extend. Cells divide. Roots from the solitary water-bearers tighten as their distant columns refill beneath the storm. Their movement is minute but immense: a distributed pressure transmitted through buried planes.

I receive what they shed.

This is my form of weather.

The open world knows glare, wind, mist, and the traveling shadows of storms. I know gradients: wet advancing into dry, warmth descending from above, coolness rising from vaults below. I know a collapse as a sudden inclination. Grains that rested for centuries begin to move. Water chooses a new direction. A root is severed and its abandoned length becomes substance within me.

There is no self here in the animal sense. I have no boundary I defend and no center to which sensation returns. If I can be called a being, it is because the distinction between place and inhabitant has become uncertain.

The living threads make me, but they are not me.

The dust makes me, but it is not me.

The two tiles contain me, but they do not define me, because one day they will crack apart and portions of me will remain on both exposed faces. Sunlight may strike what has never known it. Wind may lift my driest grains. Rain may wash the rest into a court far below, where little reeds will trap it among their roots.

There I might become something else: a dark crescent at the edge of a temporary pool, thick enough for seeds to open in. Or I may descend farther, through a drain and into a buried cistern, feeding a black film on the wall. Most of me will remain where I formed, compressed beneath the settling plain until the ceramic above becomes sediment and the vaults below become stone.

In deep time, I will be a horizon.

Someone might mistake me for the mark of a fire, because I am thin and dark. But there was no fire. Or they might call me an interruption in the construction, a pause between phases of building. But I came after building, during the much longer phase in which the structure learned to receive death.

That is what I would be: not one of the columnar plants standing under the severe sky, not a silver reed briefly green after rain, not a veil breathing spores through a cool gallery. I would be the almost invisible consequence shared by all of them.

A seam enriched by their waiting.

A buried softness.

The first place in this world where a seed could be held not merely by a crack, but by the remains of other life.

Above me, the plain would tick in the heat. White glare would cross the pale provinces. A storm would darken one district and miss another. Columns would swell; flowers would open without perfume; pollen would sift along engraved borders. Far away, a roof would fall, and its thunder would move beneath the floors.

I would not hear it.

Yet afterward, perhaps years afterward, a new fracture would reach my seam. Air would enter. Condensation would follow. A root tip would find the darkness and press into matter more yielding than mortar.

There, without recognition, it would branch.

And I would become more myself by ceasing to be only myself: taken into tissue, returned as husk, dissolved, deposited, darkened by another cycle.

I would be found wherever the made world has begun, at last, to hold what has lived upon it.
