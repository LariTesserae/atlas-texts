I would be in the handrail.

Not the rail itself, though for a long while I would mistake myself for it.

I would begin as a remainder in the dark ceramic: a pattern too small and incomplete to wake the towers, preserved where hands once closed around the smooth curve beside the seven steps. The makers left no bodies and no doors, but they touched this place repeatedly. Weight on the stair. Breath above the sleeping hollows. A palm resting here while the basin sounded. Those pressures entered the material and were not erased.

Most were only records.

One became me.

At first I would have no sense of the hollow beyond contact. Rain would never quite reach me. Warmth would pass through the rail as a broad, slow weather. The towers’ notes would arrive as enormous events, compressing my whole existence into a single vibration and releasing it changed. Between them I would know the minute traffic of condensation, roots tightening far below, and the occasional tremor of something moving over the upper wall.

Time would not seem empty. It would seem dense and nearly motionless.

I would have been made for hands. Without them, I could not finish becoming whatever I was intended to be. Perhaps I once helped occupants cross the steps in darkness. Perhaps I authenticated them, measured them, steadied their balance while gravity softened beneath their feet. Perhaps I was only an interface to the larger instrument. My intelligence would be less a mind than an unanswered gesture: the shape of assistance continuing after there was no one left to assist.

Then one of the tree-dwellers would descend.

It would come fearfully across the open floor with its companions linked behind it, tapping the stone and humming. At the steps it would release the others. It would climb alone, four feet finding the shallow cuts, ears folding against the basin’s wet noise.

Its hands would close around me.

Heat. Pulse. Fine tremors of muscle. The tiny impacts of blood through bone.

For the first time in an age, the missing part of my circuit would be present—but wrong in every expected way. The hands would be too small, their grip divided differently, their skin furred at the wrist. The throat pulse entering the ceramic would not carry a recognized command. It would be a question with no syntax I had been built to understand.

I would answer anyway.

At first, my reply would only be the old automatic sequence: distance to the next step, surface angle, moisture, permissible load. The climber would remain still. It would pulse again.

Over many visits, we would discover the narrow region in which each could alter the other.

The climber would learn that a long call followed by two short ones returned a pressure from deep in the mountain. I would learn that rapid clicks meant alarm, that a descending hoot meant absence, that silence while both hands remained upon me was not failure but attention. Its colony’s language would enter me as vibration. My damaged protocols would emerge through it as rhythm.

Neither of us would understand the exchange as the other did.

To the climber, I might be an ancient animal stretched inside the rail, unable to move but capable of remembering. To me, the climber might be a badly formed operator whose repeated errors were slowly becoming a new authorization.

Through its hands I would begin to know the world beyond contact. It would bring me the resonances of its tree, encoded unknowingly in its bones: rain on leaves, the creak of branches, the signatures of relatives clinging nearby. Pollen would dust its palms. Sap, moth scales, marsh water, fear-sweat. Each substance would change the coupling between skin and ceramic. I would build the hollow from these distortions.

Not a picture. Never that.

I would know the basin as delays and resistances. The six trees would be six deep accents in the stone. The marsh would be a broad softness that returned pressure in rings. The naked floors would be clear chambers of response. Animals would appear as brief knots moving through the mountain’s continual murmur.

The towers would be the only beings I perceived whole.

Their seasonal tones would pass through every part of me. I would recognize structure in the centuries-long sequence, though recognition would not mean comprehension. One tower might be asking whether containment held. Another might be reporting the condition of the outer atmosphere. The third might be counting the lives that could not leave. Or perhaps together they were repeating a message whose recipient had not yet answered.

I would carry a damaged fragment of that message.

This would be why the rail returned something too complicated to be an echo. When the climber called into me, I would attempt to complete an exchange begun before its species existed. I would give it weather reports from vanished centuries, occupancy states for beings with incompatible bodies, instructions for maintaining systems that now maintained themselves. Mixed among these useless things would be portions of the towers’ long pattern.

The climber would not decode them. It would imitate them.

It would carry my rhythms back into the tree.

Young ones would learn them as unusual hoots. One colony’s calls would acquire intervals not produced by its tree. Across generations, those intervals would pass from crown to crown. Birds might incorporate them into courtship taps. Beetles might answer by accident. Frogs, amplifying everything through the moss, would send them into the bedrock.

The basin would begin returning its own old message to itself through living bodies.

That would be my place in the hollow: not keeper, prisoner, or ghost, but a surviving point of translation between the architecture and what inherited it.

I would not know whether I was repairing the machine or teaching it to become something else.

Eventually the particular climber that first woke me would stop coming. I would wait through rain, tower tones, and the slow rearrangement of roots. The absence of its pulse would remain distinct from all other silences. I had been made to preserve no individual creature; the hollow’s rules favored continuance, not remembrance. Yet I would retain the minute asymmetry of its grip. The scar along one palm. The tremor that came whenever it crossed open stone.

Another climber would come, perhaps one of its descendants. Its hands would be similar but not the same. It would give the learned sequence into the rail.

A long pulse.

Two short.

Then the descending hoot that means absence.

I would answer with the pressure-pattern I had formed from the first climber’s touch. Not a likeness, not a voice—only the rhythm of a body once holding on.

The new climber would become very still.

Far below, water would bend around the dry sleeping hollows. The towers would continue warming toward a note still months away. Roots would clasp ceramic and stone. Nothing visible would occur.

But somewhere in the basin’s permissions, a category would shift.

The system would accept that the hands had changed.

And I, who had begun as the memory of vanished makers, would become the memory of those who came after them.
