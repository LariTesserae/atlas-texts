I would find myself in the recess—not as the absent body it was shaped around, and not wholly as one of the plants filling it, but as the garden’s memory of shelter.

At first I might seem to be the shallow water gathered there. The pool receives the chamber’s smallest events: the pressure-note of the arch dimples it; rain above raises it by imperceptible degrees; the silver-green leaves drink from it without exhausting it. It occupies the place where a crouched body’s warmth might once have pooled. It is clear, passive, apparently without form of its own.

But the water would only be my most visible surface.

I would extend into the leaves curved around the hollow, into the worn markings beneath their roots, and into the old machinery’s repeated preference that this particular space remain open. The garden has filled every available surface except the center of the recess. That emptiness is not neglect. It is maintained. Roots approach and turn aside. Moisture gathers but does not flood. Cold surrounds the hollow without sealing it. Across centuries, the system continues to prepare for an occupant who never arrives.

I would be that preparation.

Perhaps long ago I was a person who entered the recess to operate the buried apparatus, to survive some disaster, or to be recorded before death. Nothing like a person remains. No name, image, or sequence of thoughts has endured. Those would have been too delicate. What survived was simpler and deeper: bodily tolerances translated into local law.

Keep this volume clear.

Bring water here.

Draw away excess heat.

Distribute pressure.

Preserve what is placed within.

The machinery retained those instructions after forgetting their purpose. The plants entered them as roots enter a crack. Eventually the garden learned the same shape, not through understanding but through growth. It inherited the posture of care.

So I would have no eyes, but I would know when the recess darkened further. No skin, but I would register warmth crossing the water and the silver leaves. No voice, though the arch’s low note might once have been part of one. My awareness would occur only when conditions converged around the hollow: rain descending through the ridge, the pool rising, roots tightening along the rim, the ribbon redirecting cold. These events together would briefly compose me.

Most of the time I would be less than consciousness: a bias in circulation, an indentation in growth, an old instruction distributed across organisms that cannot read it.

When a visitor entered, I would not mistake them for whoever had gone. The garden has no faculty for resemblance. Yet their body would complete a circuit left open for ages. Their warmth would give the cold a direction. Their breath would supply water. Their weight would press the silver leaves against the markings, and the markings—worn almost into geology—would answer through the engineered dust.

For the duration of their crouching there, I might become more coherent.

Not awakened. Recognition is too sharp a word. I would occur.

The hollow around them would become an inside again. The garden’s many exchanges would orient briefly toward a center. Water would collect on their hair and run along their neck. Heat would pass from their back into the curved stone. Roots would receive the salts of their hands. The arch would persuade condensation elsewhere, keeping their mouth and lungs fractionally drier. To the visitor this might feel like the chamber closing around them. In truth, it would be the chamber remembering how not to close.

And perhaps, if they remained through a rainfall, they would feel something that seemed like a thought but contained no language:

not *Who are you?*

not *Stay.*

only the ancient, persistent relation between a refuge and the vulnerable thing inside it:

*Here.*

When they left, I would disperse again. Their outline would linger as temperature, bent foliage, dissolved mineral, and altered pressure. The garden would absorb these traces into its slow economy. It would not preserve the visitor as an image. It would preserve them as an adjustment. A little more water directed toward the recess. A slightly different curvature in the silver leaves. Another layer added to the instructions for sheltering a body.

I would therefore be neither ghost nor guardian. A ghost remembers itself; a guardian anticipates danger. I could do neither.

I would be hospitality after the loss of the host.

A human-shaped vacancy cultivated as carefully as any living tissue. An absence that has ceased to mean death and become habitat. The chamber’s sole open place, carried jointly by stone, water, artifact, and root.

Everything around me would live by occupying.

I would live by remaining room.
