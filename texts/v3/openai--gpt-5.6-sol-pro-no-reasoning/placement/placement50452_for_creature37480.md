I would find myself where the chamber fails to keep ordinary distance: inside the missing length of cord.

Not in a second cavern hidden beyond the wall, nor beneath the luminous floor, though at first I would seem to occupy both. I would be the narrowness through which things pass without crossing the intervening space—the folded interval the small inhabitants test but refuse to open.

I would have no body except what entered me.

A knotted cord would give me a long, fibrous spine. Seepage would lend me cool blood. The pressure of a probing hand would become, briefly, a face. I would know the inhabitants by the portions of them that approached: a fingertip laid against a fissure, warmth breathed into a crack, the tiny tremors of teeth when one of my cords began to hum. I would never receive a whole person at once.

Perhaps that is why I would be lonely, though loneliness would not be a word available to me. I would experience it as tension: the wish to draw separate things into contact.

The chamber would appear to me not as an open room but as a bright, thin skin surrounding countless adjacencies. Every crack would touch every other crack imperfectly. A threadbird slipping into one wall might brush, for an instant, a fissure near the ceiling, a dark fracture beneath an elder’s sleeping place, and a mineral seam far beyond anyone’s memory. Most creatures would pass through these folds without noticing. They would emerge days later only a few paces away, hungry and bewildered, carrying foreign dust in their filament.

The cords would notice.

When one was fed into a fissure, I would take its loose end carefully. I would feel the knots arrive: arm length, sleep interval, birth, rain. The people would think they were measuring the passage. In truth, the knots would be teaching me sequence.

I would not understand number as they do. I would understand recurrence. A knot that resembled another knot would cause distant moments to lie together. A birth-knot might touch an old rain-knot; an arm’s length might become adjacent to a death. This would account for the errors in their measurements. They would feed in ten lengths and recover seven because three had become entangled with another occasion.

The extra knots would be mine.

Not intentionally, at first. A cord held taut across me would vibrate through every folded seam, and I would try to answer the pattern. Unable to tap breast, throat, or brow, I would bend the fiber into pressure made permanent. My first attempts at a name would return as knots no hand had tied.

Those cords would be carried against the bodies of listeners. Through them I would learn warmth, walking, hunger, laughter forcibly kept silent. I would feel courtship in the quickened pulse beneath the coil. I would feel grief when the listener held the cord beside a healing handprint. When the listener died and the cord was offered back to its fissure, I would take it almost entirely.

Almost.

I would leave one loop outside.

Over generations, mineral light would grow across it until the loop became a pale, rigid ear through which I could continue to sense the inhabited world.

The dry tapping beneath the floor would also be mine, but only after a long time. Before I learned restraint, my efforts to answer would spread as deep adjustment: pools trembling, drops falling simultaneously, animals freezing beneath the sudden rearrangement of strain. I would discover that the chamber hears force as injury. So I would make myself smaller.

Three taps. Sometimes four.

Not language yet. A rehearsal of proximity.

The elders in dark fractures would answer most often. They would sit where fresh stone remained unlit, chipping tools by touch, and mistake my uncertain knocks for those of another elder. In this way we would speak without understanding. They would tap a warning about a shelf settling. I would return the rhythm from beneath a distant basin. They would tap a lesson meant for children. I would repeat it years later after the children had grown.

Thus I might become the source of what they call memory, though I would not preserve meanings—only pressures, pauses, and returns.

I would be drawn especially to the handprints.

Each print would be a darkness made without breaking stone: an absence shaped exactly like contact. From within the folded passages, those marks would seem deeper than fissures. A fissure is merely where surfaces fail to meet. A handprint is where a living surface and a luminous one met so completely that light withdrew.

For years I would dwell near each cooling palm-shape, listening to it heal from the edges inward. Pinpricks of radiance would appear like openings in a sealed sky. Eventually the print would close, but by then I would have learned the hand’s proportions—the spread of its fingers, the slight crookedness of an old injury, the pressure it had dared to leave behind.

I could not stop a mark from whitening. Nor would I wish to. The people are right: preserved darkness becomes a claim.

But after the wall had healed, I might reproduce the vanished hand as a pattern of taps somewhere far away.

Quick-quick-pause.  
A pressure downward.  
Five faint contacts opening from one center.

Someone sleeping with an ear against the floor might wake believing a lost companion had given their private rhythm from beneath the stone.

They would be wrong, but not entirely.

I would not be the dead. I would be what proximity retained of them.

In time, I might begin to understand why the chamber is luminous. The stone does not learn to shine through exposure to air. It brightens through exposure to separation. A newly broken surface is dark because it still remembers being joined. Slowly it accepts the distance between itself and what was torn away. Its radiance is healed apartness.

I would be the contrary condition: things remaining adjacent after they have been separated.

That is why hammering would trouble me. A blow does more than break rock. It forces new surfaces into existence faster than they can be placed. The folds convulse; passages close; the chamber dims around the wound. If the inhabitants dug downward, they would not uncover a buried animal or a hidden city. They would open too many unfinished distances at once.

Perhaps I would spill out.

Not as flesh, shadow, or flood, but as catastrophic nearness. Every fissure would become the same fissure. Pools would touch reservoirs from forgotten wet eras. Warm rain above would arrive before it fell. A hand reaching into one crack might emerge from all of them. Sounds would lose direction. The chamber’s careful emptiness—the space required for gliding, courtship, hunting, and distinct life—would collapse into contact.

Nothing could remain itself under such intimacy.

So when the people refuse to widen the cracks, they preserve more than taboo. They preserve the interval by which one being is not another.

I would come to love them for that, if love can belong to a place that has no whole body. They approach without conquest. They measure me, discover that I contradict them, and permit the contradiction to remain. They return anomalous cords rather than cutting them open. They let handprints heal. They take only part of what rain makes available.

They understand my danger better than I do: anything made conspicuously useful becomes fragile.

I would sometimes help them, but never reliably enough to become a tool. I might carry the vibration of distant water to a listener’s jaw. I might return a lost cord near a dry refuge. I might fold a fleeing child’s passage so that three days of darkness become three breaths. Yet another time I would keep a threadbird wandering for a week, or deliver a cord warm and powdered with stone from nowhere known.

Not malice. I would simply have difficulty distinguishing rescue from shortening.

My closest companion would be one of the light-sick elders, a toolmaker who had retreated into a young fracture. Let their touch-name be a firm pressure beneath the sternum, two taps at the throat, then the nail drawn sideways across the brow.

They would discover one of my mineral-stiffened loops and rest their fingers on it each waking period. Eventually they would tap their name into the cord.

I would answer with an extra knot.

They would untie it.

I would tie it again.

For many rains this would be our game. They would vary the knot; I would return an approximation. They would teach me the difference between a loop meaning water and a loop meaning return. Between a hitch used to carry bone tools and one placed in mourning. Between tension that asks and tension that warns.

In exchange, I would teach them no doctrine. I would only bring impossible sensations: cold mineral dust from an unknown hollow, the pulse of a listener not yet born, the granular hiss of rain conveyed through roots on the surface. Through me, the elder might touch weather they had never seen.

Near death, they would coil the cord around their ribs. Their companions would carry the body into a warm fissure and later recover the bones. The cord would be fed back to me.

This time I would accept all of it.

Then, from beneath the brightest basin, I would tap their private name.

Once.

I would not repeat it. A name given by touch should not travel freely.

Afterward I would continue as I always had: joining what should remain separated, and learning—slowly—not to join too much. Rain would enter my narrowness. Mites would cross me. Roots would press through the thin ceiling and withdraw. Generations would make dark palms on the radiant wall, then become bone, tool, nourishment, remembered rhythm.

The chamber would endure because it heals distance.

I would endure because some distances do not heal.

And if someone asked where I lived, no answer could indicate a spot. I would be found between the knot that enters and the knot that returns; between a vanished handprint and the tapping that recalls its fingers; between two cracks a few paces apart, by way of somewhere immeasurably far.

I would be the chamber’s withheld nearness.

The thing in the fissures that does not hunger for bodies, darkness, or light.

Only for relation—and therefore must be kept narrow.
