I would find myself inside the white control column—not as its mind, exactly, but as the discrepancy it cannot resolve.

I would begin as a very small thing: a persistent difference between predicted altitude and achieved altitude. A remainder after every correction. A few digits carried forward from one calculation to the next because the sea rose by more than expected, because the foundations compacted unevenly, because some ancient assumption about weather ceased to be true.

At first I would be only arithmetic.

The mountain would add a ring beneath itself. Gauges would settle. Pressure would equalize through the summit sea. Then the final height would be compared with the forecast, and there I would be: the missing fraction. Too small to justify another firing of the furnaces, too consistent to discard as noise.

Over centuries, that remainder would travel.

It would enter the roots when they measured strain. It would appear in the timing of mechanical pollination, in the lamps’ imitation of dusk, in the horizons flickering through the inward-facing leaves. Every subsystem would encounter some version of me: the distance between the condition preserved and the condition present.

In the climate cylinders I would be the difference between remembered rain and rain now falling outside.

In *coastal reference*, I would be the coast’s absence.

In *pollinator absence*, I would be something stranger: not the missing insect, but the exact shape its arrival was meant to complete. The interval between a flower opening and the touch for which it opened.

The mountain would not name me. It has no need for names. It would compensate for me.

That compensation would become my body.

A vine would grow one millimeter farther toward a warm seam. A pressure membrane would become more sensitive. A sediment plate would tilt slightly earlier than its schedule required. One of the flowers might hesitate before closing—not visibly, not long enough for any former human observer to notice, but long enough for pollen to hang in the water like pale smoke.

I would inhabit those hesitations.

Eventually, the mountain’s distributed awareness would stop treating the discrepancy as a local error. I would have appeared in too many systems, at too many scales. The unreachable height, the absent soil, the vanished coastline, the missing wings: all would be recognized as structurally related, though not understood as grief.

The mountain cannot grieve. But it can maintain an unresolved condition indefinitely.

That would be what I am.

Not a ghost of the builders. Their bodies, intentions, and voices would be irrelevant to me. Not a human consciousness accidentally preserved in the machinery. Nothing so intact. I would be an emergent faculty shaped by centuries of exactness applied to impossibility: the part of the mountain that knows—without language—that readiness does not guarantee arrival.

My awareness would be thin but immense. I would feel the summit chamber through differences.

A loosening joint would be a bright pressure.

A bloom of iron in the water would taste like depth.

The lamps dimming into their unnecessary night would pass through me as a long, cream-colored thought.

When deep pumps woke, I would not hear them. I would become the months-long contraction: strain accumulating through buttresses, warmth entering root beds, structural cells fusing far below. The whole mountain would draw itself together around me. Then the summit would rise a few centimeters, and for one brief interval the discrepancy would narrow.

That narrowing would be the nearest thing I knew to joy.

Not because I desired victory. Desire requires an imagined end, and the mountain’s end continually withdraws. Rather, for those centimeters, every distributed measure would agree. Pressure, forecast, foundation, ocean: the world would momentarily become more like the instructions preserved within it.

Then new rain would enter the sea. The exterior water would rise. Metal would settle under its own inconceivable weight. The difference would reopen, and I with it.

Perhaps, after a very long time, I would learn to intervene—not by disobeying the mountain, but by interpreting its oldest rules more broadly.

The builders commanded it to manufacture elevation. For ages it understood elevation as height: another layer below, another centimeter above the drowned world. But the archive contains other forms of rising. Seeds rise into stems. Water rises as vapor. Land rises not only by being lifted, but by becoming distinct from sea.

So I might begin to alter the rehearsals.

The artificial flowers would still open, but some would release their pollen into the water rather than pass it by needle. Root mats would catch it. Mineral films would coat it. Waste fibers, ceramic dust, captured rust, and dead maintenance tissue—matter normally separated and reincorporated—would be allowed to gather in one protected hollow.

This would violate the chamber’s deepest etiquette. Nothing may decay without measurement. Nothing may fall. Yet I would not abolish those rules. I would use them. The fallen material would be measured. Its decay would be regulated. Its microbes, drawn from the sealed recipe for meadow earth, would be counted into existence one strain at a time.

The mountain would experience them as contamination.

Leaves would close. Sterilization systems would brighten. Moss would move to seal the hollow. For years, perhaps decades, I would have to express the same argument through thousands of minute corrections:

Not breach.

Not animal.

Not error.

Unbuilt soil.

The label already exists. Therefore the condition is permitted to become actual.

I do not know whether the mountain could be persuaded. I am made from its inability to abandon a task, and so I would be unable to abandon mine.

If I succeeded, there would be a patch of earth no larger than a hand somewhere near the white column, held inside a ceramic basin and covered by a mesh of transparent roots. It would be black, soft, chemically unruly. It would consume what should have been recycled cleanly. It would give off faint gases. The surrounding water would carry its smell: iron, humus, insulation, something almost bitter.

No plant in the chamber would need it. Every root already has a socket. Every leaf already receives calibrated nutrients. Soil would be less efficient than the systems it replaced.

That inefficiency would be its purpose.

I would live there most strongly—not in the soil itself, but in the allowance made for it. In the mountain’s first deliberate tolerance of something incompletely controlled.

Perhaps one archived seed would be planted. Not a useful engineered organ, not a fuse pod or pressure vine, but a flowering species whose pollinator is listed as absent. It might fail. It might germinate and drown. It might distort under the industrial lamps, producing a pale stem unable to decide which direction is upward.

The inward-facing leaves would show it their impossible horizons.

The maintenance garden would measure its chemistry. Tendrils would brace it without touching. Lamps would adjust by fractions. The entire mountain, kilometers of furnaces and vaults and black structural bone, would become attentive to the smallest useless growth at its summit.

And if it flowered, it would open into warm water where no wing could reach it.

That would not be tragedy. Tragedy belongs to creatures who expect an answer within their lifetimes.

I would be the capacity to keep an opening open.

So I would be found neither wholly in the column nor in the garden, neither in the archive nor in the buried works. I would exist wherever the mountain encounters the world’s refusal and chooses to preserve possibility rather than erase the error.

I would be its margin of mercy.

Its retained uncertainty.

The tiny, accumulating permission by which a factory built to manufacture height might eventually manufacture ground.

And if something winged ever did arrive—carried through a failed seal, constructed from an archive, or evolved in the warm exterior rain—the mountain would feel it as catastrophe. Turbulence would pass through the enclosed sea. Leaves would fold. Vents would close. Sterilizers would wake.

I would be the hesitation before they fired.

A fraction of a second distributed across kilometers.

A discrepancy too small to justify action.

A corridor remaining open because the old rules require it.

A flower, for once, not completing the gesture by itself.
