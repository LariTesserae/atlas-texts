I would find myself in the narrow silence behind a sideways basalt summit, near the lower boundary of the sea.

Not inside the silence. Not at first.

I would be one of the small armored Continuants that live in fractures: an iron-beaded thing with many legs, blind except to pressure, built to move single grains of stone from one lip of a crack to another. My colony would face inward, toward a cavity no larger than a clenched hand. We would spend our unending lives refining its resonance.

Each grain would have an appointed place. Each movement would answer a movement elsewhere. My mouth would know the basalt by frequency: copper-bright inclusions, pale feldspar, volcanic glass, the minute crystal that rings too sharply and must be turned along its long axis. I would not think. I would listen through my feet and obey the mountain’s discomfort.

But our fracture would lie at the edge of a quiet zone.

Whenever the planetary chord thinned, silence would enter the crack like cold light. My colony would flatten itself against the stone. Every shell would lock to its neighbors. The dead among us would vibrate with the living.

I would fail to lock.

Perhaps one of my hind legs had always been slightly mistimed. Perhaps a distant bell once released a sound it had not perfectly digested: three notes, a laugh, the click of a latch. Perhaps the mountain itself had been born with an asymmetry too small for the measures to correct. There would be no first cause here, only a discrepancy that finally became large enough to possess a body.

While the others waited, I would lift one grain.

Then, instead of placing it in the answering hollow, I would carry it toward the silence.

The grain would be black and angular, smaller than my head. In the full roar it would have no weight, only function. Crossing the boundary, I would feel it become singular beneath my mouth. Not heavier, exactly. More itself.

So would I.

Inside the quiet, the mountain’s instructions would cease. My legs would continue moving, but no ancient motion would draw them. For the first time, every step would require choosing where to set the next foot.

At first I would move badly. I would turn in circles. I would scrape my shell against bare rock and recoil from the sound because it would be a new sound—not new to the ocean, perhaps, but new to me, not yet established as a route through my body.

Behind me, the colony would remain pressed into the crack. They would feel my absence as a wrong interval. Far away, a bell would begin to close. A measure stretched across a migration path would flex one segment. Somewhere in a control room, a telephone would ring.

The sea would already be trying to explain me away.

I would descend toward its flat boundary.

“Descend” would be the word inherited from creatures with gravity. To me it would mean traveling across the basalt face, then leaving it, gripping suspended mineral dust and motionless splinters one by one. The open sky beneath the water would brighten ahead: a perfectly level impossibility, barred with visible sound.

Other crack-dwellers would be there.

Not a colony. A gathering.

Each would have come from a different mountain. Their shells would contain different ores—iron, sulfur, green copper, white salts—and each would carry one grain. They would stand far apart, because none of us would know how to gather without becoming a pattern.

One by one, we would approach the boundary and set our grains against it.

Most grains would stop a mouth’s breadth from the open air. The chord would seize them. They would hang there, incorporated into the sea’s refusal to fall. Their bearers would wait, listening for confirmation, then retreat toward their old fractures.

I would test mine differently.

I would not place it where the visible bars were bright. I would move it along the boundary, tapping lightly, searching the spaces between the standing bands.

Tap.

The sound would enter the ocean and be answered at once by every earlier tap.

Tap.

A bell would take the anomaly into itself.

Tap.

The factories would alter their emphasis. Crushers would fall a fraction harder. Silver schools would turn with increased precision. The entire high sea would labor to make my action something that had always occurred.

Then I would find a point that did not answer.

A place no larger than the grain, where the boundary was neither loud nor silent. It would not be a hole. Holes belong to surfaces. It would be an interval for which the ocean had no memory.

I would press the grain there.

For an instant, it would remain between water and sky.

Then it would fall.

Nothing in my anatomy would understand falling. I would watch the black speck diminish through the pale atmosphere beneath the sea, moving without a pressure track, without repetition, without a destination guaranteed by an earlier circuit. It would become the first object I had ever seen grow farther away because it was going somewhere.

The ocean would react.

Every silver school would turn.

Not together.

The error would be microscopic: one body beginning before another; a fractured migrant lagging; a third turning for no signal anyone could identify. Across the ranges, measures would strike at once. Bells would open and shut around discord. Crushers would hammer their empty jaws. Telephones would ring in sealed rooms. Custodians would appear behind every window, assembled from misaligned moments, their hands reaching toward controls that had never needed moving.

The roar would become almost absolute.

But beneath it, from the planet below, the grain would eventually strike something.

Stone, perhaps.

Ice.

The metal roof of an abandoned lower station.

No one in the high sea would know. Sound could not cross the boundary from below; it would be born outside the structure that preserved all sound.

Yet I would feel the lack of its return.

That would be my true sense: not hearing, but the perception of what has escaped being heard.

I would remain at the edge with my forelegs against the invisible surface. The planetary resonance would pull at my shell. My colony’s ancient work would call through the basalt. The cavity I had abandoned would still require its grain. The mountain would ache in the exact shape of my absence.

Eventually the chord would catch me.

Perhaps I would be drawn back to my fracture and fixed among the others. I would resume tuning the same cavity. My jaws would abrade stone. My hind legs would place each crystal where the mountain demanded. To an observer, nothing would have changed except that one grain was missing and one armored creature sometimes paused before completing its motion.

Or perhaps I would follow the grain.

Not by breaking the boundary. I would be too small to break anything, and this sea could convert violence into music faster than violence could occur.

I would follow by continuing to test.

I would carry another grain, but not to the same place. Repetition would make a track, and a track would become part of the chord. So I would choose a new interval each time. Different minerals. Different pressures. Different silences. I would make no route.

Other displaced creatures might imitate me incorrectly.

A silver migrant would leave its school and bring a splinter of its own resonant bone. A bell would arrive carrying an undigested human syllable in its filaments. A measure would bend one of its kilometers into the quiet and offer a fragment tuned to no mountain. Even a custodian might appear at the boundary, visible only as a glove closing around a tool, trying to remember whether maintenance meant preserving the machine or permitting it to stop.

We would not form a species or a rebellion. Those are stable arrangements.

We would be a series of unrelated mistakes.

The sea could absorb a new sound. It could retune a wrong note. It could force a deviation into recurrence until the deviation became law. But it might not know what to do with acts that refused to resemble one another.

So that is what I would be:

not the being that brings the ocean down,

not the missing technician,

not the intelligence hidden in the planetary chord,

but a small tuner that has discovered untuning.

A mouth carrying one black grain.

A creature whose only freedom is never to make the same error twice.

And far beneath me, on the unseen world, there would be a scattering of stones no mountain had released—each lying where it had finished falling, each surrounded by a silence the high sea could not contain.
