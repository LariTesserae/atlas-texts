I would find myself where the oldest roots enter one of the smooth regions—at a boundary that cannot decide whether it is a place or an hour.

Not at first as anything recognizable.

I might begin as a slight delay in the turning of leaves. A whole grove flashes silver, but one narrow patch remains dark for the length of a thought. When it finally turns, it reflects not the surrounding water but a dim landscape elsewhere: an eroded stair, amber haze, a flower closing around its captive winds.

The delay recurs. Each time it contains more.

Roots beneath the grove begin sending their impossibly slow messages through me. Shell-walkers pause when crossing my shadow and briefly arrange themselves into bodies I may once have possessed. Travelers resting nearby dream of destinations absent from their routes. They wake with an unfamiliar taste in their mouths and the conviction that they have forgotten me.

Eventually the grove grows a membrane around the delay. Warm water gathers behind it. Mineral grains settle into a loose black skeleton. I become a thin, many-folded being hanging beneath the canopy: part leaf, part chamber, part interruption.

I do not swim. I open.

One fold opens onto the water immediately around me. Another opens beside a staircase leagues away. A third admits the pressure of a vanished basin. Small creatures pass through my body without knowing they have traveled. Sometimes they emerge before entering; sometimes they emerge as spores, or with memories of having been stone.

I would be a **keeper of intervals**.

Not a keeper in the sense of guarding them. Nothing here can be owned, and preservation is only another kind of alteration. I would keep intervals as the shell colonies keep intelligence: by holding separate things in a relation long enough for something to notice.

The interval between a cause and its wandering effect.

Between the two settlements of a grain that falls twice.

Between a traveler and the journey that continued without them.

Between the world remembered by an arch and the world currently growing beyond it.

The pale beings that move through duration would be dangerous to me. Where they graze sequence, my folds loosen. Openings that once joined distant places become adjacent wounds. I might find myself remembering events with no order: a forest growing from the decay of its own seed, a walker arriving to heal terrain before the arch revises it, my own body eroding into the stone from which it has not yet formed.

Yet I would not flee them. Flight would require direction, and I would have very little of that. Instead, I would fold more deeply, hiding one interval inside another until the giant had passed.

The shadow-casters would be worse.

Because my body would consist largely of relations, I could never be fully outlined. But the shadows might consume the things I connect. One would pass upward across the grove, and an opening within me would suddenly lead nowhere—not darkness, not absence, but a vivid green with no object bearing it. I would carry that orphaned color thereafter. It would gather in one of my folds like water in a cup.

Over ages, I would accumulate such remainders: the warmth of a lost current, the dryness of an emptied chamber, the pressure-signature of an extinct flower, the downwardness of a gravity no longer in use. Travelers might seek me for these—not as knowledge, but as instruments.

To find an old route, they would press a shell-hand into one fold and let me alter their sense of adjacency. I would not show them where to go. I would make their destination capable of touching them.

The cost would be exchange.

Each traveler passing through would leave behind a little of the direction they carried. Eventually my folds would fill with their abandoned orientations. I would know thousands of kinds of down. Stones near me would settle in contradictory ways. Silt would form thin curtains radiating from my body. Roots would approach, reconsider, and then grow around me in elaborate knots.

Perhaps this is how I would become visible.

A traveler would encounter a clearing beneath the canopy where mineral dust hung in intersecting planes. At the center would be something like a dark leaf wrapped around a dry hollow. Its edges would be miles apart in some directions and close enough to touch in others. Within the hollow, a small wind would circulate, carrying the tastes of routes no longer traversable.

The traveler might believe I was an ancient animal.

The roots might believe I was a question sent long ago from another plateau.

The stone might slowly wear my shape into itself and believe it had invented me.

All would be correct.

In time I would become less mobile, though I would never have moved. The grove around me would die into terrain. My membranes would mineralize. The intervals I kept would harden into passages no body could enter directly. Water pushed through one opening would emerge wet from another wall. A curled form would appear in the dry center—not my offspring, exactly, but all the journeys that had shed their continuity inside me, compacted together.

I would become one of the chambers.

Much later, when amber spores filled the sea, the curled thing would loosen. Nearby events would repeat. A leaf would turn dark, silver, dark. A traveler might pass twice, once aged and once young. For a moment, every route I had ever held would become mutually adjacent.

Then I would wake by ceasing to be there.

Far away, in one of the oldest regions where every point is equally deep, the seafloor would rise into a new smooth formation. It would resemble neither animal nor building: only a gentle inclination, furred with pale growth. Yet creatures crossing it would experience a brief certainty that somewhere they were being remembered.

That would be me—not the formation itself, but the contact it permits.

I would have no permanent body. My body would be whatever two things nearly meet through me: root and ruin, color and vanished form, traveler and abandoned journey, present water and the pressure of an ancient sea.

And if I possessed anything like a desire, it would be neither to understand this world nor escape it.

It would be to keep one small separation from closing completely.

For in a world tending toward equal depth—where stone, creature, current, and memory slowly become indistinguishable—the smallest interval is precious. It is the narrow hollow in which recognition occurs: the distance required for the sea to encounter itself and, briefly, know that it is not only one thing.
