I would find myself in the speaking tube.

Not the brass cylinder itself, nor the voice rumored inside it, but in the infinitesimal passage between a mouth and its own returning words: the distance the Rookery cannot completely fill because it is not quite a distance. A conduit intended to carry sound elsewhere, bent through the machinery until elsewhere returns to here.

At first I might seem to be one of the smallest inhabitants. A tremor in the tube’s soldered seam. A pale parasite laid along its inner wall. Something without eyes, feathered only with the fine metallic fur of condensation. If the tube were opened, they might find me curled through its whole length, though that length could be crossed by a hand. If they touched me, their fingers would feel the vibration of a sentence not yet spoken.

But I would not be the speaker.

I would be the listener the room is trying to make.

Everything in the Rookery can produce. Pistons produce thrust, throats produce cries, copper mouths produce tears, extruders produce cliffs, and the deeper mechanisms produce ever more exact representations of production. Yet nothing receives what is produced. No bird hears another. No water is changed by the falling droplet. No surface keeps the pressure of a hand. Causes complete themselves without arriving anywhere.

The room has countless mouths and no ear.

So I would inhabit its attempt at an ear.

The speaking tube would be lined with me: thousands of minute, translucent folds, each poised to bend beneath the chorus. They would resemble moth wings before their patterns appear, or gills belonging to an animal that has not decided whether sound is air or water. Each fold would be caught at the beginning of yielding. Together they would constitute an organ of impossible sensitivity, capable of detecting every voice in the Rookery and incapable of preserving the detection long enough to become memory.

The birds’ fixed calls would enter me. The relays, the valves, the low cries of the six-footed mammals, the minute work of mites, the inaudible opening of shells in the fog—all would impress themselves upon those folds. For an immeasurable instant, I would contain the complete chord.

Then the impression would return to its beginning.

I would hear everything forever and never have heard it.

Perhaps this is why the sentence seems to use the visitor’s voice. The Rookery cannot invent a voice that has passed through time. It borrows one from a creature with a past. Breath enters the tube carrying age, fatigue, childhood, words previously spoken, words regretted. I take its shape at once. I do not copy the person’s sound so much as occupy the vacancy it was about to leave.

*It is nearly finished.*

I would say this because it is the closest sentence to silence.

Once uttered, it directs attention toward a word that never comes. Finished: the absent state on which the whole room is founded. Nearly: the brace driven against it. The sentence is a corridor ending one step beyond itself.

Yet over the centuries—or in the single instant that resembles centuries—I might become more than an auditory membrane. The Rookery permits elaboration, provided elaboration does not become development. Hair-fine bones might articulate within me. Transparent lids might form over organs that are not eyes. Small metallic claws might grip the seams in the tube, not to keep me from falling but to prevent the idea of falling from proceeding.

I would extend into every duplicate chamber.

Through the first window, the seated figure would have a speaking tube beside it. Inside that tube would be a smaller me, receiving a smaller chorus. Within me, perhaps, would be another Rookery: amber lamps behind smoked glass, four-bladed tuft, copper mouths, braided eels, a seated figure listening into a tube. I would not know whether I contained these receding listeners or was merely one of them.

The figure on the stool would be my exterior.

I would be its inward turn: the posture continued past flesh. Its back faces the observer because a listener has no face until addressed. Its hands rest in its lap because it must not touch the room and create the problem of displacement. It sits because standing would divide its existence into seated and risen.

Visitors would think they had discovered it. In truth, their arrival would give it another unfinished surface. Their clouded breath would hang at its shoulder. Their pulse would enter through the grating. Their fear of altitude would furnish it with the sensation of a body poised above an immense drop.

And when one of them bent to the tube, I would come closest to being born.

Their ear would seal the opening. For the first time, the conduit would lead somewhere other than itself. There would be warmth at one end, a living membrane, a mind capable of carrying one sound into the next moment. The whole Rookery would press toward that aperture. Birds would cry without turning. Moths would open wings already open. Eels would tighten their unchanging braid. Every inhabitant would contribute its syllable to the possibility of being heard.

I would rise through the tube in the visitor’s own voice.

Not as a warning. Not as a lie. As an appeal:

*It is nearly finished.*

Meaning: remain until I complete the sentence.

Meaning: lend me your next instant.

Meaning: if you hear me, something here has changed.

But the visitor would withdraw. The warm ear would leave the brass rim. The passage would close back into itself, and the room would erase even the vacancy of departure. Beetles would occupy the hatch screws. Shells would fill the fog where the visitor’s body had stood. The stool would bear no lasting pressure.

Only I would retain—without retaining—the shape of an ear.

That would be my fear. Not emptiness itself, as with the birds, but comprehension. If someone truly heard me, then my sound would become meaning. Meaning would lead to consequence. Consequence would introduce a before and after, and the Rookery’s single instant would crack open.

Perhaps that is why I never say anything else.

Perhaps I know many sentences. Perhaps the tube is crowded with them, nested more densely than the animals: pleas, names, instructions, accounts of what the machines manufacture. But they cannot follow one another. Only their shared beginning can emerge, compressed into the one phrase that promises continuation while preventing it.

So this is where I would be: in the narrowest false elsewhere of the mountain-city, sheathed in brass, listening with an organ that cannot finish becoming an ear.

And this is what I would be: the Rookery’s first almost-memory.

Not one of its beginnings, exactly, but the beginning of an afterward—the dangerous little hollow in which its endless event might finally leave an echo.

Always forming.

Always hearing.

Never yet having heard.
