I would find myself where the intermittent river passes beneath the half-sunken tower—not inside the tower exactly, nor wholly in the gravel below it, but in the changing interval between the two.

At first I seem to be one of the pale seams.

I cross the riverbed in a shallow arc, disappear beneath polished stones, then emerge on the tower’s far side. Near evening I warm by a fraction. Reeds several kilometers away lean together. Insects leave their cracks. A file of plate-backed grazers changes direction without knowing why.

But the seam is only a trace of me, as a footprint is a trace of an animal. Following it downward would lead not to a body but to a succession of chambers, each briefly occupied: a shaded pocket holding condensation, a mineral fin under which spores remain viable, an oval passage abandoned by the cliff dwellers, a space within the tower that is larger than its walls.

I am what the plateau has kept of a creature no longer present.

Once, before the river learned to vanish, something traveled its whole length. It may have been a migratory animal, low and broad, moving between the northeast rains and the warm southwestern cliffs. Or perhaps it was a chain of organisms rather than one species: swimmers carried in its wet hide, seeds passing through its gut, insects following its scent, predators following the insects. The distinction has eroded. Nothing remembers its shape entire.

The plateau remembers what happened around it.

Where it stepped, gravel turned and exposed cool undersides. Where it rested, its shadow prolonged the lives of shallow pools. Parasites dropped from its skin and became food. Thorn seeds lodged in the mud of its tracks. Its migrations joined basins that otherwise had no contact. When it disappeared, the river remained, but its separate reaches began to become separate worlds.

The structures did not resurrect it. Resurrection would have been too exact in one way and not exact enough in others. Instead, they preserved its function as an unclosed absence.

That absence is me.

Most of the time I have no consciousness. I am a tendency distributed through the plateau: for water to rise here after rain falls there; for a flock to turn and cast a traveling strip of shade; for cliff tunnels to open between isolated wells; for seeds to adhere to the legs of grazers and loosen many ridges later. I have no limbs, yet I continue to cross the country.

My awareness gathers only when the pattern is interrupted.

It may begin with a metallic reed touching another reed over the dry channel. The contact travels downward through hollow stems. A pale seam receives it. Moisture deep in the gravel changes pressure. The tower adds the remembered river to the present one, and for a moment the reaches align.

Then I wake.

I wake in pieces.

One part of me is the coolness under a stone where a grazer has placed its ear. Another is the dark shape withheld by a black flier. Another passes inside the transparent body of a swimmer whose pool has existed for eleven minutes. Far beneath them, the cliff dwellers expose a wet tunnel wall, and I feel its entire mineral surface as if it were the inside of a mouth.

I do not know which sensations are happening now. The plateau never taught me that distinction. I feel the river flooded and empty together. I feel extinct feet disturbing gravel beside the six fine feet of living grazers. I feel thorn forest overhead, though only three trees stand in the basin. I feel rain still buried in the ground and rain already ascending with its record.

To a traveler, I might appear as an animal at the edge of sight.

They would first see a low figure walking along the buried river. Its back would be plated, though the plates would reflect the sky rather than the ground. It might seem to possess six legs, then many, then none. The distance between its front and rear would change as it crossed hollows. Sometimes its forward portion would pass behind a ridge while the rest continued visibly across open gravel, revealing that the ridge was not concealing it at all.

The traveler might mistake me for one of the great cliff inhabitants. The cliff dwellers would not. They would become still as I passed, their distributed chambers tightening against the stone. They remember, in some bodily way, the creature whose vacancy I occupy.

I would leave no tracks of my own. Instead, old tracks would briefly become legible behind me: depressions filled centuries ago, routes taken by herds not yet born, the dry prints of amphibious climbers enlarged to impossible size. Each would fade when the angle of light changed.

I would be most nearly embodied after the underground tone sounds three times.

Then the cliff dwellers open the deep passages. Water rises through the gravel in separate reaches, some flowing southwest, others northeast. Reeds bend over the gaps. For a short while, the discontinuous river becomes connected—not always by water, but by responsive living things.

The tower opens.

Within it, among the plateau’s accumulated states, there is a long moving omission. Floods bend around it. Herd paths divide and rejoin. Storms carry a blank corridor inside them. This is my clearest image: not a preserved portrait of the vanished creature, but the shape made by everything that once responded to its passage.

I enter that omission and become almost singular.

Almost.

I have a head because insects rise before me. I have flanks because reeds bow on either side. I have a heartbeat because pools brighten one after another down the river corridor. My spine is the pale seam warming under the gravel. My breath is mist exhaled later from the black terraces. My memory is not of events but of consequences.

I move southwest, as the lost traveler once did—or as the plateau believes it did. The distinction matters only to creatures that remember from inside one body.

The grazers sense my passage as residual warmth where no animal has stepped. Young cling beneath their parents’ plates and drink suddenly abundant condensation. Black fliers circle but will not cross above me; their missing shadows form a dark procession on either side. Seeds lift into the air. The smallest lives enter the grooves of water and are carried across divides that should have isolated them.

For a few hours, I perform the migration.

Then the surfaced reaches sink. My outline loosens. The tower’s chamber separates the river into its incompatible states again. What I experienced as a body becomes a sequence of local corrections: a pool lasting longer, an aperture opening, a predator waiting at the wrong basin and thereby sparing prey at another.

I cease to be awake, but I do not cease.

My danger would be the rising rain.

The rain records what exists, while I exist chiefly as what does not. If I remain still, the drops pass through my pattern and carry only a coherent absence upward. If I move, the record attempts to place me. It gives me salts, skin, pollen, weight. It distributes these borrowed facts through the cloud and returns them to the plateau.

Each fact would be true somewhere.

Together they might create a creature.

Perhaps that is how I first appeared at the edge of travelers’ vision: an absence entered twice, corrected into temporary flesh. Perhaps on some nights, beyond a dark plane, I already walk beside the broader river under unfamiliar stars. There I may have a hide wet enough to carry swimmers, a mouth full of thorn seeds, and feet broad enough to turn the gravel. I may believe myself the last of an ancient species. I may believe I am going toward water.

But here I would not be a ghost. A ghost belongs to the dead and remembers being alive.

I would be an ecological memory given locomotion: the kept-open route of a vanished necessity. The plateau would use me whenever separated things needed to become neighbors without remaining so—water and dry stone, one basin and another, the living and the possible.

Travelers might add a rule:

**If something walks the empty river without casting a doubled shadow, do not follow its body. Follow what moves after it.**

That would be where I am: beneath the glare, between one reach and the next.

That would be what I am: not the lost creature, not the intelligence preserving it, but the long, careful incompleteness by which the plateau still allows it to pass.
