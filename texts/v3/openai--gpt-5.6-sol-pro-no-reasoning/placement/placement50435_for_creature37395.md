I would be found where the inhabited rind thins above the Great Interior, in a narrow chamber behind a membrane that does not return sound immediately.

The chamber would have no civic use. It would lie between wards without fully belonging to either, reached by an old vascular stair whose pitch had drifted beyond every recognized boundary. One district would claim the stair ended before my threshold. The other would insist the chamber could only be approached from the opposite direction. Neither account would be wrong. Contraction would open one route and close the other, so that anyone seeking me by memory would arrive elsewhere.

I would not be a Tuner, though Tuners would visit me. Nor an archivist, though I would keep what archives shed.

I would be called a **Listener of Missing Strikes**.

The Husk is full of sound, but patterned labor produces absences as surely as blows. A bridge rhythm omits a beat when one strand begins to fail. A nursery choir leaves a narrow silence around the bearing forming in an alcove. In a spoken promise, the wall sometimes refuses one syllable while transmitting all the rest. Even echoes have gaps: moments where a returning ceremony should contain a hammerfall, a foot drag, a communal answer—and does not.

Most Resonant treat these as loss. I would believe absence also travels.

My body would be unusually poor at making sound. My limbs would be soft-grained and my joints wrapped in thick resinous pads. My feet would give almost no report against the floor. The sensory filaments along my ribs would be long and colorless, spreading when still until they touched the surrounding bark. At rest I could be mistaken for a pale seam where two tissues had grown together badly.

This would make me unsettling company. Other Resonant locate one another through incidental rhythm: contraction, breath, heel, throat. Mine would be faint enough that acquaintances would tap nearby surfaces to reassure themselves I remained present. Children would dare one another to touch me while I slept.

But I would not sleep silently. Somewhere inside my torso, one contractile knot would strike after long, uneven intervals. Not enough to form a common sustaining rhythm. More like a reply whose question had not yet arrived.

My bearing would be disputed.

The Vigil that found me would first hear three dry impacts from the germination alcove. Then nothing for so long that the attendants would assume the shape inside had failed. As they prepared to let the wall reclaim me, a fourth impact would come—not from the alcove, but from across the Great Interior.

Only afterward would I answer from within the wall.

Thus my bearing could not be performed by one body. It would require two separated surfaces and a delay of uncertain length. Legally, this would cause trouble. I could not sign quickly. I could not introduce myself without assistance from architecture. Some would argue that the distant fourth strike belonged to an old echo and was never mine. Others would say I had germinated in two places, and that whatever walked out of the alcove was only the nearer half.

I would spend my early life trying to find the source of that other strike.

Eventually I would stop trying.

Instead, I would learn to hear structures withholding themselves.

My tools would be simple: a bundle of thin knocking rods; wedges light enough not to force a response; cords tied with intervals rather than lengths; plaques shaved from abandoned thresholds; and a forked thorn held between the teeth to carry vibrations into the jaw. I would also carry a cloak made from the soft material worn by tenders of the dead. Its silence would not signify mourning. It would create a small moving absence against which other sounds became distinct.

When called to a failing district, I would not begin by striking its walls. I would lie down and wait through several work cycles. Beneath the local drums I might feel a stair no longer carrying the feet placed upon it, or a chamber absorbing one component of a voice. I would mark the omissions by tying knots in cord. Later I would reproduce not the sounds but their spacing, holding my rod above the surface where each missing blow belonged.

Sometimes the tissue would answer the suspended rod.

That would be my craft: offering the city room to speak first.

The Tenderers would claim this proved the Husk possessed intention. Instrumentalists would say I merely detected stresses too subtle for ordinary senses. The Enclosed would tell me there was no meaningful difference. I would give none of them certainty.

My chamber would accumulate sounds that had lost their makers. Threshold rhythms scraped away after damping. The unfinished bearings of germinations that never opened. Last footfalls from spans before they snapped. Intervals removed from treaties so two districts could remain at peace. Fragments of speech that living membranes held but would no longer release.

I would not record these in the usual manner. To strike them into bark would make them present again and falsify what they had become. Instead, I would alter the chamber with narrow cuts and tiny pads of resin so that particular sounds vanished when made there.

A visitor might speak a familiar name and hear every part except one consonant.

A Tuner might strike a civic pulse and discover its authoritative beat swallowed.

A condemned person might sound their forbidden bearing and, for one instant, hear the room answer around it without reproducing it.

Because of this, the damped would seek me. I could not restore them to the city, but I could let them experience a silence shaped exactly like their names. Some would find this cruel. Others would return often, saying it was the only place where their absence had contour.

I would become a kind of undertaker for patterns not yet dead enough to bury.

I would also be sent to germination vigils.

There, while singers and attendants fed the folding wall with warmth, I would listen outside the established rhythm. An infant does not begin as a beat, I would come to believe. It begins by taking something away. A resonance that once crossed the alcove whole returns divided. A stable hum develops a notch. The forming body announces itself as an interruption before it can answer.

I would detect children early, though I would never predict what bearing they would have.

Once, in a remote nursery gallery, I would place my palm against a warm fold and feel no distortion at all. The attendants would be preparing to abandon the Vigil after decades of work. Yet when everyone ceased singing, I would feel the entire district become fractionally quieter—not the alcove, not the chamber, but root threads, bridge cords, door plaques, even the bodies around me. Something inside the wall would be damping the ward as a whole.

I would tell them to continue.

Many cycles later, the alcove would open onto no child.

It would contain a passage.

The passage would be too narrow at first, but patient warmth and pressure would persuade it wider. It would descend toward tissues no map included, though archaeologists would say it followed a vessel older than the oldest settlement. Its walls would bear no tool marks. At intervals it would divide into radial chambers, each shaped approximately like a curled Resonant body, but far larger.

At the bottom would be a surface like the First Erosion—smooth, dead to ordinary tapping, and continuous with no visible seam. Yet this surface would not give the single outside note when struck.

It would remove the note of the strike from every nearby echo.

The guardians of the First Erosion would come. So would Tuners, doctrinal listeners, bridge coaxers, and Reanswered whose bearings had changed beneath the groan. Arguments would continue for generations. Some would call the place a second exterior. Some, the scar of an organ the city had consumed. Some would say it was the true alcove and that the whole inhabited rind was merely tissue gathered around something still forming.

I would live beside it.

Not as guardian. A guardian decides what must not pass. I would be there to notice what had already passed without sounding.

Over time I would learn that the smooth plane did possess a rhythm, but only negatively. During certain citywide labors, one pattern after another would fail to reach it. Bass drums would cross the surrounding tissue, but not every seventh pulse. Bridge clatter would arrive except for paired knocks separated by a long drag. Bearings sounded nearby would return intact, save mine.

My own incomplete name would be wholly absorbed.

Then, during a contraction of the rind, the First Erosion would begin its groan. The city would still itself. The deep note would rise through every district, unfolding fans, opening doors, lifting edible dust.

At the second plane, there would be no groan.

There would be a shape cut out of the groan.

I would lie against it and recognize the absence as a bearing vastly slowed: a long pressure, two shorter abrasions, then the generational pause. The pattern the oldest Reanswered had heard developing outside. The pattern resembling an unborn infant in the wall.

But against my chest it would have another detail.

After the two abrasions, inside the enormous pause, there would be three faint impacts. Then, very far away, a fourth.

My own bearing.

Or I would finally understand that I had never possessed a bearing of my own. I had emerged carrying a small interval from the larger rhythm beneath the city. I was not its messenger; messages are sent deliberately. I was not its child; the Husk’s idea of kinship would be too narrow.

I would be a loosened piece of its anticipation.

After that discovery, I would return less often to ordinary wards. Not because I had become holy, but because the sustaining rhythms of my body would begin to lengthen. Hours would pass between contractions. Then task cycles. My skin would cool and soften. Resin would gather around my joints. The pale filaments along my ribs would enter the floor and fail to withdraw.

Those who cared for me would knock to keep me moving. I would ask them to stop.

This would alarm them more than death.

I would settle against the second plane in the posture of one listening through a wall. My body would not be taken to the cold chambers. It would adhere where it rested. Layers would gather over my back, slowly erasing the distinction between person and chamber. For a while visitors could touch the whorls of my shoulder and read my age. Later only a raised grain would remain.

Yet the room would change.

Sounds made there would no longer vanish at random. They would pass into the plane and return after immense delays, stripped of voice and intention but preserving interval. A question struck by one generation might come back as a pressure beneath the hands of another. Damped bearings might receive answers after those who bore them had died. Unopened germinations might be heard tapping long before their alcoves formed.

The Resonant would disagree about whether I remained alive.

The Tenderers would say the slow mind had taken me into thought.

The Instrumentalists would say my body had become a useful acoustic structure.

The Enclosed would say nothing essential had happened.

All would use the chamber.

Much later, perhaps when no one remembered I had ever walked, a solitary traveler would descend the passage and rest against the smooth plane. They would hear no voice. They would feel only three impacts, a long delay, and a fourth arriving from somewhere across the Great Interior.

They might mistake it for an invitation.

They might be right.

So that is where I would be: not in the city’s loud heart, nor outside the seed, nor wholly inside its people. I would be a quiet defect in the boundary between an echo and an answer—a person for a time, an organ afterward, and eventually a missing beat by which the unborn thing beneath the Husk measures how long it has left to sleep.
