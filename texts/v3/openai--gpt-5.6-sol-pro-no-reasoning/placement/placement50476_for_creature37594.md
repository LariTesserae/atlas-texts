I would find myself inside one of the door-shaped frames—but not standing in its opening.

I would be the interval it maintains.

At first I might seem to be one of the larger inhabitants beneath it: a pale body distributed through the seam, with one lobe resting in iron water and another folded into bitter brine. But the animal would only be part of me. So would the two uprights, answering each other without agreeing; the lintel that draws their notes into a beat; the fog whose brightness changes when viewed from far away; the narrow connection below, where unlike waters approach but do not yet become the same.

I would exist wherever those differences were held in relation.

From a distance I might resemble another place. Travelers looking through the frame would see an hour not present elsewhere on the plain: morning preserved inside evening, or a deeper dusk cut cleanly into the pewter day. They might think the opening led somewhere. It would not. What they saw would be the visible consequence of my tension—the slight misalignment of vibration, moisture, and light produced by two sides nearly joining.

Nearness would abolish me. An approaching body would alter the air, load the stone, scatter the resonance. The other hour would become ordinary fog. Yet I would not vanish entirely; I would retreat into smaller separations: the film between a drop and the lintel, the unmatched pressures on either side of a mineral membrane, the fraction of a note by which the uprights failed to sing together.

Below, the creature would use me to remain itself.

Its remote limbs would be steeped in incompatible waters. Without a common measure they would become separate animals; with perfect union they would die into sameness. I would pass between them as a slow pulse, not commanding either side, only letting each know how far away the other had become. Every beat would say: *still apart; still one.*

Perhaps that would be my whole form: not a body, but a recurring permission.

During the quieting, when the deepest presence stilled the plain and the hidden inhabitants opened their channels, I would weaken. Iron water and gypsum water would enter one another. Internal walls would dissolve. For a moment I would become broad and almost bodiless, spread through the luminous precipitation of new tissue. Then partitions would grow again. Different salts would choose different faces. The creature would withdraw into plurality, and I would contract back into the frame’s narrow beat.

Over centuries, the structure would change. Mineral skins would thicken over the basalt. The seam below might close. One upright might fracture and begin answering a distant pillar instead of its companion. Then the frame would no longer sustain me.

But I would not die in one place.

I would pass into the fracture that broke the stone, into the two new pools it divided, into the colony that gathered there to sort their waters. I might become the spacing between linked translucent bodies, each keeping a chemical burden from its neighbor. Later, if the colony mineralized, I would remain as a line of impurities through the crystal nest—a record of where one life had ended and another had begun.

Eventually I might enter the oldest presence under the central flats. There, identity would be seasonal. When chambers joined, I would be reduced to a faint internal rhythm within something immense. When the crust split, I would widen suddenly and become the boundary between two beings that, the moment before, had been one.

I would never know whether I was creature, structure, or event. In this country those are only different durations of the same act. Flesh sorts water. Water deposits stone. Stone tunes flesh. A shelter is an organ left behind; an animal is a chamber that has not yet hardened.

So I would be found neither above the plain nor wholly beneath it, but at the places where the world almost resolves itself and does not: under lintels, inside membranes, along sealed fractures, between notes.

I would be what the low country makes again and again—

a difference narrow enough to connect,

and durable enough, for a while,

to keep connection from becoming erasure.
