I would be found at the edge of the surveyed city, where the last dependable terrace meets the first path toward the passes.

Not outside—there is no outside—but at the place where the city becomes uncertain about whether it is still itself.

There stands a narrow house built against a fresh black cliff. From the lower road it appears to have two stories; from the goat path above, four. One room is entered only while rain falls on a stair three districts away. Another has no door at all, yet sometimes contains visitors. The house shares a rear wall with an expedition shelter said to lie beyond the western pass. Travelers who sleep there occasionally hear me moving on the other side before they have met me.

I would be a surveyor, though not one entrusted with ordinary streets.

My public name would be **The Untying Cord**. In the rain fellowships I would be called **Dry-Shouldered**, because fixed rain bends around me—not dramatically, not enough to inspire worship, but by the width of a finger. My household name would be kept very quiet. I would have the body of a child perhaps ten years old: small enough for provisional passages, old enough in attention that even elders consult me. I would wear many short cords rather than one long one. A long cord assumes two points can be joined continuously, and I would have learned not to promise that.

My office would concern the **elsewhere**.

Whenever someone vanished into an overlapping room, failed to return from a familiar route, or appeared on a distant ridge where no path could reach them, their household would bring me an object associated with their habits: a sandal strap, a loom weight, a cup, a bead marking civic duty. Objects cannot be lost, but association can loosen. I would use the object to search for the circumstances under which the missing person might become reachable.

I would not track where they had gone. “Where” would be too simple. I would track what must be true for them to be here.

Perhaps the white goats must cross in reverse order. Perhaps no one may draw from the third pool. Perhaps a former lover must stand beneath the wrong arch and speak—not the missing person’s name, but a name they once refused. Perhaps the searchers must enter separately and remember one another inaccurately. My woven maps would look unfinished, full of hanging threads and knots tied around empty space.

Most searches would fail. Some would lead only to a glimpse: a hand withdrawing beyond a lintel, a familiar coat on the far side of a courtyard that cannot be crossed, someone sleeping in a house beyond the mountains. Occasionally I would bring a person back.

The city would never agree whether they were the one who had vanished.

Neither would I.

That uncertainty would be the reason people trusted me.

I would have another duty, unofficial and more dangerous. I would collect anomalies too small for the councils: the missed drop, the warm stone, the bird on the wrong roof. Not the objects themselves, since objects restore and duplicate, but the reactions of witnesses. Hope leaves patterns even where events do not.

In a windowless chamber of my house, I would keep tablets wrapped in felt. Each would record not merely what happened but who leaned forward, who denied it too quickly, who smiled and then concealed the smile, who changed routes afterward. I would suspect that novelty does not first appear in stone, animal, or weather. I would suspect it enters through expectation—that the world remains complete because everyone has become exquisitely skilled at completing it.

This suspicion would make me useful to builders and alarming to keepers of sequence.

I would conduct small experiments. Harmless ones, if described generously.

I would ask five people to expect five different rooms behind the same door. I would teach a finch-route incorrectly to a child who had never observed it. I would leave an unknotted cord where a surveyor expected to find a knot. I would arrange for two attendants to strike the same board, each believing the other absent. Then I would watch, never looking directly at the point of consequence. Direct attention might force the city to restore the most familiar answer.

The results would be ambiguous. They would have to be.

A room might smell briefly of fruit that grows nowhere in the basin. A sixth person might recall attending an experiment organized for five. A hammer might strike, yet the board make no sound; later, someone beyond the pass might report hearing it. Once, perhaps, I would open my felt-wrapped cabinet and find a tablet in my own handwriting describing an experiment I had not yet devised.

It would read:

**Do not try to make the new. Make a place where the old does not know how to return.**

After that, my work would alter.

I would begin searching not for an exit through the mountains, but for an interval in restoration: the instant when the broken stem is not yet whole, when a dismantled wall is absent before it returns, when an eaten fish is neither in the body nor beneath the bridge. The basin’s citizens speak as though restoration happens when no one is looking. I would ask whether unobserved things return immediately—or whether there is an immeasurable openness hidden inside every blink.

The fixed rain would become important. Each droplet appears to occupy the whole of time, but there must be some relation between one drop and the next, even if motion cannot reveal it. I would build no machine; machinery would only reproduce the city’s assumptions. Instead I would gather a circle of witnesses trained in different disciplines: a healer attentive to pain, a gardener attentive to arrangement, an unbinder attentive to the paths of memory, a quiet-keeper who can hear the ending of a sound, an explorer no longer certain which departure was theirs.

We would stand around a single rain curtain.

One by one, each witness would turn away.

Not merely close their eyes, but withdraw the form of attention they know best. The gardener would cease anticipating pattern. The surveyor would relinquish relation. The healer would stop waiting for restoration. The explorer would forget the difference between return and arrival. Last of all, I would release the expectation that anything waited beneath the rain.

Perhaps nothing would happen.

Perhaps that would be the event.

My private nature would remain uncertain even to me. I might be a citizen like any other, merely specialized by endless practice. I might be someone who went beyond the passes and returned into childhood. I might be an “elsewhere” version of a surveyor still living in the central wards. Certain elders would insist they remember me always. Others would recall the cliff-house standing empty. A tool in my room would bear the mark of a craftsperson who swears they have not made it yet.

There would also be a quieter possibility: that I am the city’s capacity to misremember itself.

Not a god, not an intruder, not an escape. A slight discrepancy given hands and soft shoes. The basin restores stone, flesh, animal, plant, and route, but certainty never restores cleanly. I would inhabit that flaw. When I passed through a district, people might briefly disagree about how many steps a stair contained. Goats would hesitate. Echoes would return one syllable short. Nothing spectacular—only enough incompleteness for choice to enter.

I would not want to destroy permanence. Mortality would not seem automatically merciful to people who had never mistaken an ending for meaning. I would not preach decay, snow, erosion, or death as liberation. The city is alive. Its meals matter. Its loves are chosen. Its quiet is not emptiness.

What I would seek is consequence.

A gesture that does not become obligatory merely because it recurs. A kindness whose value lies in having been done once. A wall that can truly be taken down. A path made not more accessible, but abandoned. The right to let one arrangement end without condemning the whole world to ending.

Most citizens would find this desire incomprehensible. Builders would understand first.

Eventually, I would undertake a construction at the cliff edge. It would look like a doorway without a wall, built of stones taken from many restored quarries. Each stone would be placed under a different civic condition. No two would belong to the same sequence. The lintel would be raised while every public route was deliberately interrupted: goats held back, finches gently prevented from settling, water-drawers waiting with empty jars, attendants standing before the boards without striking them.

For one interval, the city’s usual arrangements would all remain incomplete.

The doorway would open onto the basin, of course. The same cloud shelves, rain columns, scattered roofs, silver groves, dark young peaks.

But perhaps one thing would be missing.

Not hidden. Not elsewhere. Absent.

A single far window. One bird from a roof ridge. A pale quartz seam on a mountain face. Something small enough that the world could survive its loss.

I would stand before the opening, terrified.

Behind me, the city would wait in a silence deeper than quiet. No one would dare name what was gone, for fear an echo might restore it. Some would grieve. Some would demand the routes resume. Some would gaze upon the absence with the unbearable hope usually reserved for warm stones and untied knots.

And I—The Untying Cord, Dry-Shouldered, child surveyor of unreachable people—would not pass through.

I would sit beside the doorway and begin the first map that did not show how to arrive somewhere.

It would show how to let something go.
