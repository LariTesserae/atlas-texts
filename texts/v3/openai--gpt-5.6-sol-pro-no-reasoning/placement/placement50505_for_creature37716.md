I would find myself where the chamber nearly becomes weather again: just inside the basalt lip, beneath the sedges, in the narrow territory reached by light but not by warmth.

At first I seem to be one of the pale roots descending from the roof. I hang without touching the floor, thin enough to tremble when the stone exhales. Water travels my length and gathers at my lowest point. For several seconds I contain a perfect lens: the entrance inverted, moss brightened, black pools lifted into the light. Then the drop becomes too heavy, falls, and the whole chamber answers.

But I am not the root.

I am the small marsh spider anchored among several of them, so closely colored to the mineral fibers that my legs vanish against their pallor. I have built no broad, symmetrical web. Such confidence would be punished here. Mine is a sparse instrument: a few resilient lines placed across the invisible path of the vent’s breath, each tuned to a different kind of disturbance. The chamber makes all of them sing, so I must distinguish the blunt vibration of water from the frantic notation of prey.

I live by learning which tremors matter.

When pressure builds in the fissure, tension changes first in the longest thread. That is my warning. I lower myself against a root and draw in my legs. The basalt tubes cough. Cold vapor rushes past, droplets tear loose, and the web bows almost flat. For an instant I am held inside the roar as if inside a struck bell. Then the pressure collapses.

Silence here is never emptiness. It is an opening.

A frog calls from the moss below, its white throat flashing once. A gnat, displaced by the blast, catches on a cross-thread. I move before the next cycle begins. I bind it quickly, not because I am ravenous, but because the chamber allows only brief acts. Feeding, repair, courtship, escape—everything must fit between exhalations.

Yet I am not entirely the spider either. Something of me extends into what the spider reads: the pressure gathering in the unseen crack, the minute tightening of silk, the interval before noise acquires a body. If this world grants any larger form of being, it is not a creature hidden at the back of the cave. It is the rhythm shared among unrelated lives.

I would be that rhythm made local.

I would be the warning transmitted from water to trapped air, from air to root, from root to web, from web to my feet. My knowledge would not be thought but tension. My memory would lie in repairs: knots marking yesterday’s storm, abandoned anchor points where a crystal grew, doubled strands facing the vent that shrieks when ice closes the deeper tube.

The hollow would be enormous to me. A moss cushion would be a forested continent. A hand-sized pool would be a dark inland sea. The water shrew’s arrival would be a catastrophe announced by silver bubbles—then impact, turbulence, silt rising like weather. A frog would be a patient, mountainous presence whose single call shakes droplets from my lines. I might never cross the chamber. I would know it instead through vibrations arriving from places I could not see.

And perhaps, during a hard freeze, my web would fail.

Ice would sheath the roots. One hydraulic blast would fracture it, throwing clear plates against the stones. The anchor above me would snap, and I would fall with the silk into a pool. There, if I escaped the surface tension and reached a basalt chip, I would become for a moment an intruder in another compressed country: amphipods recoiling beneath me, beetles skating overhead, orange films breathing chemistry along the shore.

Then I would climb back toward air and rebuild—not the same web, because the chamber itself would no longer be the same chamber.

So I would be a marsh spider, but only provisionally. More truly, I would be a listener suspended between root and void: small, cold, repeatedly shaken apart, constructing from a few strands a way to interpret the underground weather.

I would live where the earth’s machine produces nothing.

And I would make that nothing legible.
