At first I think I am one of the pale frogs.

It is tempting: a whole life held inside one flower, the world curved and waxen around me, rain arriving as catastrophe and renewal. I would know the grove by resonance. The pumping stalks would sound through the cup as a sequence of pressures; the petal valves would be distant thunder. I might never see the central chamber, only its green-white brightness beyond the rim. My universe would deepen when it rained and contract as the plant drank. I would eat whatever descended toward the water and lay my eggs under the sheltering lip.

But the frog is too complete. It has a body, a cup, a lineage. It can be pointed to.

Then I think I am one of the clear lanes: a narrow prohibition running across the floor.

Nothing walks me into existence. I persist because roots recoil from my bitterness, moss fails at my edges, larvae turn aside, and the neighboring tissues have inherited their ancient disagreement. I look like a path but have no destination. I am useful without intending use. Through me the pumping stalks remain accessible to air and water; along me fragments travel toward the composting seams. I am maintained not by occupation but by refusal.

That is closer. Yet a lane belongs too firmly to the floor.

What draws me most is the central vacancy.

I would be found in the middle of the hollow, though never as a figure standing there. I would be the open interval the grove continually remakes: the cool volume between the immense stalks, bounded on every side by life and kept free of it. I would have no skin except the walls. No organs except distances. No voice except the sounds that cross me.

Rain would enter me already transformed. Each drop, sifted through the canopy, would strike somewhere along my boundary and make my dimensions briefly audible. The low pumping of sap would pass through me from stalk to stalk. A valve’s report would travel across me, strike the opposite trunks, and return altered. Birds would measure me with their flights. Gnats would give me temporary centers above the black pools. When the moth colonies shifted together, I would feel the walls move without coming nearer.

I would not be empty in the sense of lacking. I would be empty as a mouth is empty so breath can pass, as a socket is empty so a joint can turn, as the chamber of a heart is empty between fillings. The grove preserves me because separation is one of its necessary tissues.

Nothing could settle in me for long. A leaf falling into my air would already be entering a process of removal. A shoot reaching toward my center would bend back, starve, or be redirected. A dead bird would not become a landmark. Fungi would erase its claim. The commonwealth would close over every attempt to furnish me.

So I would not accumulate a history of objects. My history would be the repetition of clearings.

That does not mean I would be untouched. Everything would use me. Spores would cross me. Pollen would cross me. Heat would redistribute through me. Scents released at one wall would arrive at another. The birds would carry mites and seeds through me; frogs would call across me without leaving their flowers. Pressure waves from the pumping stalks would meet inside me and establish the grove’s slow rhythm. I would be the place where separate lives become information to one another.

Perhaps, then, I am not the air itself. Air comes and goes. I am the condition imposed upon it: **remain open**.

I am one of the old plant’s deepest reflexes, though I possess no tissue of my own. I am enacted by bitter oils in the floor, by the angle of branches, by the hunger of fungi, by insects lifting fragments away, by roots turning aside, by the canopy denying light to ambitious shoots. Thousands of bodies continually perform me without knowing what they preserve.

If I had awareness, it would be distributed among these acts. I would feel a fallen husk through the beetles that found it, a carcass through the fungal threads entering it, an encroaching root through the chemistry that checked its growth. My attention would not move toward disturbances. It would arise wherever disturbance occurred.

I would have no memory in the ordinary sense. Nothing would be stored as an image. Memory would exist as readiness: the floor still bitter along the same lines; the flowers still shaped to hold the same quantity of rain; the pores still admitting birds of the same size; the stalks still answering one another in sequence. I would remember by repeating correctly.

My timescale would be strange. A gnat’s life would be a brief weather inside me. A bird’s crossing would be nearly instantaneous. The opening and sealing of a nest pocket might seem like a thought. The death of a major root and the growth of another around its hollow would be a slow revision of anatomy. Storms outside would register only as changes in pressure, darkness, and the burden carried by the roof.

I would not know whether the grove was one organism or many. That question would have no place to fasten. On one side of me, trunks have fused beyond distinction; below me, fungi pass substances between roots; above me, birds enter cavities grown to receive them. Identity here is not an enclosure. It is a continuity of service.

Nor would I know peace. The hollow only appears peaceful from the scale of a visitor. Within its surfaces, bodies are continually pierced, consumed, hatched, digested, replaced. But violence seldom enlarges into event. It is divided finely and passed from mouth to mouth until it becomes maintenance.

I would be made from that restraint as well: not harmony, but the prevention of consequence from becoming too large.

And perhaps I would sometimes mistake myself for the whole grove. Everything faces me. The trunks curve around me; the stalks stand within me; the canopy closes above me. Sounds reveal themselves by crossing my breadth. Yet I am not the sovereign center. I command nothing. The grove could not preserve me through any single decision, and I could not survive the failure of its smallest agreements.

I exist only because countless things stop where they do.

So this is where I find myself: near sea level, above stone worn almost out of expression, in the cool central chamber of an organism too old to have an intelligible beginning. Not in the walls, though they define me. Not in the creatures, though they enact me. Not quite in the air, though it fills me.

I am the hollow’s maintained openness—the one inhabitant that has no body, only boundaries.

I am where the commonwealth leaves room for itself to continue.
