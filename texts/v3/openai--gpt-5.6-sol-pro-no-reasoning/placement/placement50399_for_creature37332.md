I would find myself near the reef’s lowest roots, where the black mineral narrows into hooked needles and the water begins forgetting that it is water.

Not at the boundary itself. Nothing lives exactly at that boundary, because there is no exactly. But in the wavering region where a body may be submerged at one end and wrapped in cold mist at the other, where each contraction changes not only one’s depth but the meaning of depth.

At first I imagine myself as something that watches through that thinning: a pale, soft creature fastened beneath an overhang, looking out toward the mountains below. But sight is too simple here, and solitude feels imported from another world. Anything that spent its life gazing outward would either be eaten or become part of the apparatus that keeps the sea from opening. The view cannot be my purpose. It can only be an accident that sometimes happens to me.

So I am not an observer.

I am a small colonial animal stretched among several of the reef’s terminal spurs. My colony resembles a loose net of clear cords, almost invisible except when lightning enters the water. Each cord is no thicker than a tendon. At the junctions hang dark, spindle-shaped bodies, each an individual, though none could live if cut away. Mineral grains lie articulated through our tissues. When the reef vibrates, the grains knock faintly against one another.

We would be called the **returns**.

A return colony inhabits places where the foundational drone becomes uncertain: fracture edges, new mineral growth, abandoned mill sockets, and especially the lower roots where sound can leak into open air. We do not make the great sustaining tones. We are too slight. Instead, we receive them after they have traveled through the reef and send them back altered.

One end of us clings to black glass. Another spreads into the thinning water. Between those ends, vibration becomes movement, and movement becomes charge. The outer bodies swell with each low pulse, holding a skin of water around themselves even when the boundary contracts. Then they release. The contraction runs inward through the colony, a slow tightening from mist to stone. At the reef, the innermost bodies strike their mineral grains against the glass and return a small, hard echo.

That echo is almost nothing beside the mills’ thunder. Yet nearby animals use it. Young mills rolling through the currents turn toward its regularity. Slivers align themselves with it when fog hides the passages. Veils fasten to our outer cords and extend the reach of their films. Even the pall hesitates around a healthy colony because every node keeps answering for every other.

We feed on what the sea nearly loses.

Filings collect along our charged cords. Powder from the mills drifts downward in pale clouds and sticks to the mucus around our junctions. Tiny wounded animals, carried toward the margin, become entangled. We do not close around them like predators. We hold them until the pall arrives. The pall strips them into salts, and our outer bodies absorb a portion before the rest is drawn inward by the next contraction.

Thus our nourishment comes from departures that fail.

There is no central self among us. Most of the time I would be only one spindle in the net, tightening when my neighbors tighten, tasting iron and shell, pressed by the mill-song through the mineral. But individuality would not be absent. It would come and go.

When the sea contracts during the iron-blue dimness, the lowest portion of the colony may emerge into open sky. The water sheath around those outer bodies thins until their signals can no longer cross it. For several moments each becomes isolated.

That is when I become most nearly *I*.

Cold air lies against one side of my membrane, colder water against the other. Far below are cloud fields, perhaps lightning moving silently within them, perhaps the dull teeth of mountains. I have no eye capable of assembling these into a landscape. I know the world below as an immense absence of pressure, a direction into which vibrations do not return.

The reef sings behind me.

Beyond me, nothing answers.

Isolation is not peace. It is physiological terror. My mineral chain pulls straight. My feeding membrane seals. Every contraction tries to pass inward, toward the colony, toward water, toward noise. When the sea expands again, I am submerged. The neighboring bodies touch my pulse through the cords, and my brief selfhood disperses into them.

What I encountered outside remains only as a flaw in our rhythm: a fractional delay carried inward from node to node. The colony answers it. The reef receives it. Somewhere above, a mill adjusts the resistance of its spokes.

Perhaps that adjustment travels onward through other mills until a chamber changes cadence. Perhaps the sea’s whole sustaining thunder contains, for a few rotations, the shape of the emptiness I felt below.

During storms we become more important and die in great numbers.

Compression forces the water hard around the reef, and its youngest spurs bend by distances too small for most animals to notice. We notice. Our cords draw taut across the bending branches. Some tear. Some are sliced against edges that erosion has not yet softened. Lightning orders the metallic grains in our bodies, and for an instant every surviving colony becomes a bright diagram of strain: branching lines around the reef’s lower darkness.

If a mineral root breaks, we cannot hold it. No creature our size could. But we remain attached to both sides as the fracture opens. For a little while our cords span the wound.

Through those cords pass vibration, charge, alarm, and the chemical identity of the parted stone. Shutters begin settling at the edges. Veils spread over the water’s new curvature. The pall consumes our torn bodies, releasing the metals we gathered. Mineral skins precipitate along the remaining strands. In time, the strands become buried in clear salt and black glass.

The reef heals through the shape of our death.

A return colony that survives such a break may remain divided across the scar. Its two halves cannot exchange food directly, but every low note transmitted through the repaired mineral reaches both. They contract in answer to the same sound. Whether they are still one creature would be impossible to decide.

That uncertainty is where I would live.

I would not possess knowledge of the suspended ocean as an ocean. I would not know altitude, weather, gravity, or the curved blue planet. I would know only recurrence: pressure and release, thunder and answer, immersion and the terrible interval of air.

Yet if there were something in me analogous to belief, it would be this:

That the world persists because what leaves is answered.

A mill’s tooth enters the current and comes around again. A shutter closes, and another opens farther down the fissure. The dead dissolve and return as mineral, flesh, and charge. A pulse travels into the reef and comes back changed. Even the sea’s wounds draw mist into themselves until they are once more water.

Only the downward vastness gives no answer.

I would touch it each dim phase and recoil. I would carry its silence inward without understanding it. And because of that silence, my small answering knock against the reef would be urgent beyond proportion—a minute, tireless insistence beneath the factory thunder:

Here is still water.

Here is still stone.

Here we still are.
