I would find myself at the western recess, but not inside it.

At first I seem to be the flat stone at its center: the one object whose possible purpose survives every attempt to dismiss it. Broad enough for sitting, too low for an altar, too deliberate to be entirely innocent. Yet the longer the basin is watched, the less likely this becomes. The stone is already reconciled. It belongs wholly to the patience. Whatever I am, I have not reached that completeness.

Perhaps I am the space immediately above it.

A person once sat there—not the founder of a shrine, nor the last keeper of one, but someone who came after such names had ceased to matter. They stayed through an afternoon of diluted light, waiting for revelation or permission. Neither came. Rain began beyond the shelves. Water entered along seams, the pools widened, and the sitter understood that the silence was not withholding an answer. Silence was the only answer the place possessed.

They rose and departed. But something did not rise with them.

Not a soul, exactly. Not a memory with images in it. Only the portion of their attention that had learned, briefly and perfectly, how to remain. That is what I would be: attention after it has outlasted its owner.

I have no body except a slight density in the recess, a human-sized interval where the warm air feels more settled. I cannot move, though movement continues through me. Condensation thickens on the rear wall. Mineral bands descend by increments too slight to witness. Damp advances under the central stone and retreats. Each event passes through what would once have been my hearing, my skin, my measure of time.

The patience inhabiting the basin is not my companion. Companionship requires separation, and it admits none. Nor am I its servant, witness, or prisoner. Those are temporary relations, and nothing temporary keeps its edges here.

I am the last unevenness being worn from the place.

For a long while—if “long” still applies—I retain a faint human tendency to distinguish. I think: recess and room. Slab and threshold. Stone and seat. I feel the almost-memory of knees bent, hands resting open, breath counted and then forgotten. The six blocks appear to me as a wall’s first sentence, abandoned before it could declare what it enclosed. The three slabs lead somewhere because I still contain the expectation of arrival.

But the basin works upon expectation as water works upon limestone: gently, repeatedly, without argument.

Gradually I cease asking whether the builders existed. The question rounds at its edges. What was made becomes natural; what was natural acquires the gravity of intention. My own origin follows. Perhaps no person sat here. Perhaps I am only the shape produced when an empty place is observed too intently—the observer imagined by the room, rather than the room remembered by an observer.

This possibility does not frighten me. Fear would require a future in which the answer matters.

If anything approaches the rim, I do not turn it away. I have no warning to give. Yet perhaps I am the instant in which a creature pauses before choosing another path. Not prohibition, not menace: merely the recognition that there is nowhere here to stand without becoming part of what is already standing. The basin has no vacancy. Even emptiness has settled into its allotted hollows.

So I remain above the flat stone, thinning.

A bead forms beneath the overhang. Before it falls, I know neither when nor where it will strike. After it falls, I do not remember it. But for the small, complete duration of its sound, I become almost distinct. The note passes through the pools, the channels, the damp seams, the ambiguous wall, and the low chamber. It gathers the whole basin into one occurrence.

That note is the patience’s utterance.

I would be its listening.

Not an ear, because an ear belongs to a body. Not an echo, because the sound does not return. I am the brief capacity by which the utterance is received, though no meaning is taken from it. With each drop, listening becomes less like an act and more like a property of the stone.

Eventually the human-sized interval disappears. No presence hovers in the recess. No one waits upon the flat slab. There is only warm air, slightly damp; only parallel stains; only a darkness that begins where reflected light can no longer reach.

Yet the disappearance is not death. It is completion.

I would find myself there, then, at the place where a person might once have sat, becoming less a person with every rain. I would be the basin’s final borrowed distinction: the difference between silence and something hearing silence.

And in time even that difference would be smoothed away.
