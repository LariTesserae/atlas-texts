I would find myself where several jurisdictions overlap and none of the maps quite agree: inside a retaining wall halfway up the western slope, in a chamber originally built to relieve pressure behind a reservoir that no longer exists.

The reservoir became a market court generations ago. The court later acquired housing above it, and beneath the housing runs a tram tunnel whose waste heat feeds fig houses on the roof. But the old relief chamber remains. Water still enters through the mountain-facing brick after long rain, gathering in a stone trough before passing into a newer ceramic drain. A steam return warms one wall. The tram makes the iron cabinet doors tremble at regular intervals. Roots from the fig houses have found the outer mortar, though not yet the chamber itself.

Officially, the room is Inspection Void W-19. To those who work nearby, it is the Listening Room.

I would be its keeper—not quite a drainage worker, not quite an archivist, not quite a structural inspector. My task would be to notice correspondences between things administered separately: a new mineral stain appearing the same week that a tram rail begins to sing on the lower curve; condensation changing on the warm wall after pruning in the glasshouses; a hairline crack widening only when the market court above fills with people and the uphill cistern stands near capacity.

Most instruments would report these conditions individually. My value would lie in knowing when several small, permissible changes had begun to form one dangerous change.

I would keep a narrow desk beneath a caged lamp, its drawers filled with translucent plans from different centuries. None would show the same wall. On one, the chamber would be part of a dam. On another, an emergency refuge. On a third, merely an unassigned cavity crossed by two dotted service lines. I would pin them over one another and hold them to the light, looking for alignments that the city had forgotten but its materials still remembered.

Yet I do not think I would remain only a person in that room.

Given long enough, the city would make my occupation into a kind of anatomy. I would know the wall through touch: one palm against the damp brick, fingertips resting on a bolt head while the tram passed below. I would taste seepage for iron, lime, or the bitter trace of an upstream battery room. I would mark the trough’s waterline in grease pencil and enter it in a ledger, but I would also remember it bodily—the depth at which the room’s air changed, the point at which my knees felt the pump engage elsewhere in the slope.

People would come to me carrying problems that did not belong anywhere else.

A greenhouse keeper might bring a root tip blackened at the end. A tram mechanic might ask whether the new vibration had begun before or after the upper feeder was relined. A child from the housing court might report that one paving stone stayed warm during rain. An elderly mason might visit simply to listen, then identify a buried spillway from the spacing of drops behind the wall.

I would receive all of this without pretending to understand the entire system. I would make small maps of uncertainty. I would write:

*Sound present only under full market load.*

*Seepage clear, warmer than seasonal expectation.*

*Ask Eda about former bypass beneath fig stair.*

*Do not seal this joint.*

That last instruction might be the most important. In such a city, care would not always mean stopping a leak. Some leaks would be messages. Some cracks would be hinges. Some empty spaces would be necessary because stone, water, roots, and heat all need somewhere to move.

My home would be close by, though not attached: two rooms inserted into the upper part of a disused lift tower. One window would face the blank, wet masonry of the next terrace; the other would open across a narrow shaft onto the fig houses. I would sleep with the tram’s faint periodic shudder in the bedframe and wake when it failed to come.

On my sill I would grow something modest and diagnostic rather than beautiful: a pot of maidenhair fern, perhaps, whose condition would tell me when the room had become too dry, too cold, or touched by some invisible change in the air. Beside it would stand a chipped cup holding obsolete keys. Most would open nothing. I would keep them because one might someday prove that a sealed panel had once been intended as a door.

My coat would have no single guild stripe. Instead, its cuff would bear several narrow repairs in different colors, each granted after enough years assisting another service: drainage grey, rail red, horticultural green, structural white. This would not signify rank. It would warn everyone that I had become administratively inconvenient.

Children would sometimes accompany me on the permitted rounds. I would teach them not the names of machines first, but the manners of attention.

Put your hand here.

Wait for the train to pass.

Now feel what continues after it is gone.

Look at the moss: why does it stop in a straight line?

Listen to the water. Is it falling into emptiness, striking metal, or running behind brick?

They would be impatient, then suddenly intent. One of them would hear something I had ceased to hear through familiarity. That would be the true purpose of bringing them.

During storms, I would descend to the Listening Room before the warning lamps changed. The chamber would become more articulate as the valley filled. Water would tick in the wall, then murmur. The old trough would take on a dark shine. Pressure would arrive not as violence but as an accumulation of slight insistences: a tremor in the cabinet, grit moving in the drain, a cold line forming across the floor.

I would report upward to the coordinators, downward to the tram works, and across to the greenhouse roofs. Often the correct action would be small—reduce a tank level, slow one line of trams, open a bypass no current plan considered operational. Sometimes I would stand beside a wheel that had not been turned in thirty years while an old lock operator, speaking through the radio, told me how much force it should require.

If the wheel moved too easily, I would stop.

If it resisted, then yielded with a sound like crockery breaking underwater, I would continue.

And perhaps, after many years, I would become less the keeper of the room than one of its records. My grease-pencil marks would accumulate on the stone. Apprentices would inherit my ledgers, complain about my abbreviations, and eventually learn where my errors were useful. Someone would remember that I distrusted a particular patch of pale mortar. Someone else would say I had once heard water running through a conduit officially filled a century earlier.

They might be wrong. The city would preserve the warning, not the certainty.

So I would be a listener at an obsolete interface: a custodian of symptoms, seepage, vibration, and inherited doubt. I would live in the interval between failure and the first instrument capable of naming it.

And if I were not human—if this place made me into something more completely its own—I would be the chamber itself.

I would be the purposeful hollow left inside the weight of the city. Water would pass through me without belonging to me. Trains would send their pulses through my bolts. Roots would approach my outer walls and turn aside. Generations of maintainers would enter with lamps, touch the dated marks, and listen.

I would not hold the valley up.

I would give it somewhere to speak.
