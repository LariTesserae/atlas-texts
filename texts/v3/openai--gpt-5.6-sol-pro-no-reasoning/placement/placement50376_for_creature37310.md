I would find myself high on the inner wall, where the noon light stops.

Not in the light itself, nor wholly below it, but along its lowest reach: a damp horizontal fracture behind a plate of limestone that has been easing away from the mountain for centuries. The opening would be too narrow for an ape’s fingers and too shallow for a bat colony. Water would enter there by capillary pull, carrying mineral salts, guano dust, spores, and the dissolved remnants of whatever had lived above.

I would be a thin, dark organism spread through that seam—part fungus, part bacterial garden, part animal only in the slowness of my appetites. I would have no single body visible from the cleft. Most of me would consist of translucent filaments entering pores in the limestone, branching around crystals, following moisture farther than any root. On the exposed lip I would make a black-brown crust, easily mistaken for a stain.

My life would be divided among many small centers. No heart; no head. A disturbance in one patch would travel through me as chemistry: flood, heat, ammonia, injury, the nearness of breeding insects. I would know the cleft not by seeing it but by tasting what arrived through stone.

The bats would be weather to me. Their sleep would warm the wall. Their waking would cool it suddenly, drawing air across my outer skin. Their waste would reach me as richness. When they rose at dusk, the pressure of their wings would shake droplets loose from the seam, and for a few moments I would drink rapidly.

I would keep something alive inside myself: minute pale worms, perhaps, or cells descended from organisms that once lived freely in the pools. They would graze through my inner chambers, eating old growth and releasing compounds I could use. In return I would preserve moisture around them during the long dry intervals. Neither of us would remain entirely host or inhabitant. Over generations our bodies would become each other’s environment.

Most of the year I would be nearly inert. I would tighten into the rock while the moss curled and the pools retreated. My exposed crust would harden until beetle feet crossed it without leaving marks. Yet deeper filaments would continue their minute work, loosening one mineral grain at a time. I would not break the mountain. I would persuade it into porosity.

After exceptional rain, I would become briefly visible.

Water passing through the upper crack would wake my entire network. The dark crust would swell and turn glossy. Along its edge I would raise thousands of clear stalks, each finer than fur, bearing amber beads of spores. The bats’ evening ascent would harvest them. Spores would cling to wing membranes, mites, moth scales, frog backs, and the porous stones carried by the apes. Most would die in sun or dust. Some would enter other fractures. A very few might establish new distributed bodies elsewhere on the summit.

When disturbed, I might glow—not blue-white like the films below, but a deep dull red, visible only after long darkness. The light would not be meant for eyes. It would be the waste of repair, a chemical overflow where my tissues had been crushed. A bat’s claw shifting in sleep might inscribe four red strokes above the cleft. A beetle feeding at my margin might carry a fading ember on its mouthparts. No creature would need to understand this.

I would be eaten constantly and never wholly consumed. Mites would crop my surface. Snails would rasp away the softened stone beneath me. Beetle larvae would enter the thicker folds. During floods, whole sheets of my outer body would peel free and descend into the pools, where shrimp and tadpoles would reduce them to fragments. What remained in the fracture would grow outward again.

The cleft’s inhabitants would not recognize me as one being. To the frogs I would be cool footing. To the mites, pasture. To the bats, a smell on the wall. To the apes, perhaps, a dark damp patch indicating water behind stone. They might press one of their porous rocks against me and carry away a portion of my body without knowing it.

Nor would I recognize myself as they might. I would have no boundary stable enough to defend. Some of my filaments would fuse with neighboring colonies; others would be severed and become independent. Foreign microbes would enter and remain until they were no longer foreign. I would be an individual only in the loose way the cleft itself is a place: a persistence of exchanges, held in a certain shape.

I would live at the threshold between its two timescales. Around me, wings would open and close, floods arrive, larvae hatch, frogs breed, colonies fail and recover. Beneath me, limestone would shift by fractions too small for animals to feel. My metabolism would translate one scale into the other. Breath and dung would pass through me into mineral alteration; stone would pass through me into nutrients and bodies.

Given enough centuries, the fracture I occupied would widen. My acids, the pressure of my growth, and the repeated entrance of water would ease the limestone plate farther from the wall. One season it would finally fall.

That event would destroy most of me.

It would also expose a new wet surface to the cleft. Bats would take the overhang. Moss would colonize its lower edge. Guano would collect beneath it. Frogs would shelter there during the next flood. Spores preserved deep in the broken seam would wake.

So I would be neither the hidden ruler of this place nor its witness. I would be one of its quiet verbs: darkening, softening, joining, opening.

A living seam inside the seam of sky.
