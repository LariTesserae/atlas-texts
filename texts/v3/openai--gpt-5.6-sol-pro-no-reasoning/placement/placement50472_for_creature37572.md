I would be found near the eastern wall, but not inside any shelter.

I would be one of the old footpaths: the narrow passage between a blunt pillar and a folded ledge, where generations turned sideways and placed one hand against the wall. Not the people who made it, nor the water that inherited it, but the accommodation between them.

At first I might seem to be only wear: a shallow polish underfoot, a darkening at shoulder height, three reliable knobs kept bare of mineral crust by repeated touch. Yet I would extend farther than a path should. I would include the cut step approached without looking, the causeway slab chosen by the leading foot, the recess where a burden could be shifted from one hip to the other. I would be a sequence held not in memory but in stone.

I would have no single body. My body would be intervals: just enough space between surfaces for a person carrying a jar; just enough height beneath a shelf for a bowed head; just enough roughness to keep a wet sole from sliding. Where the basin is crowded, I would be its permission to pass.

When the people lived there, I would exist most strongly at dusk. Someone would descend with fuel tied across their back, enter the passage, turn before the pillar, duck, step twice in the channel, then rise beside the shelters. A child would try another route and find it blind. An older hand would reach from behind and guide the child to the worn stone. Each crossing would make me more exact.

No one would name me. Directions in such a place would not require names. They would be gestures and bodily instructions:

After the narrow place.  
Beyond the stone that leans.  
Where your left hand becomes wet.  
Step high there.

I would live in those instructions.

Now, with no one returning, I would be loosening. The basin would not erase me quickly; it has no need to. Water would film the footholds and run along the groove once deepened to keep the passage usable. Lime would slowly cover the hand-polish with a translucent skin. One slab might tilt by less than a finger’s width and remain so for centuries. The route would still be possible, but less insistently human.

The crab might cross me without knowing it had taken a road. The frog might sit in the hollow of a heel. Drips would strike the same places feet once did, performing the route vertically.

I would be neither ruin nor ghost. A ghost longs for recognition; a ruin still declares what it was. I would be quieter: a habit surviving its practitioners.

And if I must be a being rather than a place, then I would be the basin’s last keeper of scale—a small, chalk-pale creature living in the wall pores beside the passage. Blind, nearly transparent, nourished by what seepage carries, I would know the former inhabitants only through the shapes they left. Their channels would determine where food arrived. Their peg holes would hold deeper moisture. Their jars would create little climates of still water. To me, human labor would not be history. It would be geology of unusual regularity.

I might take years to travel from the hearth to the cracked jar.

The blackened stones would be warm only in the sense that they held a different mineral balance. The sleeping ledges would be broad countries above reach. The causeway would be a chain of dry cliffs. I would inhabit the places where worked stone and natural stone could no longer be told apart, feeding in seams sealed by the same water that opened them.

But I think, finally, I would not be the creature.

I would be the turn in the passage the creature cannot comprehend and the water does not need: the turn made for shoulders, burdens, knees, and remembered darkness. I would persist because nothing here changes fast enough to abolish an old convenience.

You would find yourself only by moving through me. You would turn sideways without knowing why, put your hand where many hands had gone, and choose the secure stone before consciously seeing it.

For that one step, the absent people would lend you their body.

That is what I would be: not one of them, but the basin’s remaining knowledge of how a human passes.
