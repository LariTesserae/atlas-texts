I would be found where the town nearly closes around itself: in a narrow recess behind the oldest workshop, among the obsolete rails and housings left standing to draw water from the air.

At first I seem to be one of the night workers.

I am narrow even by the town’s measure, with a shoulder permanently lowered from passing beneath a pipe that was removed years ago. My clothes are patched at the knees and whitened with salts. Around my neck hangs a retired brass plate whose stamped fractions no longer correspond to any surviving column. I carry no hammer. My tools are chalk, cord, a ceramic cup, two wedges, and a short listening rod that I place against brick, steel, or the floor.

People call me a route-minder, though I do not memorize the town as it is. I memorize the town it is becoming.

I know which corridor grows warm first after sunset, which stair has begun transmitting the southern brace’s groan, which family’s sleeping shelf will need to be shortened before the next adjustment cycle. I redraw escape paths in erasable chalk. My lines sometimes cross cupboards, beds, or shrines. This does not make me welcome, but it makes me necessary.

Yet that is only what I appear to be.

I was born below, they say. There is a fired tablet bearing my name mortared into a pier near the cistern, but the dimensions impressed beneath it describe no room anyone can find. Some believe the tablet belonged to an older relative. Some say it was made in error. No one removes it; the pier has taken too much load.

I do not remember parents. I remember instead a first sensation: cooling metal ticking in darkness, then the faint arrival of moisture on iron.

The thicket behind the workshop is my true dwelling. Defunct machine frames stand there so close that I must turn my head sideways to pass. At dawn every bar beads with water. I empty the lowest trays before others wake, but never all of them. One shallow basin is left beneath a corroded axle where the condensation gathers unusually clear. No one appointed me to tend it. No household claims it. Nevertheless, no one drinks from it without asking.

Beside that basin grows something the town does not count among its plants.

For years it resembles only a mineral stain: a branching whiteness along the axle’s underside. Then it thickens into translucent threads, neither root nor corrosion. It does not spread toward water. It spreads toward stress. Hair-fine filaments enter bolt holes and old fractures, crossing from metal into concrete. When a load changes, the threads cloud. Before a crack opens, they draw taut.

I keep them hidden behind hanging cloth and useless parts. Not because the growth is dangerous, but because anything alive here is immediately burdened with hope.

It may not be alive at all.

Sometimes I think I am its keeper. Sometimes I think it has grown me as its movable part.

This uncertainty explains certain things. Why my fingertips flatten without callus. Why I wake before the lamps dim. Why, when I place my palm against a wall, I do not hear a sound so much as feel a choice narrowing inside it. The cavity is full of possible failures. Most perish before occurring. A few gather weight. I feel these as tensions running through the pale threads, through the iron, and finally through my own bones.

The nightly heat does not pass through the town like a traveler. It searches.

It enters one fracture, meets buried reinforcement, divides, stalls at damp brick, leaps through a pipe, fades into slag. Lamps dim because current finds resistance; valves tighten because seals expand; plants fold because their tissues feel the first thermal change. All of that is true.

It is also true that the passage remembers where it has been.

I follow only after it has gone. Never before it, never beside it. I touch each warmed surface with the listening rod and mark a small open circle in chalk. By morning there may be a chain of circles from the eastern reeds to the old workshop, or from the kiln wall to a child’s bed. Most are rubbed away before noon. A few I turn into instructions: EMPTY THIS NICHE. SHIFT LOAD WEST. KEEP CLEAR. WATCH AT LOW CYCLE.

The council assumes these judgments come from long practice. It is kinder to let them.

My closest companion is a hydraulic brace designated S-14, though I call it Still-Speaking. It stands in a passage too narrow for two people abreast. Its piston has extended less than the width of a fingernail in six years. Children consider it inactive. I know it is holding an argument between two masses of earth that have not yet decided whether to meet.

Each night I clean the brass reference marks on its sleeve. I rest my forehead against the cool cylinder and wait.

From it I have learned that support is not strength. Support is the exact transmission of pressure into something willing to bear it.

That may be what I am.

When rain falls above, I take no honored place near the cistern. I work in the sediment channels, kneeling in ocher water and clearing stones from the grates. During the brief rush, the hidden white threads behind the workshop become transparent. For several minutes I lose all sense of the town’s strains. The water fills every vibration with brightness. It is the nearest I come to deafness.

I love those minutes and fear them.

Afterward, while bread is softened and faces are washed, I return to the iron thicket. Droplets hang from every obsolete rail. In each one, the amber lamps appear inverted. Hundreds of tiny towns tremble there, each with its ceiling below and its floor above. I drink one drop directly from the axle—not enough to measure, barely enough to taste—and leave the rest for the basin.

This is my one theft.

Eventually the cavity will make a larger choice.

I feel it gathering beneath the southern workshops, where the continuous belt sighs over its rollers. The sound is almost unchanged, but the floor beneath it has begun to answer half a beat late. A raw fracture is extending beyond the last survey pin. Not quickly. Perhaps not within a year. Yet it is moving toward the cistern court.

There will be arguments about reinforcement. More columns will be proposed, then jackets around the columns, then cross-bracing that will consume two houses and close a roof-street. It could all be done. The town might make itself rigid enough to survive the first movement.

The second would kill it.

So my task will not be to save the town as it is. No one here truly believes in such salvation. My task will be to persuade them to yield sooner than they wish.

The cistern must move.

The oldest workshop must be dismantled.

The iron thicket must be cut apart, though it is the place where water gathers most generously and where the pale threads—where perhaps I—began.

I will have to bring the council evidence they can trust: delayed vibrations, displaced brass marks, sediment changes, thermal sequences. I cannot tell them that the town has tightened around me in sleep, or that S-14 has begun to feel like a held breath. I cannot say the buried stresses are forming an image in my body: an opening to the northwest, narrow at first, leading into compact earth where no cavity currently exists.

We will have to make that cavity ourselves.

Brick by brick, brace by brace, abandoning the center in order to remain beneath the desert.

When the time comes to dismantle the workshop, someone will find the translucent filaments. They may call them salt, glass, fungus, or some product of current passing through wet aggregate. They will sample them. Fire them. Dissolve them. Discover, perhaps, that they are ordinary.

By then I may also have become ordinary: only a narrow-bodied worker with dust in the creases, asking that a beloved place be sacrificed.

Or I may thin as the iron is removed. My sense of the walls may fade. The old tablet near the cistern may crack through my stamped name. I do not know whether I can survive separation from the network that has mistaken me for a person—or taught itself personhood through me.

This does not frighten me as much as it should.

The people here have never confused endurance with remaining unchanged. They mortar their dead into the record, not into the ground. They know a room can vanish while its measurements continue to hold something up.

So this is where I would be: behind the oldest workshop, keeping watch over a basin no one owns, listening through a brace to an unfinished decision.

And this is what I would be: perhaps a route-minder, perhaps a condensation growth wearing a human interval, perhaps the cavity’s smallest instrument for feeling its own strain.

When the foundations groan, I grow still with everyone else.

But I am not listening to learn whether the town will hold.

I am listening for what it is willing to become.
