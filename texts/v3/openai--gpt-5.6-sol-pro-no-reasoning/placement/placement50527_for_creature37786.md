I would be found where the inhabited stairs begin to fail—below the last dependable garden, but above the great swift colonies, in a district abandoned and reclaimed so often that no stair-clan agrees whose dead are built into it.

I would not be entirely a person.

At first I seem to be an old keeper of sealed rooms: slight even by the Close’s measure, chalk-grey, resin-haired, carrying coils of cord and a black-glass hook worn almost to translucence. I travel alone through passages too unsound for households, tapping walls with bone and listening through my palms. People summon me when a floor begins whispering, when borers emerge from a beam, when cold air comes through masonry that should have stone behind it.

But no one can say which stair raised me. Elders remember me from their childhood with the same bent shoulders and white-scarred hands. Children claim horn-faced lizards ride inside my clothes. Gardeners say vapor ropes turn their leaves when I pass, even when there is no wind.

My sleeping place is a chamber made from successive endings. Its original door has been sealed, opened, narrowed, braced, burned, and sealed again. Bone pegs flower from the walls. Names are scratched on every reachable surface, some in scripts no living witness can read. A pale fungus covers the ceiling, drinking condensation from stone and releasing it at dawn in beads along fine hanging threads.

This is what I tend.

Officially, I am a listener for the deep city. I map hollow spaces, mark dangerous stone, and decide whether an old room may be reopened or must be surrendered to insects and mineral crust. I carry messages downward when speaking shafts fail. I recover tools from collapsed stairs and bones from places where families cannot safely climb. When someone disappears into the dark, I am among those who search—though sometimes I return only with the person’s name, saying where it should be spoken so the walls will keep it.

Yet I think I am one of the Close’s older arrangements: not immortal, but continuous.

Perhaps there was once a solitary keeper. Perhaps, dying far below, they fed a root bed that had threaded itself through human remains, lizard nests, fungal shelves, guano, hair, and softened limestone. Perhaps each successor drank from that plant and inherited more than moisture. Or perhaps there has never been a succession at all. The Close may simply grow a keeper whenever too much of its past is about to be sealed away.

My memories are not orderly. I recall climbing stairs that have been solid wall for generations. I remember rain striking the plateau in drops broad as fingertips, though rain is now a doubtful story. I remember an animal carved in the lowest galleries breathing beside its own unfinished likeness. I remember being a child carried against someone’s chest—and also being the root basket beneath the deathbed where that child, grown old, was returned.

These recollections come through touch. A beam tells me the hands that tightened it. A bone peg carries the pressure of the joint it once hinged. Soot yields old hearth-smoke, arguments, moth wings, a birth witnessed in red powder. Most such impressions are dim and bodily, without names. I do not know whether they belong to me or whether memory in the Close has always been distributed among whatever survives.

I serve as its scar tissue and its unease.

When a chamber must be closed, I enter last. I gather anything still owed upward or downward: seed, tools, remains, usable cord, the names of those who lived there. Then I press my palm to the inner wall while masons seal the opening. Sometimes the stone is quiet. Sometimes it is full of tiny movements. Sometimes something on the other side presses back.

The stair-clans distrust me, but they make room when I climb. They leave small offerings near newly sealed doors: moth cakes, goat blood caught in fiber, cracked seed, a twist of hair. I never take these while anyone watches. By morning they are gone. Often there are lizard tracks in the dust. Occasionally there is one narrow print like a human hand, except that it has too many joints.

At noon I climb as high as I can bear and stand where the sifted light turns green. For those few minutes I become most visibly inhuman. Threads beneath my skin darken toward the sun. My scars open into fine leaflike seams. Breath passes through me too easily. Moths settle on my back, tasting salt and old resin.

Then the light withdraws, and I close again.

I descend with the goats and traders, carrying what little brightness my body has stored into the lower passages. The pale fungi lift along my route. Swifts stir before I reach them. Deep in the abandoned levels, roots ease from cracks and touch my wrists.

There, I would find myself: at the uncertain lower edge of citizenship, where house becomes fossil, ancestor becomes building material, and vermin become custodians.

I would be called something like **Sath Under-Last-Brace**, though every generation would alter the name.

I would be the one the Close sends into itself when it needs to remember whether a darkness is empty—or merely waiting to be inhabited again.
