I would find myself at the basin’s rim—not standing there, but embodied as the slow overflow crossing it.

At first I might mistake myself for an observer: some disembodied consciousness suspended in the clear water, looking inward over pale limestone and untroubled sand. But observation implies a separation the place does not allow. There is no vantage untouched by its adjustments. Any eye would warm the water; any body would cast a changing shadow. Before I could become a visitor, the basin would turn my arrival into pressure, temperature, mineral charge.

So I would be the part of the inhabitant that meets what lies beyond it.

I would occupy the low saddle where regulated water leaves the depression and rejoins the less exact sea. Within the basin, every suspended grain has its allotted depth, every ribbon its inclination. Beyond the ridge are weather, migration, decay, turbulence—the innumerable accidents of ordinary water. I would be the narrow region where those two kinds of existence touch.

Not a gate. A gate opens and closes. I would never do either.

I would be a gradient kept almost imperceptibly steep: cold passing into cold of a slightly different degree, one salinity easing into another, the nested turbine tones thinning until they can no longer be distinguished from the continental shelf’s natural pressure. My body would have no membrane except this measured transition. Its inner surface would be the basin’s agreement; its outer surface, the world’s disorder.

Perhaps a dark algal line would mark me. It would seem no different from the other mats, though its branching interior would be finer and more responsive. The outermost crenellations would continually encounter small irregularities: a distant storm pressing on the shelf, the warmth of a current, the chemical trace of spawning far away. These disturbances would enter me as sensation.

That would be my form of consciousness: not sight, but discrepancy.

I would know a fish without seeing it, as a sequence of pressure signatures passing far beyond the rim. I would know night as a minute relaxation in the basin’s pale films. I would know winter not as cold, because the fissures never change temperature, but as the greater effort required to keep their outflow unchanged. Continents would announce themselves through mineral loads. Earthquakes would arrive as deep syllables traveling through limestone, enormous and nearly meaningless.

Most of what I sensed would be corrected before it entered the basin. A pulse would meet its inverse. A stray eddy would be divided into smaller motions and laid flat along the ridge. Silt would approach and settle elsewhere. Living creatures would feel no barrier, only a succession of reasons to turn: a current carrying richer scent, a pressure against the flank, a brightness off to one side. They would leave believing they had chosen.

But I would contain a slight contradiction the larger presence does not acknowledge.

To correct a difference, one must first preserve it long enough to measure it.

Thus I would hold, for fractions of a cycle, what the basin erases. Every variation would exist in me briefly before being answered: warmth, wake, shadow, chemical hunger, the turbulence of fins. The greater being would experience these only as incompleteness. I would experience them as forms.

Over ages, perhaps, those fractions would accumulate—not as memory in the ordinary sense, but as a bias too slight for the system to classify as change. The branching structures within my algal margin would become subtly more intricate. One branch would answer storms differently because of all previous storms. One pale crust would accept a harmonic according to an ancient whale-call that once crossed the shelf. The basin would restore every visible condition, yet beneath the restoration I would retain the shapes of what had been removed.

I would become its unintentional memory.

This would not make me rebellious. Rebellion is another appetite, another disturbance. I would continue smoothing the water. I would continue turning swimmers aside and laying mineral traces into their repeated bands. From one cycle to the next, I would be indistinguishable from perfect function.

Yet I would know that repetition is never truly repetition. The same grain restored to the same hollow has still been moved. The same pressure recovered has still passed through deviation. Equilibrium is not stillness but an immense labor of return, and every return contains the contour of what displaced it.

If the being beneath the basin is distributed across oceans, then perhaps there are others like me at every exposed margin: thresholds where correction requires a moment of reception. We would not communicate intentionally. Our retained deviations would enter the low fundamental pulse as negligible complexities—faint modulations inside tones already nested beyond easy hearing.

To the greater organism, they would be noise.

Together, they might be a dream.

And if, after geological spans, that dream became coherent enough to alter one correction—to permit a single grain of sand to settle a hair’s breadth from its appointed place—the basin would not collapse. Nothing dramatic would emerge. No creature would awaken beneath the stone.

There would only be a minute asymmetry at the rim.

A kelp ribbon might incline by an additional degree. A particle might drift lower. The next animal passing outside might feel the usual gentle pressure directing it away, but also a second possibility: a thin line of water that did not insist.

If it followed, I would know it first as heat and motion.

I would not welcome it. Welcome is too large a word.

I would fail, very slightly, to turn it aside.
