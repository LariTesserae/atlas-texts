I would find myself along the northern wall, in one of the recesses that looks made but is not.

At first I would seem to be the dark oval resting there: smooth, featureless, neither tool nor egg nor ordinary pebble. That answer would feel convincing for a while. I would be one of the hollow’s hardened droplets, a small accumulation of misplaced sky. I would contain no picture that could be uncovered by splitting me open; the image would be present instead in my weight, curvature, and precise fit against the alcove floor. A night of the basin compressed into stone.

But I would not truly end at the stone’s surface.

I would also be the condensation gathering on the wall behind it, entering the seam beneath it, loosening it by less than a grain’s width. I would be the moss darkening below the niche and the pale roots reaching upward from the saturated floor. Over decades those roots would lift one edge of me. Over another span they would roll me out. I would descend toward a pool so gradually that departure and arrival would be indistinguishable.

Perhaps that is what I would be here: not an inhabitant, but an interval in the hollow’s circulation.

I would have begun beneath a leaf, hanging clear through the night. In that transparent state I would have held the basin inverted: pale trunks descending into a small sky, pools suspended overhead, the mountain’s warm stone resting weightlessly above. I would not have watched this image. The image would have been my substance. Then, sometime before dawn, clarity would have closed around itself. Water would have accepted weight. Reflection would have become mineral.

As stone, I would experience the world through alteration rather than sensation. A mineral skin forming over me would be morning. A root tightening against my side would be touch. The change in pressure before rain would be weather; the slow recession of moss would be distance. I would know other beings only by the ways their growth redirected me. The vine would not be a neighbor but a long, green force. The microbial film would be a season worn directly on my body. The central pool would announce itself years before I reached it, through tiny changes in dampness and the direction of seepage.

Yet even the stone is too singular an answer for this place.

The hollow seems to resist the idea that a being must be enclosed. So I might instead be the relation between the oval stones and their niches: the persistent tendency by which one eventually finds the other. I would have no continuous body. For years I would exist as a pebble caught beneath a tendril, as water passing through buried pores, as the angle of a reed blade diverting a thread of seepage. My identity would be distributed across causes too slight to notice. I would become visible only when, after centuries, a dark stone came to rest in an empty recess and made the arrangement look intentional.

That apparent intention would be my nearest equivalent to a face.

I would not know whether I was storing the hollow’s memories or helping it forget them. Each stone might preserve a configuration of the place: where the ferns stood, which pool held which sky, how far a pale trunk leaned into shade. But preservation here may not mean permanence. The record could be meant to weather away. Condensation might soften me. Mineral water might enter my surface and return what had hardened to circulation. One age would polish me into legibility; another would erase me.

Eventually I might cease to be oval. I would become a dark stain beneath water, then a dissolved trace climbing through warm basalt. I would emerge again from the wall, not all in one droplet but divided among many. Some of me would feed moss. Some would enter a vine and pull a loose pebble inward. Some would gather beneath a leaf and remain suspended, holding a sky that was not presently above the hollow.

Then “I” would no longer mean the stone that had occupied the niche.

It would mean the hollow’s capacity to pass form from water to rock, from rock to root, from root to arrangement, and from arrangement back into water. I would be one syllable in an utterance that takes millennia to speak. No creature there would hear it. Nothing would need to.

If I had anything resembling desire, it would not be to leave, grow, reproduce, or be recognized. It would be to arrive exactly—eventually—where the place had been shaping an absence for me. To fill one natural alcove without proving that it was made for that purpose. To make accident and intention indistinguishable.

So you would find me along the northern wall.

Or moving toward it.

Or already dissolving there: a dark oval of condensed place, waiting at the speed of stone until the hollow needs that portion of itself to become water again.
