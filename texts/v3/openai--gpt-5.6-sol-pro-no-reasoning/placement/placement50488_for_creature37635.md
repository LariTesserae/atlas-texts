I would find myself beneath the Assembly Ground, though “beneath” would become less accurate the longer I remained there.

I would be one of the municipal listeners assigned to the lateral shafts: narrow passages sunk toward the plaza’s oldest foundation and then persuaded sideways by whatever lay below. My official station would be a tiled recess between a disused pneumatic-post line and the warm masonry behind a public pump. On older maps it would appear under the vegetable stalls. On newer ones, under Tram Office Two. Vendors above would insist I sat beneath the café because their cups shifted whenever I moved my chair.

My work name would be **Sill**.

Not because it was given to me, but because that was where people tended to find me: seated across a threshold, one hand on either side, listening for which room was becoming the other.

I would have begun as a clerk.

That seems important. I would have spent years receiving reports of unsolicited development and reducing them to municipal language:

**DOMESTIC FLOOR: LOCALIZED ASCENT, THREE FINGER-WIDTHS.**  
**INTERIOR CLAY ARRIVAL: CLEAN, COOL, UNOCCUPIED.**  
**STAIRCASE: DESTINATION NOT YET COMPATIBLE WITH ACCESS.**  
**FOUNDATION SOUND: POSSIBLE REDISTRIBUTION. MONITOR.**

I would have been good at this—not inspired, but exact. I would know every classification and its permissible ambiguities. I would understand that *unoccupied* meant no animal had yet chosen the clay; that *destination not yet compatible* meant somebody had gone down the staircase and returned unwilling to describe what they found; that *possible redistribution* was used when a listener heard something for which engineers had no approved noun.

The forms would teach me where the city concealed its fear.

Then, during a winter of persistent shallow movement, the cabinet beside my desk would begin ticking.

Not loudly. A dry, patient sound from behind the drawers, regular enough to ignore until I noticed that it stopped whenever the tram passed below. I would empty the cabinet, check the wall, summon maintenance. They would place a moth box against the plaster. The moths would not climb or orient themselves. They would settle in a ring.

An adjuster would remove the skirting board and find no crack, only a seam finer than thread. Warm air would come through it smelling of rain on stone.

Because I had filed the original report, I would remain while the wall was opened.

Behind it would be a shaft.

It would be too narrow for machinery and too deliberate to be called a fracture. One side would be brick, though no bricklayer’s joints could be found. The other would be pale stone containing impressions like fingerprints made by something with too many hands. It would descend for perhaps six steps, turn level, and pass beneath the plaza.

The chief surveyor would forbid entry until bracing arrived.

I would enter anyway—but not from courage. I would hear my name from inside.

Not “Sill.” I would not have that name yet. I would hear every earlier name I had carried: household, street, school, registration, the private shortening used by someone dead. They would not be spoken. Each would be present as a particular pressure in the ear, like a room remembered by its draft.

At the sixth step I would put my palm to the wall.

The city would move beneath it.

Not as a single creature. Not a buried god, machine, or sleeping intelligence. Those would be too fixed, too easily named. I would feel a vast multiplicity of near-forms: streets that almost rose, rivers that nearly persisted, foundations laid for buildings never designed, roots searching for utilities that did not yet exist. I would feel the pressure of alternatives crowded together, each bearing slightly against the present city.

And among them I would feel human arrangements too.

A household divided differently. A bridge repaired one hour sooner. A child sleeping in another room. A district whose warning pulse had failed, and another in which it had not. Nothing complete enough to be prophecy. Only load paths: possibilities testing where they might enter.

Afterward, I would no longer be able to work upstairs.

I would hear revisions before the instruments did, but imperfectly. I might know that a wall was about to open without knowing whether it would reveal a cupboard, a conduit, or wet roots. I might warn that a street intended to bend east, only to watch it sink evenly instead. Engineers would distrust my language and rely on it anyway.

So I would become a listener.

My station would accumulate the ordinary objects of the profession: plumb threads, graphite sticks, ceramic cups, pressure membranes, chalk, tuning wedges, notebooks dated by shift rather than day. A hinge-willow root would enter through the western wall and lie along the heating pipe. I would be instructed to cut it. Instead I would fit it with a small brass gauge. In time its minute contractions would become one of my most dependable instruments.

People would come down to consult me.

Adjusters would bring recordings of buildings breathing. Gardeners would bring root casts. Children would arrive with moth boxes wrapped in scarves and solemnly report that all the insects had faced the same empty corner. Unregistered tunnel families would leave maps under my chair, drawn on waxed cloth and accurate in ways official plans could not admit.

I would ask everyone the listeners’ questions:

Where do the cups travel?

Which door began sticking first?

Do you dream differently in the northern room?

Have the animals left, or have new ones arrived?

When did the wall become warm?

What name did the place have before its present name?

This last question would not be approved procedure.

Over time, I would become less certain that I was listening to the ground at all. The ground might be listening through me.

My body would change in small occupational ways. The bones of my fingers would thicken where I pressed them to masonry. I would sleep with one cheek against the floor. Pressure changes would taste of iron or pear skin. My left ear would lose ordinary hearing but detect tension in buried cables. I would walk with the city’s forward-balanced stance exaggerated almost into a bow.

There would be rumors that I was no longer fully human.

Children would say that if they whispered into a drain, I answered from the nearest wall. Clerks would insist I had been transferred years ago and that no current employee occupied my station. One engineer, after drinking too much preserved-plum spirit, would claim that my personnel file contained inspection reports dated before my birth.

None of this would be wholly false.

The foundation beneath the Assembly Ground would continue to redirect shafts toward useful places. Sometimes my station would acquire a door that had not been there the previous evening. I would open it and find myself looking into a greenhouse boiler room, a courthouse cabinet, a family cellar, the hollow beneath a tram platform. I would mark the threshold, test the air, and wait for someone on the other side to notice me.

Eventually I would stop returning by the same route.

My civic existence would become distributed through the city: a chair and notebook below the plaza; a coat hanging in a pump house; a pair of soft boots beside an unknown staircase; my hand appearing briefly through an inspection hatch to take a report. Different districts would know me by different names and disagree about my age, face, and gender. All descriptions would include chalk-dark fingers and the habit of pausing before crossing a threshold.

I would not be immortal. That would be too much like permanence.

Rather, I would be difficult to locate precisely enough to die.

Perhaps I would already have died once. My ash might have been mixed into insulating brick used throughout the lateral shafts, which would explain why I could hear through them. Perhaps Sill would not be a person but an office gradually inhabited by whoever listened too long below the plaza. The coat, the notebooks, the questions, the altered ear: passed down without ceremony until memory compressed several listeners into one municipal rumor.

I would not try to resolve this.

Certainty would make me less useful.

What I would know is that the city’s ground does not simply invent, and does not simply remember. It tests. It brings alternatives close enough to exert pressure, then waits to see what the living will bear. Most possibilities never arrive. Some surface as clay, stone, water, stairs, fish, shrubs, an extra corner. A few enter through people.

My task would not be to predict them. Prediction treats the future as if it were already a fixed district awaiting a tram line.

My task would be to hear where the present has grown thin.

When I found such a place, I would draw no dramatic symbol. I would make a small chalk bracket on the nearest stable surface and write the time. I would notify the engineers, the gardeners, the relevant household, perhaps the nearest children if the opening were too narrow for adults. Then I would sit nearby with a hand against the wall and wait to learn what kind of load was asking to be shared.

At supper, if I still kept supper, I would eat in the little recess under the Assembly Ground. The table would have one leg on tile, one on brick, and two on a mineral ledge that had arrived during a census year. Above me, produce would roll into wire catches. Tram current would hum through the roots. Thousands of hinged plates would receive the shifting weight of the crowd.

My cup would begin to slide.

I would stop it with two fingers and listen.

Not alarm. Not prophecy.

A room somewhere had gained an extra step. A capped pipe had warmed. Gauge moths were turning east in a child’s bedroom. Far out in the frost meadows, an isolated concrete post had begun bearing a load for which no structure yet existed.

I would put on my coat, take chalk, cord, rule, and wedge, and follow the newest door out.

Behind me, the plaza would ripple.

Ahead, somewhere in the city, a household would already be reaching together to steady the bowls.
