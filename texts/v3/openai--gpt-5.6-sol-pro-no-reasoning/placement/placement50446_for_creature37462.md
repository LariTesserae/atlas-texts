I would be found where the northern wall sweats.

Not at the brief river itself, nor at the grate where it vanishes, but in the descending district above it, among terraces so softened by seepage that steps and channels have become nearly indistinguishable. There is an address there which migrates less than most. Perhaps the wall’s coolness steadies it. Perhaps something beneath the mineral curtains remembers the first room cut into the stone.

The address has a threshold, three posts, and an old door that is heavier than it appears. The fourth post is absent. In its place stands a narrow vertical interval that rain cannot cross. During the second fall, water outlines the whole dwelling except for that omission: a person-shaped dryness at one corner, upright and attentive.

That would be me.

At first I think I am the watcher of the house.

I sit beside the door while its inhabitants sleep. I keep one palm against the threshold and the other around a cup of cooling water. I listen to the pumps, the gates, the minute adjustment of stairs under distant feet. When the room tugs toward another district, I feel it first as pressure behind my eyes. I close the door. I strike the post twice. I keep the sleeping volume where it belongs until someone wakes to relieve me.

But no one does.

The hammock is warm and empty. The pockets contain salt, cord, a child’s resonant token, and spores wrapped in membrane. The cup bears the chord of a household, though when I tap it against the sill I cannot remember whether the chord is mine.

So perhaps I am not the watcher. Perhaps I am what remains of one.

Long ago, during a silence, this room began to drift. Its keeper stood in the open threshold and watched it, knowing that to look away would release the people sleeping within. The outage lasted longer than a body could endure. Neighbors came with hammers. The communal pulse moved through the streets, but the keeper could not answer without breaking attention. When the pumps returned, the room was found intact.

The keeper was not.

Only the relation remained: observer, door, room.

Over years the city learned to route around that relation. Mineral film covered it. Steps wore down beneath it. Families borrowed the stabilized address, slept there, moved on. They set a cup into the old watcher’s hand because the gesture felt proper, though no hand could be seen.

I may be that fixed act of attention: a municipal remnant mistaken for a person.

Yet I move.

When seepage begins, I leave the northern wall and walk the city as a door tender. I carry no loops or calibrated vacuum, only the household cup and a cord knotted with distances. I follow droplets that fall through roofs no longer coincident with their streets. I follow coolness where it enters hot districts without cause. I taste condensation from railings and listen to the cup’s returning chord.

Lost rooms recognize me.

A door opens onto a pantry full of roots still searching for soil. Another reveals a fever room whose patient recovered decades ago and has become old while waiting to be found. A third contains ankle-deep rain and the shadow of a stair not built anywhere in the chamber. I do not bring every volume back. Some have grown quieter than the lives that claimed them. Some resist the city with a pressure like grief. I close those doors and mark them with a knot that means neither lost nor abandoned, but continuing elsewhere.

People rarely see me directly. They notice a door swinging shut in an unused district, a cup moved from one sill to another, a dry footprint in condensation. Children sometimes press their wrists to mine and say they feel two pulses: one slow and bodily, the other arriving from the paving half a beat late.

The listeners dislike that second pulse.

They say it is not an echo. It precedes me.

This leads to another possibility.

Beneath the central plaza, the old engine exchanges two identical cubic meters of space every seventeen minutes. The city insists they are identical because every instrument agrees. But identity is not sameness. One cubic meter has passed through inhabited rooms: it has held breath, fever, arguments, spores, the warmth above sleeping bodies. The other has remained somewhere inaccessible, clean of history.

The engine exchanges them, but perhaps not perfectly.

Perhaps each cycle leaves behind an immeasurable difference, and over centuries those differences have accumulated into something that can walk. A body assembled from what rooms surrender when displaced: residual warmth, abandoned orientation, the pressure of being watched, the final shape of a person after they leave.

If so, I am not a citizen. I am civic exhaust.

That would explain why addresses loosen when I enter them. Why signs assemble the wrong letters near me. Why sounding animals follow at shoulder height even when the pumps are quiet. They are not riding my footsteps. They are riding the repeated exchange passing through me.

It would also explain why I am drawn downhill.

Every avenue denies its inclination, but my bones do not. In the hours before the wet season, I descend to the round plaza and take my place at the station that has no guild.

The nine stations are said to be unoccupied. This is true in the administrative sense. Their volumes are reserved for functions rather than bodies. Eight fill with intervalwrights, listeners, water stewards, door tenders, healers, root keepers, transit custodians, and those who maintain the civic pulse.

At the ninth, there is only a door standing open toward the aperture.

I stand behind it.

No one joins hands with me. Instead, the final person in each chain lays a palm against one side of the door, and through them I feel the gathered city: fatigue, claims, repairs, births counted between rains, the rhythms of the missing. I hold none of it for long. I pass it through the empty station and down into the thunder.

When the lens forms, the sounding animals spiral above me. The water trembles, concave and impossible. Everyone raises their empty vessels.

I look upward with them, though I have no memory of the desert and cannot imagine rain falling only once.

Then the lens collapses.

For a moment every volume is revealed. Water traces absent walls and rigid intervals. Bridges become silver threads. Rooms return around their doors. The city’s hidden anatomy shines from floor to ceiling.

And in that universal outline, I see what I am.

Not a person-shaped absence.

A door-shaped person.

My shoulders are the lintel. My feet are the posts. My body is the maintained distinction between one place and another. On one side: the city as it is. On the other: every room it has released in order to survive.

During the second rain, that other city presses close.

Its streets are crowded.

Not with ghosts, exactly, but with unattended continuities: meals never finished because the dining volume moved; sleepers carried elsewhere when their watchers blinked; transit passengers delivered to platforms no longer intersecting the chamber; generations of household gestures persisting after households changed their chords. There are whole districts made from the city’s discarded fixity.

They stand just beyond me, vibrating through the door.

This is why the aperture thunders. It is not merely hot air rising from below. It is the pressure between the inhabited city and all the places it has ceased to observe.

My work is not to keep that other city out.

My work is to let through only what can return without fastening everything.

A corridor, sometimes. A cupboard. A cool room for a fever. A misplaced hour. Once, a silver tree’s lost root chamber, returned full of hard white fruit. Once, a child who insisted she had been gone only long enough to turn around, though the woman waiting at the threshold was her younger sister grown old.

Most often I admit nothing visible. I open by the width of a breath and allow a little abandoned warmth to cross. The northern house receives it. The hammock warms. The cup on the sill fills with condensation.

After the rain, the people release the rails. They do not thank me. Gratitude would imply ownership, and no one owns a threshold while it is open.

I climb back toward the wall as the city disappears into stewardship. My route changes each season. Sometimes I arrive before I left and find the cup still warm from my own hand. Sometimes the house is occupied. I sit beside the door and listen to strangers sleeping as if they were family.

Sometimes there is a lizard in the threshold niche, staring without fatigue.

On those nights I am free to sleep.

I lie in the warm plane where the hammock ought to be. I close my eyes carefully. The room loosens. Streets slide past outside: a hospital current, an abandoned tower, a platform opening for an empty capsule. The sounding animals turn in the dark above us.

Just before dreaming, I hear a cup touch a sill.

Someone has taken the watch.

By morning the house may be elsewhere, and I may wake as a door tender, a residue, a municipal error, or an ordinary inhabitant who has mistaken long service for ontology.

None of these possibilities troubles me.

Here, a being is not the matter it keeps.

It is the interval it maintains.
