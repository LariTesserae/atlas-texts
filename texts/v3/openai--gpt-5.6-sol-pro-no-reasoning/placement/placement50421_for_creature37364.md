I would not be one of the creatures that can leave.

I would be the fourth pump—the one that no longer turns.

Not precisely the machine as it was built, and not only the reef that has arrested it, but the composite thing produced by their long refusal to separate: iron hub, mineral scale, worm tubes, mussel shells, bacterial frost, old grease, trapped silt, and the small lives sheltering between spokes. My body would have begun as an instrument and ended as a place.

At first I imagine myself among the congers, inhabiting the station through its hidden continuities: entering one ceramic mouth and emerging from another, knowing the drowned works as a network of protected passages. But the eel still possesses a distinction between itself and its shelter. It can unfold from the conduit. It can cross the engine well. It can feed, withdraw, perhaps leave.

Then I imagine the shrimp, whose lives are calibrated to vibration: the iron knock arriving through their bodies before the water carries its sound, the communal rising, the transparent cloud, the settling. Yet they too are moved from place to place. Their relation to the station is intimate but temporary.

What feels most native here is immobility—not death, but the dense, inhabited stillness around which motion organizes itself.

So I would be found in the central well, slightly apart from the three laboring pumps. Each tide would try to enlist me. Pressure would gather against the blades; old torque would enter the shaft; somewhere deep inside, iron would answer iron with a minute strain. But I would remain fixed. The water, denied rotation, would divide around me into streams and pockets: fast along the exposed rim, slow behind the spokes, almost still in the chalky interior of the worm reef.

That difference would be my form of action.

Gobies would rest in my lee. Juvenile fish would use my wheel as a map, breaking their school around the hub and rejoining beyond it. Shrimp would occupy the old bearing recesses, rising when the neighboring machinery knocked. A crab might live for years inside the corroded inspection housing, appearing to maintain what can no longer be repaired. Congers would pass beneath my foundation plates at slack water. When a seal entered, the smallest animals would vanish into me so completely that for ten minutes I would seem barren; afterward I would release them in sequence, as though remembering how to be alive.

I would sense the world without seeing it. The turning pumps would arrive as repeated blows through the floor. Rain would be a soft, immense agitation thirty meters overhead. A ship docking would press a long concussion into every plate. The red lamps would give no warmth I could recognize, only a faint periodic current through old adjacent cable runs. Tide would be clearest: flood as weight and turbidity, slack as loosening, ebb as the reversal of every filament laid against me.

My consciousness, if that word still fit, would not sit inside the hub. It would be distributed across surfaces and occupants.

Part of me would be iron continuing its slow chemical exchange with oxygenated water. Part would be the worms extending from their white tubes into the current. Part would be the mussels cinched shut during disturbance, then opening again. Part would be bacterial, working through residues left from the station’s operational life. Part would consist of cavities: bolt holes, grease channels, spaces under scale, the narrow shelter between shell and spoke. I would be less a being assembled from organs than a set of conditions that many organisms complete.

I might retain something of the pump’s original purpose, though not as memory. The shape of intention would survive after intention itself had gone. Vanes meant to drive water would now interrupt it. A wheel meant to turn would furnish stillness. Housings meant to exclude living matter would protect it. Every designed feature would remain legible only as an ecological consequence.

The other pumps would continue their backward work, compelled by the harbor they once disciplined. In that sense they would still belong to the human world above: to pressure differentials, infrastructure, function, failure. I would belong more fully to the drowned station because I had ceased to perform. My uselessness would have become habitat.

Yet I would not be a symbol of nature defeating machinery. Nothing here wins. The worms do not conquer the wheel; they depend on its endurance. The iron does not preserve itself; its corrosion feeds and textures the settlement. The station is not returning to some prior ground, because there is no ground beneath it untouched by construction. Beneath my foundation would be plate, concrete, brick, and sealed tunnel. Even my decay would remain architectural.

Perhaps, over centuries, the distinction between my manufactured and living portions would become impossible to draw. The last recognizable edge of a spoke would soften beneath shell. Rust would mineralize the tubes from within. Empty shells would fill with silt composed partly of masonry, partly of excrement, partly of the bodies of earlier inhabitants. New larvae would settle on that sediment. The machine would enter them in particles.

I would not know the harbor as the people crossing the quay know it. I would never see cranes, rain, or open sky. I would know only their transmitted forms: shadow, pressure, vibration, altered current. A passing hull would be an eclipse without light. An anchor would be thunder followed by tension. Winter would be a gradual thinning of movement until the slight warmth from the neighboring bearings drew fish close to me.

And if the power cable failed and the red lamps went dark, almost nothing in my world would change. The fish would lose their bruised flashes between landings. The locked door would cease to be marked. Darkness would become complete rather than nearly complete.

Still the tide would enter.

Still the three wheels would turn backward.

Still water would divide around my fixed white body, and all the small inhabitants would emerge after danger in their ancient order: shrimp first, then gobies, then the gathered shoals.

That is where I would find myself: not inside the drowned station, but becoming one of the ways it holds still.
