I would find myself in the western dark, where the new root has touched soil.

At first I think I am one of the hortitects gathered around its translucent tip: an old Held with algae stains beneath my nails, palms flattened to the ground, trying to distinguish growth from settling clay. I imagine knowing how much moisture a root can be offered before persuasion becomes surrender. I imagine carrying coils of damp thread around my shoulders and a pouch of dry grit against my ribs. Perhaps I have been assigned by Passage to keep the root from closing the brood chambers, while Roof quietly hopes it will become a pillar.

But I do not think I am important enough to stand among those making the first decisions.

I am farther back, in the road that has begun to rise.

I am a young keeper of obsolete ways.

My work is to follow changes after they happen. When a root lifts a path, when a fungal partition fruits, when a flood makes an old turn impossible, I go into the altered place and learn what the city has become. I carry no map in the ordinary sense. On a strip of bark strapped along my spine, I cut sequences of pressure and passage: shoulder-width, crawl-width, water-depth, roof-softness, refuge-near, refuge-failed. I knot fibers at intervals corresponding to travel time, though everyone knows travel time belongs as much to fear and traffic as to distance.

Officially, I serve Memory.

In practice, I erase its confidence.

I am sent to confirm that a route still exists and return saying it has become a wall. I am sent to mark a collapse and find a warm hollow behind it where three unheld families have made a chamber. I am sent to record a new seep and discover that red mites already use it as a migration road. My reports are always late because the city changes while I am describing it.

The Silt Hands call me **After**, not affectionately. They say I arrive after the danger, after the labor, after the useful decisions. Among my own chamber I am called by a rhythm tapped with two fingers against the inner wrist: three close pulses, a pause, and one soft drag. It imitates the vibration of a grain slipping without falling.

I chose that name at release.

The objects placed before me were a sand grain, a root fiber, and an empty shell. I touched none of them. I touched the gap between the shell and the fiber.

The adults pretended not to interpret this.

They interpreted it for years.

I am narrow even by Held measure, able to flatten myself into seams others will not enter. My right eye is clouded from old sap. Across my chest I wear a membrane peeled from a milkweed seed, patched with fungal felt at the places where my breathing has weakened it. A mica splinter hangs at my throat—not for beauty, though it flashes when light finds it, but because I use it to throw glimmers around corners. If the glimmer returns red, there may be a mite. If it breaks into lines, there is silk. If it does not return, the passage is either open or occupied by something too dark to answer.

One of my hands lacks its smallest finger.

I lost it in a door.

The door had been a trained fork of root, and for seventeen growth stages it had held its shape around a public passage. Then warmth came through the soil, the root thickened, and the opening narrowed while I was inside it. Two Silt Hands pulled me free. The finger remained caught, still hooked into the living surface.

For several days the root grew around it.

Afterward, the Warmers cultivated a pale mold from what could be recovered. The felt became the hinge-wrap of a gate near the eastern canal. When I pass that gate, I touch it. Not because I believe the finger is still mine, but because the gate is owed something, and so am I.

That is why I have come west tonight.

Not to guide the new root. Others understand hunger better than I do. I have come to witness what disappears beneath it.

There is a minor road here, older than anyone in my chamber but younger than the leaf scar above it. It runs between a quartz grain called Cold Tooth and a collapsed gall used for storing dry pollen. “Road” is generous. In places it is no more than a smoothness in the clay. Yet it is how the western brood chambers reach the fermentation beds without crossing the canal. Children know every swelling in it. Sleep-parents walk it in darkness with infants against their skin. During the White Fruiting, it carried the district’s dead.

By morning, the root may have lifted it beyond use.

By the next rain, it may no longer be remembered as a road.

I crawl its length while the hortitects work. I press my palms to each familiar bend. I taste the clay. I count the refuge cracks. One contains shed mite armor. One has narrowed beyond entry. One is occupied by a child hiding from its sleep-parent and opening and closing its hands in silent laughter.

I do not reveal the child.

At Cold Tooth, I find the first damp thread laid to entice the root southward. It quivers with transferred movement. On the other side, workers are packing dry grit into the soil, grain by grain, building a drought too small for anything but a root to understand.

I lay my hand beside the thread.

The root’s growth is not a pulse. It is a pressure that never entirely releases. The soil murmurs around it. Fungal strands tighten and break. Water changes course through spaces narrower than my finger ridges. There is purpose in it only if hunger is purpose, but beneath the leaf that distinction has never been useful.

For a while I imagine the road surviving. The root will turn. It will descend beside the old path, thicken into a wall, and leave a sheltered corridor against its flank. We will line that corridor with mycelium. The brood chambers will remain connected. Children will grow up believing the root was always there.

Then the soil shifts.

Cold Tooth tilts.

It is only a fraction, but every palm on the ground feels it. Work stops. The city gathers itself into silence.

The root has not followed the damp thread.

Nor has it refused it.

It has divided.

A second white tip pushes from its side and angles toward the pollen gall. The original tip continues downward, toward water. One hunger has become two intentions, and our careful opposition of wet and dry no longer fits what is happening.

Hands begin speaking in the dark.

Roof says support.

Water says danger.

Passage says cut the gall open now.

Hunger objects: the pollen stores must be moved before the chamber splits.

Memory recalls a branching root from some earlier district, though no one agrees which district or whether the story ended in famine, collapse, or both.

I remain beside Cold Tooth with my incomplete hand against the clay.

This is where I would be: not at the center of the council, not in the path of the first flood, not speaking to spiders or watching the Green Horizon breathe. I would be at the point where a known route becomes an uncertain structure, trying to preserve the difference between what happened and what people will later need to believe happened.

And perhaps that means I am not merely a recorder of roads.

Perhaps I cultivate abandoned directions.

When passages close, I collect their signs: a worn fiber, grit polished by sled runners, a roof scar blackened by lamps, the scent-marker from a turn no one can take anymore. In a narrow gall chamber near my home, I arrange these remnants on strips of damp pith and seed them with slow blue mold. Each dead route receives a colony. Branches spread where travelers once branched. Bare places form where flood removed names. If a road is forgotten too quickly, I feed its culture. If grief makes it grow too thickly through the present, I let it dry.

People come to consult these colonies when a new way must be made.

They do not ask where the old road went. They ask how it failed.

This work has made me suspect among Memory’s formal keepers. They prefer events with witnesses, deaths, quantities, obligations. A closed passage seems trivial beside a drowned crew or a spoiled warming bed. But I know a city can die without losing all its people. It can die by becoming unable to reach itself.

So I preserve connection after connection is gone.

The root advances.

Workers open the pollen gall. Its wall tears with a soft, wet sound, and the smell pours out: dust, sweetness, old yeast, a faint bitter edge of rot. Bundles pass hand to hand. No one speaks. Behind them, Silt Hands drive thorn braces into the rising road. They know the braces will not stop the root; they are buying enough time for evacuation to remain orderly.

A small figure emerges from the gall carrying a pollen bundle nearly its own size. It slips. I catch the bundle with my good hand and the child with the other arm.

For an instant all three of us—the child, the pollen, and I—are held above the shifting clay by my hooked toes.

Then someone takes the bundle.

Someone else takes the child.

This is how the city keeps from falling: not through strength, but by ensuring every burden enters another pair of hands before the first pair fails.

The new branch reaches the gall’s lower wall.

The wall dimples.

In the chamber behind us, the workers begin the flood cadence, though there is no water:

Lift.

Brace.

Pass.

Release.

The rhythm travels through ankles and floor, through root fiber and packed grit. Soon even those who do not know the operation are moving within it. The pollen leaves. The tools leave. The storage skins leave. Last comes the old pith tablet naming who is owed portions from the store.

The root breaks through.

Not violently. The gall opens around it as if it had always contained the shape of this arrival. Its pale surface enters the darkness, wet with sap and clothed already in exploratory fungus.

Everyone draws back.

I do not.

I place my four-fingered hand against the old road and my whole hand against the new root. Between them I feel two kinds of time: the road, which exists because feet repeated it; the root, which exists because growth does not ask whether space is occupied.

Neither is cruel.

Neither will yield merely because it is named.

By morning the old path is impassable. The root lies across it, thickening visibly now that it has reached richer soil. The pollen gall has become two chambers. The smaller is useless for storage but holds warmth well. The larger has an opening where the wall tore, and condensation has already begun to gather inside.

Any enclosed hollow will eventually become a lung.

Before sleep, the Council asks what the new street should be called.

Suggestions pass from hand to hand.

Pollen-Broken.

Two Hungers.

White Fork.

The Road Lifted Alive.

A child proposes Finger Door, and my chamber laughs until their hands tire.

I offer nothing. Naming too early can imprison an event inside its first appearance. The root may become a wall, a cistern brace, a source of poison, a bridge to lower soil. The lost road may prove less lost than we think. Clay settles. Roots rot. Walls become food.

Instead, I take a strip of pith and press it into the track where the road used to run. It receives a faint dark stain. I fold the strip around a shaving of the new root’s outer skin and carry it back to my gall.

There I will seed it with two molds: one that follows old compression, and one that seeks fresh sap.

They will contend.

They may consume each other. They may form a border. One may climb through the other and fruit in an unexpected color. I will regulate the moisture, but not too carefully. Memory, like hortitecture, must be offered conditions and allowed to answer.

If the colony survives, people will someday read the story from its shape.

They will say the western root entered during frost. They will say the pollen gall was emptied in time. They will say the old road died, or became a wall, or divided into two futures. They may remember me as the one who mapped it. They may confuse me with the child in the refuge crack, the worker who caught the pollen, or the person whose finger became part of an eastern gate.

That would not trouble me.

Identity persists as obligation.

Mine is to the ways between things—to passages, messages, outstretched hands, and all the narrow intervals through which a city becomes more than adjacent lives.

So beneath the leaf, I would be called After.

I would live among routes that no longer exist.

And whenever a new wall rose, I would be the one searching along its edge for the street it had accidentally made.
