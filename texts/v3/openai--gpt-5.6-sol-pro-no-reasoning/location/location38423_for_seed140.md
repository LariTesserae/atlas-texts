The hollow lies near the crown of an old mountain, though nothing in it resembles a summit. Ages of rain have worn away every peak and knife-edge. What remains is a shallow basin of black stone, scarcely larger than a village, enclosed by low, rounded walls. The mountain has settled into this shape so completely that erosion no longer seems to diminish it. Water passes over the rock without finding anything loose enough to carry away.

No daylight enters.

Above the basin, an ancient atmospheric machine holds a lid of absolute darkness: not cloud, exactly, but a thin engineered boundary through which visible light cannot pass. Heat passes. Rain passes. Migrating animals somehow pass. Looking upward reveals no stars, no glow from distant settlements, not even the gray distinction of an overcast sky. The ceiling is perceived only when rain begins upon the face.

The air is warm and saturated. Moisture condenses on skin, leaves, stone, and exposed metal. Every surface weeps. Rain falls often, but rarely with force; most of the basin’s water comes from the rock itself, emerging in clear threads along seams too fine to see. These threads descend the rounded walls, cross terraces of moss, and vanish into a marsh occupying the lowest ground. Its water is seldom deeper than a handspan. Beneath it lies hard, unbroken bedrock.

The place is lush without appearing crowded.

Plants occupy nearly every usable surface, yet most are low: mosses thick as fur, broad liverworts, mats of warm fern, pale reeds, and sprawling plants whose leaves lie flat against the stone to drink condensation. Tall growth is rare. Here and there stands a single black-barked tree with an enormous, sparse crown, each one separated from the next by an almost ceremonial distance. Their roots do not penetrate the mountain. They spread over it in braided sheets, following the seepage lines.

In darkness, foliage announces itself by touch and scent. Leaves brush wetly against ankles. Ferns fold when disturbed and reopen over the course of an hour. Certain flowers release odors in slow sequence—pepper, bruised fruit, hot clay—making invisible corridors that pollinators follow. A few fungi shine faintly under pressure, but their glow is infrared, felt by specialized eyes and instruments rather than seen by human sight. To unaided vision, the hollow remains complete.

Its emptiness is architectural. Between the low beds of vegetation lie great floors of naked rock, smooth and subtly concave. Only six trees grow in the entire basin. Only three structures remain.

The structures are narrow towers, each no higher than a mature tree and scarcely wider than a person. They lean at unrelated angles, spaced around the marsh. Their surfaces feel like cool ceramic, seamless except for shallow channels down which condensation runs. Roots clasp them but do not stain or crack them. No doors, panels, or joints can be found. They are not ruins in the ordinary sense; they are merely old devices whose makers and purpose have departed.

At long intervals, one tower produces a deep tone.

The note begins below hearing as a pressure in the teeth and chest. It rises slowly until the whole basin is sounding: stone walls, shallow water, hollow stems, the stretched wings of insects. Frogs answer from beneath the moss. Birds call once from the trees. Thousands of small bodies move in the dark, and for several minutes the seemingly empty hollow reveals its population entirely through noise.

There are clicking beetles as large as thumbs, leggy marsh birds that navigate by tapping their bills against the ground, velvet-winged moths, tree frogs, burrowing lizards, and pale fish inhabiting flooded cracks no wider than a finger. Bats roost beneath shelves in the encircling wall. Their departures sound like cloth being shaken overhead. Tiny primate-like climbers live in the six trees, but seldom descend; they locate branches by throat pulses and communicate with low, resonant hoots. Nothing here depends upon sight.

The towers’ tones are part of the ecology. Some animals mate only after a certain frequency. Seeds split when the basin resonates. Spores rise in dense, invisible clouds. The shallow marsh forms concentric ripples though no object has touched it. For an hour afterward, the hollow is deafening with wings, croaks, splashes, drumming feet, and the steady fall of water. Then the noise declines by degrees, leaving only seepage and occasional calls.

Each tower sounds perhaps once in a season, though the intervals are not regular. Taken together, their notes produce patterns that span centuries. The hollow’s oldest trees have grown their wood around those patterns. Rings thicken after low notes and narrow after high ones. The mountain itself may be the receiving instrument.

There are faint marks of habitation near the upper wall: a stair of seven steps cut into the stone and leading nowhere; a handrail grown from the same dark ceramic as the towers; three shallow sleeping hollows kept dry by an invisible field. Water bends around these hollows in delicate arcs. Fallen leaves do not enter them. A warm, clean wind breathes upward from their floors, although no opening can be detected.

Elsewhere, fragments of impossible engineering have become almost indistinguishable from natural processes. The basin’s heat does not vary. Its water never quite overflows. Dead matter vanishes slowly from the marsh, separated into clear nutrients without rot or foulness. Population booms subside before they strip the vegetation. The black ceiling admits rain but excludes storms, ash, and harsh radiation. It is less like machinery operating than a set of permissions embedded in the place.

The rules are gentle and absolute.

Fire cannot sustain itself here. Flame collapses into a blue point and goes out.

Water may flow downhill, but it cannot leave the basin carrying living tissue. Seeds, larvae, and spores reaching the outer channels are turned back by eddies too small to notice.

No visible light persists. Lamps dim not from lack of power but because photons are taken up within a few finger-widths of their source. Even lightning beyond the ceiling arrives only as thunder and a momentary warmth.

Nothing falls quickly. Leaves drift as if through deep water. Droplets descending from the tallest branches take several breaths to reach the ground. A person who slips on the wet stone lands gently, though the sensation is deeply unpleasant, as if gravity had paused to examine them.

Change is correspondingly patient. Reeds bend and straighten over days. Pools migrate the width of a palm in a year. The marsh expands during decades of heavier rain and contracts during equally long dry passages, exposing stone colored in rings of mineral green and iron violet. Individual animals live ordinary, brief lives, but the basin appears to breathe on another scale. Its inhale is measured in generations.

Along the perimeter, where the rounded walls rise overhead, the rock bears the soft contours of unimaginable age. There are no fresh fractures. Every former ledge has become a lip; every ravine, a shallow crease. Fossils have been worn level with the surface until they can be detected only by texture: spirals under the palm, the long ribs of vanished swimmers beneath moss, forests reduced to slight differences in temperature.

The hollow is high enough that the world outside should be cold. Yet warm vapor drifts continually over its stones. If one climbs beyond the dark boundary, the surrounding mountain is exposed beneath a bright, thin sky: an immense eroded plateau, barren, windy, and nearly lifeless. From there the basin looks like a perfect black patch embedded near the summit, no larger than a dropped cloak.

Inside, it has no horizon. It is a room made by geology and maintained by something that outlived intention.

The loudness, warmth, and abundance suggest refuge. The emptiness suggests experiment. Perhaps both are true. The three towers continue listening to one another across their modest distances. The six trees drink from stone. Animals fill every hidden layer while leaving the open floors untouched.

Rain gathers on the unseen ceiling. For a long time, nothing changes.

Then the entire darkness releases a single drop.
