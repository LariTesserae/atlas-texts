The place is called the High Cistern, though there is no lower country left from which its height can be judged.

It is a sealed chamber near the summit of an immense drowned structure. The chamber is not immense itself: perhaps the volume of a small cathedral, narrow at the floor and broadening overhead into a black, ribbed dome. Every surface has been made. There is no bedrock beneath it, no soil caught in seams, no accidental stone. Floor, walls, pillars, galleries, even the rubble lying in corners were shaped by hands or by tools simple enough to have been extensions of hands.

Water fills it entirely.

The water is warm, dark, and under a pressure that can be felt in the joints. It carries a faint mineral thickness, not quite brine, with the taste of old bronze and fired clay. Far below the chamber, buried conduits conduct heat upward from some enduring source. This warmth is never strong enough to make the cistern turbulent. It rises by degrees, gathering against the upper vault, cooling along the outer shell, and sinking again through shaftlike aisles. The whole room turns over so slowly that a suspended grain may take years to complete its circuit.

Nothing grows here. No weed furrows the silt, no moss films the masonry, no pale frond bends in the currents. The absence of vegetation gives the chamber a severity that its abundant animal life does not soften. Every creature seems exposed against the architecture, occupying it without ever covering it.

The walls are built of interlocking ceramic blocks: black, red-brown, and the dull yellow of old teeth. They were fired individually in shallow kilns, then fitted without mortar. Their faces bear thousands of pecked hollows and parallel chisel marks. These marks have been rounded by unimaginable ages of water until the evidence of workmanship resembles natural grain. Yet nature never entered into it. Even the clay from which the blocks were made must have been carried here from elsewhere, before elsewhere was drowned or forgotten.

The cistern’s builders possessed no elegant machinery. The vault is held up by mass, wedge, counterweight, and patience. Pillars thicken where calculation failed. Arches have been repaired with stacked slabs. Ancient copper clamps, green-black beneath mineral crusts, bridge the largest cracks. No two repairs use quite the same method. One generation understood levers; another trusted braided cord and labor; another drove tapered stone pins into every uncertain joint. The result is densely articulated, crowded with supports, braces, retaining lips, stairways, niches, gutters, cages, and platforms. Empty water is rare. Wherever one swims, something passes close: a beam, a column, a suspended jar, a chain of carved rings.

The floor climbs sharply toward the chamber’s center. At its highest point stands a circular dais only a few body-lengths below the dome. Thus the High Cistern contains a hill beneath the water, a summit inside a summit. The dais is assembled from thousands of small stone tiles, each no larger than a palm. Erosion has worn their corners down until the pavement resembles a bed of closely packed river pebbles, though every piece remains locked in its intended place.

Around this central rise are lower trenches where sediment has collected. It is not fertile sediment. It consists of powdered pottery, metal oxides, fragments of shell, bone meal, shed teeth, and the nearly weightless dust of the structure itself. In the quietest cuts it lies in colored strata. A careless finstroke can lift a century into the water.

The animals avoid disturbing it.

They are numerous, but most are small and deliberate. Blind fish with broad, translucent fins hang between the pillars, conserving motion. Their bodies are slate-white and marked with dark pressure-sensitive lines. They do not school in open water; they arrange themselves along the architecture, each keeping the width of a finger from the next, until a column seems to possess several concentric skins of fish.

In the narrow joints live soft-bodied things like folded gloves. They have no visible mouths. They press their many thin digits against the ceramic and draw nourishment from mineral films too slight to look alive. Whether these films are bacteria, chemical precipitates, or some remnant process sustained by the heated conduits is not evident. Nothing greens. Nothing blooms. The only colors are those of oxidation, flesh, glaze, and old construction.

Armored grazers move over the floor on jointed limbs. Their shells incorporate grit and broken tile, so an animal at rest becomes indistinguishable from the manufactured surface. They communicate through touch. When one finds a rich patch in a seam, it taps the pavement with a claw. The vibration travels through the tightly fitted blocks, and others begin a slow migration toward it. From a distance, one sees no rush—only that, after several hours, the floor has subtly rearranged itself.

Long ribbonlike predators occupy the rain channels near the dome. They are nearly black and rarely swim. Instead they brace their bodies between projections and wait with their jaws open. The warm upper current carries tiny animals into their mouths. Once in a great while one unhooks itself and crosses the chamber. Its passage is so smooth and silent that it appears to be a crack traveling across the ceiling.

There are shelled colonies suspended in cages of hammered copper. The cages were built for a purpose no longer recoverable. Inside them, pale animals have multiplied into dense, knotted masses. Their shells chime faintly against one another when the slow circulation turns. This is among the loudest sounds in the cistern.

Otherwise the place is nearly silent. Pressure suppresses distance; warm water absorbs the smallest noises. One hears the minute scrape of shell on tile, the tick of contracting metal, a breathlike shift through a narrow duct. At long intervals, from somewhere beneath the floor, comes a single low knock. It is never followed immediately by another. The interval may be hours or days, and no animal reacts to it.

There is almost no light. A dim blue seep enters through seven apertures high in the vault, each covered by layers of perforated ceramic shutters. They do not open. Their holes have been polished wide by water, but the incoming radiance remains weak: less like daylight than the memory of daylight transmitted through miles of flooded architecture.

Certain animals make their own illumination. Pin-sized drifters flash amber when compressed by a current. A slow cephalopod inhabiting the central dais shows rows of dull red points when alarmed. Blind fish emit occasional green pulses from organs beneath their jaws. These lights do not reveal the chamber so much as measure its darkness. A pulse touches the edge of a pillar and fails; another briefly discovers a carved stair ascending nowhere; a third shines through the empty eyes of a mask set into the wall.

The masks are everywhere.

They are simple, made with stone hammers and abrasion: oval faces with closed mouths, flattened noses, and drilled eye sockets. Some are part of the blocks. Others were tied to the structure with copper wire. None resemble the present fauna. Their repetition might suggest worship, warning, accounting, or merely a builder’s mark. Erosion has rendered their expressions calm. Water passes into one eye and out the other.

The chamber’s only doorway is at the lowest point. It is a narrow, square passage barred by vertical stone rods. Small creatures pass freely through it, but nothing larger than a hand can enter. Beyond the bars lies a descent into absolute darkness. Warm water issues from there carrying faint chemical nourishment and, occasionally, objects: a bone toggle, a flake of glaze, a bead cut from black shell, a blunt copper hook. These pieces rise until caught in the crowded braces overhead. Much of the dome is therefore festooned with the refuse of lower chambers, accumulated one object at a time.

No one maintains the High Cistern now. Still, it obeys the rules imposed by its makers.

Water must move clockwise around the central dais. The arrangement of channels, sloped tiles, and heated shafts compels it.

Sediment must descend into the outer trenches. Every surface is subtly pitched to send dust away from the summit.

Large creatures cannot enter, and small creatures cannot easily leave.

Pressure is distributed through nested arches, so that no single block may fail alone. If one shifts, dozens settle by fractions around it. Collapse, here, is communal and extremely slow.

The oldest rule is less mechanical. Nothing may remain entirely still. The water turns. Metal contracts. Minerals migrate through clay. Animals tap, molt, feed, and exchange places. Yet all motion is constrained into cycles so gradual that the cistern preserves the appearance of completion.

Its great age is not shown by ruin. Ruin would be too sudden. Age has softened it toward a final shape. Sharp carvings have become shallow basins. Stairs have become ramps. Tool marks have become ripples. Broken edges have been rounded until every injury looks intentional. The structure and the water have negotiated for so long that neither seems capable of further victory.

Warmth continues to rise through the floor. The blind animals continue to array themselves around the pillars. Shells touch softly inside their copper cages. Dust settles in bands too thin to see.

And at the high center of the chamber, on the worn circular dais, there is a low seat assembled from unfired bricks sealed inside a skin of glaze. It faces no door and commands no view. Its back is carved with two cupped hands holding a single drop of water.

The seat remains empty.

Above it, in the dark vault, the entire drowned world presses down.
