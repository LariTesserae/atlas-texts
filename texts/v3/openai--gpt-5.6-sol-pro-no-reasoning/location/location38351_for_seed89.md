At the uppermost reach of a warm, barren mountain stands a narrow cleft known only by the creatures that fill it.

It is not a valley so much as a room worn into the summit: two leaning walls of dark limestone, scarcely fifty paces apart, enclosing a steep floor of rubble. The cliffs have been weathered past sharpness. Every exposed face is pocked, fluted, and hollowed, its fractures rounded by ages of damp breath. Great scales of stone overlap like the plates of an old animal. Some have shifted half a finger’s breadth in a century and now rest at angles that appear precarious but are, in fact, ancient equilibria.

Above, the mountain continues only as a thin rim. The sky is a crooked seam.

Very little daylight enters. At noon a pale band touches one wall, descends partway, and retreats. The rest remains in amber-black shade, warm but never sunstruck. At night there is almost no distinction between stone and open space. The cleft seems to seal itself, leaving only sounds to describe its dimensions.

Water comes without generosity. Mist condenses on the high walls during certain winds, gathering along mineral ridges until drops become heavy enough to fall. Rain arrives rarely and violently, but little of it remains. It threads down grooves, darkens the rock, fills a chain of shallow basins, and vanishes through the floor. For days afterward, the cleft sweats. A hand laid against the limestone comes away wet even while the mountain outside is dusty.

The largest pool is no wider than a sleeping body. Its surface is usually hidden.

Animals cover it.

Small black frogs sit shoulder to shoulder at the margin, their slick backs reflecting what little light descends. Long-legged flies balance on them. Pale beetles push between them to reach the wet stone. In cracks below the waterline, translucent shrimp stir sediment with ceaseless limbs. Nothing here is large alone, but life occurs in such numbers that the cleft feels packed with a single distributed creature.

The walls are more densely occupied than the floor. Bats hang in layers beneath every overhang, brown, rust-colored, soot-black, some no larger than folded leaves. They arrange themselves according to minute differences in temperature and moisture. Warm colonies occupy the outer stone; mothers and young gather deeper in the humid cavities; solitary old males cling near the draughts. Their weight has polished certain ceilings.

When the colonies wake, darkness detaches from the rock.

They do not depart all at once. The movement builds slowly: a shudder passing through fur, a million claws releasing and taking hold, wings opening in cramped spaces. Then the cleft fills from floor to rim. Bats pour upward in a turning column so dense that the small strip of sky disappears. Their wings strike the air into a steady pressure. Droplets are shaken from the walls. Frogs flatten themselves beneath ledges. Moths scatter and are consumed before reaching the opening.

The noise is not a single roar but countless close sounds compressed together—wing leather snapping, claws scratching limestone, infants squealing, frogs pulsing in hollow-throated chorus, insects rasping under stones, drops ticking into pools. Deeper in the mountain, unseen colonies answer. Their calls pass through cavities too narrow for larger bodies, so that the stone itself chatters. Even during the quietest hours there is a low granular stirring, as if the mountain were full of sand moving uphill.

Vegetation survives in thin negotiations. Dark moss circles the seepage basins. Liverwort spreads beneath permanent drips, flat and glossy as green skin. A few wiry grasses root where guano and grit have gathered on ledges; their blades are pale, having known more warmth than light. Near the cleft’s mouth grows a single crooked shrub, scarcely taller than a goat. Its roots descend through several vertical cracks, following condensation into the mountain. It produces hard red berries only after exceptional rain, and on those years the entire gorge changes its traffic to reach them.

No path enters. There are no walls, steps, carvings, stakes, or shards. The place has never needed shelter built inside shelter. Yet tool use occurs here in brief, local forms.

A small population of tawny mountain apes visits at long intervals. They come over the rim in family groups, lowering themselves by natural handholds. Each carries a stone selected elsewhere: thin flakes for opening beetle nests, rounded hammers for cracking the shells of high-altitude snails, porous rocks used to soak water from crevices too narrow for their mouths. They manufacture nothing lasting. Tools are retained for an afternoon, occasionally for a season, and eventually dropped among the natural rubble, where weather and hooves erase the distinction.

The apes never remain after the bats begin to rise. They know the timing by the warmth in the walls.

On the floor, life consumes the colonies’ fallings. Guano lies in dense, warm slopes, sometimes deeper than a leg. It crawls everywhere. Beetle larvae tunnel through it in pale seams. Roaches turn the surface grain by grain. Blind crickets occupy the dryer margin. Centipedes, glossy and copper-colored, hunt through this abundance, and tiny snakes hunt the centipedes. Where seepage crosses the guano, bacterial films bloom blue-white, giving off a faint cold radiance. In the deepest dark they resemble patches of moonlit water, though they are neither moonlight nor water and burn only when disturbed.

A footstep can wake whole constellations.

The local ecology depends on events too infrequent to seem like weather. A cloud catches the summit. A night of rain floods the upper channels. Water then descends all at once, washing insects, dung, feathers, shed fur, eggs, spores, and the occasional animal into the central basins. For several hours the cleft becomes a loud brown stream. Frogs climb the walls. Beetles seal themselves into cracks. Bats press deeper into the ceiling chambers. The flow scours the floor along grooves established thousands of seasons ago, and because the same channels take every flood, the landscape no longer appears freshly damaged. Erosion has become architecture without builders: bowls, gutters, shelves, columns, keyholes, and smooth-backed ridges, all complete in form yet still enlarging imperceptibly.

Then the water drains.

Warm mist remains, and everything breeds.

Eggs hatch in temporary films no thicker than a fingernail. Winged insects rise by the thousands. Frogs call until their voices fuse into a mechanical throbbing. Predators gorge. For a few days the cleft smells of wet minerals, ammonia, crushed herbs, and animal heat. Afterward it contracts toward dryness. Pools retreat beneath mats of bodies. Moss curls in on itself. The microbial glow fades. Life does not disappear; it tightens, stacks, burrows, and waits.

The mountain’s height makes the place seem exposed, but the cleft creates its own lower world. Outside, winds tear across naked ridges. Inside, air moves reluctantly. Warmth given off by stone and animals accumulates beneath the leaning walls. Exhalations condense overhead and return as a fine interior rain. In this way the inhabitants make part of their own climate. The more life crowds into the cleft, the wetter its darkness becomes; the wetter it becomes, the more life it can hold.

Nothing changes quickly except the flight at dusk and the rare flood. Colonies grow and diminish over generations. Pools deepen by grains. Ledges bow beneath deposits of fur and dung. A boulder that appears about to fall has remained suspended since before the oldest lineage of bats arrived. The shrub at the entrance twists another fraction toward the light.

From the summit beyond, the cleft is nearly invisible: only a dark stroke in weathered rock and, at evening, a widening plume of wings.

Within, it is a crowded warm night, held high above the world.
