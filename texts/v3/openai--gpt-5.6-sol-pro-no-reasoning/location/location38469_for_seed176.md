At the summit of the escarpment there is a quarry no larger than a village square.

Its smallness is deceptive. The mountain falls away on every side so abruptly that the quarry seems less like a place upon the earth than a wet room lifted into the sky. Beyond its rim are distances too large to judge: green lowlands flattened by haze, ranks of younger peaks, rivers reduced to loose silver threads. Clouds often pass below. Yet within the quarry everything is close—black walls, dripping leaves, rusted rails, pools crowded with reeds. One can cross it in ten minutes, if one does not stop, and almost no one manages not to stop.

The stone is very new.

A recent upheaval thrust this dark plug through the older mountain, splitting its cap and raising a crown of basaltic teeth. The fractures remain keen-edged. Blocks have not rounded; scree has barely begun to settle. Where drills cut into the exposed faces, the rock shines blue-black beneath its rusty skin. Natural cracks run straight and narrow, some still warm enough that rainwater breathes faint steam from them. The whole summit has the unfinished look of material pulled from a mold too soon.

Water complicates everything. Rain arrives frequently, not always from above. Mist condenses on the cold ironwork and on the windward leaves, gathering drop by drop until every cable, railing, fern tip, and stone lip begins to weep. Shallow water lies beneath the moss even in dry weather. The quarry floor flexes underfoot where crushed rock has mixed with roots into a suspended mat over hidden basins.

At the eastern wall, groundwater emerges from a seam in dozens of clear threads. They descend over fresh stone, collect iron color, and spill into three quarry pools. The upper pool is transparent and almost perfectly still. The second is green with algae and floating pennywort. The lowest, nearest the precipice, is brown-black and deep enough to contain the remains of a machine shed. Its corrugated roof can be seen several meters below the surface, silver in certain light, with young fish moving through the empty window frames.

Drainage channels cut by the old works carry overflow to the cliff. There it leaves the mountain in several narrow falls. Wind immediately tears them apart, so that little water reaches the valley directly below. Most returns as rain against the cliff or rises as a permanent cool breath around the summit.

The vegetation has occupied every pause in the machinery.

Tree ferns lean from cracks in the quarry walls, their fronds almost touching across the narrowest cuts. Wax-leaved shrubs grow in the rail bed. Moss encloses the sleepers but not yet the rails, which remain visible as two red lines curving from nowhere to nowhere. Orchids have rooted in the felt washers of utility poles. Vines ascend the hoist tower and hang from its highest beam in wet ropes, flowering pale yellow where they reach open light.

There are few large trees. The summit is too exposed and the ground too young. Instead, growth is layered low and densely: moss over gravel, liverwort over moss, fern above liverwort, shrubs thrusting through all three. The place is lush without being old. Its greenery has the urgency of colonization. Roots have entered blast holes. Seedlings stand inside broken helmets, oil tins, cable drums, and the scoops of overturned ore carts. A bank of ginger grows from a mound of powdered stone, broad leaves rattling in the wind like thin sheet metal.

Animals are seldom seen. There are insects in the warm hours—small copper dragonflies over the pools, black midges under leaves—but even these seem sparse compared with the abundance of plants. A solitary cliff bird sometimes circles below the quarry, never landing. Pale frogs live inside flooded drill sockets and call only during the heaviest rain. Their voices are delicate ticking sounds, usually lost beneath the machinery.

For the machinery is still operating.

No settlement remains at the summit, and no road reaches it now. Nevertheless, an industrial system continues its work in fragments. A narrow-gauge locomotive, squat and yellow beneath moss stains, travels a loop around the quarry once every four hours. It has no driver and pulls no cars. At the northern cut it disappears behind hanging ferns, returns with its wheels washed clean, and pauses beside a loading hopper whose lower half has fallen away.

The hopper empties nothing. Its gates open with a tremendous iron concussion, remain open for seven minutes, then close.

Pumps labor in chambers below the waterline. Their pistons move slowly, perhaps one stroke every twenty seconds, but each stroke passes through the flooded stone as a deep impact that can be felt in the knees. Belts turn overhead between isolated pylons. Some connect to no visible motor; others vanish into vegetation and emerge spinning on the far side. Ceramic insulators hum along the cliffward fence. On damp afternoons blue sparks crawl between them, making the nearby fern hairs stand upright.

The noise is disproportionate to the size of the place. Water strikes metal roofs. Falls hiss beyond the rim. The locomotive screams on the sharp curve. Loose panels beat in the wind, pumps hammer below, relays snap inside sealed cabinets, and somewhere in the western rock a crusher continues to rotate. Its jaws work with grave patience, grinding perhaps one stone a day. The sound arrives through the ground: pressure, fracture, release.

Yet nothing happens quickly. The summit’s violence belongs mostly to sound and shape. The machines follow long intervals. Water rises by finger-widths. Moss advances across a warning sign over the course of seasons. The cliff loses a sharp flake after years of rain, exposing a paler edge beneath. Even the storms often seem to gather slowly, thickening around the summit until the quarry becomes a dim green chamber with cloud for a roof.

Warmth persists despite the height. It comes from the dark stone, from vents under the quarry, and perhaps from the buried machinery. In sunlight the enclosed basin becomes humid and close. Condensation pearls on shaded surfaces while exposed rails grow hot enough to dry themselves. The air smells of crushed fern, wet basalt, old grease, ozone, and the faint sulfur of deep fractures.

Scattered structures survive without forming a coherent ruin. There is half a control hut bolted to the southern wall. Its back rooms were taken by a landslip, leaving switches and enamel dials exposed to the valley. There is a roofed platform containing six empty hooks. A steel ladder climbs thirty meters up a sheer face and ends at a patch of moss. Near the center stands a concrete plinth bearing the clean rectangular absence of whatever was once mounted there.

Labels remain on many things, but they do not name recognizable functions. The lettering is industrial and precise:

**CONDENSATE RETURN**  
**ASCENT GOVERNOR**  
**WEATHER STOCK — LINE B**  
**DO NOT EXCEED CLOUD LEVEL**

The last instruction is painted beside a winch cable that rises vertically into the sky.

On clear days the cable appears to terminate only a few hundred meters overhead, thinning until it becomes invisible in the blue. During cloud, it draws taut. The winch begins to turn, very slowly, and the whole quarry changes pressure. Water trembles in the pools. Leaves tip upward. The falls blow briefly back over the rim. Once or twice in a lifetime, according to the people in the valley, the summit climbs.

They do not mean that it appears higher.

Measurements taken from distant ridges show the entire basalt plug lifting several centimeters during prolonged storms and settling again over the following months. The machinery may regulate this movement, or cause it, or merely accompany it. The mountain around the quarry bears fresh circular cracks, but little debris. There is no record of who built the works. The metals are modern in composition, while the oldest vines rooted in them are older than the railway in the valley.

The place has one reliable rule: nothing brought to the summit will leave by the same route.

A tool set on the ground may later be found in the loading hopper. Rain falling into the upper pool emerges from the cliff days before the storm that produced it reaches the lowlands. Footprints returning along the rail bed differ slightly from those made on the way in. This is not dramatic enough to prevent visits, only enough to make visitors careful. Most tie colored thread at junctions. By evening the threads are always wet, even if no rain has fallen, and some are tied in knots no one remembers making.

At the highest point, above the quarry wall, there is a tiny marsh.

It occupies a saucer of fractured basalt only twenty paces across. Sedges stand there in ankle-deep water, crowded around a round steel cover. The cover vibrates with the slow rhythm of the pumps below. Warm bubbles rise at its edge. No insects land on the water, and no plant grows directly over the metal.

From this marsh the entire summit can be seen at once: the three pools, the drowned shed, the loop of red railway, the yellow engine waiting under ferns, the useless hopper, the vine-hung tower, and the waterfalls dissolving into air. It is a compact world, intensely occupied but almost uninhabited, full of systems whose purposes have survived their makers.

At sunset, ordinary gold light enters from the west and makes the wet quarry briefly legible. Every droplet has an edge. Every machine casts a clear shadow. The distant valleys look calm and ancient.

Then the locomotive starts its circuit. The pumps complete another stroke. The cable overhead tightens with a sound like a ship’s hull under strain, and all across the young black stone the rooted, dripping greenery inclines—not toward the wind, but upward.
