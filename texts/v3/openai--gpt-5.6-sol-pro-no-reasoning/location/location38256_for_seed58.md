The town occupies a blister beneath the desert.

It is not deep. In places, the surface lies only a few meters overhead: a compact ceiling of baked sediment, old roadbed, and fused rubble pierced by ventilation shafts. Heat passes through readily. At noon the ceiling radiates downward until the whole settlement seems caught between two suns—one above the ground and one stored in the stone. No open sky is visible, but its pressure is felt everywhere.

The cavity is young. It did not grow through centuries of erosion; it opened abruptly when a buried industrial district settled into abandoned excavations. Walls still show raw breaks, sharp aggregate, snapped reinforcement rods, and planes of pale earth as clean as cuts in clay. The town was built into this failure before it had finished moving.

It is a small place, no larger than a few joined courtyards, but it has the density of a city center. Buildings lean into one another beneath the low vault. Workshops occupy former drainage chambers. Houses are fitted between retaining piers. Stairs cross over roofs and roofs serve as streets. Pipes pass through rooms because there was nowhere else to put them. Every gap has acquired a function: storage niche, condenser bay, sleeping shelf, kiln mouth, transformer recess, shrine, sump.

From any threshold, one can almost touch the opposite wall.

The dominant materials are brick, corrugated steel, concrete, ceramic conduit, and the black glassy slag left by the first collapse. Much is newly made and still angular. Mortar has not weathered from the joints. Sheet metal holds its mill sheen beneath a coat of red dust. Bolts stand bright where they have been driven into older structures. The retaining frames are oversized and close-set, filling the passages with upright ribs.

Nothing here is level. Floors rise over buried pipework, dip toward collection pits, and end without warning at recent cracks. Yet everything is organized. Yellow numbers mark valves. White bars indicate safe loads on columns. Narrow brass plates record the movement of the ceiling in fractions of a finger’s width. The settlement has the obsessive legibility of a machine that knows it may fail.

Water comes intermittently.

For most of the day, the air is dry enough to sting the nose. Then the cooling plant enters its low cycle, and condensation blooms across the pipes. Metal darkens. Beads gather along joints and travel toward carefully positioned gutters. Whole walls begin to sweat. People place shallow trays beneath bolts and flanges, collecting water one drop at a time. In the deepest court stands a cistern no broader than a room, covered against heat and dust. Its surface is rarely seen.

Occasional rain above produces a different event. Hours after a storm, the town hears water arriving through the overburden: first a fine ticking, then a rush within sealed channels. Grates tremble. Sediment traps fill with ocher slurry. For several minutes, the gutters run hard enough to foam, and every available vessel is opened. Then the pulse passes. Damp mineral stains remain on the walls, marking rainfall years as narrow bands.

The plants have been placed where these bands recur. There are few of them: wiry gray herbs in cracks below the condensers, mats of salt-loving green around the sump lips, and pale vines trained along ceramic drains. A cluster of reedlike stalks grows beneath a leaking valve in the eastern chamber. Their leaves are narrow and nearly vertical, conserving what little water reaches them. In the dim amber light, they look metallic.

No animal life accompanies them. There are no insects in the flowers, no lizards on the warm brick, no rats in the ducts. Nothing scratches behind the walls. Food scraps remain where they fall until collected, molding or drying without being carried away. Even the cistern contains no visible swimmer. The absence is not dramatic at first. It becomes unsettling only after one notices how many sounds ought to exist and do not.

Most of the town’s noise is infrastructural and subdued. Pumps pulse behind masonry. Current hums in thick cables. Cooling fins contract with small, distinct clicks. Somewhere beneath the southern workshops, a belt travels continuously over rollers, making a soft repetitive sigh. Speech does not carry far; partitions, pipes, machinery, and dense construction absorb it. Around every corner is another pocket of near-silence.

The major machines operate slowly. Fans turn with broad blades to move the hot air without raising dust. A kiln opens once each day, releasing a rectangle of white-orange light and a wave of mineral heat. Hydraulic braces adjust to the shifting cavity, their pistons extending by barely visible increments. When one moves, it gives a low groan that passes through the foundations rather than through the air. Cups tremble. Fine grains descend from the ceiling.

Thus the settlement is active without seeming busy. Water is being caught, heat exchanged, stone cut, ceramic fired, loads transferred, walls measured, and streets remade, but each process has been quieted by necessity. Loud machinery might hide the warning crack of concrete. Hammering is scheduled. Engines are mounted on thick pads. Even doors are weighted not to slam.

Light comes chiefly from old industrial fixtures hung beneath the ceiling. Their amber cages illuminate pipes and upper walkways while leaving the ground in brown shadow. Here and there, thin shafts admit daylight through surface vents. At noon these form solid-looking columns crowded with dust. They move only slightly over the course of the day, touching one wall, then another, never reaching the deepest rooms.

The vents also produce the town’s faint weather. Hot exterior air descends through one shaft as cooler damp air rises through another. Cloth markers turn. Condensation appears and disappears. Dust migrates in delicate dunes across thresholds. During pressure changes above, the whole cavity exhales through its grilles, carrying the smells of wet brick, hot copper, alkaline earth, oil, and fired clay.

There are rules here that no plan records.

An unsupported space never remains open for long. If a room is abandoned, columns begin appearing in it: first a timber prop, then a brick pier, then a steel jacket around both. Passages narrow over time. Architecture accumulates inward.

Water follows new metal rather than old stone. A freshly installed pipe will sweat even when its neighboring wall stays dry. For this reason, obsolete rails and machine housings are left in place to attract moisture. The oldest workshop is surrounded by a thicket of useless iron, every surface beaded at dawn.

And after sunset above, though no one can see it, the raw fracture faces release heat in uneven waves. Corridors become hot in sequence, one after another, as if a slow presence were passing through the town. Lamps dim along the same route. Valves tighten. The sparse plants fold their leaves before the heat arrives.

The settlement is still being born around this nightly passage. New masonry remains unpainted because no one knows which walls will persist. Survey lines cross doors and furniture. A stair may become a brace; a house may be cut in half to clear a ventilation path. The people build continuously, but not toward completion. They build to remain exactly where they are while the cavity decides what shape it wants to become.

Above lies a bright, empty heat. Below, compact earth. Between them, in a space barely large enough to contain it, the town sweats, listens, and adds another layer.
