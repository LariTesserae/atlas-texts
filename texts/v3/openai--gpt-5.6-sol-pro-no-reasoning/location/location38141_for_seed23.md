The town occupies a shallow bowl high in the mountains, no wider than the distance a shout might carry in gentler country. Here, shouting is discouraged. The air is too thin for it, and the bowl has a way of returning loud sounds altered.

From the rim, the place resembles a crowded basin of white stone filled to overflowing with roofs, stairs, cisterns, birds, and brilliant water. There is almost no open ground. Houses stand wall to wall, leaning into one another as if cold has driven them together. Passageways squeeze between foundations; bridges cross passageways; ladders join bridges to terraces hardly broad enough to kneel upon. Beneath all this construction runs another, older architecture of drains, channels, flooded chambers, and natural fissures. Water is everywhere but seldom gathered into anything so generous as a pond. It beads on walls, breathes from cracks, threads under doors, hangs from eaves, and sheets noiselessly down polished flights of steps.

Everything is intensely bright.

The basin receives the sun as a mirror receives fire. Pale limestone, snow crust, wet plaster, mica, and moving water throw light from surface to surface until even the narrowest alley appears illuminated from within. Shadows exist, but they are thin and sharply cut. At noon, a person stepping outdoors must cover the eyes with a slotted bone visor or risk hours of blindness. The inhabitants limewash their walls not because they desire brightness, but because untreated stone grows a translucent blue rind that slowly softens it. The lime arrests the change. For a while.

The cold does not lessen under the sun. Meltwater freezes wherever it stops moving, so the town’s channels are laid steep and narrow, urging every trickle onward. Ice forms chiefly in fringes: under lintels, around well mouths, along the braided cords that carry water from roof to roof. It is clear as glass and full of trapped bubbles. Toward evening those bubbles begin to move. No one agrees whether they are air, eggs, or some smaller weather living inside the ice.

The mountain above is riddled with water-bearing stone. Every few minutes pressure shifts in the rock, and a new spring opens somewhere in town. It may begin as a darkening line across a kitchen wall, a clear bead swelling between paving slabs, or a sudden silver filament issuing from the mouth of a carved saint. The people respond at once. Clay gutters are fetched from communal stacks. Flat stones are levered up. Channels are diverted, plugged, deepened, or sacrificed. Whole streets alter direction around a spring that did not exist at dawn.

Elsewhere, a stream simply ceases.

That is more dangerous.

Running water carries heat from deep in the mountain and keeps the foundations flexible. When a channel goes dry, walls begin to contract. Roof beams complain softly. Mortar powders out of the joints. Families leave their meals and press ears to floors, listening for the fine inward crackle that precedes a collapse. Then the town changes again: braces rise, doorways are filled, bridges are cut loose before they can drag neighboring houses down. Construction here is not an effort to complete the settlement. It is the settlement’s principal weather.

Nothing is straight except newly made tools.

The oldest walls have been worn into waist-deep curves by generations passing through them. Stairways sag in the middle. Doorposts shine where shoulders have touched them. Water has sculpted runnels into every horizontal surface, and the inhabitants have accepted these furrows into their plans. A house may have one room divided by a natural stream; a bed may rest on stone islands; a stair may narrow to nothing, requiring the traveler to use handholds cut by ancestors into the adjacent façade. Buildings are repaired with fieldstone, ice, timber scavenged from old roofs, fired clay, woven reed, horn, hide, and occasionally copper beaten so thin that sunlight glows through it red.

There are few plants. The altitude, cold, and lack of soil permit only black lichens, low cushions of silver moss, and a hard red sedge that grows wherever warm water reaches daylight. The sedge is too valuable to waste as decoration. It is braided into seals for pipes, stuffed between wall stones, steeped for fever, and fed to nursing animals. Tiny roof plots hold bitter bulbs beneath panes of polished ice. These gardens are watched more carefully than treasuries.

Yet the town teems with life.

Birds occupy every ledge not actively defended. White cliff-martins crowd the eaves in layered colonies, their mud nests making entire buildings appear honeycombed. Long-legged water crows patrol the channels, lifting translucent larvae from the current with surgical care. Small round finches sleep inside wall vents where mineral warmth leaks upward. They emerge in sudden clouds so dense that the town briefly acquires a roof of beating wings.

There are mammals too: blue-gray goats with split, rubbery hooves; pale stoats that slip through drainpipes; broad-faced rodents that build nests from human hair; and shaggy, low-bodied beasts kept for milk and warmth. These domestic animals are rarely tethered. They know the town’s moving paths better than people do and can sense a pressure change before water breaks through stone. If every animal leaves a room at once, the people follow.

The gutters support their own ecology. Colorless shrimp graze on mineral films. Flat worms adhere to the undersides of sluice gates. In the deeper cisterns live slow fish without eyes, marked by faint geometric shadows beneath their skin. These markings change when nearby springs shift. Water readers catch the fish in horn bowls, study the pattern, and advise where walls should be opened next.

No one kills a patterned fish. Not deliberately.

The town has no streets in the lowland sense. It has routes, and a route may consist of a roof, three steps, a tunnel through someone’s pantry, a plank over a drainage slot, and a rope descent into a public court. Ownership is correspondingly porous. A family owns the dry surfaces of its dwelling, but flowing water belongs to the town, and anything the water touches becomes negotiable. If a spring appears beneath a marriage bed, inspectors may arrive before breakfast and remove the wall. If a major channel crosses a shrine, the shrine is rebuilt around it. If floodwater enters through one house and leaves through another, both households must host the annual accounting feast, even if they are enemies.

The settlement’s government is conducted by masons, channel keepers, animal watchers, and three elderly listeners who spend much of each day lying with cheeks against the oldest foundation stones. There is little writing. Decisions are recorded in knotted cords sealed into niches above the waterline. Maps are made on thin clay tablets and remain useful for no more than a week. Children learn the town not by memorizing paths, but by understanding tendencies: water seeks the eastern fissures after bright mornings; birds abandon warm roofs before a deep surge; blue ice means pressure from below; a stair that hums must not be climbed.

The town is almost silent despite its constant activity. Felt wraps the mallets. Wooden wedges are soaked before being driven. Market bargaining is done with fingers, counters, and expressions. Doors have hide hinges. Even livestock bells are made from pierced shells and produce only a dry whisper.

This quiet is partly practical. Sharp noise can loosen icicles and startle nesting birds into dangerous swarms. More importantly, the mountain imitates.

A hammer blow may return hours later from beneath the central cistern. A name shouted at noon may be whispered from six drains after sunset. Once in several years, according to the listeners, the rock repeats an entire day: footsteps, shutters, goat cries, water spilling through channels that have long since been rerouted. During such repetitions everyone remains indoors. No one answers a familiar voice heard under stone.

At the center of the basin stands the oldest feature: a circular shaft enclosed by densely built houses. It is called the Bright Well, although no one draws water from it. Its rim is scalloped by immense erosion, smooth as old bone. Light rises from its depths with the same cold brilliance that falls from the sky, so that at midday the shaft seems to pass straight through the mountain and open onto another sun.

Mist constantly pours into it.

Nothing thrown down is heard to land. Birds sometimes descend in spirals, but different birds emerge: mature martins go in, white chicks come out; water crows vanish, and pale fish fall upward in their place, writhing briefly through the air before slipping into roof channels. During pressure storms, the well produces architecture. Wet stone swells from its inner walls into arches, stairs, sealed doorways, and small furnished rooms. These forms climb toward the rim over several frantic hours while the town dismantles nearby structures to make space. By morning, the growth stops. Over succeeding months it weathers, joins the town, and becomes ordinary.

At other times the well takes things back. A wall liquefies without warmth. Steps soften and stream inward. Whole chambers fold quietly toward the shaft, carrying pots, bedding, nests, and sometimes sleepers into the brightness below. There is no reliable warning except that all nearby water becomes perfectly still.

Thus the town is ancient without accumulating size. It has been built continually for longer than its inhabitants can reckon, yet remains confined to its little mountain bowl. Every addition answers a loss. Every ruin becomes a quarry. Every route is both old and temporary.

Toward evening, the brightness intensifies before it fades. The western snowfields cast the sun back into the basin, and the wet town ignites in white, gold, and blue. Thousands of birds settle simultaneously. Workers stop with their hands on ropes and sluices. Animals face uphill. For a few breaths, even the water seems to hesitate.

Then the sun drops.

The cold tightens. Channels glaze at the edges. Mineral mist rises from the stones and fills the lower alleys, leaving only roofs, bridge rails, horns, and human heads above a luminous white surface. People light shallow bowls of fat, though the flames are dimmer than the ice around them. Beneath floors and behind walls, the hidden water changes course.

All night the town listens and rebuilds itself in darkness.
