Beneath the salt flats, where the surface heat has baked the ground into white plates, there is a town built inside a recently opened fault.

It is not deep enough to be cool. Heat follows the inhabitants downward and remains there, held between black walls of fresh stone. The air is damp without being wet, and every breath feels used. Condensation gathers on iron lintels, on pipe joints, on the undersides of stairs. It hangs in trembling beads until enough have joined to fall. Rain, here, is the irregular ticking of ceilings.

The fault is narrow—less a cavern than a set of rooms forced apart by some recent subterranean movement. Its stone still has sharp edges. There are no broad stalactites, no smooth mineral curtains, none of the patient ornaments of ancient caves. The walls look broken yesterday: glassy seams, pale stress marks, splinters of basalt driven into softer strata. In places, roots descend through cracks made by the same upheaval, fine at first and then thick as wrists, seeking the humid air.

The town has occupied nearly every available surface.

Brick chambers are fitted into stone recesses. Balconies overhang workshops; workshops sit on cisterns; cisterns are crossed by footbridges carrying bundles of cables and hot-water pipes. Walls have been extended with sheet metal, fired clay, boiler plate, discarded machine housings, and blocks of a chalky local stone that sweats brine. A person can stand in the central cleft and look upward through seven or eight irregular levels, though none of them forms a proper street. Everything is stair, gantry, landing, roof, and threshold. No volume is left unused except the thin vertical slots reserved for ventilation.

The inhabitants call the place the Fold.

Its oldest structures are only a few decades old, but age is deceptive belowground. Iron reddens quickly in the damp heat. Mortar blooms with salt. Wooden rails become black and soft, then are wrapped in wire rather than replaced. New additions look ancient within a season; old foundations still rest on angular rubble that has not yet settled. Every building bears the contradiction of the Fold: premature decay on immature ground.

At its lowest point, a narrow river appears from beneath a wall and disappears under another after fewer than two hundred paces. It is the town’s only dependable water. The current is warm, slow, and iron-colored. Channels branch from it into covered reservoirs, washing troughs, boiler tanks, garden beds, and cooling coils, so that by the time the river leaves, much of it has passed through the town in one form or another. Regulations governing its use are painted on ceramic plaques at every junction. Drinking water first. Roots second. Engines third. Washing only after the second bell. No blood in the east channel. No soap upstream of the settling vats.

In dry months the river contracts to a chain of coppery pools connected beneath the gravel. Then the Fold lives on condensation. Broad metal leaves—hammered sheets shaped to a careful curve—are fixed above alleys and machinery. They collect moisture from rising air and guide it into jars. At night, when the engines cool a little, these leaves begin dripping all at once. For an hour the town sounds as though it is inside a restrained rainstorm.

Vegetation thrives wherever a wall is warm and wet enough, which is almost everywhere but never evenly.

Moss upholsters shaded masonry in deep green pads. Ferns emerge from boiler cracks. Pale vines are trained along copper trellises, their leaves thick and dark, their tendrils stained blue where they touch oxidized metal. Gardens are built vertically in pockets of silt packed behind mesh: peppers, bitter greens, small yellow tubers, climbing beans, medicinal fungi. The plants overlap the architecture so completely that it is difficult to tell whether some rooms were built as greenhouses or gradually became them.

Near the river, the growth is lush to the point of obstruction. Roots push between paving bricks and lift them into steep little ridges. Long-leaved water plants choke the diversion basins. A translucent reed, found nowhere on the surface, grows in the warm runoff of the machine hall. Its hollow stems pulse faintly when the pumps are operating, filling and emptying as if breathing with the town.

Above the inhabited levels, plants become stranger. Blind flowers hang downward from the ceiling cracks. They open not toward light but toward heat, and close whenever touched by moving air. Their waxy petals collect condensation in cupped interiors. Once full, they invert with a quiet snap, throwing water onto the roots beneath them. Whole overhead gardens turn themselves over this way, one flower after another, in slow, irregular sequences.

There is little light. Narrow electrical lamps burn behind cages, each surrounded by its own halo of mothless humidity. The main passages are lit with low amber tubes powered by the river turbines. Workshops use white arc lamps when precision is needed, but curtains are drawn around them; sudden brightness is considered dangerous because it wakes the ceiling growth and draws heat-sensitive molds from dormant seams.

Most of the Fold is illuminated indirectly. Light slides under doors, leaks through floor gratings, reflects from wet pipes, or arrives green and diluted through leaves. Faces appear piece by piece: brow, cheek, fingers, the rim of an eye. The black rock absorbs everything else.

The machinery is dense but subdued. There are pumps, grain mills, presses, ceramic kilns, cable drums, ventilators, and a small foundry recessed into the hottest wall. They do not operate continuously. Fuel is scarce, and noise travels too well through the fault. Machines are run according to a schedule measured in pressure, temperature, and communal tolerance. A mill may turn for twenty minutes, then remain silent for three days. The great ventilation fans revolve so slowly that each blade can be followed with the eye.

Belts cross some chambers overhead, transferring power between levels. They move with a soft leather murmur. Pistons sink and rise. Valves release brief sighs. Water taps inside pipes. Otherwise, the town is quiet.

Speech is kept low, partly by custom and partly because the stone returns every sound altered. A shout does not echo cleanly. It goes into the walls and comes back minutes later from an unrelated crack, thinned to a murmur. Hammering is worse: a blow struck in the foundry may surface in a nursery or cistern as three faint knocks. For this reason, builders coordinate their work by colored lamps. Red means drilling. Blue means the fault is answering. White means stop.

No one is certain what “answering” means, though everyone recognizes it.

Sometimes a sound repeats too many times. Sometimes a wall ticks in patterns unrelated to cooling stone. Once or twice a year, every pipe in the Fold resonates with a tone too low to hear directly; people notice it as pressure behind the teeth. During these events the river becomes perfectly still, although it continues entering at one end and leaving at the other. The inhabitants extinguish all exposed flames and wait.

Nothing violent has ever followed. That makes the ritual more solemn, not less.

Animal life is sparse. There are small gray lizards around the kilns, nearly transparent river shrimp, and a breed of long-legged beetle that lives in cable conduits and feeds on insulation fungus. Birds cannot navigate the crowded galleries. Rats brought by the first settlers vanished within a generation. Occasionally their descendants are glimpsed far above: hairless, pale things moving through root mats, too cautious to enter lamplight.

The largest native creature is seen only in the river’s deepest basin. It resembles a broad, folded piece of cloth and lies beneath the silt for months at a time. When the water level drops, it shifts downstream by a few handspans. Children leave polished washers at the basin edge for it, though no one has observed it eating or reacting. Over years, the washers disappear.

Movement through the town is constrained and intimate. Two people cannot pass on many stairs without touching. Doors fold upward because there is no room for them to swing. Bedrooms are stacked like drawers. Public squares are no larger than workshops, distinguished only by the absence of tools. The broadest open place is the Reservoir Court, a chamber perhaps thirty paces across, crowded with pillars, pipe risers, hanging gardens, and the roofs of three submerged buildings.

Those buildings predate the present water level by only twelve years.

The Fold changes slowly, but it never stops changing. Moisture migrates. Stone warms. Roots thicken by imperceptible increments until a stair tilts or a pipe is pinched shut. Settling rubble shifts foundations a grain at a time. New cracks appear as hairlines traced in salt, and engineers mark their ends in chalk. If a crack extends beyond its mark, the nearest rooms are emptied. If it sprouts moss, the evacuation is canceled; moss is believed to mean that the stone has finished deciding.

Construction therefore resembles negotiation. Walls are made with deliberate gaps. Bridges rest on sliding shoes. Pipes include loops to absorb movement. Nothing important is anchored in only one stratum. The builders have learned that the fault is young, hot, and not yet accustomed to being hollow.

On the highest inhabited level, close to the inaccessible surface, there is a district called the Dry Roof. It receives little condensation. Here the walls are dusty, plants grow in isolated ceramic pots, and the air smells of salt and heated stone rather than moss. A sealed shaft leads upward, blocked by debris from the original collapse. Every few years crews excavate a little farther, removing one basket of rubble at a time. They work without engines. Progress is measured in finger-widths.

Some residents dream of reopening the route to daylight. Others quietly return stones to the shaft.

They say the surface is too empty: too wide, too bright, too exposed to weather that falls from an unreachable sky. In the Fold, everything is near. Water has a pipe. Heat has a wall. Light has a cage. Every root can be assigned a trellis, every tremor a chalk mark, every silence a prescribed duration.

Yet the town’s order is incomplete. Beneath the lowest river channel, surveyors have found warm air rising through a grate of natural stone. It smells of wet leaves and hot metal. Seeds left near it germinate overnight, but their roots grow downward through the rock instead of into the soil.

There is thought to be another cavity below.

The Fold has begun building stairs.
