Far below the surface lies a city without roofs.

Its streets spread across the floor of an immense limestone chamber, broad enough that the farther districts disappear into luminous haze. The ceiling is seldom visible. Where it can be glimpsed, it resembles a second landscape suspended upside down: pale shelves, worn domes, and long mineral folds, all softened by an age of condensation. There are no stalactite forests here. Those broke, dissolved, and fell millions of years ago. Time has rounded their remnants into low white islands among the buildings.

The chamber is bright—not warmly, and not from any central source. Light lives in the stone. Great planes of chalky rock emit a cold, diffuse radiance when damp, and the faint moisture of the cavern keeps them shining. In dry intervals they fade to pearl-gray; after the hidden rains they blaze almost blue-white. Thus illumination passes across the city in slow fronts. Whole neighborhoods brighten while others sink into twilight, as though cloud shadows were moving through a buried sky.

Nothing green grows here. There is no moss in the cracks, no root descending from the remote world above, no lichen softening a wall. The city’s builders know no plant except as a disputed ancestral image: a branching thing said to have stood upright in open air. Their world is mineral, animal, ceramic, hide, bone, and water.

The city is called, in translation, the Scatter.

It is not dense. Its structures stand far apart along an enormous grid of channels and worn processional ways. A house may be separated from its neighbor by a hundred paces of bare pavement. Kilns, cistern courts, animal pens, markets, and shrines form small clusters divided by pale vacancies. In some districts, the streets remain but the buildings have vanished entirely, leaving only doorstones and circular hearth scars. The emptiness is deliberate. People here distrust continuous walls. They say that stone joined too closely begins to remember pressure.

Most buildings are low and handmade. Their walls are courses of uncut rock fitted with clay, or slabs leaned together into blunt triangular vaults. Roofs are ceramic scales laid over ribs of bone. Metals are rare and treated almost as pigments: a copper hook, a soft lead weight, a bright bead hammered from some fallen fragment. Work is done with stone mauls, shell scrapers, wedges of antlerlike material, cord made from animal gut, and fire fed by rendered fat. The city possesses roads, drains, reservoirs, civic boundaries, counting houses, and an elaborate law of property, yet no wheel strong enough to carry freight. Everything moves on backs, sledges, or in shallow water.

The water does not form a single river. It appears in episodes.

At irregular intervals, pressure changes in the stone above. Condensation pearls simultaneously on ceilings, walls, statues, tools, and skin. A fine rain begins—not falling from clouds but assembling out of the air. For several hours, sometimes several days, every surface sweats. Channels fill. Dry fountains awaken. Courtyards become shallow mirrors. Water races along streets polished by ages of such brief floods, dividing and rejoining around old foundations.

Then the chamber dries again.

The turbulence of the Scatter belongs to these water cycles. In ordinary times the city seems almost abandoned. Figures cross luminous squares singly. Traders sit beside widely spaced mats bearing salt cakes, bone needles, fired bowls, translucent stones, and sealed bladders of drinking water. Conversations are conducted close to the face. Footsteps are softened by mineral dust. A listener may hear a clay vessel being set down three streets away.

When the rain begins, the quiet is not truly broken; it is multiplied.

Millions of droplets strike stone with a fine, continuous hiss. Citizens rush into the channels to raise gates, clear silt, anchor buildings, set traps, launch cargo bowls, and divert water toward their household cisterns. Walls are dismantled before they can collapse. Bridges are assembled from prefitted slabs. Market stalls migrate uphill. Pens open and streams of animals pour through the streets. The whole population labors furiously, but almost without shouting. Sound carries too unpredictably in the cavern, and a loud command may return several minutes later from the wrong direction. Communication is done with hand signs, shuttered lamps, and poles bearing geometric panels.

The city can alter its shape in a single wet cycle.

A residential court becomes a reservoir. A reservoir fills with gravel and is remade as a plaza. A channel cuts through an ancestral hall, and rather than divert it, the inhabitants remove the roof and declare the new watercourse public. Maps record not locations but tendencies: where a street is likely to go, where a district prefers to collect after flooding, which foundations have remained trustworthy over the last forty rains. Permanence here belongs only to the cavern. The city survives by refusing it.

Its animals are everywhere.

Most are small and pale, though not blind. In a world so bright, eyes have not vanished; they have become narrow, shielded things, silver-lidded and difficult to surprise. Herds of long-legged grazers wander the bare flats, but they do not graze plants. They lower rasping mouths to the luminous stone and scrape away a nearly invisible film of mineral-feeding cells. What they harvest looks like frost and regrows only after condensation. Their passage leaves dark calligraphy across the glowing ground.

There are soft-bodied filterers in the cisterns, jointed white runners in wall cavities, and fist-sized creatures that inflate themselves when the air grows wet. These drift briefly on the rain-laden currents, trailing threads that catch microscopic life. Flat, many-footed scavengers follow human settlements and eat hide glue, spilled fat, shed skin, and the crumbly edges of old bones. Their shells are used as scoops.

Higher in the chamber, enormous sail-backed animals move along the ceiling. They cling there for months, almost indistinguishable from folds of rock, then detach during a wet period and descend through the luminous mist. They are nearly weightless for their size, their bodies latticed with air chambers. Entire streets stop work to watch them pass. They make no cry. Only the soft tightening of their membranes can be heard, like hands smoothing cloth.

Predators are rare but conspicuous. The most feared are the corridor beasts, which imitate architecture. A resting one resembles a low arch or a pile of fitted slabs. When prey passes beneath, the “stonework” closes. For this reason no citizen walks through an unfamiliar doorway without first throwing a marked pebble through it. The pebble must remain visible for a count of thirty.

This precaution has become law, ritual, and greeting. At the threshold of every inhabited house lies a bowl of tested pebbles.

The oldest parts of the landscape are older than any tale. Water has erased sharp geology from the chamber. Cliffs have been reduced to inclines; boulders to oval masses; ravines to shallow, wandering depressions. Even the ruins of previous cities have been planed nearly level. Here and there, lines of harder material emerge from the floor: the foundations of unknown structures extending beneath current districts and out into the empty reaches. Their geometry is immense and subtly wrong. They meet at angles the Scatter’s surveyors cannot reproduce twice.

These lines shift during floods.

Not visibly. No one has seen them move. Yet when the water recedes, a buried avenue may point several degrees away from its former landmark, or a foundation once beneath a kiln may surface in an animal yard half a district away. People blame errors in measurement until too many errors agree. The oldest surveyors believe the chamber floor is composed of vast, slow rafts sliding over mud. Priests say the lost cities are rearranging themselves below, making room.

Both explanations may be true.

Beyond the inhabited districts, the radiant flats continue for days. There are isolated towers there, each barely taller than a person and separated from the next by hours of walking. They are made of stacked bowls sealed mouth to mouth. Nobody remembers their purpose. During dry weather they stand silent. During condensation events they begin to turn, one bowl against another, though no axle joins them. Their motion is exceedingly slow but spreads across the plain in waves. Seen from a distance, thousands of towers seem to bow toward something moving beneath the floor.

The people avoid the direction of the bowing.

The Scatter has no ruler. It is governed by water crews, wall circles, animal keepers, and custodians of brightness. Authority expands during rain and contracts afterward. A person who commands hundreds while the channels run may return the next day to scraping mineral film from a public stair. Wealth is measured partly in sealed water, partly in labor owed, and partly in empty space. To possess a large unbuilt interval around one’s house is a sign of security. To fill that interval would be considered both vulgar and dangerous.

The most respected craft is unbuilding.

Unbuilders walk through the city tapping walls with stone hammers and listening through their fingertips. They can identify a structure that will fail during the next wet period, a drain likely to reverse, or a foundation beginning to follow the movement of the ancient lines. Their work is not to preserve such structures but to take them apart gracefully. Every stone is numbered with soot and carried to a new site. Some houses have been disassembled and raised again hundreds of times, accumulating slight changes in proportion with each migration. Families do not say, “This is where our house stands.” They say, “This is where our house has paused.”

Despite the constant rearrangement, the atmosphere is not frantic. The turbulence belongs to matter and labor, not to noise. Even in emergencies, the city keeps its hush. Water murmurs in low channels. Ceramic roof scales tick as temperature changes. Animals rasp the glowing floor. Far away, a collapsing wall produces a muffled sequence of impacts, then stillness.

The cool air smells of wet stone, clay dust, animal oil, and the metallic freshness that precedes condensation. Breath is visible only in the coldest hollows. People dress in layered hides painted white to reflect the cavern glow. Their shadows are faint and multiple, spreading beneath them like stains.

There is no sunrise. There are only brightening walls, the arrival of moisture, and the long withdrawal of water.

There is no harvest. There are hatchings, migrations, mineral blooms, and the scraping season.

There is no wilderness in the sense of a place untouched by habitation. Roads continue far beyond the city, crossing ancient leveled expanses where no person now lives. Cisterns fill by themselves. Doorways stand without rooms. Herds follow abandoned civic boundaries as though obeying laws no one enforces. The cavern has been inhabited for so long that its emptiness is urban emptiness: plazas after vanished crowds, streets after vanished destinations.

And beneath all this—the handmade walls, the moving markets, the pale herds, the brief rain—something continues to revise the ground.

After each flood, the Scatter wakes subtly changed. A familiar route takes longer. A square has acquired a shallow tilt. Water drains east where east did not used to be. The bowl towers bow in a new direction. Surveyors walk out with cords and knotted rods, setting markers in stone older than memory, while behind them unbuilders loosen the first roof scales.

The city does not resist.

Across the bright, cool underworld, thousands of quiet hands begin moving it again.
