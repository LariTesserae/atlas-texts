The place is no larger than a cupped hand, though it contains an altitude sufficient to kill.

It lies inside a fresh fracture in black volcanic glass: a narrow pocket opened when the larger rock cooled too quickly and split. From outside, the fracture appears ordinary—a wet seam breathing faint steam. Within, its walls draw apart into a precipitous country of ledges, needles, and translucent escarpments. The far wall may be reached with the point of a bone awl, yet seems several days’ descent away. Distance behaves differently across the crack. Depth gathers here without needing width.

The landscape is young. Its peaks have not rounded; its ravines are splinters. Every surface retains the bright, cruel edge of its making. Obsidian flakes overlap like scales, smoky at their centers and colorless along margins finer than hairs. Some cliffs are bubbled basalt, still warm enough to redden droplets on contact. Others are pale mineral crusts deposited within the last hour, soft-looking but rigid, arranged in fans and shelves that break at a breath.

There is no soil and nothing green.

Water nevertheless occupies almost everything. It sweats from the glass, collects beneath overhangs, and hangs between opposing walls in clear bridges. Rain from the outer world enters only occasionally, one drop at a time. Each drop arrives as an event of geological scale: a bulging ceiling descends, quivers, and falls through the pocket with scarcely a sound. It strikes a ledge, divides, and becomes a temporary system of rivers. Floods pour down three-millimeter chasms. Basins fill, overtop, and empty. For several minutes the whole interior gleams with linked cataracts; then heat begins taking the water back.

Evaporation should dry the fracture, but the vapor cannot entirely escape. It ascends, cools near the mouth, condenses, and returns. Thus the pocket has its own weather, a close and repeating circulation: seep, flood, steam, mist, rain. Warm condensation coats every surface. On the lowest shelves, water reaches nearly to boiling. Higher up it is merely hot, and at the narrow entrance it can briefly become cool enough to bead without hissing.

The illumination is plain daylight admitted through the split. There is no permanent glow, no dramatic darkness. When the outer sky is clear, white light travels down the wet facets and is broken into modest rainbows. Clouds passing overhead dim the entire country at once. A fingertip laid across the fracture would cause an eclipse.

Life is everywhere, but none of it is vegetable.

The apparent mineral crusts stir.

The clear beads along the walls are crowded with animals—if animals is the correct word for forms that do not reliably preserve their bodies from one moment to the next. They are nearly invisible in water and metallic in steam. Some are flat, jointless things that adhere to the glass with concentric mouths. Some have forests of legs surrounding an empty center. Others resemble knots tied in transparent thread. They pack the pools layer upon layer, feeding, mating, dividing, devouring one another, and sometimes passing bodily through one another without resistance.

A single hanging droplet may hold thousands.

Their abundance does not create commotion. Movement is minute and mostly concealed by the water itself. A pool will seem still until its surface tilts contrary to gravity, revealing that what appeared to be water was a dense congregation carrying a thinner film of water on its backs. A pale mineral shelf may peel away from the cliff and migrate downslope. Steam sometimes gathers into a narrow procession and descends instead of rising; in it travel creatures that can inhabit vapor but drown in liquid.

Predation is swift, intimate, and almost silent. Hook-mouthed grazers browse films of bacteria-like dust from the stone. Larger glass-bodied forms pursue them by bending the local slope. Their prey flees uphill, only to discover that uphill has changed allegiance. In the hottest basins squat thick, dark hunters, each surrounded by a zone where reflections arrive late. Anything crossing that zone appears twice: first alive, then already opened.

The only persistent noises are the click of cooling glass, the soft tick of droplets touching hot stone, and an occasional dry whisper as innumerable feet change direction together. Even the floods are strangely quiet. Sound has difficulty traveling across the pocket’s distorted distances. A splash made nearby may be heard faintly from far below, several minutes before the drop falls.

The laws here are not absent, but compressed and poorly aligned.

Gravity adheres to surfaces more faithfully than to mass. Each wet facet possesses its own down. Animals cross from wall to wall at edges where these gravities meet, pausing to feel which direction currently has greater authority. Water is less obedient. It remembers the outer world’s gravity for a time, then accepts the local slope, sometimes branching upward and downward from the same bead.

Scale is similarly unstable. Creatures do not grow by becoming larger; they grow by acquiring more interior. An animal small enough to stand on a crystal point may contain a warm cavity with weather of its own. Parasites enter those cavities, and predators enter the parasites. No one has established a smallest inhabitant. Nor is the black rock certainly the largest enclosing body. Under magnification, some of its facets show another wet fracture, with another range of sharp peaks descending within it.

Heat comes from below, from stone that has not finished being born. At intervals, a red thread opens at the bottom of the pocket. No lava emerges. Instead the opening exposes a brightness with no visible source, and all nearby water leaps into steam. The fauna responds as one organism: wall-clingers seal themselves flat; swimmers climb into droplets under the roof; vapor-dwellers bloom through the mist. For a few breaths the fracture is densely occupied in every dimension, its air granular with wings, bladders, loops, and drifting jaws.

Then the red thread closes. Condensation begins again.

There are signs of tool use, though no structures and nothing that remains an artifact for long. Certain many-legged beings take fresh obsidian splinters in paired forelimbs and chip them smaller. They use these edges to open armored prey or score channels that guide condensation toward their eggs. When the task is done, they push the flakes into hot water, where mineral skins rapidly cover them. Perhaps this is concealment. Perhaps it is ritual. Perhaps the creatures understand that a made object attracts something.

Now and then, after such a flake is abandoned, the entire pocket becomes attentive.

Every animal stops. Water ceases to drip. Steam hangs in place. Reflections turn toward the tool even when the surfaces producing them face elsewhere. In the resulting quiet, a pressure passes through the crack—not a sound, exactly, but the sense of something feeling along the landscape from outside its available dimensions.

The shaped edge disappears first.

Then one of the tool users disappears, not suddenly but retroactively. Its channel remains, its eggs remain, and nearby creatures retain the habit of avoiding the place where it stood. Yet no body remembers having seen it. The pocket closes over the inconsistency with a brief fall of warm rain.

Nothing here builds shelter. There is nowhere safer than anywhere else, and permanence appears to be the chief condition that draws danger. Life survives through abundance, speed, and revision. Bodies are provisional. Species last for hours. A lineage may rise in one flood and be extinct before the same water returns as condensation. The dead leave no loam. Heat cleans the glass; scavengers take the rest; dissolved minerals plate the cliffs in thin new skins.

By evening the entering light turns amber. Every wet edge catches it, and for a moment the tiny fracture resembles a crowded mountain world immediately after creation. Peaks stand sharp above steaming abysses. Rivers span impossible heights. Uncountable animals cling to the shining faces, waiting for the next drop from the vast, unreachable sky beyond the seam.

From outside, there is only a black stone after rain, warm to the touch.

If lifted to the ear, it seems silent.

If held long enough, however, a single cool bead forms deep inside the crack. The whole hidden country begins climbing toward it.
