The place is called the High Orchard, though it occupies no more space than a chapel cupboard.

It is hidden inside the summit of a black mountain, within a narrow fault whose walls stand almost together. To enter, one must descend rather than climb: through an abandoned pressure door, along thirteen iron rungs, and into a vertical chamber scarcely four paces across. Yet the chamber contains an altitude far exceeding the mountain around it.

Its floor is not visible. The shaft drops through ranges of miniature cliffs, ledges, escarpments, and needle peaks, every one compressed into the crowded dark. Looking down produces the bodily certainty of gazing from a tremendous height. Dust thrown into the opening does not fall in proportion to the chamber. It dwindles, catches on remote-looking shelves, and remains there like snow on a distant range. A hand can span what the eye insists is a valley hundreds of kilometers wide.

There is no water. None has ever condensed here. The air is cool, metallic, and perfectly dry; exposed skin tightens almost at once. The mineral surfaces bear no channels, splash marks, or stains of seepage. Their erosion is the patient abrasion of powder against stone. Every ridge is fluted by grains of salt and magnetite circulating in sealed currents, and every summit has been worn into the stable shape it will keep forever.

The vegetation is abundant, though nothing in the Orchard is green.

It grows so densely upon the cliffs that bare mineral can be found only by breaking something. Pale fronds overlap in tight scales. Black filaments knot themselves into cushions. Stiff silver fans emerge from cracks and unfold into branching lattices no thicker than frost. There are trunks, but they are made of corky ceramic and stand no taller than a finger near the entrance; farther down, where scale becomes unreliable, those same forms appear as forests of colossal white columns.

These organisms use no liquid chemistry. They drink voltage from the rock.

The mountain’s slow piezoelectric strain passes through veins of quartz, and the plants root directly into those veins with copper-colored tendrils. Their tissues are laminates of carbon, salt, silicon, and flexible crystal. They grow by depositing one molecular layer after another from mineral vapor. There is no sap, no damp soil, no rot. A snapped frond reveals nested glass tubes, all clean and empty.

Their lushness is architectural rather than soft. Every available surface has been colonized. Growths fill the airspace between opposing walls until the shaft is less a void than a solid thicket with innumerable narrow passages through it. Branches interlock without touching, separated everywhere by a hair’s breadth. Root foams pack the crevices. Dust forests crowd dust valleys. Even the old machinery has been furred in crystalline leaves, although none of those leaves have moved since the equilibrium was reached.

Nothing here changes.

That is not merely an impression. The bent fronds do not spring back. Dust hangs where it hangs. A loose screw rests halfway down the shaft without falling, caught at the exact boundary between competing scales of gravity. The mineral vegetation neither grows nor dies, yet remains metabolically poised: charge enters each lattice at precisely the rate charge leaves it. No bud opens. No branch becomes brittle. No particle migrates far enough to alter the whole.

The loudness of the place therefore feels impossible.

A continuous industrial roar fills the chamber. It has no onset, pulse, or modulation. It is as constant as pressure: a broad mechanical chord made of turbines, crushing mills, transformer hum, and the distant hammering of vast pistons. Conversation is possible only mouth to ear. Touching the wall sends the sound through the bones of the hand and jaw.

Near the access door stand the apparent sources: three compact engines bolted into niches, an electrical cabinet with cloudy ceramic switches, and lengths of ribbed conduit descending into the thicket. The engines are old enough for their maker’s marks to have been abraded away. Their flywheels do not turn. Their belts sag motionless. Nevertheless, each machine emits one component of the roar.

Deeper in, other structures appear. A narrow maintenance bridge crosses a gulf that is only thirty centimeters wide and seems bottomless. Tiny derricks stand on ledges below it. There are smokestacks fine as needles, staircases too small for a fingernail, abandoned ore carts no larger than seeds, and factory roofs repeated by the hundreds among the pale trunks. Whether these are models, actual industrial settlements diminished by the chamber, or mineral growths imitating machinery cannot be determined. No inhabitants remain, and there are no bones, droppings, shells, tracks, webs, or nests.

There has never been animal life in the High Orchard. No pollinator is needed. No grazer trims the dense growth. There are no microbes to soften the dead, because nothing dies. The absence is complete enough to become a presence of its own. One hears factories and expects labor; one sees forests and expects movement. Neither arrives.

Light comes from thin amber filaments embedded in the vegetation. They do not flicker. Their glow catches on edges rather than illuminating spaces, so the Orchard reveals itself as layers of outlines: a silver frond, an iron railing, the lip of a precipice, a cable vanishing through trees, another precipice impossibly far beyond. Shadows remain crowded with detail. Looking into them long enough discloses more ridges and more structures, packed behind one another without any final background.

The air smells of hot insulation despite being cold. There is also the clean bite of ozone, powdered stone, and old iron. No breeze can be felt, though the sound suggests a hurricane trapped just beyond every surface.

The chamber obeys several rules.

Distance increases under observation. A ledge approached by hand remains near enough to touch; viewed through a lens, it retreats into mountainous remoteness. Vertical distance is especially unstable. Descent enlarges the world below without appreciably changing the world above. Climbers have lowered kilometers of cable into the shaft and recovered every meter without finding a bottom. The cable returns cool, dry, and densely overgrown with silver fans that are already ancient and have always been there.

Sound has no travel time. A tap made on the lowest reachable railing is heard simultaneously from every factory in the chamber, added to the roar but never altering it.

Damage is permitted, but consequence is not. A branch may be snapped, a railing cut, a mineral shelf chipped away. The fragments remain where they land. On looking aside and back, the damaged thing is neither repaired nor broken: it occupies both histories as overlapping structures, each densely grown over, each apparently weathered for ages. Repeated interference does not transform the Orchard. It only packs more versions into the available space.

For this reason, visitors are warned not to bring elaborate tools. The scattered drills, winches, measuring frames, lamps, and survey stakes visible throughout the lower forests may not have been left by earlier expeditions. Some bear serial numbers belonging to instruments still stored outside the mountain. Others are built to standards no known industry has used. All are old. All are motionless. Most are connected by cables to the three dead engines at the entrance.

At the center of the shaft—or what the eye chooses as its center—rises the Orchard’s dominant feature: a single pale tree rooted on an anvil-shaped summit. It is close enough that its smallest twigs can be counted and far enough that weathered factories cluster like soot around its base. Its crown fills nearly every remaining gap in the chamber. Black roots descend through terraces of salt, wrap around conduits, pierce machine housings, and vanish into the unseen depth.

The tree does not sway. Its leaves do not fall. Its roots do not thicken.

From within its ceramic trunk comes the deepest note in the industrial chord: an immense, unwavering resonance, as if the entire mountain were one engine held forever at operating speed.

Nothing powers it.

Nothing needs to.
