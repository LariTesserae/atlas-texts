High on the sunward face of an ancient plateau, a city lives inside a crack.

From above, there is almost nothing to see. The plateau is a single breadth of ochre stone, rounded by such immense age that it no longer has peaks or sharp escarpments. Wind has combed it flat. The old riverbeds are only pale seams, and the basins that might once have held lakes are floors of salt and powdered gypsum. Under the warm, dim sky, the land appears finished: dry, stable, worn down to its last durable shapes.

The crack is narrow enough to step over in places. A person lying on the stone and looking down would see roofs, ladders, hanging gardens, footbridges, and moving animals far below, compressed into a slit no wider than a village street.

It is called the Close.

The Close descends through many levels of sandstone, though no one living has seen its bottom. Its walls lean together overhead and admit only a muted stripe of amber light. Every ledge is occupied. Houses are pressed into natural hollows or built outward on brackets of thornwood. Their walls are mudless masonry: dry-fitted slabs, bone pegs, braided root, hide, and chalk. Roofs overlap like scales. Stairways double as gutters for grain. Bridges pass through upper rooms. Public squares are scarcely larger than sleeping mats, and families conduct their business vertically—one household above another, one person’s floor serving as another’s ceiling.

Nothing here is wasted on breadth.

The air is warm and densely inhabited. It smells of dust, resin, dung, crushed leaves, hot stone, smoke, and the sour sweetness of fermenting pods. Moisture is too scarce to stand in pools or flow openly. Water, when there is any, exists as breath, blood, sap, and tiny beads of night condensation guarded on waxed cloth. The inhabitants do not speak of wells. They gather dew with combs of bone, but their true wealth comes from plants that do not drink as lowland plants do.

The gardens of the Close are lush in a dry, compact way. Grey-green ropes descend from cracks in the rock, braided around one another until the walls appear furred. Paddle-leaved shrubs grow from baskets of powdered limestone and animal droppings. Their leaves are thick as tongues and sealed beneath a dusty skin. Red bladder-fruits swell directly from their trunks. Thorny vines spread fans toward the narrow light, while pale fungi live deeper down, feeding on dung, shed hair, old baskets, and the soft limestone mortar made by burrowing insects.

These plants collect vapor from passing air. At night their leaves loosen and cool; by dawn, each inner fold holds a trace of dampness. Families squeeze the leaves between carved boards and drink the green, bitter liquid. Cutting a living stem without permission is treated much like breaking into a granary.

Fauna are more abundant than people and less containable.

Small horn-faced lizards crowd every warm surface, blinking through curtains of dust. Fat moths sleep beneath roof lips by day and fill the chasm at dusk, striking walls, lamps, faces, and one another in their urgency. Cliff mice run along cords no thicker than fingers. Beetles churn compost bins. Pale cave swifts nest in black colonies on the deeper walls and erupt upward whenever the heat shifts. Their droppings sustain whole terraces of cultivation.

Larger animals squeeze through the same city. Long-legged browsing goats, narrow enough to turn on a stair, climb between balconies to crop vine tips. Families mark them with cuts in the horn rather than collars, because anything hanging loose catches on the city. Bristle-backed scavengers nose beneath kitchens. Bright little carrion kites hop along railings and wait for butchering. At the highest levels, lean cats lie indistinguishable from sun-warmed stone until the swifts rise.

The city is never quiet. Wings beat in the confined air. Goats knock their horns against walls. Stoneworkers tap wedges into old seams. Hand mills grind seed in a thousand rooms. Traders call upward and downward rather than across. Children warn one another of falling baskets. The wind, forced through the narrowing crack, produces a low organ note that changes with the hour. Beneath all of it is the continual dry whisper of insects eating, nesting, shedding, and moving through the walls.

The Close has no wheels. There is nowhere to roll them. Its machines are levers, slings, counterweights, treadles, and pulleys carved from horn or hardwood. Loads travel vertically in hide nets. Messages are shouted into speaking shafts. Grain is milled with stone. Metal is rare enough that a copper awl may belong to an entire stair-clan, kept wrapped in felt and signed out by witness. Most tools are chipped stone, fired clay, bone, shell brought up from some vanished inland sea, and the glassy black nodules found in the plateau.

Construction proceeds slowly, but the city itself is always changing. Rooms are cut deeper. Old chambers are sealed when the stone becomes unsound. Bridges are moved to follow shade. Gardens migrate toward new cracks. Whole neighborhoods rise or descend over generations according to the behavior of insects, birds, and wind.

The animals set much of the city’s calendar. Mothfall marks the warm season. Swift-rise begins the harvest of bladder-fruit. The passage of migrating cliff antelope across the plateau brings meat, sinew, horn, and danger: sometimes one slips into the crack, smashing roofs and bridges as it falls. Then the city descends upon the carcass in a frenzy of ropes, knives, shouting, dogs, kites, beetles, and competing claims. By the following day almost nothing remains but polished joints built into doorways.

Predation is ordinary here, not hidden at the margins. Cats take mice from sleeping rooms. Swifts devour the moths; children trap the swifts; bristle-backs steal the children’s traps. Tiny red mites drink from goats and are picked off by birds that, in turn, are snared for their feathers. Life circulates rapidly because water cannot. Every body is a temporary reservoir, and death releases what the living need.

There are strict rules governing shade. No one may extend a roof so far that it starves a lower garden. No hearth may burn during the upward migration of moths. No blood may be spilled onto bare stone; it must be caught in dust, fiber, or a waiting root bed. Waste belongs first to the household beneath you. Anything dropped deliberately becomes theirs. Anything dropped accidentally may be reclaimed before the third shout.

The oldest parts of the Close are barely recognizable as construction. Steps have been worn into shallow bowls. Doorposts have rounded beneath uncounted hands. Generations of soot have glazed ceilings black and smooth. Some walls are composed almost entirely of repairs: stone tied to wood, wood replaced by bone, bone encased in mineral crust, all tightened with new fiber. In certain deep passages, the city seems less built than fossilized.

There are carvings far below the inhabited levels—simple animals cut into the rock, many representing species no longer known. Their grooves have softened until they resemble natural veins. The people say these were made when the crack first opened and the plateau still had edges. Whether that means centuries or an age beyond counting does not matter much. The Close measures antiquity by wear: how many feet it takes to hollow a stair, how many nesting seasons to bury a room in guano, how many grandmothers can name the same beam.

At noon, the stripe of sky overhead becomes briefly bright. The upper city whitens in the glare, but below, the light arrives sifted through leaves, bridges, wings, smoke, dust, and woven awnings. It reaches the deepest occupied terraces as a greenish dusk. For a few minutes every plant turns toward it. Lizards flatten themselves to the walls. Moths retreat. The cats open their eyes.

Then the sun passes beyond the slit. Warm shadow rises through the Close, and with it comes the evening’s movement: swifts pouring home, goats descending, cooks striking sparks, traders hauling baskets, insects beginning their vast percussion. The plateau above returns to silence and apparent emptiness.

Only from the crack comes the muffled roar of a whole dry world packed into hiding.
