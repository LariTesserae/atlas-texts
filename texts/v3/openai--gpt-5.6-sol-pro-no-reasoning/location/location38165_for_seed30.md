The place occupies a shallow saddle near the top of a mountain, though the mountain itself is young enough not to have decided on a shape. There are no long ravines, no weathered pillars, no scree polished by centuries. The ground rises in blunt folds of dark mineral matter, glossy in places, granular in others, as if lifted only recently from a furnace and cooled beneath rain. Cracks begin and stop. Channels barely exist. Water has not yet learned where to go.

The saddle is small: a person could walk its visible circumference in an hour, were the ground willing to remain where it was. Low ridges enclose it on three sides. On the fourth, the land ends at a wall of cloud glowing faintly from the warmer country below.

Water is everywhere but rarely gathered. It beads on black stone, trembles in shallow depressions, and emerges from microscopic seams as clear, convex blisters. Rain passes often, not in storms but in warm, abrupt curtains. Afterward every surface weeps. Condensation hangs beneath ledges in strings of heavy drops, each drop swelling for minutes before falling with a resonant metallic note.

The atmosphere is warm despite the elevation. Moist air rises through the saddle continuously, brushing the skin like breath. It carries almost no soil-smell. Instead there is wet iron, hot glass, ozone, and a faint sweetness produced by organisms living in the mineral film. Mist condenses so quickly that moving bodies leave brief hollows behind them: silhouettes in vapor that persist for several seconds after their makers have gone.

Vegetation is sparse and does not form a continuous cover. Pale reedlike stems occur singly along the cracks, each one separated from the next by several body lengths of bare ground. They have no leaves. Their translucent shafts are packed with green motes that climb toward dimness rather than light. During rain, the stems bend until their tips touch stone, completing some chemical circuit; the surrounding rock then warms, and thin roots advance a few millimeters into it.

Elsewhere, rust-red cushions cling to vertical surfaces. These are neither moss nor fungus. They loosen the newly made mineral grain by grain and arrange it into the first true soil. In protected pockets, coin-sized black leaves float on films of water without visible attachment. When disturbed, they contract into beads and roll uphill, drawn toward patches of greater humidity.

The saddle’s dominant feature is a scattered system of structures embedded in the raw geology. At first they look like ruins: twelve narrow arches, several roofless cells, and a leaning column no taller than a tree. Yet none are eroded. Their surfaces are too exact, their edges almost painfully clean. They are unfinished rather than ruined.

They are being built from within.

Dark stone gathers around invisible frameworks. Individual grains lift from the ground, hover, and settle into courses without mortar. Sometimes a wall grows by the width of a fingernail during a single rain. Sometimes it becomes permeable and allows an animal, a gust of mist, or a sheet of falling water to pass through before hardening again. Doorways migrate. Steps appear overnight and lead into blank faces of rock. A chamber that is small from outside may contain a warm descending corridor several kilometers long, although those who turn around within it emerge only a few paces from where they entered.

There are no machines in the familiar sense. The technology is distributed through the place: in the mineral lattice, the droplets, the animals’ shells, perhaps in the geometry of the saddle itself. Under magnification, every wet surface is busy with articulated specks assembling and disassembling molecular structures. They do not merely move matter. They negotiate its possible histories. A fresh wall may display mineral banding associated with ten thousand years of burial, then become chemically newborn when the rain stops.

This is why erosion has barely begun. The landscape is protected not by hardness but by indecision. Wind cannot abrade a surface that revises where it has been. Water scratches tentative paths into the ground, but the channels close behind it or branch backward, producing deltas at their sources. Pebbles are sharp, unrounded, and sometimes still soft. If held in the hand, they pulse as their crystals align.

The fauna is abundant, though much of it is small and perpetually in motion. Cloud-midges fill the warm air in loose columns. Their transparent wings make no buzzing sound individually, but thousands together produce a continuous roar like distant surf. They drink condensation directly from stone. Predatory ribbons—flat, silver animals with six folding legs—run vertically up the mist columns, using differences in air pressure as if they were solid ground.

Shallow pools crowd with copper-backed swimmers. Whenever rain begins, they leap out all at once and cling to the undersides of ledges, leaving the pools empty. This is not to escape the rain. Something large and nearly invisible descends with each shower: broad distortions in the falling drops, moving from basin to basin and grazing on whatever remains in the water. Its passage can be tracked by sudden clearings in the mist and by a slow, concussive clicking beneath the ground.

Among the arches live round-bodied animals called, by the few visitors who have seen them, bell grazers. They stand on three slender legs and carry hollow shells perforated by irregular openings. Wind passing through those shells produces low chords. The animals answer one another across the saddle, changing posture to alter pitch. At dusk their calls overlap with the midges, the dripping ledges, the subsonic clicks of the rain-creature, and the sharp reports of new rock crystallizing. The place is seldom quiet. Even stillness is accompanied by a dense infrastructural hum, felt more strongly in teeth and joints than heard.

There are larger signs as well. Tracks appear in wet mineral flats: five-lobed impressions wide enough to lie down in. They do not progress in a line. One appears near an arch, another on the underside of an overhang, a third halfway up a newly forming wall. Sometimes the prints close like mouths. Small scavengers gather around them to feed on the bright gel that wells briefly from their centers.

The light remains dim through most of the cycle. Clouds seal the saddle from direct sun, and the black ground absorbs what little arrives. Illumination comes diffusely from the mist and, after rainfall, from the water itself. Droplets caught in cracks emit a gray-blue glow proportional not to their volume but to the depth of the stone beneath them. A bead lying over a deep fault can shine brighter than a lantern. Thus hidden structures are mapped at night: buried chambers, narrow shafts, and cavities far below appear as constellations of luminous moisture on the surface.

Those constellations change.

Beneath the saddle, something is expanding the mountain. It works in intervals. First comes a vibration and the bell grazers fall silent. Next, warm water rises from every seam. Then the ground lifts by a few centimeters, not as a single plate but in tessellated patches, each tilting in a different direction. New stone extrudes into the gaps, slick and dark. Arches lean, correct themselves, and continue growing. Pools spill uphill. Animals scatter into the cells.

The event ends with a sound like a vast inhalation.

Afterward the saddle is slightly larger.

Its rules are local and inconsistent beyond its rim. Gravity points mostly downward, except near active walls and during heavy condensation. Distances behave normally in open air but stretch inside completed enclosures. Water remembers the lowest point it has touched and will sometimes return to it against the slope. Living things cast no stable shadows. Construction accelerates in the presence of witnesses, though direct observation prevents any single joint from closing.

For this reason the structures remain partial. Creatures are always looking at them.

Perhaps the system was intended to build a refuge, an instrument, or a gate. Perhaps the saddle is not a construction site but an egg, and the arches are merely the first rigid pieces of an anatomy assembling itself. There are faint marks on one inner wall—parallel incisions too regular to be animal scratches, too shallow to be writing. When rainwater fills them, they produce a moving diagram of the saddle as it may look centuries from now: terraced wetlands, tall resonant towers, dense aerial forests hanging root-down in the cloud.

The image never completes. Tiny swimmers drink the water from the grooves. Mist closes in. A new seam opens underfoot, clear water beads along its edge, and from somewhere inside the unfinished mountain comes the loud, patient labor of a world making room for itself.
