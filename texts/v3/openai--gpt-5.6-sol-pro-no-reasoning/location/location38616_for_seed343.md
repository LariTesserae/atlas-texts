The country begins above the clouds.

Not with snow peaks or knife-edged ridges, but with an immense, weathered tableland lifted so high that the lower world is usually hidden. Its horizon is broad and almost level. Whatever mountains once stood here have been worn down to long-backed swells, shallow basins, and low escarpments whose exposed stone has the dull polish of great age. The land is extreme in elevation but not dramatic in shape. It has survived its own sharpness. Ravines have widened into airy notches; cliffs have slumped into terraces; ancient riverbeds wander across the plateau as chains of pale gravel and dark soil.

At first the tableland appears empty.

The distances are too large for the few upright things in them. A traveler can walk half a day toward a solitary trunk and discover that it is not a tree but the bleached rib of a plant-city, rooted in a hollow several valleys away. Shelters, watch platforms, and cairns stand miles apart. Between them lie open expanses in which nothing rises above the knee.

Yet almost every surface is alive.

The apparent ground is an old vegetal mat hundreds of generations thick: braided roots, corky runners, compressed fronds, fungal felts, and the slowly collapsing bodies of giant colonial plants. Soil exists mostly as something caught inside this fabric. Even the bare-looking flats are covered by a tight skin of silver scales that open after rain. The gravel channels are bound beneath by roots. Brown ledges mistaken for sandstone flex faintly in the heat, and if cut they weep bitter sap.

Plant life here does not crowd upward. Wind, age, and poor moisture have pressed it into the horizontal. Vast cushion organisms spread across whole slopes, their upper surfaces hardened into cracked plates. Low forests travel underground, showing only scattered fans of leaves separated by hundreds of paces. Ribbon plants follow dry watercourses, folding into themselves during clear weather and unfurling green walls when runoff arrives. In protected depressions, immense hollow trunks lie on their sides, still alive, their interiors large enough to hold groves and pools. Roots form arches over eroded gullies and living bridges across open chasms.

The vegetation fills the country without filling its air. It is substrate rather than scenery.

Water is occasional and intensely local. Most days are warm, with dry wind and a clear, ordinary sun. Moisture comes as condensation before dawn, beading on waxed leaves and running through fine grooves into root mouths. Brief rains pass violently across the plateau. From a distance they look like gray pillars walking beneath the clouds. They may drown one basin while leaving the next untouched.

When rain does fall, the ground changes shape. Dormant leaves spring up. Tight seams open into channels. Root cisterns overflow through natural spouts, and water races along courses older than any surviving species. Pools appear in the bowls of giant leaves and in stone-like knots of living wood. Most last only days. A few deep pockets remain through the dry intervals, shaded beneath woven canopies and guarded by thorns, insects, or human walls.

These water pockets are the anchors of nearly everything that moves.

And nearly everything moves.

The emptiness of the plateau is deceptive because its animal life is not evenly distributed. It travels in tremendous aggregations. For hours, a valley may seem vacant except for wind combing the low vegetation. Then the first scouts appear along the horizon: lean, many-legged grazers with bodies narrow enough to slip between leaf plates. Behind them comes a moving front miles wide. Herds pour over the old land in dense streams, stripping seasonal growth, trampling dead tissues into the root mat, and leaving rich black trails that bloom within days.

Above them gather the sailers. These are broad-winged animals that rarely flap, climbing the warm air rising from exposed flats and descending upon the herds in spirals. Some feed on insects stirred from the vegetation. Some take newborn animals. Others carry seeds hooked into the fringes of their wings, and entire plant communities follow their migrations.

Smaller life is more numerous still. Burrowing mouths rasp constantly beneath the ground-skin. Transparent mites occupy the condensation grooves of leaves. Beetle-like pollinators emerge by the millions after rain and form dark columns between distant flowering patches. In the hollow trunks, pale amphibious things crowd around persistent pools, climbing the walls when water rises and sealing themselves into the wood when it vanishes. Predators wait in the same hollows: long-bodied, nearly blind animals that read vibrations through their jaws.

All this abundance produces the defining fact of the plateau: its noise.

There is almost nowhere quiet.

The wind alone is immense. It has thousands of miles of high country in which to gather itself, and it blows across the living ground with a continuous oceanic roar. The plants are shaped to answer it. Dry leaf rims chatter. Hollow stems bellow. Root arches hum like enormous strings. In some regions, whole slopes are composed of flute-grass whose chambers sound at different pressures, making the weather audible as layered, booming chords.

Animals add their own volume. Migrating herds drum through the root mat until shelters shake. Sailers cry overhead in numbers too great to distinguish. Breeding colonies fill cliff hollows with clacking, coughing, and hornlike calls. After rain, the plateau becomes almost unbearable: wings batter the air; frogs boom in flooded trunks; insects pulse in synchronized waves; swollen seedpods rupture with reports like splitting timber.

Sound travels extraordinarily far in the thin, open air. A herd beyond the horizon may be heard before dawn and arrive after noon. People identify distant valleys by their notes. A particular root bridge moans in an east wind; a nesting basin rings at sunset; a deep cistern makes a low thud when full. Silence means something has interrupted the ordinary machinery of life—an approaching storm, a mass predator, a ground collapse—and is treated as a warning.

Human presence is scattered and small. There are no cities, perhaps because the plateau cannot support fixed concentration, or because anything permanent is eventually claimed by the living floor. Settlements consist of a few structures around reliable moisture: low shelters made from shed bark slabs, woven root fiber, hide, and fitted stone. They are tied down rather than raised high. Roofs are curved to shed wind and lined with condensation cloth. Water is stored in cured gourds or in the sealed bladders of local plants.

Metal is rare. Most tools are chipped from the glassy mineral concretions found inside dead roots, or carved from bone, horn, and resin-hardened wood. Long hooked poles harvest water from leaf basins. Broad scraping blades remove edible cambium without killing the parent growth. Travelers wear layered fiber veils against insects and carry sounding rods to test whether the “ground” is a solid root shelf or merely a crust over a hollow.

The oldest structures are simple alignments of standing stones and dead trunks. Some mark safe crossings. Some point toward seasonal water. Others appear to have been erected only to alter the wind. Their drilled holes and carved mouths produce tones that can be heard for miles. These monuments serve as beacons in a country where paths vanish beneath growth and the horizon offers few landmarks.

No one owns land here in any lasting sense. Routes are remembered as sequences of sounds, flowering times, and animal movements. One travels “from the three-voiced arch to the basin where red wings descend,” not north by a fixed road. A path valid in the dry season may become a feeding corridor after rain and therefore impassable beneath migrating bodies. A village may dismantle itself and move when its cistern-plant dies, when a colony nests nearby, or when the supporting root mat begins to split.

Fire is tightly controlled. The vegetation is too continuous, and much of it stores oils against desiccation. An escaped flame could run invisibly beneath the surface and reappear days later over a ridge. Cooking is done in covered pits with heated stones. Light at night comes from jars of luminous beetles or from pale fungal cultures painted onto bark.

The plateau’s great age is visible not as ruin but as accommodation. Erosion and life have reached an ancient compromise. Water cuts channels; roots line them. Wind strips soil; plants become soil. Cliffs collapse; colonies spread over the debris and bind it into terraces. Even bones are rapidly incorporated into the ground. Nothing keeps its edges for long, yet the broad shape of the country scarcely changes.

There are stranger places within it.

Along the western escarpment, enormous root systems have grown out over empty air after the rock beneath them eroded away. They form hanging plains: thin, living shelves suspended across chasms, green on top and tangled beneath. Herds cross them in single file while winged scavengers nest among the dangling roots. The shelves rise and fall slightly in the wind. Those standing on them feel the motion through their feet long before they can see it.

Farther inland lie the Quiet Scars, narrow belts where vegetation abruptly ceases. Exposed material there resembles gray stone but bears repeating cellular patterns. No animal crosses these bands willingly. Wind passes over them without making much sound, and the usual underground rasping disappears. Travelers hurry across, following old marker stakes, disturbed by the sudden presence of their own breathing.

In the east are the rain bowls, shallow basins covered by plants that can invert their leaves. When clouds gather, millions of pale undersides turn upward at once, transforming entire valleys from green-brown to white. Water funnels toward central reservoirs inhabited by dense, furious ecologies. For a few days the bowls become lakes alive with spawning bodies and floating flowers. Then the water is drawn down through the substrate, the leaves reverse, and only damp air and masses of eggs remain.

At evening the normal sunlight reddens across the worn horizon. Warm air begins to fall from the higher flats into the basins. Sailers descend to roost. Herd noise becomes a distant continuous thunder. Condensation starts first beneath shaded leaves, then spreads outward in a glittering film. The living ground tightens as it cools, making soft cracks and deep internal knocks.

From a lone shelter, the plateau seems both vacant and impossibly occupied: an open world with very little standing in it, yet scarcely an inch that is not feeding, breathing, listening, or waiting. Its life has learned not to oppose the ancient emptiness, but to become its surface.
