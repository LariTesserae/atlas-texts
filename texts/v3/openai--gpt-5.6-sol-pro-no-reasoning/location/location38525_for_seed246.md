The city lies high on an old mountain whose summit has been worn almost level.

It is not a ruin. Ruins imply that something was made and then abandoned. Here, streets, courts, towers, gutters, and colonnades are all natural continuations of the stone. No chisel founded them. No hand planned their austere geometry. For ages beyond counting, frost entered hairline fractures; rain ran along mineral seams; roots tightened in cracks; whole strata loosened one grain at a time. Erosion did not reduce the mountain to rubble. It clarified it.

What remains is a small city in final form.

Its boundary is abrupt: a shallow, oval basin no more than a few hundred paces across, enclosed by low walls of banded gray rock. Within stand narrow blocks, terraces, roofless halls, and broad stairs descending nowhere. Some structures are freestanding needles, others long arcades pierced through with door-shaped openings. The tallest reach perhaps thirty meters. Their edges are softened by immeasurable weathering, yet retain a disquieting regularity. Alleys meet at clean angles. Slabs overlap like masonry. Gutters converge beneath plazas and carry water into vaulted darkness.

Seen from the surrounding uplands, the place appears unmistakably urban—and utterly empty.

There are no tools here, no lamps, hinges, tiles, carvings, ash pits, or worked surfaces. Close inspection dissolves architecture into geology. Walls are exposed dikes. Pillars are resistant cores of stone. Stairways are alternating strata. The pavements are beds of slate split flat by winter. Even the windows are places where nodules softer than the surrounding rock have vanished. The city has neither builders nor inhabitants. Architecture is simply the shape into which the mountain has aged.

The air is cold even in daylight. At this elevation, summer is a brief loosening rather than a season. Sunlight arrives clear and ordinary, casting blue shadows through the arcades, but gives little warmth. Frost survives under ledges throughout the year. Pale condensation beads on the shaded walls and gathers at their feet, feeding black moss, miniature ferns, and low cushions of alpine plants. From a distance these growths look like gardens carefully kept in courtyards. Up close they are dense, wet thickets only a hand high.

Vegetation is abundant where moisture permits and absent everywhere else. This makes the city seem simultaneously lush and bare. Empty plazas of silver stone lie beside wells of green so concentrated that every crack overflows. Matted sedges occupy the drainage channels. Red-stemmed moss climbs the lower colonnades. Tiny waxy leaves spread over sheltered terraces, and translucent fungi emerge after rain in crowded tiers, resembling balconies added overnight. Wind-pruned shrubs inhabit three protected courts. Though centuries old, they are scarcely taller than a kneeling person, their trunks braided into dark knots.

Water is never plentiful, but it is always arriving or leaving.

Clouds frequently drag across the summit. They fill the streets without rainfall, reducing towers to nearby silhouettes and clothing every surface in droplets. When the cloud lifts, thousands of beads release at once. Water clicks from sill to sill, races through stone gutters, and falls into fissures below the plazas. There are pockets beneath the city where it remains: cold cisterns carved by dissolution, clear enough to show the pale floors many meters down. During dry intervals these hidden chambers breathe damp air through pavement cracks. In winter their vapor freezes around the openings, raising rings of white crystal like delicate wellheads.

Actual rain comes hard and briefly. The city answers with noise.

It is loud here, although almost nothing lives.

Wind is forced through windows, lanes, shafts, and vaulted passages, and every opening selects its own note. A row of narrow arches produces a deep, fluctuating chord. Chimney-shaped pits howl. Flat slabs knock against their beds. Loose grains hiss along the pavements in silver streams. From underground come booms as trapped air shifts above the cisterns. Water plunging through the drains makes a continuous subterranean applause. On storm days the entire summit sounds inhabited by foundries, organs, crowds, and distant animals.

None of these sounds repeats exactly. The city has too many passages, and the weather is always changing. It can roar under a clear sky and fall briefly quiet in fog. At certain temperatures, ice seals some apertures and opens others; whole districts change voice overnight. Thawing creates sharp reports as frozen seams release. In the coldest months, contracting stone utters slow cracks that travel from wall to wall like signals.

The emptiness between structures matters more than the structures themselves. Courts are broad, alleys long, interiors hollow. A person crossing the basin would spend much of the time exposed, a single warm figure moving through vacant architecture. There is nowhere that feels furnished or intimate. Even the green places are low and inaccessible, packed into crevices, while the great volumes above them remain bare. The city offers countless thresholds but almost no shelter. Doorways open into wind. Rooms have skies for ceilings. Stairs ascend to ledges from which other stairs can be seen, descending across the basin.

Animal life is sparse. White-winged mountain birds sometimes cross overhead but seldom land; the resonant gusts unsettle them. Small dark insects wake among the moss during the short thaw and vanish again by afternoon. There are pale, nearly transparent springtails in the wet cracks. Once or twice each season, a solitary grazing animal may enter through the broken northern wall, nibble the court shrubs, and retreat at the first subterranean boom.

The most enduring fauna are little stone-colored moths that sleep in the deepest arcades. They emerge only when cloud covers the summit. Their wings carry patterns resembling cracks in slate, and in flight they are almost invisible except when moisture catches on them. They gather around no light. Instead, they circle the mouths of sounding shafts, apparently orienting themselves by the low notes. When the wind changes, the whole population lifts at once and moves to another quarter, a faint storm passing through the streets.

There is a further strangeness in the drainage.

Every channel in the city leads inward. Rain falling near the enclosing walls runs toward the central courts, then disappears beneath them. Yet below the summit there are no springs, no wet cliff faces, no streams carrying the collected water down the mountain. The outer slopes are cold and nearly dry. Snowmelt vanishes under the stone and is not seen again.

Nor do the cisterns rise.

They remain at the same dark levels in flood and drought, as though their surfaces mark not quantities but boundaries. Pebbles dropped into them strike water, but produce no returning splash. Moss filaments hanging at the rims incline downward even when strong wind passes overhead. During rare moments of perfect calm, observers might notice dim rings spreading across the pools before any droplets have fallen—ripples beginning at the stone edges and traveling toward a center that does not reflect the sky.

This is the city’s one apparent rule: weather enters, sound multiplies, water descends, and nothing comes back by the same route.

Everything else circulates. Mist condenses and evaporates. Plants green, seed, and withdraw into roots. Frost builds its temporary cornices and breaks them down. Moths move from tower to tower. Wind pours continuously through the hollow blocks. Even the ancient stone remains active, shedding scales, opening seams, advancing by infinitesimal collapses toward some still more exact arrangement.

The place is deeply aged but not dead. Its age is not an accumulation of relics; it is a stripping away. Softness has been removed. Unstable forms have already fallen. What stands has survived so many cycles that change now seems less like destruction than maintenance.

After rain, under ordinary afternoon light, the summit becomes briefly vivid. The walls darken almost to blue. Moss burns emerald in every joint. Water races along the streets, moths shift silently through the arcades, and the stone voices deepen as wet air fills their chambers. The city appears to wake—not because anything enters it, but because all its emptiness begins working at once.

Then the cloud passes. Sun returns. The gutters empty into the center, the walls pale, and the plants contract into their cracks.

The noise continues long after the water is gone.
