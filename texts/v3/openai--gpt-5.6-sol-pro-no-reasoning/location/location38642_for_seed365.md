The town occupies the crown of a young mountain, though *town* is perhaps too generous a word. It is no larger than a walled grove: seven steep lanes, three public cisterns, a score of houses, and a square so narrow that two laden animals cannot cross it without touching. Beyond the final walls, the mountain falls away in abrupt gray facets, still too fresh for scree to have softened them.

From a distance, the settlement resembles a dark growth on pale stone. Its roofs are shingled with bark and overlapping slabs of volcanic glass. Its walls are woven from green wood, plugged with moss, clay, feathers, and the felted hair of small animals. There is no masonry in the usual sense. The mountain offers stone everywhere, but it is difficult stone: sharp, resonant, inclined to split along unexpected seams. The people use it for blades, rain gutters, thresholds, and warding spikes, but rarely stack it above knee height.

Everything taller is grown or lashed.

The elevation makes the air cool even in the warm season. Clouds travel below the town as often as above it. They fill the surrounding chasms with a dim, luminous substance through which distant ridges appear and disappear. Sunlight is seldom direct. It filters through cloud, roof smoke, leaves, and the vast drifting bodies of flying creatures until it reaches the streets in a green-gray dilution.

Rain is infrequent but intimate. Storm fronts often pass beneath the summit, leaving the town dry while lightning moves silently through the cloud floor. Most moisture arrives as condensation. At dusk, the walls bead with water. Roof edges weep. Broad leaves angle themselves toward clay cups set beneath them, and braided cords draw droplets into household jars. On certain mornings every hair, whisker, spider line, and grass awn carries a single clear bead. By noon most of it is gone.

For this reason, the town is lush without being wet.

Plants do not carpet the ground. They occupy it in dense, deliberate islands, separated by bare ledges and swept courtyards. Ferns crowd shaded walls. Waist-high cabbages grow in baskets of imported soil. Pale vines lace the walkways but leave the open stone untouched. Trees are few and immense: perhaps nine within the walls, each planted in a vertical shaft cut down into the mountain’s cracks. Their roots vanish into darkness where hidden moisture collects. Their crowns meet overhead, making the settlement feel roofed even where it is open to the sky.

The largest tree stands in the central square. Its trunk is hollow, and a spiral stair has been pegged into its living interior. Storehouses nest among its upper branches. So do thousands of animals.

Animals are the true population here.

They are everywhere, yet the town is astonishingly quiet.

Small six-legged grazers cling to the walls, cropping moss with slow sideways movements. Mothlike creatures fold themselves beneath eaves in ranks, their wings the color of wet ash. Long-bodied birds hang head downward from branches and open their eyes only during rain. Translucent frogs gather in the mouths of water jars. Furred things no bigger than thumbs sleep in knots of rope, while larger, antlered beasts walk the outer lanes with such caution that their hooves make scarcely a click.

Nothing cries out. Nothing sings in the ordinary way.

The mountain’s predators have no external ears, and many prey animals seem to navigate by tremor, air pressure, warmth, or some shared faculty the townspeople cannot name. Loud sound is therefore rare and dangerous. A falling pot can empty an entire district of wildlife in seconds—and draw pale hunting shapes up from the ravines.

Speech is conducted close to the listener. Doors are padded with moss. Tools are bound in hide. Grain is ground between wooden surfaces rather than stone. Infants wear soft seedpods at their wrists so sudden movements produce a faint rustle, warning nearby sleepers without carrying far. Even the town bell is silent: a suspended wooden disk painted white on one face and black on the other. Turned toward the streets, it announces danger, assembly, rain, or death by pattern and orientation.

The quiet is not stillness. Motion saturates the place.

Every shadow contains feeding, mating, molting, stalking, or migration. Beetles open the evening flowers. Thread-thin snakes enter the beetles through their joints. Flat lizards wait for the snakes. Above them, gliding mammals stretch membranes between four forelimbs and sail from roof to roof, collecting lizards with delicate, almost courteous bites. At intervals, all these movements reverse without obvious cause. Hunters hide. Grazers raise their heads. Wings close. For a few moments the whole town becomes rigid.

Then something enormous passes overhead.

It may be seen only as a darker dimness crossing the cloud, accompanied by a slight downward pressure in the ears. The people do not look up. The animals do not look up. Shadows here are believed to notice attention.

The settlement was built where it is because this mountain summit emerged recently. Within living memory, according to the oldest bark records, the peak thrust through an existing upland forest. The event did not destroy the forest so much as lift portions of it. Patches of ancient soil remain stranded on terraces, carrying mature trees, burrows, fungi, and entire pond communities high above their former range. Elsewhere the rock is newborn: knife-edged, unweathered, streaked red and violet where minerals have met the air for the first time.

Some streets end at these raw seams. Warm breath seeps from them, though the surface air remains cool. In winter, animals gather there by the hundreds, layering themselves along the cracks without fighting. The townspeople also use them as ovens, kilns, and sickrooms. Clay pots are lowered into the deeper fissures on braided cords. By morning they return fired, often in unfamiliar colors. Occasionally they return with small footprints on the inside.

Below the town lies the Hanging Wood, a forest caught on the near-vertical slopes. Its trunks grow outward first and upward later, making elbowed forms from which curtains of moss descend. It looks dense from afar, but within it there are broad intervals of empty air. A traveler moves from one root platform to another over ladders and vine bridges, crossing gaps in which the clouds can be seen far below.

Water gathers in the elbows of the trees. These suspended pools are rarely wider than a basin, yet many contain fish.

No one agrees how the fish arrived. They are black, eyeless, and warm to the touch. During dry weeks they vanish into the wood itself. When condensation returns, they emerge tail-first from pores in the bark. Children collect them in woven cups, but adults release any fish bearing the small white mark that resembles an open door.

The outer wall follows the summit’s natural rim. It is more boundary than fortification: a breast-high weave of thorn, antler, and living willow. Openings are narrow and never directly opposite one another. This is not to hinder invaders. It prevents the wind from learning the town’s layout.

The wind here is considered a kind of animal.

It noses through alleys, worries shutters, lifts loose fibers, and sometimes remains for days in an empty room despite still air outside. People trap it in skin bladders for use in fires and flutes, though instruments may only be played below the cloud line. A bladder filled during a storm can power a small forge-bellows for months. One filled during clear weather is useless, except as bait.

There is little metal. A few copper hooks and needles circulate as heirlooms. Most cutting is done with glassy mountain stone, bone, teeth, or the razor shells of cliff mollusks. Sledges replace wheeled carts. Burdens are carried by people or by low, broad creatures with moss growing naturally across their backs. Construction proceeds by communal leverage: poles, fiber ropes, counterweights, and patient labor. Houses are continually altered as trees thicken, fissures widen, or animals claim portions of them.

Ownership is similarly flexible. A room occupied by a nesting colony for three consecutive nights is considered theirs until they abandon it. A cistern where frogs have spawned may not be drawn below half depth. Roof spaces belong to whoever molts there. Human residents retain only the hearth, sleeping platform, and tools they can name in darkness.

This arrangement is practical. The animals predict nearly everything.

When the transparent frogs climb, condensation will be heavy. When the antlered walkers descend, rockfall is coming. When the wall grazers turn violet, a cold snap approaches. When every animal in town faces inward, the gates are closed and bound, though no one knows what is being kept out—or kept in.

The town has no lamps along its streets. Flame unsettles the aerial creatures, and oil is scarce. Instead, shallow bowls cultivate a blue fungus fed on bark dust and old feathers. Its glow is weaker than moonlight. It shows edges but not colors, hands but not faces. Under it, the settlement becomes a collection of partial things: a ladder ascending nowhere, a rack of antlers, wet leaves, a row of closed shutters, the back of an animal too large for the lane.

At night the silence deepens until tiny sounds acquire shape. Condensation ticks into jars. Claws travel across bark shingles. Roots tighten in the rock with slow wooden groans. Far beneath, stones split as the mountain continues to rise.

The town rises with it.

Every year the central square sits a finger-width higher than the eastern gate. Every few decades a new ledge emerges from the western slope, clean and sharp as a freshly struck blade. The residents carry baskets of soil onto it, plant a tree in the deepest crack, and wait to see which animals arrive first. Only after the first nesting, the first predation, and the first successful collection of dew do they build houses.

Thus the settlement does not expand outward so much as appear in scattered pieces, each separated by ledges, gardens, ladders, and deliberate emptiness. It is urban not because it is large, but because every small space is negotiated—between households, species, roots, weather, stone, and the unseen traffic of the air.

In the cold hour before dawn, cloud often pours upward through the Hanging Wood and spills over the wall. It moves down the lanes like a slow white flood, filling doorways, courtyards, and hollows. Animals emerge into it until the streets are dense with silent life. Their bodies pass close to sleeping houses: fur against woven wall, antler under dripping leaves, folded wings brushing roof edges.

By sunrise the cloud drains away.

It leaves the town glittering with water and covered in tracks—hoofprints, handprints, hooked prints, the fine writing of insects, and sometimes a single continuous groove that enters through a gate, circles the central tree, and ends at a wall without crossing it.

No one erases that mark.

By custom, the square remains empty for the day. Work continues around it. Roofs are repaired, larvae gathered, cisterns strained, bridges tightened. Predators pursue prey through the gardens. New leaves unfold in the cool dimness. Somewhere below, another section of young mountain breaks open without a sound anyone can hear.

And all through the settlement, thousands upon thousands of living things pause, listen to the stone beneath them, and begin moving again.
