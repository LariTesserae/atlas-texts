The basin lies beneath the old surface, though no one here can say how far beneath. Elevators descend for nearly an hour through sleeved shafts, passing numbered strata that are not geological but municipal: freight layers, pressure partitions, abandoned districts, coolant levels. At the bottom, the doors open onto weather.

It is a cold, dim country built entirely by hand.

The sky is a ceiling of ribbed steel, too high for its supports to be visible all at once. Lamps suspended in the trusses make small, jaundiced moons. Their light is caught by condensation and spread into a permanent gray dusk, brightest around the machinery and almost absent in the long spaces between installations. Occasionally, a maintenance flare moves along the ceiling on an overhead rail, briefly revealing pipes as broad as roads and ranks of dripping baffles. Then it passes, and the vault closes again.

Below is the sump plain: several townships of hammered plates, poured ceramics, glass aggregate, grating, and black composite slabs. There is no soil. Where the floor appears muddy, the mud is finely abraded gasket rubber mixed with iron oxide and oily water. Where frost resembles lichen, it is a bloom of salts extruded through weld seams. Every pebble is slag, every cliff a retaining wall, every horizon the side of some machine.

The plain was built recently, or rebuilt recently, and its forms have not yet softened. Drainage channels have sharp corners. Embankments rise in raw, angular lifts, each stamped with fabrication codes. Cut faces show nested honeycombs and bright reinforcement mesh. New causeways cross the low ground on skeletal piers, their bolts still silver. Some terminate abruptly over flooded construction fields, awaiting districts that have not arrived. Orange survey lamps blink at these edges, reflected below in black water.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming abundant. It falls from the ceiling in localized curtains, beads on cables, crawls along rails, and gathers in shallow polygonal pans. Pumps keep it from deepening. The basin’s innumerable drains inhale constantly, drawing down pools until their rims glitter with fresh ice; then a valve cycle begins somewhere, pressure changes, and water rises again through the grates.

After a ceiling purge, rain crosses the district in straight, manufactured bands. One avenue may be hammered by drops while the next remains dry and rimed with frost. Water strikes sheet metal with such force that conversation becomes impossible. It runs along grooves milled into the floor, leaps through clear-walled culverts, and vanishes into turbine mouths screened by rotating combs. For hours afterward, rocklike structures weep: imitation escarpments made to brace the vault, aggregate cliffs around substations, huge banks of sintered ash. Moisture works out through their pores and freezes in pale quills.

The cold comes chiefly from the machinery. Refrigeration towers stand at wide intervals across the plain, isolated like monuments. Their fans turn behind armored louvers, pulling warmth out of buried conduits and casting plumes of white vapor into the low air. Around each tower, railings and ladders grow thick sleeves of ice. The temperature is not uniform. There are warm strips above power trunks where water steams, and bitter depressions where dense air collects ankle-deep. Walking across the basin means passing repeatedly through invisible boundaries: breath fogs, clears, and fogs again; wet fabric stiffens; exposed metal burns the skin.

Despite the engineered rain, vegetation is sparse. Narrow beds were installed along certain service routes, recessed into steel troughs and filled with manufactured substrate. Most are empty now. The survivors are dark sedges with translucent blades, low mats of moss beneath leaking pipes, and colorless fungal fans feeding on cable insulation. Tiny horsetail-like plants grow around inspection hatches, warmed from below by current. They bend whenever a train passes beneath them.

Farther out, there are forests only by courtesy: widely spaced carbon scrubbers clad in porous green panels. Condensation streaks their sides, and algae have colonized the wettest faces, giving the towers a bruised emerald tint. From a distance they might be enormous tree trunks. Up close, each one ticks with relays and exhales air that smells faintly of iodine.

Fauna appears mostly as evidence. Tracks cross the silt inside drain mouths. Something small chews the soft seals around warm junction boxes. Pale moths sometimes gather around ultraviolet fault lamps, but they are seen one at a time, their wings oddly heavy in the damp air. There are translucent shrimp in some of the catch basins, descended perhaps from organisms introduced to clean the water. During pump reversals they tumble across the grates like fragments of glass.

The largest animals are the duct grazers: low, six-legged maintenance machines whose articulated mouths strip algae and ice from ventilation intakes. Their soft tires leave paired trails through the condensation. They move without drivers, pausing beneath signal masts as if listening. When two meet, they exchange bursts of coded noise and turn aside with ponderous courtesy. From afar, in the dusk, their work lights resemble eyes close to the ground.

The basin is loud even in its emptiest places. It has the spacious loudness of an ocean contained in metal. Fans beat slowly overhead. Pumps throb below the floor. Freight lines shriek around distant curves. Expansion joints crack without warning as whole districts contract in the cold. Water adds countless smaller voices: drumming on housings, hissing on hot conduits, ringing from cable to cable, gargling in sumps.

At regular intervals a deeper sound passes through everything—a subsonic starting pulse from the foundation engines. Pools corrugate. Frost sifts from handrails. The ribs of the ceiling answer one after another, so that the vault seems to possess an enormous spine. A few seconds later, machinery across the plain awakens in sequence. Gantries roll. Sluice gates lift. Crane heads rotate toward unfinished embankments. Driverless carriers emerge from tunnels with loads of fresh plate, precast channels, pipe segments, and sealed tanks of aggregate slurry.

The landscape is still being made.

Its weather and construction are parts of the same process. Rain cools new castings. Runoff sorts industrial fines into settling chambers. Frost tests welds, opening weak seams so repair systems can find them. Each thaw carries traces of the basin through sensors that analyze corrosion, leakage, microbial growth, and wear. Nothing here erodes accidentally for long. Damage is observed, classified, sometimes repaired—and sometimes encouraged.

Along the eastern works, deliberate erosion fields spread for kilometers. There, jets of abrasive water cut young artificial ravines into blocks of sacrificial material. The channels are bright-edged and raw, with no sediment except what has been produced that hour. Robotic excavators redirect the flow, studying how it divides, undercuts, and braids. The resulting forms are used elsewhere in the basin to improve drainage. Thus the place teaches itself how to become a landscape by destroying small versions of itself.

Human habitation is scattered. Pressurized shelters stand on pylons above the wet floor, each connected to the next by long illuminated walks. Their windows are narrow and warm, but often dark. Crews travel in sealed trams or in hooded maintenance carts, emerging only in insulated suits. They mark faults with luminous paint, kneel to listen to bearings, lower instruments into water, and disappear again. Their voices, carried over radio, are clipped and distorted by echoes.

No settlement has a conventional center. The nearest equivalent is the manifold district, where hundreds of pipelines converge around a circular reservoir. The reservoir is roofless beneath the greater roof. Its water rotates slowly, black and perfectly organized, while ice forms along the outer rim and is broken away by mechanical hammers. Around it stand workshops, dormitories, a clinic, pressure kitchens, and a silent hall containing maps of the levels above. Elevated walkways radiate from the circle, making it look from the ceiling cameras like a wheel submerged in fog.

There are rules here, some written and some learned from injury.

Never step onto a dry plate in a wet district; dryness means heat below, electrical leakage, or a recent chemical wash.

Never follow a line of lights unless they blink in threes. Continuous lights belong to machines and may lead into moving assemblies.

When the warning horns sound once, seek a railing. Twice, climb. Three times, remove anything magnetic and lie flat.

Do not break clear ice. It often caps a sensor well, and the systems beneath interpret intrusion as environmental change.

If rain begins to rise from the floor rather than fall from the ceiling, leave the sector immediately.

That last event is uncommon but not unknown. Under certain pressure failures, water is drawn upward in beads and threads. It climbs pillars, lifts from pools, and hangs shivering in the air. The lamps turn each droplet into a dull point of light. For several minutes the plain seems filled with a reversed storm, all its moisture migrating toward unseen machinery in the vault. Then gravity resumes, or is permitted to resume, and the suspended water comes down at once.

Beyond the active districts, the basin opens into unfinished reaches. Lamps are fewer there. Rails vanish beneath shallow flooding. Half-built towers stand on broad foundations, their interiors exposed, each floor edged in frost. Wind from ventilation tunnels travels unobstructed across kilometers of plate and produces low organ tones in empty conduits. There are no plants, almost no animal traces, only construction beacons and the slow sweep of inspection lights.

Yet these regions do not feel abandoned. Survey drones pass overhead. Buried cutters shift beneath the floor. New walls rise inside curtains of steam, assembled by cranes whose operators may be hundreds of levels away. By the next maintenance cycle, a familiar route may have become a canal, a refrigeration field, or a sealed district with no doors.

The basin has no true bedrock and no wilderness against which its artifice can be measured. Dig beneath a marsh pan and one finds mesh, conduits, old decks, pressure foam, another ceiling, perhaps another rain system. The marsh is a machine resting on a machine resting on a machine. Its cold is manufactured; its water is circulated; its sparse ecology survives in tolerances and oversights. Even emptiness has been budgeted into the plan as clearance, reserve capacity, flood volume, or room for structures not yet designed.

Still, from a high service bridge, the distinction can become difficult to care about. Fog drifts over the black pans. Sedge beds whisper in the fan-wind. Distant towers emerge and fade like mesas in winter rain. Water searches for lower places with an insistence older than engineering, though every lower place was chosen for it. Ice widens a seam by a fraction. Moss darkens a bolt. A solitary moth crosses a field of yellow light.

Far below, pumps answer one another, and the made earth goes on becoming.
