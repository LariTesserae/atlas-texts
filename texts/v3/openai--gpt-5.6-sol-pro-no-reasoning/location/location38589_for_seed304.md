The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a few fields. It is low enough that water is always arriving: through the pale clay beneath the floors, down the surrounding rock shelves, out of seams in the walls. No river crosses the basin. Instead, the whole place behaves like the edge of a marsh, though scarcely any marsh plants grow here. Rain collects in footprints and mortar joints. Wells fill almost to their rims. On cool mornings, beads of condensation form beneath lintels and along the undersides of bridges, falling at intervals into the lanes.

There is very little open ground.

Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder, packed in irregular rings around several dark pools. They are made from freshly broken stone—blue-gray, angular, still showing bright scars where it was split—and from bricks of compressed silt reinforced with rush fiber. Roofs overlap like low shelves. One roof drains onto another, and then another, until the runoff descends through the town in a sequence of carved troughs, stone lips, hollowed logs, and hanging chains of fired clay cups.

Nothing is level. The basin floor is still settling, and every year some quarter sinks by the width of a finger. Doorways lean. Courtyards become cisterns. Steps vanish beneath new paving. The inhabitants respond not by demolishing but by adding: a raised threshold, a second floor laid over the first, a narrow plank path through a room that has become a channel. Thus the town is densely layered despite its modest size. Beneath many homes lie older homes, intact except for mud and water, their ceilings now serving as foundations.

The streets are scarcely streets at all. They are slots between walls, often only broad enough for one person carrying a basket sideways. Their paving stones are sharp-edged and recent, not yet rounded by generations of feet. Water travels beneath them with a faint, continuous whisper. In some places it wells up between the joints, making the stones tremble. People mark these living springs with circles of white chalk and do not build directly over them. A spring may migrate overnight; a chalk circle found dry in the morning is considered a small death.

Beyond the outermost houses, the land rises only slightly before ending in a broken rim of young rock. The rim is not mountainous. It is a low, jagged collar, perhaps twice the height of a house, thrust recently from the clay by slow pressures below. Rain has only begun to work upon it. Its corners remain keen, its gullies shallow and undecided. Water seeps through hairline fractures and hangs in clear drops along the black faces. Thin curtains of moisture darken the stone without yet carving it smooth.

Vegetation is sparse, but the town is not empty of green. Small mats of liverwort cling wherever roofs remain damp. Pale rushes grow in isolated knots around the pools, each knot carefully protected by woven fences because rush fiber is needed for brick, rope, sandals, strainers, and fish traps. A low red moss appears on new cracks in the basin rim. It is the first colonist of exposed stone and, according to local belief, remembers the pressure that raised it. When crushed into water, it stains the liquid rust-red, though there is little metal here and none worked in abundance.

Wood is rarer than stone. Beams are reused until they become black, polished, and oddly shaped by successive buildings. A single straight pole may pass through five roofs, each belonging to a different household. Ownership follows use rather than boundaries: one family owns the lower span, another the portion above the third floor, while the section exposed to rain belongs to whoever maintains its clay cap.

The town’s tools are simple. Stone adzes, shell scrapers, bone needles, and a few treasured copper blades do most of the work. Pottery is everywhere. Because there is so little space, pots are fitted into walls, stacked beneath stairs, suspended from rafters, and buried under floors. The dense architecture has the appearance of a reef constructed from jars, bricks, channels, ladders, mats, and human habit. Every surface has a second purpose. Benches cover drains. Stairs are also spillways. Exterior walls contain nesting hollows for water vessels. Flat roofs serve as workshops in dry weather and settling ponds after rain.

The largest pools are nearly round and unnaturally deep. Their surfaces lie still even when rain strikes nearby water. No one knows their bottoms. Soundings return inconsistent depths, and weighted cords sometimes come back tied into loose knots. These pools are the town’s chief strangeness and the reason it exists.

Water drawn from them is cold, clear, and faintly luminous only when carried into darkness. It is not considered safe to drink until it has spent one night in an unglazed vessel. During that night, moisture gathers on the outside of the pot while the water level within falls slightly. In the morning, a thin skin remains inside the rim, patterned with lines resembling streets. Potters study these markings before choosing where to repair walls or dig new drains. Often the patterns are accurate. Sometimes they include alleys and rooms that do not yet exist.

This has produced the town’s principal rule: no construction begins without water having imagined it first.

The rule is followed pragmatically rather than reverently. Builders place several pots in different locations, compare the mineral tracings, argue, and then lay foundations where the patterns agree. Since the town is crowded and every useful site has already been occupied many times, new construction usually means inserting a wall through an old chamber, extending a platform above a pool, or tunneling carefully into waterlogged foundations. Building is constant. So is shoring, draining, patching, and lifting. The settlement is never finished long enough to become old.

Its atmosphere is cool and close. Even at midday the air carries a post-rain chill. Light enters plainly from the pale sky but breaks quickly against walls, awnings, roof edges, and suspended jars. Upper levels are silver-gray; lower passages are dim and greenish from reflected water. There is little smoke, because fuel is scarce. Cooking is done in shared ovens lined with hot stone, and their warmth draws people into tight evening gatherings.

Despite the density of structures, the town is mostly quiet. Water supplies the background: dripping from chains, ticking into jars, slipping under paving, occasionally pouring with sudden force when a roof gate is opened. Footsteps are cautious on wet stone. Voices remain low because walls transmit them readily. A conversation in one room may travel through a drain and emerge, distorted but intelligible, three lanes away.

Hammering is rare and conspicuous. Most construction sounds are dull and intimate—the scrape of stone against clay, the slap of wet brick into place, the creak of rope, the rasp of a shell tool dressing a timber. During heavy rain, however, the whole town becomes an instrument. Every gutter finds its pitch. Ceramic mouths whistle. Hollow walls thrum. The pools answer with deep, isolated knocks from somewhere below.

Animals are uncommon. A few slate-colored frogs inhabit the warmest drains, but they call only once or twice each season. Small blind eels occasionally emerge from wells after storms; they are returned to the water immediately, as their arrival is thought to warn of subsidence. Birds rarely land because there are few trees, little seed, and nowhere undisturbed to nest. Now and then a long-legged marsh bird stands upon the basin rim, surveys the crowded roofs, and departs.

The most numerous living things, aside from people, are tiny translucent snails that graze on the damp undersides of pottery. Their shells are nearly square. Potters tolerate them because they leave clean trails through mold, and builders watch their migrations. When the snails climb, lower rooms are likely to flood. When they seal themselves into cracks, cold dry weather is coming. When they gather around a chalked spring, the spring will soon move.

Life in the town follows water rather than sun. After rain, everyone works: clearing channels, setting jars, checking foundations, harvesting the brief flush of moss and rush shoots. As the basin drains, potters take over the exposed mud shelves. During dry intervals, builders descend into the lower layers to recover bricks and timber. Then condensation thickens, the wells rise, and the buried rooms close again.

There are no gates in the conventional sense. The jagged basin rim and the crowded outer buildings define the town’s boundary. Entry is through six narrow cuts in the rock, each crossed by a shallow channel flowing inward. Visitors wash their feet there, not for cleanliness but to introduce their weight gradually. The basin is believed to notice pressure. A person who enters too quickly may cause doors elsewhere to stick or a shelf of clay to collapse.

At the center, where the roofs almost meet above the darkest pool, stands no palace or temple. There is only a circular platform of fitted stones, renewed every few months as it settles. Here the town keeps its longest cords, plumb weights, water clocks, and memory pots. The platform is always occupied by someone measuring: the rise of a pool, the angle of a wall, the interval between subterranean knocks, the widening of a fracture on the northern rim.

Nothing measured remains constant.

The landscape is young, but the settlement has learned to live as though permanence were a dangerous superstition. Walls are respected because they will fail. Water is cherished because it will move. Space belongs provisionally to whoever can keep it habitable. The town’s density is not an attempt to conquer the basin; it is a way of sharing the few surfaces that, for this season, have agreed to hold.
