At an elevation where ordinary mountains should already have ended, a broad shelf runs between black peaks. It is neither summit nor valley. The surrounding ridges rise from it in slow, eroded walls, and beyond them are further walls, dimmer and apparently no lower, until distance loses the power to make anything small.

The shelf is known, if anything can be said to know it, by its emptiness.

Its ground is pale stone worn smooth without being rounded. Long shallow channels cross it, braided like the beds of vanished rivers, yet they contain no gravel, no debris, no sign that anything was carried away. Moisture lies in them intermittently: dark films of water, strings of motionless pools, condensation gathered beneath overhangs. The pools are only a finger deep, but their reflections descend farther than sight. Looking into one reveals no bottom and no face—only another cold plateau under another low sky.

Rain is always present over some part of the shelf, though it cannot be seen approaching or departing. Beneath one cloud, drops hang in the air in vertical ranks. Under another, rain rises quietly from the stone and disappears into the gray. Elsewhere the ground is dry and powdered with salt, as though no water has touched it for centuries. These districts keep their boundaries. The wet stone never spreads its dampness into the dry; the dry stone never drinks from the neighboring pools.

The cold is complete but not violent. Nothing freezes further. Thin ice clings to the shaded sides of rocks, old and cloudy, with dust embedded in it at depths that suggest many winters. Yet no season follows another here. Breath would plume and remain, a white shape tethered before the mouth, neither dispersing nor falling. Frost traces every crack with fine branching marks, and each branch ends abruptly at the same invisible limit.

There is little vegetation. In protected fissures grow mats of black lichen so flat that they seem painted onto the rock. They have no soft edge and no visible growth. Their circular colonies overlap like diagrams of eclipses. Along three of the water channels stand low, jointed stalks, colorless except at their tips, where they carry hard blue bladders full of condensed water. The stalks do not bend. If broken, they are found intact again when looked at from another angle.

Nothing here moves much, but the place is loud.

A continuous roar fills the shelf. It resembles a great river in flood, close enough to shake the ribs, although the channels hold only their thin skins of water. Sometimes it resembles avalanches descending all the surrounding mountains at once. The slopes remain still. No snow billows. No rock falls. The sound has no direction and casts a kind of pressure; in narrow clefts it becomes louder rather than softer, until the stone itself seems to ring.

Within the roar are recurring details. A crack like lake ice breaking. A deep impact, followed by a long stony roll. The abrupt wingbeat of something immense passing just overhead. These sounds repeat exactly. They do not echo after one another so much as coexist, each permanently at the same point in its own event. The avalanche is always beginning. The ice is always at the instant of fracture. The unseen wings are always completing a single downward stroke.

The shelf’s most prominent landmarks are upright slabs of the same pale stone as the ground. They stand far apart, sometimes days of walking between them, though distance behaves poorly here. Each slab is roughly the size of a doorway, weathered through with holes, and inset into a low ring of fitted rocks. They suggest construction but not technology: no tool marks, no mortar, no hinges, no inscriptions. Perhaps they are exposed ribs of the mountain, and the rings have assembled naturally around them. Perhaps they are shelters reduced until only the idea of an entrance remains.

Passing through one does not lead to the opposite side.

It leads onto the same shelf, under the same low light, with the same black peak fixed to the north—but the traveler’s footprints may now be visible far ahead, already impressed in the damp mineral film. Approaching them does not make them nearer. Turning back reveals the doorway standing alone at the horizon, though it had been within reach a moment before.

There is no sun. The sky is a uniform lid of blue-gray cloud with a weak illumination behind it. Shadows exist, but they do not agree with one another. Every slab casts its shadow eastward. Every plant casts its shadow westward. Animals, when they appear, cast shadows directly beneath themselves, elongated downward through the stone.

The fauna are few and widely separated. White insects live under flakes of shale beside the pools. They have no apparent legs and leave paired trails in the condensation. Small, heavy-bodied birds stand along the channel edges, feathered in gray and rust, their beaks tucked down. They do not feed or call. Their feathers tremble in the roar, but the birds themselves remain fixed, even when approached. One may blink; a moment later there will be no bird, only two precise footprints filled with water.

Higher on the slopes, at the limits of visibility, solitary horned shapes occupy ledges. They are never seen climbing or descending. Each appears as old and settled as the ledge beneath it. Their horns repeat the curve of the surrounding ridgelines. At times an observer realizes that one supposed animal is a boulder. At other times an entire boulder field seems to be watching.

The air is thin, but altitude is not measured here by ascent. Walking downhill brings shortness of breath. Climbing may thicken the air and deepen the pools. The highest points sometimes lie physically below the lowest, visible through cracks in the plateau as inverted summits suspended in darkness. Snow rests on their undersides.

Erosion has finished its work without destroying anything. The mountains are deeply cut and visibly ancient, their faces grooved by rivers and glaciers absent from the present world. Talus fans spread below the cliffs in perfect, stable angles. Every fallen stone has found its final place. Some balance on corners so narrow that balance should be impossible, but they cannot be pushed over. They have already fallen; this is where their falling ends.

Even damage is part of the equilibrium. A fracture opened in a slab is always opening and always healed. Water drops strike a pool and hold their crown-shaped splashes. Along one escarpment, a landslide occupies the air: thousands of rocks hanging between cliff and slope, motionless individually, while their collective roar never ceases. Mist threads among them. From below, the suspended stones form the shape of a vast recumbent body. From above, they spell nothing recognizable.

There are scattered remnants that resemble habitation. A roofless enclosure no larger than a shepherd’s hut. Three walls fitted from dark blocks, the fourth absent. A circular depression lined with smooth stones and filled not with water but with cold, dim sky. Low cairns at irregular intervals. None contains ash, bone, pottery, cord, metal, or any other proof of hands. They may be geological coincidences. Yet each enclosure is oriented toward the same point on the plateau: a shallow basin near its center where the roar is strongest.

The basin holds the landscape’s only substantial body of water, though it is not large. It is an oval tarn perhaps a kilometer across, dark as basalt, enclosed by bare shores of salt and frost. No stream feeds it. Its surface is perfectly level and perfectly still, despite the noise that seems to pour from it.

On the far shore, the mountains are reflected with impossible clarity. The reflected peaks are taller, sharper, and crowded with sparse forests absent from the slopes above. Their trees are black needles against a bright sky. Tiny animals move among them. Clouds race. Snow falls. Rivers shine and visibly descend. Everything missing from the shelf—weather, growth, motion, abundance—continues in the reflection.

But the reflected world makes no sound. All of its sound is here instead.

The shelf bears the thunder of those waterfalls, the splitting of those glaciers, the wind through those forests, the cries of their unseen creatures. The stationary land surrounding the tarn is burdened with another land’s motion. This is why the air trembles while nothing stirs.

At intervals along the shore, shallow footprints enter the tarn. They belong to birds, horned animals, and other shapes that have no counterparts on the plateau. None come out. The water remains ankle-deep at the edge, then becomes optically bottomless a pace farther in. Its cold does not increase. Its surface does not break.

The place has rules, though they are rules of preservation rather than sequence. Nothing begins here, and nothing quite ends. Moisture condenses but does not evaporate. Rain occupies the act of falling without arriving. Creatures appear only in postures they can hold forever. Rockfalls retain collapse without accepting change. Sound is permitted motion; reflection is permitted time.

Beyond the tarn, the broad shelf narrows into passes between the peaks. They seem to promise descent. Every pass reveals another section of plateau, another doorway-slab, another motionless rainstorm, another small roofless enclosure. There may be many such shelves stacked through the mountain, or only one folded repeatedly through itself.

The weak light never fades, but the eye tires and calls it evening.

In that invented dusk, the frost patterns on the stone become visible as a single connected design. They run from crack to crack, pool to pool, doorway to doorway, over miles of empty ground. Seen from no possible vantage, they might form the drainage map of the moving country in the tarn.

The map does not change.

The country it maps never stops changing.

Between them, under the immeasurable noise, the cold plateau waits at an altitude that has no world beneath it.
