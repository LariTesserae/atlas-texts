The land is low, warm, and almost entirely hidden beneath its own life.

From above—if anything could remain above it long enough to look—the region would resemble a dark green flood spreading across recently cooled earth. There are no mountains, only shallow upheavals: black ridges thrust through the canopy like the backs of buried animals, their stone still angular and glassy, unsoftened by centuries. Between them lie broad depressions filled not with lakes but with vegetation so dense that its surface rises and falls like a second terrain. Water appears in scattered pockets: rain basins, warm channels, flooded hollows, and beads of condensation collected in leaves large enough to drown in. No continuous river crosses the region. Instead, water migrates through it in pulses.

The darkness is not nocturnal. Day exists somewhere overhead, but reaches the ground only as a diluted green-brown murk.

The upper canopy is made of fan-crowned trees standing close enough for their crowns to collide. Their trunks are pale and smooth, swollen with stored water, and threaded by red breathing pores. Between them grow thinner trees whose jointed branches continually rearrange themselves toward whatever light briefly opens. Vines travel across the crowns in heavy cables. Some are woody and thorned; others are translucent tubes through which fluid and small swimming organisms can be seen passing. Epiphytes weigh every limb. Broad leaves overlap in tiers, sweating steadily from their tips.

When the wind begins, this immense roof does not sway as one. It breaks into contrary currents. One grove bows east, another west. Vines tighten and snap. Crowns grind together with a noise like wet timber being split, and showers of insects, seeds, bark plates, nests, and small animals fall through the lower layers.

Nothing stays open for long.

A fallen giant may tear a bright clearing into existence, exposing fresh volcanic soil and shards of young stone. Within minutes, winged creatures fill the light. Within hours, pale shoots rise from ruptured root mats. By the following warm cycle, creepers have crossed the trunk; soon afterward the clearing is roofed by leaves again. The forest is lush not because it is stable, but because it is constantly being demolished and remade.

At ground level, the terrain is a cramped labyrinth.

Roots stand above the soil in sharp fins. Fungi swell between them in stacked shelves and bladder-like towers. Fallen leaves do not lie flat: they catch on tendrils, horns, shells, and each other, making deep suspended drifts beneath which the smaller fauna travel. The true earth is rarely visible. Where it does appear, it is dark, hot, and newly laid, a mixture of ash, spores, droppings, shredded plant tissue, and mineral grit. In places, the crust is only a handspan thick over warm cavities. A heavy foot can puncture it and release humid breath smelling of iron and overripe fruit.

The youngest ridges are naked by comparison. Their black rock forms abrupt blades and terraces, still sharp enough to cut skin. Plants have begun their occupation in colored bands: first silver films, then orange crusts, then tufts of violet moss, followed by rooting bulbs that wedge apart the cracks. Colonies of small six-legged grazers follow each stage. Behind them come the hunters. Behind the hunters come scavengers, parasites, seed-bearers, and things whose role changes according to what has just died.

The region is deafening.

Its sound is not a chorus with pauses, but a pressure. Billions of wings produce a continuous, granular roar. Burrowing animals knock on root walls. Tree crowns creak and strike. Amphibious creatures bellow from water pockets no wider than a footprint. High in the canopy, unseen bodies make explosive calls that pass from grove to grove, each answer slightly altered until the original signal becomes a rolling storm of sound.

At irregular intervals, all this is overwhelmed by the migrations.

They begin without warning. The undergrowth starts to tremble, then streams of animals pour through it: velvet-backed grazers no larger than fists; long-bodied browsers moving on dozens of delicate legs; brilliant, hard-shelled things that climb every vertical surface rather than go around it. Predators run among them openly. Some seize prey; others merely keep pace, waiting for exhaustion. Trees shed bitter sap as the animals pass. Flowers open in response to their breath. Parasites cast threads into the moving mass. For hours the forest floor appears to flow in one direction, and then the current disperses, leaving bones polished clean, stripped stems, and millions of eggs pressed into mud.

Above, aerial life is even denser. Wings brush leaves at every height. There are gliders with transparent membranes, darting needle-beaks, floating gas-bellied filter feeders, and dark ribbons composed of tiny animals linked head to tail. Some airborne colonies are so broad that they temporarily deepen the already heavy darkness. Their passage makes the canopy hiss.

Rain comes violently but briefly. The first drops are huge and warm, battering the crown leaves like thrown stones. Then water descends everywhere at once. It runs down trunks, fills plant cups, floods burrows, and turns dry channels into churning brown streams. The forest responds immediately: sealed blossoms unfold; dormant animals break through clay skins; translucent larvae rise from the ground on threads of mucus. Every pool becomes crowded within minutes.

But the rain rarely lasts. The temporary streams divide, vanish into root beds, or drain into deep fissures in the young rock. Pools shrink. Mud skins over. Water remains trapped above ground in living containers—in stems, folded leaves, spongewood, animal bladders, and hanging gardens of moss. Thus the place is damp without being truly wet. Moisture is abundant but jealously partitioned, carried in bodies and released by rupture.

Condensation is more dependable than rainfall. At the coolest part of the cycle, warm vapor rises from fissures and settles beneath the canopy. Leaves gleam. Droplets gather on fur, shell, and web. Some creatures stand motionless with comb-like spines raised, harvesting mist. Others follow them and lick the water away. By the time the unseen sun has warmed the upper forest, nearly every bead has been drunk.

There are no paths, no walls, no dwellings, no abandoned foundations. Nothing in the landscape suggests that it has ever been cleared or measured. Yet tool use exists here—not as a civilization laid over the land, but as another ecological habit.

In the dim lower forest, small upright foragers move in temporary bands. They build no lasting shelters. They carry flakes of fresh black stone struck from the volcanic ridges, use them until the edges dull, and leave them where they fall. Even these discarded blades do not resemble artifacts for long: mineral-eating films cloud their surfaces, insects lay eggs along the fracture lines, and roots lift or bury them.

The foragers split fruit husks, scrape fungus from bark, and cut openings in water-storing plants. They know which hollows will flood and which trees shed lethal branches before a storm. Their tools are simple because possession is difficult here. Anything set down is buried, stolen, eaten, overgrown, or carried away in the next migration. Knowledge survives better than objects.

Even fire is transient. Damp growth smothers ordinary flame, but the youngest stone sometimes vents hot gases that ignite dry canopy debris. Then a narrow fire runs briefly through one layer of the forest while rain-soaked layers above and below remain alive. Animals do not always flee these burns. Some follow at the edge, feeding on exposed grubs. Certain trees burst their seed pods only in the heat. Green shoots may appear through warm ash before the last embers have gone dark.

The strangest feature of the region is its speed of response. It can feel as though the landscape anticipates disturbance.

Cut a vine, and nearby leaves contract before the blade reaches them. Stand too long beside a pool, and reeds lean inward to collect the salts of sweat and breath. A loud call reshapes the local soundscape: insects alter their rhythm, predators change elevation, blossoms close, and distant colonies reply with imperfect copies. The forest does not possess a single mind, but information travels through it so quickly—by vibration, scent, root chemistry, spores, alarm light, and imitation—that intention is difficult to distinguish from reaction.

There are places where this responsiveness becomes dangerous. In certain depressions, every organism seems linked to the next. Touching one pale stalk can make an entire grove fold down. This exposes warm ground, which draws feeding swarms, which attract hunters, whose movement ruptures water bladders in the trees, causing a sudden local flood. The flood wakes buried eggs. Their hatchlings strip the folded grove. New fungi rise on the remains. By the next cycle, the depression bears no resemblance to what it was.

The land’s few open spaces are therefore events rather than locations.

A basin may exist for years beneath a woven mat of plants, then collapse into a steaming pond when the substrate gives way. The pond fills with animals, turns opaque with spawning, and is drained overnight by a fracture in the rock. A flat of glittering mud remains. Within days it becomes a meadow of knee-high fronds. Herds arrive, graze it to bare soil, and depart. Thorn scrub follows. Then a canopy closes over the entire sequence, leaving only a faint change in the pitch of the insect roar.

Distance is hard to judge. Visibility ends after a few trunks, but sounds suggest a world extending far beyond sight. Deep bellows roll across unseen valleys. Storms can be heard advancing for hours. At times, the canopy ahead brightens with sheets of pale flicker—not lightning from the sky, but synchronized displays traveling through airborne colonies. The signal crosses miles of forest in waves, revealing for an instant the size of the living plain.

There is no silence here, except one.

Very rarely, the calls cease in widening rings. Wingbeats settle. Even dripping seems to stop. The darkness acquires depth, and the forest holds itself rigid. No single creature knows why; each is answering the stillness of its neighbor.

Then something vast passes beneath the root mat.

The ground rises gently, one long swell moving between the young stone ridges. Trees tilt and straighten. Pools spill from their hollows. Burrows collapse. The swell travels onward through the forest until it is lost in distance.

Behind it, the noise resumes with redoubled force. Animals pour into the newly broken soil. Roots enter fresh cracks. Water gathers in unfamiliar places. The terrain has changed again—not much, but enough—and every living thing begins at once to use the difference.
