The place is hidden in the summit of a mountain.

Not beneath it, exactly. Excavation finds only black schist, pale seams of salt, and dry cavities narrowed by ancient pressure. Nor is it visible from the slopes. It occupies a flaw in the mountain’s altitude: a minute fold where two definitions of “high” overlap. Crossing into it requires no door. At a particular notch near the summit, if one steps uphill while facing down, the next footfall lands elsewhere.

Inside, the landscape is scarcely larger than a room, though its horizons suggest otherwise.

A shallow basin extends under a dim, depthless sky. From edge to edge it may measure twenty paces. From the central plain to the apparent mountains on the horizon, the distance seems closer to several hundred kilometers. Neither measurement prevails. An object thrown across the basin lands nearby after a long, visibly diminishing arc. Walking toward it takes only seconds, but ages the object’s shadow by hours.

The ground is dry mineral, compacted into broad polygons. It resembles a salt field, but there has never been water here. Its white crust is a deposition of abandoned probabilities: alternatives once available to matter, precipitated when choices became impossible. Underfoot, it fractures with a soft ceramic click. The broken edges show extremely fine layers—gray, ivory, smoke-black—each too thin to have thickness, yet together forming slabs several centimeters deep.

Nothing grows. There are no seeds in the seams, no lichen on the stones, no microbial haze awaiting warmth. The sterility is not recent and does not feel hostile. Life was simply never among the basin’s permitted arrangements.

Cold resides close to the surface. Metal placed on the plain gathers a rim of frostlike crystals, though there is no vapor to freeze. Skin feels the chill more slowly: first as numbness, then as the peculiar conviction that one has always been cold. Breath does not cloud. It falls in faint translucent grains and lies on the ground for a few moments before returning to the lungs, sometimes out of order.

Across the basin stand seven structures, widely separated.

The smallest resembles a cairn assembled from black glass plates, each plate balanced on its edge without touching the next. The largest is an open frame no higher than a person’s knee: three tarnished rods surrounding an empty cube. Perspective fails around this frame. Viewed from one side, it is plainly nearby. From another, it stands on a remote plateau beneath a field of extinguished stars. Its rods are engraved with marks too small to resolve, even under magnification; enlarging them reveals only longer, more intricate edges.

There is also a low arch of chalk-colored material, half buried in the crust. It casts no shadow. Instead, shadows nearby bend toward it and vanish under its curve. Anyone reaching through the arch feels resistance, not from a surface but from distance itself. A hand inserted to the wrist appears many kilometers away, enormous against the false horizon. Withdrawn, it returns uninjured, carrying a thin dust that falls upward until it disappears.

The remaining constructions are less legible: a row of five sockets sunk into the plain; a shallow dish that contains darkness rather than matter; a filament stretched between two stones, vibrating too slowly for the eye to see; and a narrow tower whose upper half is always obscured, despite the absence of cloud or mist.

All are products of extraordinary engineering, though none exhibit the familiar signs of machinery. They have no controls, joints, sources of power, or distinction between component and environment. The structures appear to operate by persuading physical laws to become local customs. Near the sockets, mass accumulates sideways. Beside the dish, events lose their order. Under the filament, an object may possess two temperatures without averaging them. The tower seems to hold the basin in place—not against gravity, but against interpretation.

It is difficult to know whether these artifacts were built here or whether the place condensed around them.

Their surfaces are weathered. Corners have rounded; inscriptions have thinned; the black plates bear minute pits. Yet there is no wind, no rain, no drifting grit. Erosion proceeds by exposure to existence. Anything that remains definite for too long is gradually abraded by all the states it excludes. Thus the oldest objects become smooth, simple, and nearly perfect. A stone left here long enough loses first its blemishes, then its mineral distinctions, then the property of being any particular stone. Eventually it becomes a pale oval with no measurable composition. Later, it becomes a location where a stone is strongly implied.

These implications make up much of the landscape.

Sparse boulders rest on the plain, separated by generous intervals of emptiness. Some are solid. Some can be seen but not approached. One is present only when another is hidden behind the observer. Another is visible as a depression in light, its shape inferred from the way dimness leans around it. There are footprints between several stones, though no creature could have made them. Each print is identical, including the cracks crossing its floor, as if one footprint has been placed repeatedly rather than a walker having passed.

The sky gives little illumination. It is a dull violet-gray, without sun, moon, or stars. Light seems to seep from the basin’s dimensions, a weak radiance produced by the act of having extension. Colors survive poorly. Black remains black; white becomes bone-gray; every other hue retreats toward a muted metallic blue.

At irregular intervals, a narrow band brightens along the horizon. This is the place’s closest equivalent to dawn. The band does not rise. It tightens until it is a single brilliant line, then divides into two horizons: one above, one below. For several minutes the basin hangs between them. During this interval, loose fragments lift a finger’s width from the ground, and the seven structures align in ways their positions should not permit. Then the lower horizon closes, the fragments settle, and dimness returns.

The cycle takes somewhere between nine days and eleven thousand years, depending on where one stands.

There is almost no sound. Footsteps are muffled, as if the crust absorbs not vibration but consequence. Breaking a mineral plate produces its small click only once; further fragments break silently, the sound having already been used. Near the filament, one may hear a tone lower than hearing—a pressure behind the teeth, repeating at intervals long enough to be forgotten between occurrences.

The loudest event is the tower’s adjustment.

Once in a great while, its obscured upper half inclines by an immeasurably small angle. Nothing visibly moves. Nevertheless, the whole basin issues a faint settling sigh, like a distant building cooling at night. Horizons shift. One or two boulders exchange properties. A crack in the plain closes at one end and opens at the other. Visitors may discover that a memory has become a prediction, or that an action they postponed now lies behind them.

Then the place is still again.

No fauna crosses the mineral field. No wings disturb the air; no burrow marks interrupt the polygons. There is not even decay, because nothing organic has ever accumulated here. The scattered artifacts have no custodians. If they communicate, they do so through geometry: altering the angle between objects, delaying one shadow, repeating an absence in exact intervals.

Their purpose may once have been practical. Perhaps the basin is an instrument embedded in the summit, using altitude as a lever against causality. Perhaps it is a repair site where damaged constants were brought into tolerance. Perhaps the mountain is only camouflage grown around a device that required geological ages to complete its calibration.

Or perhaps the seven structures are restraints.

This possibility becomes persuasive near the basin’s center, where the white crust slopes gently toward a shallow, circular hollow. The hollow contains nothing. Unlike the ordinary emptiness between scattered things, however, this emptiness has texture. Looking into it gives the impression of immense depth compressed without reduction: a gulf folded small enough to hide under a hand.

Measurements taken across it disagree. A rod laid over the hollow emerges older and subtly curved. Light directed into it returns before being emitted. A stone dropped there remains visible at the bottom, though there is no bottom, and can still be picked up from beside one’s foot. The seven structures all face it in whatever manner they possess a front.

The hollow changes slowly. Over centuries it broadens by the width of an atom. Over longer spans it may contract, though records cannot agree which condition came first. Its rim is the most heavily eroded material in the basin, polished beyond smoothness into a continuous edge with no atomic irregularity. This is where reality wears against whatever it has enclosed.

Standing near it, one senses no threat, intention, or presence. There is only a gentle loosening of categories. Near becomes a kind of before. Weight becomes an opinion held by the ground. The distinction between observing the hollow and being the place from which it is observed begins to thin.

The basin does not collapse. It has endured too long for catastrophe to be its natural mode. Its impossible features have settled into equilibrium, each violation balanced by another. The tower corrects the arch. The dish absorbs the excess sequence shed by the sockets. The filament distributes contradiction among the stones. The cairn, perhaps, counts.

Everything functions with immense patience.

To leave, one must find the mountain notch again, though from within it appears only as a hairline fracture in the dim sky near ground level. Passing through it returns a traveler to the summit, usually moments after entry. Occasionally it is years. Occasionally the traveler arrives before approaching the notch and sees, far down the slope, a small figure climbing toward the place.

Behind them the mountain remains high, cold, and ordinary.

In a pocket no map can contain, the dry basin continues its slow adjustment. The false horizons divide and rejoin. Breath-grains rise from the crust in anticipation of visitors. The empty hollow deepens without increasing in size.

Seven ancient structures keep their sparse vigil around a flaw no larger than a bowl, while erosion patiently smooths the difference between the world and what the world cannot allow.
