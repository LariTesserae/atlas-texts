The ocean begins above the mountains.

There is no surface in the ordinary sense—no clean boundary where water gives way to air. Instead, the planet’s upper atmosphere thickens gradually into a cold, transparent sea. Vapor becomes mist, mist becomes suspended droplets, and droplets become a continuous body of water extending thousands of kilometers upward. What passes for sky is merely the thinner, brighter reach of the same fluid. Sunlight enters from far above, pale and steady, descending in enormous columns that bend with pressure fronts. At great depths those columns become green-white shafts, broad enough to contain whole weather systems.

Within this planetary ocean, ranges of newborn stone rise to impossible elevations.

They are not old mountains. Nothing here has had time to become old. Their flanks are unweathered: black glass, pale mineral foam, metallic blue crusts, and ridges so sharp they seem cut from recently cooled liquid. Some summits stand high in the illuminated water, while their roots descend beyond sight into denser, darker strata. There are no beaches, valleys, or rounded boulders. Erosion has only just begun to invent such things.

At intervals, a cliff face gives a single brittle crack. A slab larger than a city separates cleanly, hangs almost motionless in the dense water, then begins a centuries-long descent.

The emptiness between the ranges is immense. One can look across a basin hundreds of kilometers wide and see almost nothing in it but light, pressure, and slowly turning mineral dust. The water is exceptionally clear, though never still. Distance gives it color: turquoise nearby, indigo farther away, and at the limits of sight a smoky violet. Mountain chains appear as dim islands floating in the volume, disconnected from any visible ground.

The sea has weather inside itself.

Cold currents descend in vertical fronts, while warmer, mineral-heavy water wells upward from the forming crust. Where they meet, the water folds into transparent storms. Vortices the size of continents pass between the peaks, detectable first by the bending of sunlight and then by the roar. The sound is not the familiar crash of waves. There are no waves with air above them. Instead, the whole ocean carries pressure as thunder: long concussions, harmonic groans, sudden metallic shrieks from stressed stone. A storm can announce itself for days as a deepening note felt through the water before its clouds of white crystal arrive.

At times, the noise becomes continuous. Currents strike a mountain range and divide around it, making every ridge vibrate. Cavities in the fresh rock act as organ chambers. One peak may sound like a low bell; another produces a harsh, layered moan. Faults grind far below, transmitting impacts through the fluid with such clarity that a fracture on the opposite side of the world may arrive as a sharp crack followed by hours of diminishing reverberation.

Yet there are no animals to hear it.

Nothing swims here. There are no shoals, no drifting larvae, no eyes flashing in the dark. The open water contains no pursuit and no flight. Its vastness is not wilderness in the usual sense, because wilderness implies lives concealed within it. This is a living-looking emptiness with no animal life at all.

Vegetation exists only marginally, if vegetation is the right word. On certain high ridges, where the light is strongest and dissolved metals collect, sparse fans of green-black tissue cling directly to the stone. They have no leaves and few recognizable cells. Each colony is a thin, pleated membrane, sometimes kilometers long but only a finger’s width thick. It harvests light and pressure gradients, growing along lines of mineral conductivity. From a distance these colonies resemble dark stains or inscriptions on the mountainsides.

They do not sway with the currents. They alter their internal density and let water pass through them.

Most rock remains bare. Around the membranes, faint lawns of microscopic crystal-growth spread for a few meters and stop. There is no soil. Death has not accumulated long enough to make any.

The strangest feature of the place is also its technology, though no structures were ever built.

The water engineers itself.

Throughout the ocean, molecules assemble into transient computational lattices under particular combinations of pressure, illumination, and magnetic stress. These lattices are invisible except when they refract light into geometric halos. They form spontaneously, calculate for seconds or centuries, and then dissolve back into ordinary water. No maker placed them here. They are a phase of matter, as natural to this world as ice, but their behavior exceeds what chemistry alone should permit.

A lattice may occupy a volume larger than a mountain. It has no walls, machinery, or central core. It consists only of aligned regions in the liquid—subtle differences in spin, charge, and molecular orientation. Currents pass through without destroying it. Within those regions, the ocean models its own future.

When a continental vortex approaches, thin bands of brilliance appear in empty water. They intersect in vast curves, forecasting paths the storm has not yet taken. Sometimes the vortex follows them. Sometimes it changes course, as though the forecast has acted upon what it predicted.

This is why the mountain ranges are so young.

The oceanic lattices concentrate tides and redirect heat. They bring deep minerals upward, arrange convection into focused plumes, and coax stone out of solution. Mountains precipitate inside the water. At first they are pale, porous shapes like frost on an unimaginable scale. Then pressure compacts them, metals migrate through them, and their interiors harden. For several decades they may grow visibly, adding ledges and needles in pulses timed to distant magnetic storms.

Creation is violent. A new ridge begins as a tremor in clear water. Mineral haze condenses along an invisible plane; lightning-like discharges crawl through it; then black stone knits itself together with a sound like continuous breaking glass. Hot fluid floods outward, though the overall sea remains cool. Towering chimneys appear, split, and are reabsorbed. Entire provisional landscapes form and fail before one survives.

The ocean does not preserve every experiment.

Some ranges are dismantled almost as soon as they emerge. Their crystalline bonds are loosened by fields passing through the water, and solid peaks become clouds of glittering grains. Because ordinary erosion is still weak, these disappearances are strangely clean. A mountain does not slowly wear down; it loses coherence. First its edge blurs. Then light enters it. Finally it is no longer a mountain but a suspended continent of dust, turning silently until the currents seize it and the sound returns.

Other formations remain: walls thousands of kilometers long, isolated horns, deep shelves suspended above unseen foundations. Their geology is raw and undecided. Veins end abruptly. Caverns close while currents are passing through them. Ridges branch in forms that resemble frozen lightning rather than tectonic uplift.

No horizon stays reliable. Refraction can place a summit overhead that is actually far below, or stretch a nearby cliff into a dark band encircling the world. The usual meanings of high and deep are weakened here. Gravity still pulls toward the planet’s center, but buoyancy, current, and the ocean’s distributed fields contest it. Loose stone may rise. Dense water may travel upward. Mineral dust sometimes falls sideways for years.

Normal daylight survives despite all this. There is morning, though it reaches the depths slowly. Brightness descends from the upper ocean in a visible tide. The highest summits glow first. Later, long slopes emerge from blue darkness, and finally the basins receive a diffuse gray radiance. Night reverses the process, but the sea does not become wholly black. Computational phases linger as broad dim geometries: rings, grids, branching sheets, and solitary points that appear to watch without being alive.

Near one newly forming range, the water is filled with kilometer-wide loops of faint silver light. They tilt through one another without touching. Stone rises at their intersections in slow black spires. Every few hours, a pressure wave runs through the region and all the loops tighten at once. The resulting note is overwhelming—clear, sustained, nearly pure. It passes through peaks and open basins, through sparse green membranes and uninhabited trenches, until the entire submerged planet seems to be ringing.

Then the geometry relaxes. Several spires collapse upward into dust. Others continue growing.

Nothing observes the difference. Nothing names what has been lost or made. The world performs its advanced, incomprehensible engineering without tools, architects, ruins, or purpose. It is untouched not because change has spared it, but because change is the only presence here.

The landscape is still deciding what a landscape is.
