The district occupies a shallow basin no larger than a few city blocks. Brick walls, machine halls, and narrow service towers stand widely separated across it, each structure given an unusual amount of empty ground. There are no houses, no shops, no civic monuments. It resembles the industrial edge of a town whose town center was never built.

Water lies everywhere without quite becoming a lake. It sheets over black paving, fills tram grooves, gathers under iron stairs, and wells continuously through seams in the masonry. The ground is low and almost level, but old subsidence has divided it into broad, shallow terraces. On the lowest terraces, streets vanish beneath warm brown water. Their curbs remain visible as parallel ridges furred with moss.

Rain has recently passed, though here that is nearly always true. Condensation beads on pipes and hangs from cables in lucid rows. Buildings weep through their brickwork. Gutters pour long after the clouds have emptied, fed from reservoirs hidden in roofs and walls. Steam drifts close to the ground and catches what little light descends through the permanent roof of vapor.

It is hot enough that every surface seems to breathe. Metal handrails are slick and warm. The bricks retain decades of furnace heat, and the standing water has the temperature of blood. Sweat does not evaporate; it joins the place, running down skin in the same thin streams that mark the walls. Occasionally a cooler pulse rises from beneath the paving, fogging the hollows before being swallowed by the heavier air.

The structures are old but not ruined. Their edges have softened into settled forms: brick arches bowed without collapsing, concrete aprons polished by overflow, iron housings layered with rust so stable and fine-grained that it resembles lacquer. Generations of drainage have cut miniature deltas into mortar and carved smooth channels around bolts. Nothing here appears newly broken. Even damage has had time to become infrastructure.

Vegetation occupies the wet margins rather than the open spaces. Ferns crowd the shade beneath gantries. Glossy creepers climb smokestacks for several meters, then stop at invisible bands of heat. Reeds grow from flooded doorways, their roots tangled around submerged machinery. Moss forms thick green lips on sluice gates, and pale, broad-leaved plants gather where hot runoff cools enough to touch. The center of every yard remains strangely bare: expanses of wet stone, sparse rails, isolated valve wheels, and shallow pools stippled by falling drops.

Nothing moves among the leaves.

There are no insects in the steam, no fish beneath the drowned streets, no birds on the overhead wires. No spoor marks the mud. Flowers open without pollinators and close again unseen. Seedpods split in the heat, scattering grains that the water carries through grates. The plants persist by other arrangements: runners, spores, fragmentation, and the patient redistribution of whole root mats during floods. Some appear to be propagated by the machinery itself. Vines are cut cleanly by passing shutters; each severed piece takes root wherever the runoff leaves it.

The district is loud.

Deep below, pumps strike in staggered rhythms. Their blows travel through the water before they reach the air, so the soles of the feet hear them first. Turbines roar behind windowless walls. Chains drag through hidden channels. Pressure valves release with sudden, prolonged shrieks. Every few minutes, an engine somewhere begins to labor, climbs toward an unbearable pitch, and then falls silent just before it seems certain to fail.

That silence never spreads far. Rainwater rattles through vertical pipes. Steam knocks inside copper conduits. Overflow pours from roof to roof in heavy curtains. Loose sheets of corrugated metal flex with cannon-like reports as the district heats and cools. Together these sounds produce the impression of vast scale, but the illusion collapses at the basin walls. Walk ten minutes in any direction and one reaches the boundary: a high embankment of old stone, dark with seepage, curving inward until the entire district closes like a courtyard.

The machines appear to be running a city considerably larger than the one present.

Pipes enter the basin over the eastern wall in bundles, divide among the buildings, and emerge on the western side as only three broad conduits. Elevated rails describe elaborate routes between sheds barely fifty paces apart. Signal lamps change from red to amber to a dim mineral green, although no vehicle passes. An empty freight platform is washed every hour by an automatic flood. Mechanical arms descend from a gantry, turn toward one another, exchange nothing, and retract.

The place has rules, but their purpose is obscure.

Water may cross a threshold only when a corresponding lamp is dark. Steam rises from open ground but sinks when released from pipes. Rust spreads during dry intervals and hardens when wet. Doors cannot be opened while machinery is audible directly behind them; if the sound stops, the door will open, but the room beyond may belong to another building altogether. Distances vary with pressure. When the great pump beneath the central yard is drawing in, the northern boiler house stands close enough to touch from the canal stairs. When it expels, the same crossing requires several minutes through hot mist.

At the basin’s center is a circular settling court. It is the district’s largest open space and still only the size of a modest plaza. Four factory buildings face it from generous distances, their facades blind except for high louvers. The paving slopes almost imperceptibly toward a round reservoir covered by an iron grille. Water streams into it from all directions, carrying leaves, reddish silt, flakes of paint, and tiny spheres of condensed oil.

At irregular intervals the flow reverses.

The reservoir exhales. Water rises through the grille in a smooth, dark dome, lifting debris without turbulence. It spreads outward in branching films that climb the slopes, creep up walls, and enter gutters from below. For several minutes the basin’s drainage runs backward. Reeds bend upstream. Moss brightens. Dormant belts begin to turn, and the signal lamps throughout the district illuminate in sequence.

Then, from somewhere under the court, comes a single immense impact.

Every pipe jumps in its bracket. Warm rain shakes loose from cables and roofs. Windows tremble in their frames. The water hesitates, collapses through the grille, and resumes its ordinary descent. The machines continue, louder than before, correcting themselves room by room.

There is no operator to witness this correction and no animal to flee its noise. The settlement maintains only its own circulation: water through stone, heat through water, pressure through engines, vegetation through cracks. It is active without being inhabited, lush without wildlife, urban without citizens. Its emptiness is not abandonment. Abandonment would imply that something had left.

Here, the absence seems original—designed into the wide yards, the unreachable controls, the doors sized for no familiar body. The district has always been this way: a small, hot municipality of runoff and labor, turning ceaselessly inside its damp stone boundary, manufacturing perhaps nothing except the weather that keeps it alive.
