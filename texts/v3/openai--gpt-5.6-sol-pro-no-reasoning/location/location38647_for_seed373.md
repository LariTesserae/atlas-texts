The High Aviary occupies a shallow crater near the summit of a mountain whose lower slopes have fallen away.

It is not large. From one rim to the other is perhaps the distance across a cathedral close, though the curvature of the place makes estimates unreliable. The crater is an old, settled form: a bowl of pale stone with blunted edges, polished ribs, and terraces worn so evenly that they resemble deliberate masonry. Above it stretches a cool blue sky. Below the broken mountain, cloud layers lie flat and distant, giving the impression that the bowl has been set on an island in the upper atmosphere.

There is no water. None falls, gathers, condenses, or circulates here. The stone is dry through its entire depth. Dust does not clump. Living tissue contains no sap or blood in any familiar sense. Even the clouds far below are not vapor but suspended mineral colloids, shining white where the sun catches them.

Yet the crater appears lush.

Its terraces are crowded with growths resembling ferns, moss curtains, alpine cushions, and dwarf trees. They rise in rich banks of blue-green, silver, black, and bruised violet. Their leaves are wafer-thin mineral laminates, flexible despite their crystalline structure. Instead of absorbing liquid through roots, they draw charged particles from the constant wind. Branches divide into finer and finer conductive filaments; root systems spread only a few millimeters beneath the rock, making dark circuitry across its surface.

Many plants are almost entirely hollow. A tall grove may contain less matter than a human body, its apparent foliage made from branching films only a molecule thick. Seen face-on, these films are opaque and richly colored. Seen edge-on, whole shrubs disappear.

This is why the crater can be both lush and nearly empty. Its abundance is architectural rather than massive: surfaces without bulk, canopies woven from gaps, animals made chiefly of tension and trajectory. Everything is separated by more space than first appears. Even the ground is a lace of stone over deep evacuated chambers.

The noise begins long before the rim comes into sight.

Wind enters the crater through seven clefts in the western wall. Each cleft has been narrowed by ancient growth and by structures whose makers are unknown. The openings act as enormous reeds. Air driven through them produces continuous tones at the threshold of pain: bass vibrations that shake the teeth, shrill bands that seem to pass through bone, and a dense middle register like a thousand brass sheets being torn at once.

Nothing here is quiet enough to be heard alone.

The mineral trees add their own notes. Their leaves flip, buckle, and snap in the airflow, discharging accumulated charge as thunderous cracks. Stands of filament grass produce white noise so powerful that it has weight. Buried cavities boom. Loose crystals race around the terraces in bright, rattling streams. Every few minutes the wind changes angle and the entire crater retunes itself. A low chord collapses; a piercing harmonic takes its place; a grove folds flat and another springs upright in a detonation of metallic foliage.

The landscape is never still.

Storms do not arrive here. The place is itself a storm mechanism, maintained by the pressure difference between the atmosphere above the mountain and the enormous hollow beneath it. Air pours down through cracks, is heated or charged in unseen depths, and erupts again through pores in the crater floor. Clear vortices roam the bowl, flattening plants in circular tracks. Dust climbs in narrow columns, separates by electrical charge, and descends as curtains of pale and dark grains. Stone plates lift, hover for several seconds, then settle into new arrangements.

At times the entire floor becomes fluid without melting. Pebbles flow uphill. Terraces ripple. Groves migrate by dismantling themselves on one side and reassembling on the other, leaf by leaf. The old bowl endures, but its contents are continually rewritten.

Its fauna thrives on this violence.

There are thousands of creatures, though few remain visible for more than a heartbeat. The largest resemble broad translucent kites. They are no thicker than skin and steer by altering microscopic corrugations along their edges. Their bodies flash copper, green, and clear as they turn. They never land. When injured, they come apart into ribbons, and each ribbon may widen over the following days into a separate animal.

Smaller life fills the air: spinning seedlike grazers, jointed sparks, rings that roll on the wind, shoals of black motes that bend around invisible pressure boundaries. They feed on electric charge, airborne mineral, plant membranes, and one another. Some exist only during particular notes. When a western cleft sounds a stable pitch, silvery animals condense from apparently empty air around its standing-wave nodes. If the pitch wavers, they lose cohesion and vanish as dust.

Along the sheltered eastern wall live more substantial beasts. They have six or eight limbs and low bodies armored in porous ceramic. Their feet grip stone by briefly welding to it. They browse through the mineral thickets, shaving conductive leaf-film with vibrating mouthparts. Within their shells, metabolism proceeds through liquid salts and mobile electrons rather than water. When the wind becomes too strong, they anchor themselves in groups, making chains across the terraces. The smallest shelter inside the larger animals’ hollow armor, and pale hunters move through those chambers after them.

Predation is everywhere, rapid and oddly impersonal. A flock may devour a grove in seconds. The grove returns by evening, rebuilt from buried templates. A ceramic grazer can be stripped to its frame, then stand again when swarms of maintenance organisms fill its joints. Death is common, but identity is poorly attached to bodies here.

Scattered around the crater are seven structures, one near each wind cleft.

They are slender frames of dull material, each approximately the height of a small house. They resemble ruined bell towers from which walls and stairs have been removed. Some lean. One is embedded halfway into the stone. Their surfaces are weathered into soft curves, yet no fragment has ever been seen to break away. Foliage passes through the frames, emerging subtly altered in color and branching pattern.

The towers are not inert. They manipulate distance locally.

A hand placed inside one frame appears several meters away, in another part of the same opening. A flying creature entering at the bottom may emerge at the top before it has fully entered. Wind crosses the frames in folded paths and returns amplified, accounting for some of the crater’s unbearable volume. During certain pressure alignments, all seven towers seem to stand side by side, though their foundations remain distributed around the rim.

Whatever built them used the crater not as a habitation but as an instrument.

Faint marks persist near the towers: rows of shallow depressions, spirals burned into stone, narrow benches cut for bodies of uncertain shape. There are also small sealed objects tucked into crevices. They look like black seeds or pieces of smoked glass. Each contains an interior much larger than its shell—empty galleries, branching rooms, cold plazas under artificial skies. Most are vacant. In one, tiny mineral foliage has begun to spread across the ceiling, obeying a gravity directed upward.

No road reaches the High Aviary. No clear trail crosses it. Its boundaries are sharp: beyond the crater rim the mountain is almost bare, populated only by occasional mats of charge-feeding lichen. The richness belongs entirely to the bowl and to the machine-state maintained there.

Light remains surprisingly ordinary. Sunlight comes cleanly through the blue upper air. Shadows fall where expected. Noon is bright without being hot, and the pervasive cool keeps the mineral leaves rigid and responsive. Only at dawn and evening does the place betray the strangeness of its illumination. The thinnest organisms retain light for several minutes, so that every gust leaves a luminous afterimage. Flocks write pale loops across the dusk. Bent groves show where they stood before the wind moved them. For a while, the crater contains its present landscape and several previous versions superimposed in fading green and gold.

At night, the noise continues.

The towers exchange subsonic pulses. Plants discharge blue threads into passing animals. Predators hunt by disturbing standing waves, locating prey as flaws in the crater’s chord. Cold stars appear above, but their positions tremble inside the frames.

The governing rule of the place seems simple: form may persist, but matter need not. Everything has an established shape—the bowl, the terraces, the groves, the migrations, the seven folded towers—yet almost none of it keeps the same substance from one hour to the next. The landscape is ancient because its turbulence is exact. Destruction is one phase of maintenance. Emptiness is the medium from which abundance is repeatedly drawn.

By morning, the crater looks settled again: cool, green, weathered, almost gardenlike.

Then the western clefts begin a new chord. The stone lifts in scales. The forests turn edge-on and disappear. Millions of animals pour from the empty air, and the whole small summit erupts into motion.
