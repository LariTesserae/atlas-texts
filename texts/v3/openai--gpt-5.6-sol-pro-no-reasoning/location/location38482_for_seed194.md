The city exists inside a seed.

Not metaphorically. The seed is real, though no one living within it has seen its exterior or could say what species once bore it. It lies somewhere beneath a mineral plain, in a country that has been dry longer than most rocks have names. Its shell is black, thick, and nearly imperishable. Ages of abrasion have rounded it smooth. Sand and salt once scoured its outer surface; later even the sand wore away. The seed remained, sealed and dormant, with a city occupying the hollow where an embryo ought to have been.

There is no water inside. There has never been rain, mist, blood, dew, or sap in any form recognizable as liquid. Life here is vegetable, but it proceeds by another chemistry.

The seed’s interior is mostly empty.

Its inhabited world clings to the inner wall: a thin, dark rind of terraces, bridges, hanging districts, root galleries, and hollow chambers. Beyond their edges opens the Great Interior, a cavity so wide that sounds cross it only after long delays. Nothing illuminates that distance. There are no stars and no pale opposite shore. The far side may be hundreds of paces away or several days’ climb; dimensions are inferred by echoes, and echoes disagree.

The city is called the Husk by its inhabitants, though they distinguish neither city from landscape nor landscape from organism. Buildings are grown from the same tissues as streets. Streets are flattened veins. Houses are blisters induced in the wall, coaxed into chambers with bone wedges and stone hooks. Towers are old vascular bundles exposed by careful cutting. Bridges are braided rootlets stretched over clefts in the rind, tightened over generations as they dry.

Everything is plant, or plant-made.

The load-bearing columns are petrified cotyledon fibers. Doors are plaques of bark that curl shut in cool air. Stairs consist of shelf growths arranged around the hollow stems of ancestral vessels. Whole neighborhoods occupy the chambers of dead cells: immense polygonal rooms separated by membranes thinner than cloth, though tougher than leather. Where a membrane has ruptured, the tear becomes a public arch. Where three or four chambers meet, people establish plazas on knots of woody tissue.

Yet the Husk is not lush. There are no leaves, flowers, vines, or green surfaces. Its vegetation has the austere appearance of something distilled past ordinary life. It is charcoal-black, umber, chalk-gray, and the dull red of old resin. Growth is measured in hairbreadths per lifetime. Pale root threads cross vacant rooms and vanish through pores in the walls. Fungal-looking fans unfold, but they are not fungi; they are the seed’s remaining sensory tissues, opening to catch pressure, heat, and breath.

The living matter feeds on gradients so slight that no hurried creature could survive here. It absorbs warmth from the hands of citizens. It digests vibration. It takes in carbon from exhaled air and minerals scraped from its own ancient shell. Certain tissues harvest differences in electrical charge between compressed and relaxed fibers. The city’s loudness is therefore not incidental. Noise is agriculture.

All through the inhabited rind stand resonators: tall, hollow stalks lashed together with cords of bast. Some are no larger than flutes; others descend through many districts. Workers strike them with wooden mallets, stone pendulums, or their bare heels. Great bark drums are stretched across abandoned cell chambers. Split reeds clatter in slow-turning wheels. Rows of citizens pull ropes that bend the largest vascular columns until they release a groan deep enough to tremble in the teeth.

The sound never entirely stops.

It is not music in the ornamental sense, though patterns emerge. Each district keeps a pulse suited to the tissues it cultivates. The lower terraces maintain a continuous bass vibration, long beats separated by silences in which the living wall drinks. The bridge wards use rapid wooden knocking to strengthen braided spans. In the nursery galleries, thin membranes are sung into activity by choirs whose voices rise and fall in close, wavering intervals.

From far across the cavity, every labor returns as echo. Thus the city hears itself as another city in the dark: delayed, distorted, answering from nowhere.

This makes distance socially complicated. An unseen ward may cease its drumming, yet its old rhythms continue arriving for minutes or hours. During large ceremonies, echoes overlap until it seems that generations of the dead have joined the work. Some citizens claim this is literally true. They say the seed stores every vibration in its fibers and releases them according to cycles too slow for human accounting. A hammer blow made now might be heard by a child not yet born.

There are no animals. There are no insects in the bark, no worms in the substrate, no moths in the chambers, no vermin scavenging crumbs. Pollination, predation, decay, and dispersal—if those processes exist at all—are performed by hands.

The inhabitants alone move at an animal pace, though whether they are animals is disputed. Their bodies are light and dry. Their skin carries fine grain like split wood. They do not bleed when cut; wounds darken and seal with aromatic resin. They eat shaved tissue from cultivated nodules and inhale the dust of spore-like cells released from communal loaves. They have mouths, fingers, and voices, but no one has seen one born in the ordinary way. Infants are found at immense intervals in warm alcoves where the city wall has folded around a human shape.

This, too, is called germination.

Because usable matter is scarce, the city is broad in organization but sparse in presence. A whole district may consist of twenty chambers, six bridges, a resonator tower, and one keeper. Streets cross long intervals with no doors. Terraces large enough for markets stand bare except on appointed days. An individual walking alone may hear thousands at work elsewhere and meet no one for a week.

Urbanity here is arrangement rather than crowding.

Addresses are rhythmic. A house is identified not by number but by the sequence sounded on its threshold: three slow knocks, a pause, five scraping strokes. Civic boundaries follow differences in resonance. If a bridge changes pitch as it ages, it may legally enter another ward. Maps are not drawn. They are beaten into memory on tablets of layered bark, each route represented by tempo, pitch, and texture.

There is no visible light—not even fire. Combustion is feared both for its heat and for the precious gases it would consume. The inhabitants navigate by touch, echo, air pressure, and the subtle temperatures of living tissue. Their tools are stone flakes, shell-hard seed fragments, hardened thorns, fiber cords, wedges, levers, and polished knocking rods. Metal is known only as rare cold grains embedded in the outer shell. A few ceremonial blades contain such grains pressed into resin, but they cannot be sharpened well and are considered more prophetic than useful.

Work is done with extraordinary patience. A doorway may take three generations to persuade open. Instead of cutting through a wall, builders score it, drum at its margins, warm it with bodies, and insert wedges as it slowly grows away from pressure. Demolition is similarly gradual: a building is deprived of vibration until it stiffens, hollows, and separates along its grain. Entire avenues migrate over centuries as the rind contracts.

The city is cool everywhere, but cold pools in the deepest chambers. There the plant tissue is almost inert, pale and dense as old ivory. Sound carries poorly, swallowed by soft walls. These places are considered dangerous because a person can vanish acoustically before vanishing physically. The dead are taken there and seated in niches, their bodies pressed against dormant tissue. Over decades, wall and corpse become continuous. Later builders may discover the curve of a shoulder or a row of finger bones transformed into wood within a supporting beam.

There is no rot. Nothing has enough moisture to decay.

Age appears instead as polishing, thinning, fatigue, and loss of distinction. Ancient stairs have been worn into shallow slopes by bare feet. In the oldest quarters, room, road, and dwelling have eroded into one gently undulating surface. The former city has settled nearly flat against the inner shell. Only dark seams reveal where walls once stood. Residents cross these elder districts carefully, tapping the floor before every step, because the substrate is riddled with historical hollows.

Some hollows contain earlier settlements, compressed beneath newer growth. Their architecture is cruder: chipped apertures, rough lashings, squat resonators blackened by hand oils. Beneath those lie still older arrangements, scarcely recognizable as intentional. Archaeologists identify them by repeated strike marks. At the deepest accessible layer, the marks cease to resemble tools and become little radial scars made by fingernails or thorns.

Below that is the First Erosion: a vast, smooth plane worn into the tissue before the city’s oldest stories. Nothing grows there. Nothing responds to knocking. When struck, it returns a single, impossibly loud note.

The note seems to come not from below, but from outside.

For this reason the First Erosion is kept under guard. Once every long cycle, when the rind cools and contracts, the plane sounds by itself. The whole city falls silent to hear it. Drums stop. Bridges cease clattering. Workers hold their mallets away from the stalks. In the absolute dark, a pressure builds underfoot.

Then the seed groans.

The sound begins too low to hear. It enters through the soles, passes through bone and wall, and slowly climbs into audibility. District after district joins the resonance. Doors curl open. Dormant fans unfold. Root threads tense across vacant rooms. Fine edible dust lifts from the terraces in clouds that cannot be seen but can be tasted—bitter, mineral, faintly warm.

The groan may continue for days.

During it, the city feeds more deeply than at any other time. New alcoves form. Old spans tighten. Somewhere, perhaps, another infant is enclosed by the wall. The citizens lie flat against the substrate and feel vast abrasions traveling over the outer shell: not footsteps, they think, and not weather. Something much slower.

Perhaps the mineral plain above is still moving. Perhaps the seed is buried beneath a glacier of salt, sliding one grain’s width every century. Perhaps there is no exterior plain at all, and what they call the outer shell is merely another wall within a larger organism.

The Husk has no instrument capable of deciding.

Afterward the groan descends below hearing. Labor resumes one ward at a time. Knocks propagate into the cavity; old echoes return; the urban rind drinks the noise. The city goes on cultivating itself in darkness, empty and thunderous, alive by increments too small to witness.

And around it—or beyond it, or within the next enclosing layer—the ancient dry world continues wearing everything toward its final shape.
