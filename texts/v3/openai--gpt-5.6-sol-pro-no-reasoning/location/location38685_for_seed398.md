The city is hidden inside a mountain that has not yet finished arriving.

From outside, there is little to see: a black fang at extreme altitude, newly thrust above the surrounding cloud deck. Its ridges are sharp enough to divide fog without gathering frost. The rock is young—basalt, metallic pumice, planes of volcanic glass—and still holds the heat of its ascent. Nothing grows on it. No lichen marks the age of a ledge; no wind-borne seed has found purchase. Even ordinary weather rarely reaches this height. The mountain stands in a dry upper atmosphere, under a sky so dark that noon resembles the last hour before night.

Near the summit, behind a seam only a handspan wide, is the city.

The seam opens not by moving stone but by briefly making thickness irrelevant. Beyond it lies a hollow the size of a room and, impossibly, an entire urban district. Its scale does not remain consistent under observation. A person entering can cross the first plaza in twelve steps, yet the towers around it rise for kilometers into the mountain’s interior. Streets recede to hairline distances. Bridges pass behind one another in parallax that the available volume cannot support. Seen from certain corners, the whole city appears embedded in the glossy wall like a circuit beneath translucent enamel. From others, the enclosing mountain becomes a remote horizon.

This is not a crowded place. It is an urbanism of absence.

There are avenues, terraces, transit wells, conduits, doors, and thousands of narrow structures, but they stand far apart in the distorted interior. A tower may have no neighbor within visible distance, while its foundation is threaded through the foundations of every other tower. Vast plazas contain only a single dark column. Platforms wait beside rails finer than scratches. Empty dwellings present rooms without furniture, built around recesses whose purpose cannot be guessed. The city has the grammar of settlement without the pressure of inhabitants.

Its materials are almost entirely mineral. Walls are cast from pale ceramic foam, black glass, and alloys that behave like folded light. Some surfaces are warm and granular; others offer no friction at all. Handrails, where they exist, are too thin to be grasped. Windows do not look outward. They expose neighboring depths: a street upside down beneath the viewer, a distant chamber filled with dim red haze, or the same window seen from its opposite side.

There has never been vegetation here. There are no animals, no insects, no drifting spores. Dust exists only where the mountain intrudes through fractures, and the city continuously sorts even that into narrow gray bands along the floors. Whatever built this place did not imitate ecosystems. It made no parks, no fountains, no decorative leaves in metal. Its few repeating motifs resemble branching lightning, stress cracks, and the geometry of cooling lava.

The air is hot but not uniform. Heat gathers in invisible cells. One doorway opens onto breathable warmth; the next contains an atmosphere that scorches exposed metal. The city manages these differences through ducts without vents and pumps without moving parts. At intervals, a transparent wave passes down an avenue, bending every vertical line. After it goes by, the air is cooler, and condensation beads suddenly on the dark walls.

This is the city’s water: brief, localized, and meticulously recovered.

Droplets gather in grooves too small to see until they fill. They run upward, sideways, or diagonally toward collection seams. Some streets receive a short indoor rain, no more than a minute long, released when pressure shifts in the mountain. The drops fall slowly despite the extreme gravity implied by the city’s verticality. They strike the warm pavement with soft ticks and vanish into steam. In lower chambers, moisture condenses into shallow, perfectly still pockets, each no wider than a coin. These reservoirs are connected through folded space; disturbing one makes ripples appear in dozens of others throughout the city.

No reservoir is allowed to overflow. Overflow is one of the few events to which the architecture reacts visibly. Doorways close. Floors tilt by fractions of a degree. Whole blocks alter their distance from one another, isolating the excess water until heat takes it back into the air.

The mountain itself remains active. Deep beyond the walls, magma rearranges chambers and presses fresh stone into old voids. Cracks open with glassy edges, invade a corridor, and are immediately incorporated. The city does not resist these intrusions. It builds around them. A new basalt blade may rise through a plaza in the course of an hour; by the next dim cycle, it has acquired stairs, conduits, and a precise collar of white ceramic. Thus the settlement grows without expanding. It treats geology as new construction.

Erosion has scarcely begun. Corners remain sharp. Tool marks—or the equivalents of tool marks—still show in some surfaces as fields of parallel grooves finer than fingerprints. Newly exposed rock gleams blue-black before oxidation dulls it. There are no rounded stones, no sediment beds, no softened ruins. Even structures that seem ancient have the fresh severity of objects removed from molds moments ago.

The light comes from no lamp. It leaks from stress.

Where the mountain presses hardest against the city, seams shine a muted violet. Compression makes the transparent alloys cloud with amber. Fine cracks send blue pulses along lintels and bridge cables. The illumination therefore shifts with the mountain’s internal weather. A district can fade completely when strain moves elsewhere, leaving only the dull red of hot stone and the ghostly afterimages of roads. Another quarter may brighten as a tremor approaches, every edge outlined at once.

Most activity is seen indirectly. Elevators descend without cabins. Signals travel beneath floors as moving coolness. A bridge changes its curvature when no one is near it. Faraway towers repeatedly divide into thinner copies, then merge. Transit lines activate according to schedules measured not in hours but in changes of altitude: a platform wakes when the summit rises another millimeter, or when the atmospheric pressure outside falls below a value last reached centuries before the mountain emerged.

The quiet is nearly complete. Without life, there is no rustle, breath, wingbeat, or distant call. Without loose objects, there is little clatter. The prevailing sound is a low mineral tension felt through the teeth before it is heard. Condensation ticks against glass. Somewhere far below, stone gives way with a brief, intimate snap. Occasionally a pressure door closes across an avenue, producing a soft tone like the rim of an enormous cup.

The loudest event is rain on the outer summit. It happens rarely. High storms brush the peak, and water flashes to steam on the black slopes. Some enters through new fractures. For several minutes the hidden city fills with scattered tapping, hissing, and deep resonances as its infrastructure captures each intrusion. Pools appear in unexpected recesses. Walls sweat. The hot avenues become hazy, and violet stress-light diffuses through vapor until the empty towers resemble silhouettes at dusk.

Then the mountain dries. The water is drawn away. The city returns to its sharp dimness.

Its technology is difficult to distinguish from local law. Distance behaves as though allocated rather than measured. Gravity changes direction at designated thresholds. Heat can be stored in angles: unfold a particular wall and the room becomes hotter; close it and the energy disappears from ordinary space. Some streets permit only one direction of causality. A crack may be repaired before it forms, though the sound of its formation still arrives later. There are chambers in which dust rises into footprints belonging to no visible walker, each print fading just before the next appears.

This does not necessarily mean the builders remain. The city may itself be the builder, continuing a plan whose purpose required no population. It may be an instrument for steering the mountain’s growth, using streets as stress channels and towers as anchors in dimensions the rock cannot enter. It may be a settlement waiting for occupants compatible with its impossible density of space. Or perhaps the apparent buildings are only the machine’s internal texture, interpreted as urban because avenues and rooms are the nearest familiar comparison.

Near the city’s innermost point is a chamber smaller than the entrance hollow. Its walls are raw volcanic glass. Suspended at its center is a bead of water, warm and perfectly spherical. All the city’s impossible perspectives seem to converge in its reflection: every empty street, every dim tower, every bridge spanning volumes larger than the mountain.

The bead never evaporates.

When the mountain rises, it trembles. When a new fissure opens, it becomes slightly larger. During the rare rain it darkens, though no additional drop reaches it. Around it, delicate structures assemble themselves from ceramic grains, persist for a few hours, and return to powder. They look less like buildings than like proposals for buildings—small rehearsals of districts not yet made.

Here the city is most active and most empty. Nothing lives, nothing visits, and nothing visibly commands it. Yet construction continues. Streets refine their alignments. Fresh doors appear in blank walls. The mountain grows around the hidden urban hollow, while the hollow quietly edits the mountain in return.

From outside, the summit remains a bare black fang above the clouds.

Only at night, when internal pressure is high, a hairline violet glow appears along one seam. Condensation forms there, gathers into a single drop, and falls upward into the dark.
