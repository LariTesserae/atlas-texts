The high country is entirely built, though nothing here has ever known a tool.

It occupies a broad shelf midway up an otherwise unseen range, warm despite its elevation, open to a pale, ordinary sky. The shelf extends for many leagues: terraces, shallow basins, long causeways, detached towers, and spacious courts laid out with an unmistakable intention. Yet there is no stone beneath them. There may be no stone anywhere.

Everything is plant.

The ground is a continuous load-bearing mat of fused roots, pale and hard near the surface, dark and water-swollen below. Its upper layers have grown into slabs so regular that they resemble masonry: broad polygonal plates joined by green seams. Where a plate has split, there is no rubble. The exposed edge is fibrous, damp, and faintly translucent, and tiny white rootlets stitch the opening without ever quite closing it. These breaks look new, as though the whole plateau has only recently emerged from its mold, but they remain unchanged year after year. Formation here is not a phase. It is the permanent condition of things.

Across the plateau stand structures grown from immense vascular plants. Their trunks rise in square columns, flare into buttresses, and meet overhead in distant ribs. Some form halls without walls. Others outline roofless compounds around empty courts. A few have been persuaded—or have persuaded themselves—into bridges: rigid arches of lacquer-brown wood spanning wetlands full of their own submerged roots. Their surfaces carry bands, moldings, recesses, and repeated leaf-shaped apertures. No hand carved these features. They are growth habits, as inherent as branching.

The result is architecture without builders, construction without technology. The distinction between organism and edifice has no meaning here.

Vegetation is nearly total, but the country does not feel crowded. Immensity separates its forms. A single colonnade may cross half a valley, each column standing hundreds of paces from the next. Between them stretch open floors of low moss, waxy liverwort, and red-veined ground leaves pressed flat enough to walk upon. Towering seed-vaults are scattered on the horizon like abandoned keeps. Each is alive, each hollow, each holding a dark vertical lake inside its stem.

Water is everywhere without becoming a sea. It lies close beneath the root pavement and rises through pores in a constant cool sweat. Clear beads gather on railings of vine and on the undersides of broad leaves, swell to identical size, then hang there indefinitely. The larger courts contain shallow pools whose margins are precise circles or long rounded rectangles. Their bottoms are lined with blue-black bladder tissue. Pale reeds emerge at measured intervals, never clustering, so that every stem has a wide jurisdiction of reflected sky.

Along the plateau’s edge, water passes through walls of braided roots and appears as curtains of droplets. These waterfalls do not fall. Every bead occupies a fixed place in the air, forming luminous chains from the weeping wall to the basin below. Seen from a distance they resemble glass porticos. Up close, each drop trembles violently without descending.

The air is warm, moist, and breathable, rich with the green odor of crushed stems although nothing is being crushed. Condensation softens distant structures. A tower may appear white and insubstantial at noon, then darken as one approaches, resolving into overlapping leaves folded so densely that they support entire gardens. Light is unremarkable in source and color: gentle daylight, neither theatrical nor dim. Its normality makes the plateau stranger. Nothing hides in shadow. The impossible is plainly visible.

There is little soil. In sheltered seams, a brown substance resembling soil accumulates, but it is itself alive: minute root hairs, spores, pollen husks still metabolizing, and soft granular nodules that contract around moisture. There is no dead matter in the usual sense. Fallen leaves remain connected by threads too fine to see. Wood never rots; it loosens into a network of smaller living bodies. Even the clear water contains suspended green cells arranged in slow spirals.

The plateau seems newly made. Ridges are blunt and unweathered. Drainage channels begin suddenly and end just as suddenly, their edges soft, their beds unscoured. Long folds push up under the root pavement like mountains considering whether to exist. Elsewhere, embryonic ravines are indicated by parallel creases no deeper than a finger joint. They never deepen. There is water enough for erosion, yet erosion has barely begun and cannot proceed.

Nothing proceeds.

The landscape is not frozen. Leaves are supple. Water trembles. Fine tendrils bow as though under passing wind. Pollen hangs in the air. Membranes pulse visibly in the translucent walls. But these motions repeat within extremely narrow bounds and produce no consequence. A vine leans outward, returns, leans outward. A drop lengthens, rounds, lengthens. A flower opens by the width of a hair and closes by the same amount. There is activity everywhere and change nowhere.

This equilibrium has no perceptible day of origin. No leaf yellows. No pool rises after rain, though the air always smells post-rain. No tower grows taller, although pale tissues at its crown have the tender color of new growth. The entire region is caught in the posture of becoming.

It is also loud.

Water voices fill every open space: drumming inside hollow trunks, rushing beneath the pavements, knocking rhythmically in root pipes, roaring behind sealed green walls. Yet the visible water barely moves. The sound seems to belong not to motion but to pressure—to immense quantities of water forever attempting to go somewhere. Low notes travel through the living ground and enter the bones through the feet. In the great halls, suspended droplets produce a continuous metallic hiss. The reed courts give off single booming tones, each stem sounding at a different pitch, together forming chords too large to recognize as music.

Plant sounds layer over these. Seed-vaults crack without opening. Fibers creak under loads that never shift. Broad leaves clap though they never touch. Far away, the plateau’s buried root mass makes a sustained noise like a forest falling, but nothing falls. Silence exists only in small, sharply bounded patches: circles around certain white flowers, narrow strips beneath archways, the interiors of unopened buds. Crossing into one of these zones feels less like losing sound than entering a substance that excludes it.

Animals are rare enough to seem accidental.

A long-legged amphibian sometimes stands in a pool court, pearl-gray and almost without features. It does not hunt. No insects gather around it. It may remain with one foot raised for years, skin exchanging moisture with the air, neither hungry nor asleep. In the upper ribs of the largest halls live small black creatures that might be birds or winged seeds. They travel the same short route between the same two perches, their wingbeats contributing a steady stitch to the greater noise.

There are also transparent grazing things under the root pavement. They can be seen only where the floor thins above submerged chambers: slow outlines passing through amber water, each with a green plant growing through its center. Whether the creature carries the plant or the plant constructs the creature is unclear. Their paths form sparse loops, and no track deviates.

Nothing appears to eat anything else. Life is sustained by circulation rather than consumption. Water moves—or intends to move—through every body and structure, carrying sugars, salts, warmth, and sound. The plateau may be one organism divided into architectural gestures. Its distant towers could be organs. Its courts could be exposed chambers. The bridge groves might be joints between sections of a body too broad to perceive.

From the highest accessible terrace, the full arrangement becomes partly visible. The region spreads across a succession of warm upland basins, green structures standing far apart in pale mist. Causeways connect them in a branching pattern that is almost leaf venation and almost the plan of an enormous city. Beyond, higher shelves appear through condensation, each bearing the same sparse monuments and geometric wetlands. Below, the land descends in unfinished steps toward clouds pooled in the valleys.

There are no ruins because nothing has aged. There are no construction sites because everything is already complete. Yet every surface carries the freshness of something just uncovered: wet grain, pale seams, curled edges, tissues not hardened into their final state. The world feels assembled a moment ago and kept at that moment by a rule deeper than time.

One can travel here, but departure is difficult in an unusual way. Paths do not move or rearrange. Distance does not distort. Instead, every route outward is also a structural line inward. Causeways divide, join, and return with botanical logic. Following a road is like tracing the vein of a leaf: one reaches finer branches, margins, and tips, but never truly leaves the leaf.

At the outermost terraces, where the shelf appears to end, enormous root walls descend into bright vapor. Water beads stand along them in vertical constellations. Warm air rises from below carrying the same thunder heard underfoot. From gaps in the mist come glimpses of further arches, inverted or sideways, suggesting that the plateau is not resting on a mountain.

It may be the mountain.

Or the visible country may be only the upper surface of a vast living construction, still embryonic, perfectly finished, and eternally on the verge of beginning.
