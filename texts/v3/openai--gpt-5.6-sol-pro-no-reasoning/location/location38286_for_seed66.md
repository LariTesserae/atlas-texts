The city lies across a high plateau so broad that its edges vanish beneath weather. It is not built on the plateau so much as pressed into it: masonry, slag, ceramic, rust-dark iron, and native stone compacted into one continuous crust. District follows district beyond visibility. Chimneys rise by the thousand, aqueducts cross one another at several heights, and roofs descend into basins where warm rainwater gathers in shallow silver sheets.

There is no vegetation. No moss softens a gutter, no weed exploits a crack. Courtyards remain bare mineral even after centuries of neglect. The usual green haze of a wet place is absent, making every distance strangely legible: black retaining walls, pale lime deposits, ocher runoff, copper roofs turned not green but sealed beneath gray oxides. Wherever masonry splits, the opening reveals more masonry below, or bedrock scored by old tools. The city appears never to have possessed soil.

Yet it is crowded with life.

Animals fill almost every space not occupied by stone or machinery. They gather on ledges in layered multitudes, sleep beneath warm pipes, pack the vaulted interiors of unused stations, and move along the canals in dense, deliberate streams. Most are small: pale six-legged grazers rasping salt films from walls; flat-bodied things clinging beneath bridges; black, jointed runners that travel in columns along expansion joints. Above them swarm gliders with translucent skins, rising on the heated breath of factories and settling over towers until entire facades seem furred and trembling.

The larger creatures belong to the streets. Broad-backed, slow-footed animals push through flooded avenues, their hides plated like kiln brick and polished along the flanks by generations of contact with walls. They travel fixed routes between cisterns, squeezing beneath arches built to their dimensions. Herds can take hours to pass. Citizens, where there are citizens, wait on elevated walkways while the animals move below with the grave momentum of barges.

No one agrees whether the city was designed around this fauna or whether the fauna inherited and reshaped it. Doorways occur at several scales. Some stairs are too shallow for a human stride but exactly suit the many feet of the wall-runners. Vast ramps descend into warm underground chambers whose floors are grooved by claws. Along the oldest viaducts, stone troughs carry mineral slurry rather than water, and immense beaked animals stand shoulder to shoulder, filtering it for microscopic life.

The world is warm despite the altitude. Heat rises from the rock itself, conducted through deep foundations and released by vents, boilers, cracked conduits, and natural fissures. Rain falls frequently, but seldom cold. It arrives as fat, widely spaced drops from low clouds formed within the city’s own atmosphere. Water strikes dustless stone, runs briefly black, then clears. It pours from carved mouths, ticks down hanging chains, and disappears into gratings polished by centuries of flow.

Much of it never reaches the drainage works. It beads on warm walls, condenses beneath bridges, and seeps from the plateau through long horizontal fractures. Whole cliffs weep. Beneath the city’s western wards, water emerges in curtains no thicker than cloth, descending over terraces of calcite into reservoirs roofed with smoke. In low districts the paving stones are permanently submerged by a finger’s depth. Animals move there with soft splashing sounds, while wheels pass on raised iron tracks.

The plateau has been weathered into equilibrium. Nothing looks newly ruined. Corners are rounded, inscriptions reduced to shallow shadows, and old industrial embankments have slumped into stable angles that no longer change from one lifetime to the next. Rust has gone through its violent stage and become dense, dark, protective skin. Broken towers lean against neighboring towers and have done so for hundreds of years. Their contact points are polished smooth by minute thermal movement.

The city’s erosion is not destruction but settlement. Rain finds channels worn precisely to its volume. Footfalls have hollowed the paving to match habitual migrations. Chimney smoke has etched ceilings into scalloped caverns. Even collapse has acquired conventions: walls fail along ancient seams and come to rest on buttresses constructed by earlier failures.

Industry continues inside this weathered body, though slowly and on a scale that makes individual action difficult to perceive. Engines turn under entire districts. Their pistons may complete one stroke in several minutes, lifting groundwater into high cisterns through pipes broad enough to contain streets. Chainworks pass overhead with the patience of celestial mechanisms, each link the size of a room. Kilns glow behind latticed walls, firing replacement blocks from powdered ruins and deep-quarried clay. Foundries pour metal only a few times each year, but when they do, orange light moves through the canals at night and the local rain becomes briefly metallic.

Power travels by heavy exposed conductors strung from tower to tower. In wet weather they hum blue at their joints. This electricity is not clean or silent: relay houses clap like distant thunder, dynamos groan below the paving, and porcelain insulators crack with sharp reports. But the system is old and carefully balanced. No machine runs faster than its foundations can tolerate. Speed is treated as a form of structural ignorance.

The loudness of the place comes less from sudden events than from accumulation. Rain on iron roofs. Water in conduits. Claws on stone. Wings slapping in shafts. Boilers breathing. Herds calling to one another through long horns in their skulls. Chains dragging over wheels. A million small mouths scraping mineral films. Every sound is moderate by itself, but there is almost nowhere from which fewer than a hundred sources can be heard.

Together they form a continuous, inhabited roar.

Conversation takes place in niches lined with felted animal membrane or behind thick ceramic shutters. Signals are commonly visual: semaphore arms on rooftops, colored lamps under arches, rotating mirrored panels flashing across rain-dark districts. At noon, when the cloud deck thins and ordinary sunlight reaches the plateau, thousands of shutters open in sequence. The movement passes over the city like a slow geometric wave.

The light is otherwise plain and honest. Stone looks like stone. Water shines where it should. Shadows gather beneath bridges without becoming supernatural. The city’s strangeness lies not in distortion but in proportion: too much animal life where there is no plant life, too much construction to have a discernible center, too much age for anything to feel abandoned.

The ecology begins in the warm mineral water. Transparent microbial skins grow inside pipes, in seepage lines, and across the submerged walls of cisterns. They are nearly invisible except for a faint opalescence. They feed on dissolved sulfur, iron, electrical leakage, and compounds vented from the deep plateau. Tiny rasping animals feed on these skins; larger animals filter or hunt the raspers; the city’s aerial swarms rise from flooded basements where their young develop in rust-colored pools.

Nothing feeds on sunlight. The sun marks time, warms upper masonry, and guides migrations, but it is not the foundation of life. The true fields are the pipes. Reservoir interiors are pastures. Condenser vaults are breeding grounds. Corroding metal is fertile ground in the only sense the city recognizes.

For this reason, infrastructure and ecology cannot be separated. A clogged drain may starve a district. A broken electrical cable can produce a bloom of conductive microbes, followed years later by an increase in gliders. Herds are permitted through pumping stations because their plated bodies carry spores—or eggs, or mineral seeds—from basin to basin. The largest factories employ whole colonies of heat-loving animals to clean boiler scale from unreachable chambers.

The inhabitants maintain boundaries, but not dominion. Low walls redirect migrations. Bells warn of nesting seasons. Streets are closed for molting, spawning, or the emergence of subterranean broods. Buildings are sealed not against animals in general, which would be impossible, but against particular sizes and temperatures of animal. Every grate, sill, and vent expresses a negotiated admission.

Beyond the central constructions, the plateau breaks into ravines. The city crosses them without thinning. Bridges support neighborhoods; neighborhoods support reservoirs; reservoirs feed waterfalls that descend through inhabited cliff faces. Far below, warm mist conceals the bottom. Calls rise from it at intervals, resonant enough to shake droplets from the railings.

At the plateau’s rim, erosion has exposed the city’s depth. Layer beneath layer appears in the cliffs: current streets, buried streets, aqueducts sealed within older aqueducts, foundations cut through forgotten chambers, natural basalt threaded with pipes. Fossilized machines protrude from the rock. Some still turn when groundwater fills their chambers. Others have become dens for pale, heatless creatures whose shells resemble bolts and washers.

From a distance, the plateau might be mistaken for a geological formation. Its towers are worn into buttes; its smoke merges with cloud; its outer walls descend in strata. Only the regular intervals of bridges and the night lines of amber windows reveal intention.

Change comes, but slowly. A district floods over thirty years. A migration shifts one avenue east in the course of a century. A tower bends by the width of a hand between generations, and engineers construct an entire buttressed quarter to receive it. Species become fitted to one valve shape or one furnace rhythm, then spread along new conduits at the pace of construction.

Rain falls. Engines lift it. Animals drink, filter, carry, excrete, and redistribute its minerals. Masonry dissolves and is fired again. Iron rusts, is eaten, collected, smelted, and returned to the roofs. The city does not seem to advance toward either completion or ruin.

It has become a climate, a watershed, and a food chain made partly of architecture.

And across its high, wet immensities, under ordinary daylight and low warm cloud, the patient noise continues.
