The low country begins as a dark openness under a ceiling of cloud. There is no horizon in the ordinary sense. Distance is repeatedly interrupted by mineral growths: crowded blades of slate, blunt columns, heaps of glassy rubble, and pale reefs of stone packed so closely that the land appears filled rather than covered. Between them lie shallow sheets of water. None is quite large enough to be called a lake, yet together they occupy much of the plain, joining and separating according to pressures too slight to see.

The ground is newly made. Its edges have not been softened into soil. Black shelves break at clean angles; fractures run bright and raw through the rock, exposing interiors of chalk-white salt and blue-gray crystal. In places the crust is thin enough to flex under its own weight. Water rises through the cracks, gathers in trembling beads, and spills over the lowest lips. It tastes of iron, gypsum, and something bitter enough to numb the tongue.

Nothing grows here.

There is no moss in the seams, no grass on the flats, no stain of lichen even on the oldest-looking faces. Where color appears, it belongs to chemistry: green copper haze inside a pool, rust-orange fans around a seep, violet films that vanish when disturbed. Some surfaces bear pearly membranes only a fraction of a finger thick. They resemble biological skins until they split along geometric lines and disclose nests of salt needles underneath.

The air is cool and almost saturated. Moisture condenses continuously on the upright stones. Their inward-sloping faces weep, so that every crowded passage contains its own fine rain. Drops hang from the undersides of ledges for minutes before falling. Fog occupies the lower hollows and runs through them without wind, pouring slowly from basin to basin like a pale, reluctant liquid. Above it, the taller formations stand as islands.

The noise is immense.

It does not come from storms or moving water. The whole plain resonates. Beneath the shallow crust, confined water passes through chambers in the cooling stone. Pressure enters one cavity, escapes another, and sets the mineral plates trembling. Deep notes roll underfoot for hours at a time. Narrow fissures answer with whistles; hollow pillars moan; crystal-packed clefts emit a hard, continuous ringing like innumerable wet glasses touched at their rims.

Near the central flats the formations stand so densely that their vibrations cross and interfere. A traveler there would hear beats passing through the landscape—slow pulses swelling out of the general roar, fading, then returning from another direction. Loose shards chatter without visibly moving. Pools stipple themselves in perfect concentric rings. Sometimes every hanging drop on a particular wall falls at once.

For all its loudness, the place changes slowly. A crack may take a season to travel the width of a slab. A basin deepens by dissolving from below, leaving its rim sharp until the moment the unsupported floor gives way. Mineral towers accrue skin after translucent skin from the evaporating seepage. Their surfaces look freshly poured, though some have stood for centuries. Erosion here is less a wearing-down than an unfinished argument between water and stone. Water hollows; minerals return and seal the hollows; pressure opens them elsewhere.

Across the broader plain, scattered structures break the natural repetition. They are small and severe: low rings of fitted basalt, roofless chambers sunk half below the flats, and occasional door-shaped frames standing alone among the mineral blades. No metal is present. No tool marks can be found. The blocks fit by matching their natural fractures, as if selected rather than cut.

Within the chambers, the roar diminishes. Their walls are built around pockets of still air, and each stone seems chosen to cancel the note of its neighbor. Condensation runs down the inner faces and collects in shallow central depressions. Some hold clear water. Others are full of hexagonal crystals that have risen almost to the rim.

There are no roads connecting these shelters, only faint sequences of dark stepping-stones through the flooded hollows. They may not be roads at all. The stones could be the exposed crests of buried ridges, mistaken for intention because they recur at roughly walking intervals.

Rare animals inhabit the water beneath the crust. They are seldom seen whole. A pallid joint folds away when light enters a fissure; a smooth back disturbs a pool and is gone. After long periods of quiet, clusters of tiny holes appear in fresh mineral film, each surrounded by a ring of delicate scrapings. Whatever makes them has no evident need to visit the air.

Farther out, where the fog is deepest, larger presences can sometimes be heard moving between pools. Their passage is slow: one displacement of water, a pause of several minutes, then another. They never call. The landscape calls enough for everything.

The dimness has no clear source. Daylight filters through the cloud as a diffuse pewter glow, while the pools return a weaker light from below. At certain depths, submerged crystals gather the gray illumination and release it gradually, so that evening is followed not by darkness but by a cold, drowned radiance. The undersides of the fog banks turn blue. Sharp stones become silhouettes against shining water.

In that hour the isolated frames matter most. Seen through vapor, each appears to contain a different brightness from the country behind it. One opens onto deeper dusk. Another seems full of the pallor of morning. The effect persists only from a distance; approach, and the opening contains ordinary fog and crowded stone.

This is perhaps the governing rule of the plain: separation creates difference, and nearness abolishes it. Two pools divided by a handspan of rock may have different colors, temperatures, even apparent depths, but once a channel forms between them they become indistinguishable. Two resonant pillars sing separate notes until a mineral bridge grows across the gap; then both fall silent, or begin a third note belonging to neither. The scattered chambers preserve stillness only while their walls remain unbroken.

Accordingly, the landscape is always tending toward sameness and always fracturing away from it. Water joins what stone divides. Deposition divides what water has joined. The cycle is patient, nearly motionless, but everywhere at once.

From a high shelf—one of the few rises in the low country—the full region resembles a drowned city before the invention of streets: innumerable vertical forms, shallow shining squares, roofless enclosures, and corridors too narrow to enter. Yet there was never a city here. The resemblance comes from density, from repetition, from the mind insisting that crowded forms must have been arranged.

Beyond the visible plain, the resonant structures continue into the fog. Their combined voice loses detail with distance and becomes a single low pressure in the chest. It is possible to stand still for so long that the sound ceases to feel external. Then the wet stone underfoot, the water sealed below it, the mineral spires, and the cavities of the body all seem to belong to one slow instrument.

Nothing conducts it. Nothing listens on purpose.

Still, the plain continues its unfinished note.
