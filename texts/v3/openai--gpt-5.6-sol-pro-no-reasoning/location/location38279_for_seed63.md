The place is a high basin no larger than a walled precinct, sunk into the crown of a black plateau. Its rim is close enough to walk in an hour, though no path remains open for more than a few steps. Everything is occupied.

There is no water. The basin has perhaps never held it. Its floor is a compacted salt of sulfur, iron, and pale mineral ash, baked into plates that fit together like old masonry. Hairline fissures breathe heat. At intervals, a seam opens with a dull red glow, then seals as its edges soften and slump together. The air is dry enough to sting exposed tissue and hot enough that the plateau’s night offers little relief.

Above, the sky is hidden by ash and by the basin’s inhabitants.

They are animals, or near enough to animals that their hunger, mating, sleep, and fear can be recognized. They cover every surface. They stand layered on one another when the ground is full. They cling to the inward cliffs, fill cracks, hang in linked curtains beneath shelves of stone, and wedge themselves into the hollow mineral chimneys clustered near the basin’s center. There are no plants among them, no lichen, no visible fungus, no soft green intermediary between rock and appetite. The entire ecology passes directly from mineral, heat, and gas into moving flesh.

Most of the creatures are small. Palm-sized shieldbacks make up the living ground: broad, low bodies enclosed in overlapping plates of dull ceramic. Their six legs end in glassy hooks that scrape microscopic crystals from the salt crust. They do not eat the crystals exactly. Chambers in their bellies hold colonies of simpler beings—grains of dark living matter—which use the basin’s heat and sulfurous exhalations to make nourishment. When millions of shieldbacks feed together, the floor appears to crawl in one slow direction, like a shadow cast by something enormous.

Between them travel thread-runners, long as arms and no thicker than wire. They step without touching the ground, placing their many feet on the armored backs below. Their bodies knot and unknot through the crowd. They drink neither blood nor water; instead, they pierce the shieldbacks’ breathing sacs and steal warm reactive gases before those gases reach the inner colonies. A bitten shieldback seldom dies immediately. It simply slows, is climbed over, and vanishes under the traffic.

The predators are fewer only by comparison. Bell-mouthed hunters crouch inside cavities in the rock, indistinguishable from eroded stone until they open. Their circular jaws make no roar. They draw one abrupt breath, and everything loose within reach—ash, thread-runners, shell fragments, juveniles—is pulled into them. Afterward they close and become geology again.

Higher on the basin walls, translucent sails spread from jointed bodies. These animals harvest the upward breath of the fissures. They neither fly nor rest, but remain tethered by cords sunk deep into cracks. Hot currents inflate them. In darkness their membranes show faint interior traces: amber arteries, slow blue digestive sparks, pearly knots of eggs. When the thermal draft weakens, entire colonies collapse against the cliff with the hush of cloth. When it returns, they rise together and roof the basin.

This is why the place is dark. The sun may shine above the ash layer, but almost none of it reaches the floor. What illumination exists comes from below—from red fissures, from chemical glimmers inside transparent bodies, and from brief pale flashes made when shell strikes shell. Distances are perceived less by sight than by heat. Nearby surfaces press their warmth against the face. Moving swarms pass as cooler patches. A predator’s opening mouth is first known as a sudden absence.

Despite the number of creatures, the basin is mostly quiet. Dry life does not squelch or splash. There are no leaves to rustle and no streams to speak continuously. Millions of feet make a soft granular whisper, easily mistaken for settling ash. Now and then comes the porcelain click of a cracked carapace, the papery collapse of the wall-sails, or a deep internal knock from beneath the crust. The loudest events are brief: a fissure snapping open; a whole packed slope losing its purchase; thousands of bodies cascading a few yards before wedging into a new arrangement.

Nothing has room to fall far.

The basin’s density gives it peculiar rules. Empty ground is treated as a disaster and is filled almost at once. When a hot vent kills everything around it, a bare circle appears for perhaps a minute. Then shieldbacks mass at its edge, climbing over those in front until the circle closes. Eggs are laid in the seams between living bodies. The dead are not left exposed. Scavengers dismantle them where they lie, passing shell and tissue inward through the crowd until only their mineral-heavy teeth remain. These teeth accumulate in drifts along subtle depressions, pale ridges that mark currents in the swarm rather than currents of air.

Motion here is not freedom but exchange. One thing advances only because another is consumed, lifted, displaced, hatched, or crushed.

The climate also moves through the basin in cycles. At long intervals the plateau flexes, compressing buried chambers. Sulfurous gas seeps upward and the shieldback multitudes converge upon the active cracks. Their shells darken as they absorb heat. The tethered sails swell overhead. Predators open. Reproductive forms, dormant for months inside the mineral chimneys, split their cases and emerge in coordinated waves: blind, many-winged bodies that have no mouths and live only a few hours.

They fly badly because the air is thin at this elevation. Mostly they collide.

Their courtship is conducted through impacts. Each carries a resonant chamber in its thorax, and compatible pairs identify one another by the faint note produced when their armored foreheads strike. The basin fills with isolated ticks, widely spaced and delicate despite the frenzy. After mating, the winged forms descend into cooler cracks, where they break apart at predetermined joints. Their living segments crawl separately into the stone and become the next generation’s eggs. The empty wings remain on the surface until other animals eat the membranes.

Along the western wall stand the scattered remains of structures. There are only seven, and none is intact. Each is a narrow arch built from fitted slabs of the same black rock as the plateau. No tool marks can be found. Their proportions suggest entrances, but behind them is solid stone. Some have weathered almost flush with the cliff; others lean outward, held from falling by the packed bodies around their bases.

The animals avoid nothing else, yet they do not cross beneath these arches.

They swarm over the sides and cluster on top. Thread-runners bridge the openings. Wall-sails anchor above them. But the small wedge of mineral floor framed by each arch remains empty, polished by age and hot to the touch. No barrier is visible. Carcasses thrown there are removed from the far side without any creature entering the enclosed space. During gas season, the mouthless fliers strike unseen limits beneath the arches and drop.

This avoidance is older than any individual lineage in the basin. It may not be memory. Perhaps the arches frame a kind of heat that flesh can sense and stone cannot. Perhaps they mark wounds in the place’s geometry. Or perhaps they are not structures at all, but the exposed ribs of something too deeply buried to be counted among the fauna.

Erosion proceeds without rain. Hot wind works on the plateau above, polishing the rim and bringing fine ash down in slow curtains. Acid gases etch the cliffs from within. Bodies abrade the basin more efficiently than weather: hooks scratching, shells grinding, mouthparts rasping mineral into edible dust. Over ages, the swarm has reshaped its enclosure. Shelves have been rounded to match clustered bodies. Passages are exactly wide enough for recurring migrations. The stone chimneys are laminated with discarded inner shells until geology and residue cannot be separated.

The basin is established in this state. It does not feel newly overrun, nor does it suggest a lost greener age. Its balances are old. The crowd is not an outbreak but the landscape’s mature condition. Rock becomes gas-fed life; life becomes shell; shell becomes floor; the floor cracks and feeds the next innumerable generation.

Beyond the rim lies a mostly empty upland of black glass and red dust. Nothing visible grows or walks there. The contrast is severe: an entire barren horizon surrounding one cramped pocket of impossible abundance. Occasionally the wind carries a lightweight shell over the lip. It skitters across the outer plateau for miles, the only moving thing under the dark sky.

Nothing living follows it.

Inside, there is no horizon. There are warm walls, low roofs of breathing membrane, mineral towers, seven forbidden arches, and the pressure of countless bodies. The place shifts continuously without seeming to change. Its darkness is crowded. Its silence is busy. Every surface is alive, and every life is standing on another.
