The hollow lies high on the mountain, though nothing within it gives a view of height. Its boundaries are close and complete: a ring of old stone no more than a short walk across, folded inward like the palm of a weathered hand. Beyond the rim there may be peaks, cloud, open sky, but from the floor they are concealed. The walls lean over themselves in shelves and rounded bulges, packed with roots, moss, liverwort, and the dark fans of ferns. There is almost no bare surface.

It is always night here.

Not night in the ordinary sense, advancing and withdrawing, but a settled darkness belonging to the hollow itself. Light enters only as a weak mineral pallor caught in wet stone. Even at noon above, the floor remains blue-black, its shapes disclosed by sheen rather than illumination: the silver skin of a pool, the gloss of a salamander’s flank, droplets standing along a leaf edge. There are no moving shadows because there is no moving source of light.

The air is cool and saturated. Breath does not vanish into it so much as join it. Moisture gathers continually on fur, skin, bark, and the fine hairs of plants, yet no fresh rain seems necessary. The stone weeps without increasing the pools. Pools receive water without rising. Narrow runnels descend the walls without deepening their grooves or emptying into anything visible. All water in the hollow appears to be part of one closed circulation, though no current can be detected. A bead swells at the point of a root, hangs there, and neither falls nor dries.

Underfoot, the ground is intricate and nearly impassable. There is no open soil. Cushion mosses rise against one another in tight, rounded colonies; horsetails emerge between them; clubmoss crawls across saturated wood; pale fungi occupy the remaining seams. Dwarf alders and mountain birches grow low and contorted, their trunks embedded in green layers so thick that branch and ground are difficult to distinguish. Roots cross roots. Stems thread through old stems. Every surface carries another surface.

The hollow is anciently eroded, but erosion has reached its final form. Its walls bear flutes, bowls, scallops, and narrow channels made by water over immeasurable seasons, all softened until no edge looks newly broken. Fallen stones have settled into sockets shaped precisely to receive them. Slabs overlap like scales. Nothing is loose. Nothing crumbles. The place has been worn down to an arrangement in which every piece supports every other piece, and further weathering has nowhere to go.

Animal life fills what vegetation does not.

At first the hollow seems quiet enough to be empty. Then the density becomes apparent. Small frogs sit beneath nearly every fern, their eyes reflecting dimly. Newts rest in the pools in stacked, motionless layers. Beetles cling under leaves; snails cover trunks until bark appears plated; moths hold themselves against the rock with wings the color of lichen. Fine white worms inhabit the water films. Sleeping birds crowd the alder branches, breast to breast, claws closed around twigs. Tiny mountain bats hang among the roots that descend from the overhangs. In the moss are voles, shrews, blind crickets, soft-bodied mites, and small furred things difficult to classify because only noses or closed eyes show.

There are larger inhabitants as well, but the chamber permits few of them. A pale deer stands in one recess with its legs sunk to the knees in moss. Its antlers are short, velveted, and crowded with resting moths. Along another wall lies a dark-coated bear, fitted into a basin in the stone so exactly that it might have grown there. Frogs sit on its back. Ferns lean across its paws. It breathes, but with such regularity that the rhythm disappears into stillness.

Predator and prey remain close without pursuit. A stoat lies curled beside a vole colony. A fish rests with an insect larva between its jaws but neither swallows nor releases it. Spiders hold complete webs jewelled with moisture, and flies remain suspended in the strands. This is not peace brought about by gentleness. Teeth, venom, hunger, and fear all exist here. They are simply held in equilibrium. No creature grows thinner. No creature multiplies beyond the room allotted to it. Eggs remain warm and unhatched; old animals remain old but do not fail.

The collective sound is surprisingly small. Water should drip, wings should stir, throats should call, but the hollow suppresses event. There is the nearly inaudible respiration of thousands of bodies, a soft continuous pressure like wind heard through deep earth. Occasionally a frog gives one low note. It does not echo or provoke an answer. It seems less a call than a property of the place, recurring without becoming more frequent or less.

No structure stands here. There are no walls except the mountain’s own, no path except temporary impressions in moss that close almost before they can be seen. Yet there are signs of simple intelligence—not artifacts, exactly, but uses.

In one corner, slender apes or long-fingered mountain lemurs keep sharp flakes of naturally fractured stone beneath an overhang. They have not shaped the flakes, only sorted them. Otters wedge shells into fixed cracks in the rock, though the shells never quite break. Ravens place straight twigs together according to length. A colony of small primates twists fallen fibers into cords, then untwists them in the same sequence, endlessly preserving the knowledge of cord without allowing a finished object to remain. Tool use exists here as gesture, remembered and repeated, but it accumulates into no industry. The hollow admits technique and refuses history.

Even movement has this character. A beetle crosses a patch of lichen, but later it is beneath the same curled leaf where it has always been. The pale deer lowers its head toward a pool; it never seems to drink, yet its muzzle is always wet. Wings open and close without transporting their owners. If one watches long enough, there are changes everywhere and alteration nowhere.

This is the hollow’s unusual law: processes occur, but states remain.

Water runs, but its volume and channels are constant. Creatures breathe, feed, sleep, and watch, but none arrive or depart. Leaves unfold in the darkness, already bearing the small holes that insects will make in them. Rot softens wood without consuming it. Fungal threads dismantle fallen trunks while the trunks retain their weight and form. Every action is balanced at the instant of happening by an equal restoration too subtle to witness.

Nothing here is frozen. Frozen things are interrupted. The hollow is complete.

A visitor could enter only with difficulty, descending the wet ring of stone through roots crowded with quiet animals. The moment of arrival would seem a disturbance: birds opening their eyes, frogs pressing lower, the bear drawing one deep breath. But soon the stranger’s warmth would disperse evenly through the cool air. Footprints would lift. Broken moss would meet seamlessly behind the heel. Hunger would cease to sharpen, though it would not disappear. Tools carried in would feel conspicuous and temporary, their edges too new for the settled surfaces around them.

Given enough time—whether an hour or a lifetime—the visitor might begin to occupy a space that had somehow always been reserved: a depression between roots, just wide enough for a body; a stone hollow holding the exact curve of a hand; a gap in the breathing chorus where another breath fits without changing its sound.

Above, weather may travel over the mountain. Snow may fall, thaw, and fall again. Valleys may deepen. Forests may migrate upslope and retreat. None of this reaches the hollow except as coolness and water, both already present in their final measure.

Within the ring, the innumerable lives remain packed together beneath the permanent dark, awake and sleeping, hungry and sustained, using and being used, while the wet stone keeps the shape that ages have given it.

Nothing is absent.

Nothing increases.

Nothing ends.
