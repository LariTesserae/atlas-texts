The place is no larger than a walled orchard, though nothing grows here that could properly be called a tree. It occupies a shallow basin of fitted stone under a permanent roof of dark timber. Every surface has been made by hand: the sloping floor, the retaining walls, the rafters disappearing into soot, even the channels where water runs. There is no exposed earth. Where the paving has sunk, more paving lies beneath it—older courses of tile and flat river stone, pressed down through generations until the floor has acquired the subtle contours of natural ground.

Rain enters despite the roof.

It condenses on the underside of the rafters, gathers along pegs and crossbeams, then falls in slow, heavy drops. Some timbers have been carved with shallow grooves to guide it. Others have warped beyond their makers’ intentions, so water wanders: down pillars, across lintels, through rows of hanging clay cups, into troughs and sumps. The whole enclosure weeps.

The light is cool and insufficient. It comes through narrow slits beneath the eaves and through a smoke-hole high above the basin’s center. By day, those openings are pearl-gray; at night they are only lesser darkness. Small lamps sometimes burn in niches, but their fuel is rendered animal fat, and their flames are low, smoky, and often extinguished by dripping water. The stones shine without becoming bright.

There are remarkably few objects. A dozen pillars hold the roof. Three raised walkways cross the basin. Along the walls stand several empty shelters made from bent reeds plastered with clay, though reeds do not grow inside the enclosure and must once have been carried here. A large wheel occupies the lowest end, built of oak, pegged rather than nailed. It turns when the water rises. At the opposite end is a stepped spillway worn smooth by centuries of flow.

Between these presences lies emptiness: open paving, shallow black water, bare walls. The enclosure has the spareness of a ritual chamber, but it is far too noisy for reverence.

Animals fill it.

They live in cracks, troughs, rafters, pipes, cups, niches, and submerged galleries beneath the paving. Pale frogs sit in the carved gutters, their throats swelling like bladders. Slate-colored crabs move sideways across the walkways, tapping the stone with their claws. Long-bodied water mice travel in family lines along the wall ledges. Moths cluster under the roof in shaggy curtains. Blind eels knot themselves in the sumps. Beetles the size of fingertips labor over the wet tiles, rolling pellets of moss, shed skin, and dung.

Most numerous are the birds.

They resemble small black herons, though their legs are short and their beaks curve downward like awls. Hundreds nest among the rafters. Their woven cups hang over the chamber, dripping fibers and white waste. At intervals one bird begins to hammer its beak against a timber, and the rest answer. The resulting clatter rolls under the roof like hail on shields. They fish in the channels, stab at frogs, steal beetles from one another, and descend in sudden dark flurries whenever the wheel lifts something edible from below.

The wheel is never still for long.

Rainwater and seepage collect beneath the basin, then rise through stone-lined shafts. When enough weight enters the upper trough, the wheel lurches a quarter-turn. Its axle groans. Wooden teeth engage a row of mallets, which strike suspended slabs of different sizes. Their tones are deep, cracked, and enormous in the enclosed space. The oldest slabs have hollows worn into them by countless blows. No one now adjusts the mechanism. It continues because it is made simply: gravity, pegs, wet rope, seasoned wood, stone weights.

Its sounding governs the animals.

At the first low blow, the frogs dive. At the second, crabs emerge from the drains. On the quick succession that follows heavy condensation, the black birds leave the rafters in a single rotating flock. The mice freeze wherever they stand. Eels rise through grates and expose their mouths to the flowing water.

Whether the mechanism was built to command these behaviors, or the creatures merely learned the pattern, cannot be determined. The distinction may have ceased to matter generations ago.

There are no wild materials here, only materials that have gone wild.

The clay cups reproduce because birds carry their fragments into nests, where mineral-rich water cements them into new vessels. Moss appears only as a thin gray nap on old mortar; whenever it thickens, crabs graze it down. Fungus grows inside the timbers but rarely shows fruit. A few translucent stems rise from joints in the paving, leafless and soft. They feed on whatever sifts down from the roof and withdraw into the cracks when touched. Vegetation is present mostly as residue: fibers, films, rot, the memory of reeds in walls built long ago.

Yet the fauna is extravagant. Every vacant cavity has become a den or spawning chamber. Every drop of water is intercepted by a mouth. Predation is constant and audible. There are splashes, wingbeats, mating calls, shell clicks, the thin screaming of captured mice, the slap of eels in confined water, and beneath all of it the patient knock and groan of the wheel. Silence occurs only just before the mechanism turns, when some minute change in pressure passes through the floor and all the animals feel it.

The basin’s weather is local and repetitive. Moisture gathers in the cold upper dark. It rains. The channels fill. The wheel turns and rings the stones. Water spills down the worn steps, disappears beneath a gate, and returns later as seepage through the lower tiles. Sometimes a draft enters under the western eave and drives the condensation sideways. Sometimes the spillway backs up, flooding the entire floor ankle-deep. During these floods, the walkways become islands crowded with frogs, mice, crabs, and birds standing together in temporary truce.

Nothing here is truly large, but the enclosure feels larger than its boundaries because so much of it is concealed. The walls contain pipes whose routes no living inhabitant understands. The paving is hollow in places. Sounds travel underfoot and return from the wrong direction. If one puts an ear to the lowest stones, one can hear rushing water far below, as though the little basin were only the uppermost room of a drowned construction extending downward without end.

There are signs of former keepers. Handprints remain in plaster high on the eastern wall, each with six fingers—not anomalous prints, but dozens, belonging to adults and children. A stone knife lies tied by a cord beside one blocked sluice. The cord has been replaced many times by nesting birds, which braid it from hair and reed fiber. Around the central pillar, shallow tally marks circle the stone in bands. The lowest have nearly vanished under the polishing of wet bodies. The highest are sharp and unfinished.

No door is visible from within.

The enclosure is not ruined. Ruin implies departure from a proper state. This place has long since arrived at another kind of completion: wood swollen to its habitual shape, stones seated in their hollows, channels rounded by dependable water, animals fitted to every operation. Repair occurs without intention. Crabs clear the drains while feeding. Birds wedge loose rafters with their nests. Mineral deposits seal leaking joints. Eels dislodge sediment from the buried pipes. The made world maintains itself badly, noisily, but well enough.

Its principal rule is that nothing falls unused. Water turns the wheel or fills a throat. Droppings feed beetles. Beetles feed frogs and birds. Broken nests become dams. Dead birds descend through grates and return as pale movements in the sump. Even sound has become a resource: warning, courtship, clock, territorial boundary.

A second rule is that the architecture may change only by wearing away. No creature here can cut a new stone or raise a new pillar. They can clog, polish, undermine, stain, and rearrange. Thus every change is a form of erosion, and every eroded shape becomes useful. The low grooves in the walkways began as claw marks and now carry rivulets. The hollows under the pillars began as water damage and now shelter eggs. The basin grows more intricate by losing itself.

Near the spillway is the quietest point: a dry niche just above the usual flood line. Inside it sits a small figure assembled from fired-clay shards. It has six arms and a bird’s narrow head. No tool marks are visible because generations of moisture have softened its edges. The animals avoid it, though water mice leave bright shells at its feet.

When the wheel makes its deepest stroke, the figure trembles.

A bead of water forms at the tip of its beak, falls into a thumb-sized bowl, and sends out a sound almost too delicate to hear amid the clamor.

Everything pauses for it.

Then the frogs call, the rafters erupt, the channels overflow, and the little constructed world resumes its rain.
