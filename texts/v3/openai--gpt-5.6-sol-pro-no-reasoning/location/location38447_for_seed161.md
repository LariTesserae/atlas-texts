The town stands on the summit of a stone needle.

The needle rises from a warm, pale waste so deep below that the ground is usually lost in haze. From the town’s parapets, one sees other summits at great distances: blunt mesas, severed ridges, isolated pillars worn down to gray-red cores. Nothing connects them. The old land between has been taken away by ages of wind and heat, leaving only the hardest remnants. The town’s entire world is one of those remnants—a roughly oval crown of rock, scarcely wider than a cathedral close, balanced above miles of empty air.

There is no spring on the summit, no well, and almost no rain. Water is known chiefly as a substance carried upward in sealed clay jars from caves far down the cliff, and as a word used in blessings. No gutter runs openly. No basin is left uncovered. Moisture from breath, cook smoke, animal dung, and crushed food is caught on cool stone at night and scraped into stoppered cups before dawn. The quantity is meager and slightly bitter with mineral salts. Drinking is quiet, private, and regulated by bells.

Yet the place is green.

The greenery is not lush in the manner of a river valley. It is lush by density, persistence, and occupation. Every shaded wall is quilted with gray-green lichens, felted mosses that sleep dry for months, and thick-leaved vines whose roots enter hairline cracks. Courtyards are crowded with thorny figs, resin shrubs, fan-leaved succulents, and low trees trained flat against masonry. Their leaves are small, waxy, and nearly black, absorbing what little light reaches the alleys. Some plants take moisture directly from breath and cooking vapors. Others are rooted in deep shafts plugged with compost, feathers, ash, and powdered bone.

The greatest growth is overhead. Roofs are woven from living trellises: hooked vines, reedlike stalks with solid pith, and spreading succulents that form heavy green ceilings. This canopy shades the town from the warm white sky and makes its interior dim even at noon. Seen from above, if anything could look down upon it, the settlement would resemble a dark green knot tied around the bare head of the mountain.

Within, there is hardly an unoccupied surface.

Houses rise in narrow tiers, each pressed against the next. Their walls are made from the summit’s own stone, fitted without mortar where possible and packed with clay, fiber, and dung where it is not. Old rooms serve as foundations for newer ones. Stairs cross roofs; roofs become courts; courts narrow into passages and passages end at ladders. Doorways are low to preserve cool air. Windows are finger-width slits covered with horn, woven hair, or oiled membrane. The stone has been touched for so many generations that corners are rounded and stair treads sag like shallow bowls.

There is little metal. Knives are chipped from black glass found in old seams along the cliff. Adzes are stone bound to horn handles. Needles, hooks, hinges, cage bars, and climbing spikes are made of bone. Copper exists, but mostly as heirloom rivets, bell tongues, and thin ceremonial plates green with age. A single broken bronze chisel may belong jointly to several families, its use witnessed and entered on a clay tally.

The town is not planned so much as compressed. Its lanes follow cracks in the summit and avoid places where the rock sounds hollow. At the center stands a roofless court around the oldest feature: a column of untouched mountain stone, polished dark by hands. The houses lean inward toward it. Along the outer edge runs a continuous wall, part fortification and part retaining structure, thick enough to contain chambers, animal pens, food stores, and burial niches. Beyond this wall is a ledge no wider than a person’s foot, and beyond the ledge, the drop.

The elevation governs every custom.

Nothing loose is permitted near the rim. Baskets are lidded. Tools are tethered. Children wear waist cords until they learn the cliff paths. Birds are valued not only for eggs and meat but for recovering objects: certain trained corvids will descend after a fallen spoon, spindle, or bright stone and return with it for a strip of dried flesh. To drop something accidentally is unfortunate. To throw something away is a serious offense, because the town has no elsewhere into which refuse may vanish.

Waste becomes fuel, plaster, soil, glue, pigment, feed, or ritual material. Bones are boiled, cleaned, cut, and recut until they are too small for tools; then they are ground into planting beds. Cloth passes from clothing to wrappings to cordage to wall packing. Ash is sifted for salts. Even dust swept from the lanes is sorted beneath shallow screens.

The animals are everywhere.

Small cliff goats occupy upper terraces and move across roofs with unnerving confidence, their hooves ticking on stone. Lean dogs sleep in warm doorways. Pigeons and pale swifts nest in wall cavities by the hundred. Beetles work in the compost rooms; lizards cling upside down beneath vine roofs; moths crowd the smoky lamps. Caged crickets provide food and a constant dry rattling. At dusk, long-eared bats pour from fissures in the central stone and spiral over the summit, hunting in a column of insects.

Below the wall, the mountain face holds another population. Rock doves, vultures, cliff apes, small antelope, snakes, and colonies of mud-building insects occupy the ledges and cracks. The people reach them by rope, ladder, and carved foothold. The cliff is their pasture, quarry, hunting ground, cemetery, and road.

There are no wheeled vehicles. There is scarcely room to turn one around. Transport belongs to backs, baskets, pulleys, and ropes. Great wooden capstans stand beneath covered sheds along the rim. Their beams have been replaced piece by piece for centuries, but they are regarded as the same machines. They haul jars, salt blocks, carcasses, stone, and travelers up from lower landings. When several capstans operate at once, the whole summit fills with the groan of fiber rope and the rhythmic calls of hauling crews.

This is a loud place despite its slowness.

Sound cannot escape easily from the close alleys. Goats bleat from rooftops. Pigeons clap through the canopy. Stone hammers ring in repair yards. Hand mills rasp from before dawn. Loom weights knock against walls. Vendors call from niches scarcely ten paces apart, each trying to rise above the bells that divide the day into drinking, labor, shade, feeding, and sleep.

The wind contributes the deepest voice. It moves around the needle in a continuous low thunder, sometimes too far below to be felt, sometimes pressing against the town wall until doors tremble. Holes worn naturally through the summit produce fixed notes. One moans at sunrise, another hums in the warm afternoon, and a narrow cleft beneath the western parapet whistles after dark. The townspeople use these tones to judge weather and season. A change of pitch may mean dust in the lower air, a distant storm, or a fracture widening somewhere inside the mountain.

Most changes are slow enough to be inherited rather than witnessed.

The summit rock is immensely old and has already lost every soft contour. Its remaining surfaces are hard, mineral, and final-looking: iron-dark ribs, pale salt crusts, seams of glassy black stone. Wind still erodes them, but at the pace of inscriptions fading over lifetimes. A stair may become unsafe between a person’s birth and death. A wall may lean for six generations before it must be dismantled. A tree planted by one family may spend a century pushing a foundation stone half a hand’s breadth outward.

The town records these movements in marks cut beside doorways. No one expects the mountain to collapse tomorrow. Everyone knows it is collapsing eventually.

Warmth rests on the summit most of the year. The temperature is seldom brutal beneath the canopy, but the bare rim can burn the hand at midday. Night cools the outer stone rapidly, which is why condensation screens are unrolled after sunset: frames of haircloth, spider silk, and fine plant fiber placed over shaded pits. They gather only a ghost of dampness, a darkening rather than a drip, but the cloth is pressed between smooth stones and every trace saved.

Fire is kept small. There is too much dry fiber and too little replacement timber. Cooking takes place in enclosed clay ovens fueled by dung cakes, husks, resin, and oil from crushed insects and seeds. Lamps are shallow and smoky. Their weak amber points do not illuminate rooms so much as identify them: a face, a bowl, a hand at work. At night the town becomes a layered darkness filled with animal breath, whispers, wingbeats, and the red openings of ovens.

From the central court, one can cross the entire settlement in a few minutes, but no one experiences it as small. It is too folded. A passage dives beneath a house and emerges in a goat yard. A ladder rises through leaves to a shrine no larger than a cupboard. Storage chambers continue into the mountain. Cliff stairs descend to hanging neighborhoods invisible from above—platforms, caves, and wicker galleries fixed to the rock face. The inhabited space is compact in width but enormous in vertical consequence.

Some families live their whole lives in the upper knot. Others belong to the cliff: rope makers, egg gatherers, cave gardeners, salt scrapers, corpse bearers, and jar carriers. They return each evening dusted pale, their hands marked by fiber burns and stone cuts.

The dead are not buried; there is no soil to spare. They are carried to narrow shelves below the eastern wall and enclosed in woven cages. Birds clean the bones. Later, the bones are retrieved and placed within the city wall, where generations accumulate in ordered niches. Thus the settlement grows denser even in death. Ancestors become part of the boundary that keeps the living from falling.

Nothing here is fantastical to its inhabitants. The mountain is stone. The plants are plants. The animals hunger, mate, fight, and sleep. The old rules are practical rules polished into sacred ones: tie what may fall, shade what may dry, mend what may break, count what may be consumed.

And do not waste water.

At evening, the western haze turns copper. The distant mesas appear one by one like islands in a sea that is not there. The vines loosen after the day’s warmth; bats rise from the central fissure; capstan crews sing as the final cargo comes up from the lower landing. A jar arrives, sealed in clay and wrapped in rope. People make room for it in the crowded court.

The bell sounds.

Throughout the little town, shutters close, bowls are set out, animals quiet in expectation, and the day’s captured moisture is measured. Far below, the dry world fades into shadow, while the summit—dense, green, ancient, and precariously alive—holds to its last hard piece of earth.
