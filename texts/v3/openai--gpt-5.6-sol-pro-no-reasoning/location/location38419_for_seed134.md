The place is a small sea suspended high in the atmosphere.

It occupies no basin. Its boundaries are held by pressure alone: an oblong mass of cold, salt-poor water lodged between converging winds, perhaps two kilometers above the unseen ground. From a distance it would resemble a dark, stationary cloud with a polished underside. Up close there is no surface in the ordinary sense. Mist thickens to droplets, droplets become continuous water, and a traveler passes from sky into ocean without crossing a line.

Within, the sea is scarcely larger than a cathedral precinct, yet it feels crowded beyond measure.

At its center rises a freshly formed reef of black mineral—not coral, not stone weathered from any continent, but glassy matter precipitated directly from the water. It branches in knives, shelves, hollow chimneys, and tight spirals. Every edge is sharp. Erosion has only begun to dull the thinnest points, leaving pale scratches where currents carry grit across them. The reef hangs rather than rests. Its lower roots taper into open blue, and its highest pinnacles disappear into the silver turbulence along the sea’s crown.

There is no vegetation. No weed drapes the ledges, no photosynthetic film greens the stone. The available surfaces remain black, blue-gray, or plated in salts. In sheltered cracks, translucent mineral skins grow a fraction thicker each year. Some spread in hexagons; others gather as pearly blisters that split when internal pressure becomes too great. These can resemble colonies of simple organisms, but they have no metabolism anyone would recognize. They are consequences of temperature, charge, and dissolved metal.

The water is cool enough to numb exposed skin. Its temperature varies little. Light enters from every direction, sifted through the outer mist, and so the interior is plainly illuminated without having an obvious sun. Shadows are soft near the margins but hard around the mineral reef, where black blades divide the light into bright chambers and sudden blue darkness. At intervals the whole sea turns luminous white as lightning passes through the surrounding cloud layers. The flash reveals every animal at once.

There are astonishingly many.

Dense shoals circulate through the narrow spaces between the spires. Some are silver and laterally flattened, swimming edgewise through fissures barely wider than their bodies. Others resemble bundles of clear tubes with red knots moving inside them. Small armored creatures grip the mineral branches with radial claws, all facing into the current. Long, jointless hunters coil in hollows and open along their length when prey approaches, becoming mouths more than animals.

Nothing here feeds on plants. The food web begins instead with electricity and chemistry.

Microscopic drifting fauna contain chains of metallic granules. In the weak electric field generated by the sea’s friction against the wind, these granules catalyze reactions among dissolved gases. Larger animals graze on the charged drifters. Predators consume the grazers, and when anything dies, clouds of almost invisible scavengers reduce it to salts before it can sink out through the lower boundary. Life is therefore abundant but nearly colorless: glass, mercury, chalk, smoke, the dark violet of bruised water. Bright pigment would serve little purpose in a world lit from all sides.

The strangest creatures are the mills.

They are not machines, though they sound like them. Each is a heavy, wheel-shaped animal with many overlapping mineral teeth around its circumference. It turns slowly in persistent currents, filtering prey and grinding surplus shell into powder. Groups lodge in the reef’s natural sockets. Their rotations synchronize through vibration until entire chambers throb with the measured cadence of industrial works: belts without belts, presses without pistons, turbines with living muscle at their hubs.

Elsewhere, colonies of bell-bodied animals pump water through branching stone chimneys. Their contractions draw dissolved metals from colder layers and expel warmer, depleted water above. Valve creatures knock shut. Hinged shells clap in sequence. Thin predators emit piercing detection pulses that ricochet among the crowded spires. The combined sound is immense.

It is not the roar of rapid motion. Almost everything moves slowly. The noise accumulates because there are so many sources, each patient and tireless. Grinding, drumming, low hydraulic booming, the hiss of cavitation, and the deep note of the entire water mass under tension overlap into a factorylike thunder. A person inside would feel it first through the ribs and teeth. Speech would be useless.

Despite this mechanical impression, nothing has been constructed. There are no ruins, pipes, cables, tools, or forgotten makers. The reef’s regular chambers are crystal habits. Its apparent valves are animals. The long straight grooves in the glass are tracks left by generations of slow-feeding mouths. What resembles a production line is an ecological circuit: mineral formation, grazing, predation, dissolution, precipitation. Industry has appeared here as a shape available to nature.

The laws within the suspended sea differ subtly from those outside it. Weight still acts downward, but buoyancy is angled toward the reef. Dense objects descend in spirals around the central mineral mass rather than falling cleanly through the underside. Sound exerts a faint pressure of its own. The strongest low tones help stabilize the water against the wind, and the mills’ synchronized grinding is part of what keeps the sea aloft. Silence would be catastrophic.

This may be why the fauna never truly rests.

During the dim phase of each day, when the surrounding sky becomes iron-blue, activity diminishes but does not stop. Shoals wedge themselves among the black branches. Hunters close. The bell colonies pump less often. Yet the great wheel animals continue their laborious revolutions, one tooth after another, maintaining the foundational drone. The floating ocean contracts by several meters. Its outer water turns to dense fog, and the lower boundary lifts until the reef’s lowest tips briefly protrude into open air, streaming upward rather than dripping down.

Growth is slow. A mineral spur lengthens by a handspan over a century. Channels widen under abrasive bodies. Species alter their migration paths over generations. Storms do not churn the interior quickly; they compress it, increasing the light and sound until the suspended sea rings like struck glass. Only rarely does a fragment break away. Then a globe of water, crowded with startled animals, falls from the underside and dwindles into rain long before reaching the world below.

Afterward, the wound closes over days.

The shoals return first. The valve animals settle into new cracks. Chemical skins seal the fractured mineral. The living mills adjust their rhythm one by one, and the sea’s enormous noise regains its old coherence.

From inside, there is no sense of being high. There is only water, black young stone, crowded movement, and pale omnidirectional day. But near the lower margin, if one looks outward between curtains of mist, the water thins enough to reveal distances impossible to reconcile with the cramped interior: cloud fields far beneath, mountain summits like dull teeth, and beyond them the curved blue haze of the planet.

Then a shoal passes through the view, silver bodies packed scale to scale. The mills turn. The reef vibrates. The opening clouds over, and the little airborne ocean becomes complete again.
