The lowlands continue farther than sight, a warm, level country assembled entirely from manufactured parts.

There is no soil beneath it. No bedrock, mantle, or buried natural world waits below. The ground is a load-bearing skin of dark ceramic plates fixed over successively deeper frameworks: girders beneath girders, pressure vaults beneath vaults, until depth ceases to have a useful meaning. Every horizon belongs to the same construction. Even the distant haze is maintained by atmospheric machines.

Recent rain lies everywhere.

It beads on vitreous paving, hangs under rails, fills shallow seams in the plates, and collects in enormous settling basins whose rims are only a few centimeters higher than the surrounding plain. Warm water is always near the surface. In some districts it rises through calibrated pores and spreads into thin reflective sheets; in others it travels beneath translucent flooring, visible as a slow darkness underfoot. Condensation gathers on pylons and drips from cable bundles, though the drops fall so regularly that they seem less like weather than part of the machinery.

The rain itself is industrial. High overhead, beyond a pale ceiling of cloud, kilometer-wide condensers remove moisture from one zone and return it to another. Their cycle was established long ago. Each storm crosses the same corridors, releases the same mass of water, and leaves the same depth in every channel. The clouds reform without variation. From any particular plate, rain begins at the appointed hour.

Yet nothing here feels clockwork at first. The scale is too great. Weather hides its repetition.

Across the plain stand dense arrays of structures, so numerous that open distance is rare. Black cooling towers flare upward like truncated flowers, though flowers have never existed here. Rectangular refineries form long walls of pipe and wet steel. Gantries stride over canals. There are low fields of heat exchangers, their fins packed tightly enough to turn the horizon gray, and forests of vertical cylinders whose surfaces sweat continuously in the warmth.

They are not ruins. Their edges are too sharp, their joints uncorroded, their poured composites scarcely abraded. Everything has the severe finish of a facility commissioned yesterday. No new section is being added, however, and none is dismantled. The entire planet-sized works arrived at completion in some unreachable past and has remained in its first state ever since.

There is industry everywhere, but very little visible motion.

Turbines turn inside sealed housings without vibration. Pumps hold pressure. Current passes through buried conductors. Furnaces maintain reservoirs of molten salts, and compressors sustain the thick, damp air. There are no production surges, no exhausted inputs, no accumulating products. Each plant receives exactly what the next plant returns to it. Heat, water, gases, charge, and fabricated matter move through closed circuits so balanced that the landscape has no history of wear.

Its industry does not make things. It keeps made things made.

Between the structures, shallow canals divide the plain into immense geometric provinces. The water is tea-dark where it covers carbon composite and silver where it crosses polished alloy. It is warm enough to mist in the cooler hours, although “hours” are merely changes in illumination. There are no reeds, no algae, no film of organic green. Wet surfaces remain perfectly bare. Mineral accretions are intercepted molecule by molecule before they can cloud a pipe or round an edge.

Absence of vegetation does not make the place empty.

The fauna is overwhelming.

Animals occupy nearly every available ledge, conduit, basin, and sheltered aperture. They gather in such numbers that at a distance they resemble materials: a pale granular coating on roofs, a dark nap along pipelines, a trembling metallic sheen at the margins of water. Only nearby do the coatings resolve into bodies.

Most are small. Six-legged waders move across the flooded plates on feet broad as washers. Warm rain ticks against their enamel backs. They lower narrow heads into drainage slots and withdraw threads of nutrient gel dispensed from ports below. Hundreds stand in each shallow basin, spaced with uncanny regularity, never disputing territory.

Soft-bodied filterers hang beneath gantries in dense curtains. They are translucent, almost colorless, and filled with looping blue vessels. They feed not on plankton—the water contains none—but on standardized suspensions released by the circulation works. When the flow begins, thousands of mouths open at once. When it ends, they close. Their synchronized feeding makes no more sound than cloth settling.

Larger creatures inhabit the service corridors. Broad, many-jointed grazers pass their rasping jaws over sacrificial ceramic panels, removing a precisely replenished surface layer. Tall quadrupeds sleep standing in the steam beneath condenser stacks. Their skin is smooth, hairless, and patterned with registration marks that look printed rather than inherited. In the deepest canals, immense backs are sometimes visible just beneath the water: slow armored animals dimensioned to the channels, turning only where circular basins have been built to permit the maneuver.

Nothing hunts in any recognizable way. Predation has been replaced by allocation.

At fixed intervals, wall apertures offer measured blocks of protein, mineral, and warm saline. The animals arrive without haste, each species at its assigned opening. They consume exactly the quantity provided. Reproduction is equally regulated. Eggs are deposited into heated sockets in the infrastructure; larvae emerge into designated ducts; adults die in quiet receiving chambers where their matter is separated and returned to the fabrication cycle.

There are no bones lying beneath the towers, no nests made from gathered debris, no tracks that persist after the next wash. Abundance leaves no accumulation.

It leaves almost no sound, either.

The land ought to roar. It contains uncountable animals and machinery sufficient to maintain a world. Instead there is only a layered hush: water ticking from metal, a distant electrical hum, the whisper of pressure through valves, the occasional soft contact of shell against shell. The fauna rarely vocalizes. Signals pass through skin color, low-frequency tremors in the plates, or conductive touches to rails woven through every habitat.

Once in a great while a sound crosses the plain—a deep hornlike exhalation from something in a canal, or the collective click of a million small bodies changing posture. It travels far in the dense air, reflects between factories, then fades. Nothing answers unexpectedly. If there is an answer, it comes from the same quarter and after the same interval as before.

The light is ordinary in intensity and extraordinary in origin. Broad luminous panels occupy the sky at an altitude too great to judge. They imitate a temperate sun diffused through wet cloud: white at midday, honey-colored toward the scheduled evening, gray during rain. Their brightness is comfortable, revealing rather than theatrical. Shadows fall properly. Water flashes. Wet machinery has the familiar luster of machinery after a storm.

But there is no sun behind the panels and no outer sky. The luminous canopy is another manufactured surface, curved around the lowlands at a distance that makes it atmospheric. Some migrating creatures fly toward it each year, following light gradients laid down for them. Their routes trace enormous loops and return them to the same towers. There are no seasons, yet migration continues.

Every apparent change is recurrence.

Rain falls, but total water remains fixed. Animals are born, but population and distribution do not alter. Panels are grazed away and rebuilt at the same rate. Warm fronts cross the flats, though the temperature at every location, averaged over a cycle, has never shifted by a measurable fraction. Even erosion is constrained. Water wears microscopic channels into designated surfaces; beneath them, extrusion systems raise fresh material, preserving the same young, sharply incised forms.

Thus the terrain always appears newly exposed. Drainage cuts retain crisp lips. Canal walls have no softened corners. Artificial cliffs, where one platform drops several hundred meters to another, look freshly fractured despite being molded from monolithic composite. Water weeps from their faces in parallel lines, feeding marshlike aprons of perforated metal below.

The closest thing to wilderness lies at these platform margins.

Here maintenance access is sparse, humidity is high, and animals mass in tiers along the wet escarpments. Winged forms cling beneath overhangs. Flat-bodied swimmers crowd the plunge pools. Pale burrowers move through cavities inside the cliff, visible through occasional inspection windows. The concentration of life can be difficult to comprehend: millions within a single structural bay, billions across the escarpment, each accommodated by vents, grooves, feeders, warming elements, and waste-return capillaries.

Yet not one plant grows between them. No root prises open a seam. No moss dulls the factory surfaces. The world knows bodies, but not greenery; metabolism, but not succession. Its animals have no unmanaged food web and perhaps no concept of an environment that was not prepared for them.

From a high gantry, the constructed lowlands resemble a wet continent at peace. Towers diminish into cloud. Canals shine between black districts. Herds move like slow stains over the plates, while airborne multitudes hang nearly motionless in the warm updrafts. Here and there, rain descends in straight gray columns, each storm anchored over its allotted watershed.

The strangeness arrives gradually. It is not in any single animal or engine, but in the impossibility of disturbance.

A footstep leaves water displaced from a shallow seam. The ripple travels outward, touches the canal wall, and returns. Pumps correct the lost heat. A ceiling condenser replaces the moisture disturbed into vapor. Tiny cleaners remove whatever was shed from the foot. Within minutes the plate is as it was.

Within the deeper systems, balances close.

The landscape permits passage but not consequence. It can be seen, crossed, breathed, and heard. Its warm rain can soak a traveler; its dense herds will part around an unfamiliar body; its canals will reflect a face that has never appeared there before. But the world will not retain the visitor. No path can be made. No creature can be permanently startled from its route. Nothing can be harvested without an equal quantity emerging elsewhere from a fabrication port.

Far below, among the pressure vaults, control machinery keeps an account of every atom.

Whether the place is a preserve, a factory, a vessel, or a model has ceased to matter. There is no overseer visible, and perhaps there never was one. Purpose has been worn away without wearing the structures that served it. What remains is a complete environment, low and immense, densely alive and perfectly manufactured, holding itself at the first instant after completion.

Warm rain begins again over the eastern basins.

The small waders turn their enamel backs to it. Condensation gathers on the rail. Beyond them, across distances large enough to generate their own weather, every sharp edge shines as though newly made.
