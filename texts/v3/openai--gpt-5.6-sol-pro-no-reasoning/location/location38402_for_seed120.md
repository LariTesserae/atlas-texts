The place is called the High Garden, though it fits inside a chamber no wider than two outstretched arms.

Its height cannot be measured.

You enter on your knees through a woven hatch and find yourself crouching at the brink of an abyss. The threshold remains close behind—warm reeds against your heels, a thumb-sized bar of bone holding the little door open—but ahead, terraces fall away for miles. Above them rise innumerable peaks, black and wet, their summits lost in a copper-colored haze. Clouds drag through valleys small enough, from the doorway, to cover with your palm. Thunder moves below.

Nothing here is natural.

The mountains are heaped from hand-pinched clay, fired in cookfires and stacked piece by piece. Their cliffs are roof tiles set upright. Their exposed strata are alternating layers of brick dust, woven grass, charcoal, shell lime, and potsherds pressed edge-on into wet mortar. Valleys have been thumbed between them. Ravines retain the scores of wooden blades. The newest peaks are still soft: blunt cones of red paste shivering under their own impossible weight while workers, no larger than pepper seeds, push baskets up ladders laid against the slopes.

There has not yet been time for erosion. Rain does not round the ridges. Streams do not cut downward. Instead, the builders manufacture every gully deliberately. Teams score channels into the clay with stone teeth; other teams line the cuts with wax so water cannot alter them. Whenever a bank collapses, it does not look weathered. It looks unfinished. Beneath every apparent geological surface is scaffolding.

The whole landscape is under construction at once.

It is also already overcrowded.

There is almost no open ground. Every ledge bears a stair, a cistern, a shrine, a seedbed, a drying rack, or a village of tiny hooded structures. Bridges overlap bridges. Vines are trained over roofs; roofs are built over canals; canals pass through tunnels whose ceilings support fields, and those fields carry clay ranges that appear, from within them, as true mountains. Even the clouds are caught in nets strung between pinnacles. When the nets sag with condensation, workers strike them using forked poles, and warm rain shakes loose in narrow, scheduled curtains.

The water has no single source. It beads out of walls.

Sweating ceramic pipes fill the deep places, though no pipe is larger than a reed stem near the entrance. Their glaze is dark green and always cool. Moisture gathers on them, swells into clear lenses, and drops. Inside the garden each falling drop becomes a storm front. It strikes a roof with the violence of a felled tree, races down steps in white torrents, fills a chain of bowls, and disappears through perforated stone into lower districts.

After rain, the place steams.

Heat rises continuously from the foundations. Somewhere beneath the lowest visible terraces, charcoal chambers are kept burning by bellows made of leaves and fish skin. Their warmth draws water upward through clay wicks, feeds the condensing pipes, and makes the entire garden breathe. Inhalation is marked by fog sinking into the valleys. Exhalation sends hot vapor climbing through chimney forests. The cycle is quick and uneven; one slope may be drenched while its neighboring face bakes hard enough to crack.

The cracks are repaired immediately.

Repairers carry bowls of slip on their backs. They kneel beside a fissure and press wet clay into it with flat pebbles, chanting the dimensions of the mountain as they work. Their arithmetic is ritual rather than measurement:

*One hand beneath the summit.*  
*Two breaths around the lake.*  
*Seven shadows from here to morning.*

The dimensions change while they are recited.

This is the first rule of the High Garden: **distance belongs to the traveler, not to the ground**.

Seen from the hatch, the farthest ridge is perhaps a cubit away. Descend toward it and the path lengthens underfoot. A tiled stair of twenty steps acquires landings, switchbacks, roadside wells, then entire provinces. Turning around does not restore the original scale. The doorway may hang in the sky behind you like a narrow golden moon, close enough to touch and years away.

The second rule is that **nothing may be removed whole**.

Break off a shard and it becomes a new escarpment in the hand. Pluck a moss frond and its root brings up a wet forest floor, inhabited and deep. Scoop water from a cistern and the bowl contains a weather system: mist, rain, floating gardens, lightning no brighter than blue mold. Objects can be rearranged, crushed, mixed, fired, or built upon, but they cannot be carried beyond the woven hatch without opening into landscapes of their own.

Perhaps this is why construction never ends. Every building material already contains another site that requires tending.

Vegetation packs the remaining surfaces. There are miniature breadfruit trees espaliered against hot walls, liverwort orchards under dripping eaves, moss paddies brilliant as green enamel, and pale fungi that climb ropework overnight. Root mats are used as floors. Leaves bridge canals. In the warmest hollows, red creepers grow so quickly that teams must hack passages through them with obsidian flakes tied into wooden handles.

No plant grows wild, although many have escaped their assigned forms.

The gardeners braid roots into retaining walls. They stitch broad leaves into awnings and train tendrils around clay armatures to make towers. Seeds are pressed into precise holes with bone needles. Yet the humidity is extravagant, and growth continually exceeds design. Moss seals doorways. Fungi digest ladders. Green shoots lever apart unfired ridges before the clay can harden. The builders respond by incorporating each intrusion. A burst wall becomes a hanging garden; a fallen trellis becomes a district roof; a fungal bloom is hollowed into dwellings while still expanding.

Creation and overgrowth proceed so rapidly that there are no ruins. Nothing remains abandoned long enough. Failure is simply another construction material.

Animals are uncommon and treated as catastrophes.

A single gnat moves through the valleys like a black-winged god. Its arrival is announced by shell horns. Lamps are covered. Water crews lie flat beneath woven mats as its legs descend among the towers. When it drinks from a reservoir, the water level falls for a season. When it lifts away, the wind destroys bridges.

There are also white mites that graze on the fungal terraces. They are harnessed, when possible, with loops of hair and used to drag sledges of brick. Most refuse all direction. Their trails pass through walls rather than around them; settlements rebuild in the smooth tunnels left behind.

Far below, something croaks at irregular intervals. No one has seen it. Each call shakes droplets from the ceiling and makes the artificial mountains ring. The inhabitants distinguish these tremors from thunder, hammering, kiln cracks, net-rain, bellows, and the constant roar of water through the narrow pipes. To an outsider the sounds merge into one enormous clamor, impossible for such a small enclosure: a continent being fired, irrigated, and assembled inside a basket.

There is little light. A few oil flames sit in mirrored niches near the upper terraces, but the mirrors are flakes of mica and beaten shell, and their reflections do not agree about where the flames are. One lamp can appear as a red sunset over five separate horizons. Some valleys receive only reflected reflections, a green-brown twilight in which wet surfaces shine but objects remain obscure.

Lightning supplies the clearest illumination. It forms spontaneously inside ceramic bells hung from the ceiling. Charge gathers between glaze and damp air until a blue filament snaps across a bell’s mouth. For an instant every scaffold, leaf, stair, and streaming channel stands out with painful precision.

Sometimes the flash reveals structures that are not present afterward.

A sixth range behind the fifth. A staircase climbing downward into the sky. Workers vastly larger than the mountains, standing motionless among them with their faces covered. Another woven hatch on the opposite wall, with someone kneeling beyond it and looking in.

The builders do not discuss these sights. They scrape a fresh layer of clay over the nearest surface and continue.

Toward what should be the center lies the Unmade Peak. It is the newest and highest thing in the garden, a narrow cone assembled from unfired black clay around a framework of reeds. It trembles in the heat. Its summit nearly touches the chamber ceiling when viewed from outside, yet from its base it rises beyond the cloud nets and through several altitudes of darkness.

Thousands labor on it. Brick carriers ascend. Water carriers descend. Kiln tenders crawl through inner passages and light small fires to harden the mountain from within. Gardeners follow, laying moss over the cooled flanks. Canal cutters score routes for rivers that have not been condensed yet. Behind them come surveyors with knotted strings, changing the placement of valleys by tightening or loosening a single loop.

The peak is not eroding. It is deciding what erosion will mean.

On one face, gullies are carved upward so streams climb toward the summit. On another, landslides happen before the slopes beneath them are built. A delta waits halfway up a dry cliff for its river to arrive. Fossils are pressed into the youngest clay: leaf shapes, shell spirals, tiny handprints, and the impressions of creatures no one has seen. These are not records of the past. Here, a fossil is an instruction. Once sealed into the mountain, it obliges the world to invent the organism later.

That is the third rule: **evidence comes first**.

Near the summit, the heat becomes almost unbearable. Rain turns to vapor before touching the ledges. Workers wear caps of wet moss and carry bricks between cloths so the clay will not fire in their hands. The roar from below thins into individual notes: mallets, dripping pipes, reed whistles, stone blades scratching designs into glaze.

The uppermost scaffold supports a single empty niche.

Every route in the garden eventually bends toward it, though many take generations to arrive. All the waterworks, root walls, cloud nets, furnaces, and impossible distances seem arranged around that waiting recess. Its interior is smooth and damp. It is exactly the size of whatever looks into it.

If you reach the niche and lean close, you do not see clay at the back. You see the room outside the High Garden: the woven hatch, the floor, your own kneeling body.

Behind that body stands a mountain of wet black clay.

Workers cover its slopes.

Clouds cling to its upper dark.

And somewhere near its summit, impossibly small but growing clearer, is a woven hatch through which warm, dim air is blowing.
