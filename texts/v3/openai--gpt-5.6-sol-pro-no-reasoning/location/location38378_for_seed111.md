At an elevation where most mountains have already thinned into snow, there is a cleft called the Black Nursery.

It is not large. A person could walk its perimeter in less than an hour if there were a perimeter one could safely follow. The cleft occupies a newly split crown of basalt, a room-sized ravine nested near the summit: two steep walls, a tilted floor, and an open seam overhead through which the sky appears as a narrow gray wound. Nothing has had time to become gentle. The rock is black, blue-black, and iron-dark, broken into blades that still preserve the pale fracture-marks of their birth. Corners remain acute. Gravel has not yet accumulated enough to soften the hollows. Water has scarcely begun its patient work.

The cleft was opened by frost and pressure, perhaps within the last few centuries, perhaps last winter. From below, it is invisible. From above, it resembles a crack no wider than a hand.

Inside, it widens.

The walls lean together and nearly touch in places. Their surfaces are congested with ledges, fins, bubbles, glassy knobs, and narrow vertical pipes left by gases trapped when the stone was molten. These cavities give the place its density. There is always another recess behind the visible one, another shelf above the shelf, another black aperture the size of a thumb or a skull. The stone seems less like a mass than a tightly packed assembly of hiding places.

Almost all are occupied.

The darkness moves.

Small animals cling in ranks to the walls: furred, feathered, scaled, chitinous, and forms for which those categories are inadequate. Pale hook-footed gliders sleep folded into cracks, packed flank to flank like dry leaves. Black mountain swifts nest on shelves so narrow that their eggs touch the precipice. Long-bodied mites graze the birds’ feathers. Blind silver crabs inhabit wet pores in the basalt, hundreds of vertical miles from any sea. Translucent salamanders wedge themselves beneath cold stones, their ribs visible around hearts that beat perhaps once each minute. Beetles with fused wing cases push through the felt of droppings on the floor. Thin worms suspend themselves from the ceiling on single threads and feed on condensation.

No species is abundant everywhere. Every species is overwhelming somewhere.

The largest creatures are the veilbacks. They are six-limbed, no bigger than foxes, and almost weightless for their size. Their outer limbs support pleated membranes of smoky skin, which they spread against the walls to collect moisture from passing cloud. A veilback may remain in one place for weeks, gray body flattened over dark stone, its only movement the slow tightening of the membrane as droplets converge toward its mouth. Colonies overlap until whole sections of cliff appear upholstered in breathing leather.

When the sky clears, the veilbacks fold themselves and descend into the warmest fissures. Thousands do so together. The noise resembles heavy cloth being shaken in a cavern.

There is water, but no stream.

Clouds pass through the summit seam and strike the cold interior walls. Moisture condenses instantly on some mineral faces and not on others. Thus the cleft contains scattered wetness: a chain of droplets beneath an overhang, a black basin holding two fingers of water, a curtain of mist that exists only at dawn, beads buried in porous stone. Rain seldom falls directly inside. It is harvested from air, breath, feathers, and fog.

The water moves downward so slowly that its path is measured in generations. A drop enters a pore, freezes, widens it by a grain’s breadth, thaws, and vanishes into another chamber. Some pockets are sealed under skins of clear ice no thicker than an insect wing. Inside them swim tiny red animals. When the ice opens during the brief cool summer—not warmth, exactly, but an easing of cold—the animals disperse across wet rock in branching processions and disappear before the basin closes again.

There are plants, though they are easy to mistake for stains.

Rust-colored lichens occupy the highest margins where a little diluted daylight reaches. Moss grows in dense thumb-sized cushions under the nesting shelves, fed by droppings and feather dust. A few jointed herbs emerge from cracks where water persists. They have no broad leaves. Their stems are violet-black and hollow, and each bears one waxy white flower facing the rock rather than the sky. The flower is pollinated by cave moths that land upside down beneath it. Seeds fall into fissures and may wait decades for the next wet interval.

Lower in the cleft, vegetation gives way to fungal veils. These are thin as breath on glass and shine weakly green when touched. Grazing insects continually remove them, so they survive in inaccessible pores and beneath the feet of sleeping animals. The green light is rarely enough to reveal anything clearly. It shows a claw, an eye, the wet edge of a wing—and then goes out.

Day does not properly enter the Black Nursery. At noon, the seam overhead becomes pewter. At night it disappears, and the cleft’s dimensions are reconstructed from sound.

There is always sound.

Wings knock against stone. Claws tick over glassy basalt. Nestlings rasp. Water gives isolated taps from ledge to ledge, each drop answered by the hollow pipes in the walls. The wind blows across the summit opening and turns those pipes into flutes. Because their mouths are irregular, they do not make a chord. They produce long, wavering tones that seem to bend around one another without meeting.

Under this runs a denser noise: breathing, grooming, chewing, the friction of packed bodies shifting by fractions. It is loud not through violence but through accumulation. In so small a place, ten thousand quiet creatures become weather.

At intervals, perhaps once every several days, all of them fall silent.

The silence moves from the upper fissures downward. Swifts close their beaks. Beetles stop among the droppings. Veilbacks contract their membranes. Even the suspended worms draw themselves into the ceiling. The hush reaches the floor in less than a minute and may endure for hours.

Then something climbs through the rock.

It is never seen whole. A pressure passes behind the basalt faces, deep enough to make droplets tremble. From one crack comes a sound like a stone being slowly bitten. From another, far across the chamber, comes the answer. The walls flex inward by less than the thickness of a hair. Animals with eyes turn them toward the stone; animals without eyes flatten themselves against it.

Local life is organized around this passage. Eggs hatch after it. Moths emerge before it. Veilbacks choose colonies along the routes it seldom takes. Moss grows thicker on walls that have not sounded in a century.

Whatever moves within the mountain does so at the pace of geology. Its transit through the cleft’s hidden depth may take a full night. Its complete circuit may take hundreds of years. It may be one animal, or a colony, or a mineral process mistaken by living things for an animal. The distinction has little meaning here. The mountain feeds, exhales, fractures, and carries young.

The cleft has rules.

Nothing falls all the way to the bottom. Feathers, seeds, loose stones, dead insects—all catch on wings, threads, bodies, ledges, or upward drafts. The space is so occupied that descent becomes transfer.

Warmth sinks. It collects not near the open sky but in narrow lower cracks, where dense mats of animals hold and recirculate one another’s heat.

Fresh water belongs first to the smallest mouths. Larger creatures drink only what runs past them.

No creature calls alone. A sound made once is ignored; made twice, it is repeated somewhere else; made three times, it propagates through the cleft until every wall seems to contain its source. This is why predators hunt silently and courtship can last for days.

Death is swift, but decay is slow. Cold preserves bodies in the dry upper niches. Below, scavengers dismantle them grain by grain. Bones are rare because even bone is eaten, softened by condensation and passed through generations of pale jaws. Nothing becomes soil quickly. The floor is not earth but a compact layer of husks, mineral dust, eggshell, moulted skin, and black pellets, bound together by fungal threads.

There are no paths, hearths, cairns, cords, blades, or carved marks. Yet tool use occurs in fleeting forms. Certain birds select flakes of newly fractured basalt and carry them to nesting shelves, where they use the edges to open the tough egg cases of cliff beetles. A small long-fingered mammal presses moss into dripping cracks to make temporary reservoirs. Veilbacks wedge shed claws into vents to alter the note of the wind, though whether this is a trap, a warning, or an aesthetic act is impossible to tell.

Nothing is kept long. Frost scatters the arrangements. Other bodies reuse the pieces. Craft appears and vanishes without becoming architecture.

Beyond the cleft lies the exposed summit: open cold, pale stone, killing wind. Almost no animal remains there for more than a crossing. The strange abundance exists because of the Black Nursery’s boundaries. Within a few dozen paces are darkness, moisture, mineral salt, trapped heat, shelter, and innumerable surfaces on which to cling. Outside are distance and sky.

The place changes slowly. A ledge shears off once in fifty winters. A new fissure opens once in the life of a colony. Condensation enlarges the pores. Lichen advances at the rate of a fingernail’s growth over decades. The summit itself is rising, grain by grain, pushed upward by forces too deep to hear except during the great silences.

Eventually erosion will widen the seam. Light will enter. Rain will establish a channel. The sharp walls will round; gravel will bury the lower chambers; the dense populations will disperse or be eaten by newcomers from below.

But that age has not arrived.

For now, the Black Nursery is young stone crowded with old hungers: a dark, damp room at the roof of the world, still ringing from its creation, containing more life than it seems to have space for.
