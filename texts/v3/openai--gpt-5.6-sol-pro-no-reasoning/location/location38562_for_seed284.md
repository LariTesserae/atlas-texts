The city occupies a crack in the summit of a mountain.

From outside, almost nothing announces it. The peak is a young black fang, one of the highest in the range, its faces too fresh for soil and only lightly scored by wind. Snow does not settle here. The air is dry enough to steal frost directly into vapor, leaving seams of pale salt in the stone. Near the summit, two slabs lean together without quite meeting. Between them is a crevice scarcely wider than a hand.

The city begins inside.

The crack descends farther than its narrowness should allow. Its opposing walls are crowded with gantries, dwellings, lifts, cages, pipes, braces, cableways, and little iron balconies, all fitted into a vertical space that sometimes narrows to shoulder width and sometimes opens into chambers no larger than workshops. Nothing stands on level ground. There is no level ground. The inhabited surfaces are walls, ceilings, pillars, moving platforms, and the ribbed backs of machines.

The highest streets lie nearly eight thousand meters above the plains, though “street” means a ladder of nickel-steel rungs, a chain of footholds, or a tram basket traversing twenty meters of darkness. Buildings are wedged between the rock faces like shims driven into a split beam. Their fronts look inward. Their backs are bare stone. Many rooms are only deep enough for a cot or workbench, and their doors open directly over the abyss.

The settlement is dense not because it is large, but because everything is near everything else. A refinery wall may also be the outer wall of a nursery. Ventilation ducts pass through bedrooms. Freight chains descend between kitchens. Signal cables share anchor bolts with aviaries and shrines. From any balcony one can reach across and touch another structure, although doing so may mean putting a hand against a furnace casing, a live conduit, or the trembling membrane of an animal’s nest.

The rock is basaltic, dark and faintly metallic, broken recently by pressure from beneath. Its surfaces remain angular. Corners have not rounded; fallen splinters are still sharp enough to cut rope. Mineral veins cross the walls in silver, dull green, and sulfurous yellow. Under electric light they give the impression of diagrams drawn through the mountain: circuitry on a geological scale.

There is almost no water. The city was founded around something else—a seep of dense, colorless refrigerant that rises through hairline faults and boils at temperatures far below the local air. It is called ghost brine, though it contains neither salt nor water. Captured in ceramic tubes, it cools engines, condenses industrial gases, preserves food, and drives pressure turbines as it changes phase. Every district depends upon it. A leak appears as a low white crawling fog, followed by a brittle coating on metal and the sudden, dangerous tightening of bolts.

Moisture is considered contamination. Doors carry absorbent mineral pads. Breath is reclaimed through masks in the deeper factories, not principally for oxygen, but to prevent vapor from corroding the fine mechanisms. Any true water brought up from below is stored in sealed ampoules and measured in drops. Most inhabitants have never seen enough of it to form a reflective surface.

No plants grow in the crack. The few things called gardens are beds of piezoelectric crystal seeded along vibrating pipes. Their branching forms thicken in response to machinery, pale needles opening from iron mesh. They are not alive, though they are pruned, traded, and inherited. In dim chambers, colonies of dry fungi—or organisms close enough to fungi for convenience—feed on lubricants, shed skin, and electrical insulation. They produce leathery gray fans without moisture, metabolizing hydrocarbons and metal oxides. Maintenance crews tolerate them because their color changes in the presence of gas leaks.

Animal life is everywhere.

Most species are small, climbing, and thin-bodied. Six-legged rock curs run along cable bundles, their feet gripping through magnetized pads. They hunt pale mites that infest warm motors. Flocks of coin-sized gliders inhabit ventilation shafts, folding themselves into cracks when turbines start. Wireworms braid nests from copper filings. Blind bell-backed beetles gather around transformers, feeding on insulating wax and making the casings chime faintly as they strike them.

Larger creatures move through the central void. Sailbacks, with bodies no heavier than a cat and membranes spanning several meters, drift upward on waste heat. Their hides are dark above and mirror-bright below. In certain light they seem like holes opening and closing in the city. Workers attach message capsules to their leg rings. No one owns them, though every neighborhood claims to recognize its local flock.

The settlement’s most numerous beasts are the burden climbers: compact, many-jointed animals bred to carry loads vertically. Their ancestry is obscure. They have four grasping limbs around a central barrel-shaped torso, a head capable of turning entirely backward, and dense insulating fur that crackles in dry air. They ascend rungways with unnerving patience, freight panniers hanging beneath them. Bells are forbidden, so their approach is announced by the faint click of claws and the smell of hot stone.

The city is mostly quiet because noise is dangerous.

The entire crevice acts as a resonating instrument. A dropped hammer can travel through bracing for hundreds of meters. Certain frequencies loosen anchors or disturb the unstable rock. Engines are therefore mounted on counter-vibrating beds. Factory presses close with slow hydraulic force. Gears have ceramic teeth. Citizens speak softly and use hand signs near load-bearing walls.

Yet the place is never still.

Its turbulence is structural rather than loud. The mountain continues to split by fractions of a millimeter. Every hour, gauges twitch. Every day, some stair no longer meets its landing, some doorway pinches shut, some bridge acquires a slope it did not have in the morning. Buildings ride telescoping beams and sliding collars. Pipes coil to absorb movement. Whole neighborhoods are occasionally winched upward or lowered deeper to escape compression.

There are no fixed addresses. Locations are given by anchor lineage and current tension: Third Red Brace, below the humming seam; Six spans inward from the bent chimney; opposite the old pulley, if it has not moved.

The youngest districts are assembled rapidly in newly opened pockets. Their architecture is bright galvanized metal, raw ceramic, fresh cable, and chalk markings. The oldest have been crushed so many times that they resemble compact geological strata—walls folded into walls, rooms flattened to hand-width seams, utensils and nameplates embedded in pressure-polished metal. Salvage crews mine these dead districts whenever the crack shifts open again.

Industry fills nearly every available cavity. There are mills that grind high-altitude minerals into conductive powder; kilns that fire vacuum ceramics; shops that draw wire fine enough to float in static fields. Tiny foundries hang in cages above the depths, their crucibles no larger than cups. Because space is scarce, production lines run vertically. Ore enters from a hatch overhead, passes downward through grinders, separators, furnaces, rollers, and cutters, and emerges hundreds of meters below as finished components.

Nothing is wasted except heat, and even heat is used to maintain lift for the sailbacks or create upward drafts in freight chimneys. Scraps become fasteners. Worn fasteners become powder. Animal bone becomes insulation. Fungal hide becomes gasket material. Dust is collected from every vent and sorted magnetically grain by grain.

Light comes from shielded amber strips, inspection lamps, furnace mouths, and the occasional blue blink of an arc welder. The dimness is partly economy and partly courtesy. Bright illumination startles the shaft fauna and makes depth difficult to judge. Residents prefer small pools of light that reveal the next rung, the next face, the next moving hook. Beyond them, the city dissolves into layers of silhouettes.

Far below, the crevice has no visible bottom. It narrows into cold darkness full of suspended machinery. Far above, the opening shows a blade of sky—black at noon, star-pricked when weather is clear. Sometimes the high-altitude wind crosses the mouth at tremendous speed. It cannot enter directly, but it plucks the mountain like a bottle. The resulting note is deep, almost below hearing. Dust lifts from every ledge. Animals flatten themselves. Conversations stop.

Then the city moves.

Counterweights descend. Shutters close. Hundreds of sailbacks spiral through the central shaft. Trams halt between stations. Flexible buildings draw inward on their braces as the peak flexes beneath the wind. Indicator crystals brighten along the walls, each responding to a different strain. In moments the whole hidden place becomes a shifting constellation of warning lights.

There is no panic. This is ordinary weather.

The people wait with one hand on the nearest rail, feeling the mountain alter around them. Somewhere, quietly, a new fissure opens. Somewhere else, a room ceases to exist. Survey crews are already fastening on their masks and gathering ceramic wedges. Developers listen for echoes. Scavengers release gliders into the unknown gap. Factory owners calculate whether the opening will take another workshop.

When the great note fades, the city resumes—not where it was, exactly, but where the mountain has permitted it to be.
