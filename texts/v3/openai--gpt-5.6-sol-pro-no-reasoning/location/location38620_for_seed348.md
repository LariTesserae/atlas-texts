The city lies low in a shallow basin, no larger than a few adjoining districts, bounded by worn embankments of black brick. From the rim, it resembles an abandoned industrial quarter: roofless foundries, settling tanks, crane rails, square chimneys, and narrow streets paved with vitrified slag. Nothing rises high. Generations of weather have rounded every edge. Rivets sit flush with rust. Stone steps are hollowed into smooth bowls. Iron lintels have swollen and flaked until their surfaces resemble bark, though no tree has ever grown here.

There is water, but never enough to gather the city into a river. It appears in pockets: beneath grates, in wheel ruts, at the bottoms of tiled cisterns. Condensation beads continuously on the cold sides of boilers and pipes, rolls downward, and taps into zinc troughs. A narrow canal occupies the lowest street, filled only to ankle depth. Its dark surface is so still that noise seems unable to disturb it.

The air is cool and carries the smells of wet masonry, hot insulation, animal musk, ozone, and old metal.

No plants soften the masonry. The seams between paving blocks are bare. Courtyards contain no weeds, roofs no moss, walls no lichen. In places a pale crust grows around drain mouths, but it is mineral efflorescence—or something so close to mineral that the distinction is of no practical use. The city’s only colors are brick red, furnace black, dull nickel, wet gray, and the occasional blue-white flash of an arc lamp.

Its factories remain in operation.

Belts turn between buildings. Flywheels revolve behind iron screens. Presses descend into empty dies with measured concussions. Elevators rise and fall in shafts whose upper stories no longer exist. Chains move through ceiling pulleys, carrying hooks from one vacant loading bay to another. Compressed air vents from brass valves. None of the machines accelerates, catches, or fails. Each has reached the final wear of a long life and remains there: polished bearings neither thinning nor heating, cracked belts neither breaking nor healing, gears holding the same minute defects tooth after tooth.

The mechanisms produce almost nothing. One plant stamps shallow tin cups and drops them onto a conveyor that returns them to the press, where they are flattened and stamped again. Another grinds slag into powder, mixes the powder with condensation, fires it into black bricks, and feeds the bricks back into its own furnace. The canal pumps water uphill through a network of pipes; the same water returns by gravity, colored briefly with iron.

This closed labor is not futile here. Futility requires the possibility of an end.

The fauna are the city’s true population.

They cover it without filling it.

Every ledge bears animals, yet the streets remain oddly open. Vast numbers inhabit particular stations: clustered under gantries, packed into pipe galleries, layered behind broken windows, hanging beneath bridges in living curtains. Between these concentrations lie broad floors and yards entirely empty, as if invisible municipal boundaries regulate every species.

Most are small and difficult to classify. Six-legged things with translucent shells run in synchronized files along steam pipes. Pale, hand-sized grazers scrape oxidation from iron plates with rotating mouthparts. Black-bodied flyers circle the lamps, their wings rigid and their bodies pivoting beneath them like suspended compass needles. In the flooded cellars, eyeless swimmers pack the water so densely that it seems to clot; each remains a finger’s width from its neighbors, all turning at once around submerged turbines.

Larger animals occupy the streets. Low, broad-backed creatures stand beneath dripping gutters and receive condensation into rows of mouths along their spines. Long jointed beasts cross the canal on fixed routes, placing each foot into depressions worn over centuries. On roofs, colonies of ribbed sacs inflate and deflate in unison, breathing factory exhaust. The tallest chimneys are ringed by heavy winged forms whose flights never deviate: each loops the same circuit, passes the same brick, and returns to the same updraft.

Their abundance should make the city chaotic. Instead, it makes the stillness more severe.

Nothing here is born or dies. Predation occurs, but it is not an event. In one alley, a silver animal has its jaws closed around a smaller red one; the red body is half swallowed, legs braced against the pavement. Both continue to breathe. They have occupied this instant so long that rain has worn channels around their feet. Elsewhere, a swarm erupts continually from a vent, wheels above the roofline, and pours back into the same vent. Its membership never changes. Not one creature leaves the pattern, and not one collides.

The loudness is immense and perfectly steady. Machinery hammers, wings buzz, claws tick on sheet metal, valves shriek, pumps throb, and innumerable throats produce layered calls. There are no pauses and no crescendos. The combined sound has weathered into a physical property of the basin, like pressure or gravity. Loose bolts tremble without migrating. Water surfaces dimple in permanent geometries. A person standing in the central yard feels the noise through teeth and bones more clearly than through the ears.

Speech is possible only in certain acoustic shadows: behind the casting hall, inside a derailed tram, beneath the arch of the east sluice. These quiet pockets are marked with white ceramic tiles. Whether someone placed the tiles for that purpose, or the silence formed around them afterward, is unknown.

Daylight enters normally. Clouds pass over the basin, though their shadows cross it at an unvarying speed. Light rests cleanly on wet roofs and polished rails. Lamps burn where daylight is already sufficient. At no hour does the city look theatrical; its strangeness survives complete visibility. Every impossible thing can be inspected at arm’s length.

Occasional rain falls, always fine and vertical. It wets the dust without washing it away. Drops striking the hot housings become steam, joining low white veils that linger beneath the pipe bridges. Puddles reach familiar boundaries and stop rising. By an obscure accounting, exactly as much water leaves through drains as arrives from the sky.

The built surfaces show ancient erosion, but the erosion does not progress. Each groove, rust bloom, collapsed wall, and rounded curb has become an equilibrium. Fragments poised beneath arches do not fall. Corroded ladders accept weight. Broken windows remain sharp in the same places and blunt in the same places. The city is neither preserved nor decaying. It has simply completed its weathering.

At the basin’s center stands a circular works surrounded by a broad empty plaza. Animals crowd every roof and railing around it but do not enter the paving. Within the works, visible through open doors, a horizontal engine turns at the speed of a slow breath. Its pistons drive no shaft. Instead, each stroke seems to confirm the positions of everything else: every wingbeat, drip, footfall, press blow, cloud shadow, and revolving hook.

There is no posted law explaining this. There are only maintenance plaques, their lettering nearly erased:

**KEEP ALL MOTION WITHIN TOLERANCE.**  
**DO NOT INTRODUCE UNUSED WATER.**  
**VACANCY IS A LOAD-BEARING COMPONENT.**

Beyond the embankments lies pale, level ground without tracks or structures. The city’s noise carries some distance across it, then ends at a precise boundary. From outside, the innumerable creatures appear to writhe over the factories like smoke.

From inside, each is motionless in time, even while running, feeding, circling, calling, and breathing.

The place is overwhelmingly alive, but it never becomes more alive than it already is.
