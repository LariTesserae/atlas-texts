The valley hangs almost vertically.

It is not a valley in the ordinary sense, not a hollow pressed between mountains, but a long region of depth suspended among faces of raw stone. The mountains have not yet decided where their slopes belong. Cliffs lean away from their own foundations. Ridges begin, rise for miles, and end in blunt, unweathered planes as if the remainder has not been made. Far below—or perhaps merely in a direction the eye interprets as below—dim mineral flats extend between the roots of the heights. They are pale, dry, and seamless, without channels, sediments, or any stain left by water.

Nothing here has ever been wet.

The rock is young without being warm. It has emerged cold: gray-green basalt, blue pumice, slabs of translucent feldspar, and enormous masses of salt that are not salts of any familiar sea. Most surfaces remain sharply geometric. Scree is uncommon. There are few cracks, fewer rounded stones, and no soil except for thin drifts of mineral powder collected in sheltered angles. Erosion has only just begun its work, moving one grain at a time across a landscape too immense to show the effect.

Yet there is growth.

Dark vegetation occupies the mountain faces in widely separated colonies. At a distance each colony looks like a forest, lush and densely folded, but up close it has no trunks, leaves, roots, or sap. It consists of layered fans of carbonaceous tissue that grow directly from stone. Some spread like black ferns across vertical walls. Others rise in soft pillars, branching into velvet antlers ten or twenty times the height of a person. Their surfaces are cool and dry. They draw no moisture from the rock; instead, they feed on stress.

Where the mountains bear their own weight badly, the growth is richest.

Hair-fine filaments enter microscopic faults and drink the pressure there. Over centuries they relax one crystalline bond after another, converting strain into slow metabolism. A grove can postpone an avalanche for millennia. It darkens and thickens as the burden above it increases, until the whole colony blooms in pleated sheets of deep violet. If the pressure is suddenly released, the growth turns white, collapses into brittle lace, and never revives.

Thus the lushest places are also the most dangerous.

There are no animals. Nothing flies among the peaks, nothing burrows under the powder, and no small movement troubles the vegetation. Spores do not drift. Seeds do not fall. New colonies appear at great distances from old ones without crossing the interval. One century a cliff is naked; the next, black fronds are already folded through it, mature in shape but no larger than a hand. Their expansion is nearly imperceptible afterward.

The light comes from no visible sun. A dim pewter radiance fills the upper air and weakens toward every horizon. Shadows point in several directions, sometimes climbing the objects that cast them. The translucent mountains hold faint internal glows, revealing suspended seams and embryonic crystals. At irregular intervals a ridge goes dark from base to summit, not because anything passes before the light, but because for a few hours that ridge ceases to possess a lit side.

Through all of this runs the sound.

It is deafening, continuous, and almost without variation: a vast low roar, so powerful that loose powder trembles into patterned ripples and the pressure-fed forests keep their fronds pressed flat against the cliffs. The roar does not arrive through the air. Ears blocked with wax and hands still hear it. A stone held between the teeth makes no difference. It is present equally inside bone, tools, walls, and empty space.

No source has been found. The mountains do not visibly shake. The mineral flats do not fracture. The air, thin and bitterly cool, remains almost still.

Those who live here say the noise is the sound of distance being made.

Across the valley, space continuously enters the world in quantities too small to see and too great not to hear. Two stone markers set a hundred paces apart will be a hundred and one paces apart after several generations, though neither has moved. A hut left against a cliff gradually acquires a narrow gap behind it. Trails lengthen. Journeys home take longer than journeys out. The landscape is expanding, but not outward into anything; additional country is appearing between what already exists.

The process is slow enough to build around and too relentless to ignore.

Scattered along the middle elevations are small shelters made from fitted stone, woven mineral fiber, and the dead white lace of pressure-groves. They stand singly, often days apart. Each is low, windowless, and partially sunk into a notch cut by hand. Their builders use stone adzes, wedges of native copper, cord made from peeled black fronds, and resonant rods that expose hidden faults when struck. Fire is rare and difficult. There is little to burn, and flame behaves poorly: it bends toward the nearest unfinished edge of the world and sometimes narrows into a bright line that continues through solid rock.

The shelters are not villages. The widening land would pull any village apart.

Instead, each dwelling serves as both refuge and measurement station. Lines are scratched into the floors. Pegs are driven into opposite walls. Knotted cords span doorways. Every inhabitant keeps records of how much room has appeared overnight. A finger’s width in a lifetime is ordinary. A room that gains a full pace is abandoned, because sudden expansion is often followed by a change in direction.

A few structures are older than the people who tend them: stairs cut into cliff faces, platforms around forests of violet tissue, cairns composed of perfectly angular stones. Some now stand far from the features they were meant to reach. A stair may climb through open air a hundred paces from the mountain. A bridge may terminate over a flat plain with its opposite anchorage visible beyond several miles of newly made emptiness. No ruins decay here; they merely lose contact with their purposes.

Navigation depends on rules that are reliable only locally.

Up is usually toward the dimmer sky, but in the vicinity of certain unfinished peaks it lies sideways. Falling stones can descend past a traveler and later be seen resting on a ledge overhead. Echoes arrive before the sounds that cause them. Two people walking toward one another may both see the other receding. A path marked with cairns remains traversable so long as every cairn can see the next; if one is toppled, the intervening ground may fold, lengthen, or turn its exposed face inward.

For this reason, travelers greet stones before moving them.

They also avoid counting peaks aloud. The total changes according to the method of counting, and the mountains appear to resent being forced into agreement. From most ledges there seem to be seven principal summits. In polished copper there are nine. Shadows indicate twelve. During the long intervals called night, when the ambient light fades from pewter to charcoal, an immense thirteenth elevation becomes visible beneath the mineral flat, pointing down into a second sky.

Night lasts anywhere from an hour to three years.

No celestial bodies mark it. The cold deepens only slightly, but the roar grows more intricate. High tones emerge from it: sustained metallic cries, grinding chords, and a pulsation so slow that a single beat may occupy an entire season. The pressure-forests answer by opening their pleats. Their undersides glow with a muted green, illuminating vertical gardens along the cliffs while leaving the open spaces untouched.

In that glow, the plants seem abundant enough to cover the world. Every shelf carries fans; every fault is furred with black filaments; enormous canopies hang upside down over gulfs. Yet between colonies lie miles of untouched stone. Lushness here is concentrated, almost hoarded. Life does not soften the barrenness. It makes the empty distances more obvious.

There is no thirst in the valley because bodies adapted to it contain no water. The inhabitants—if inhabitants is the right word for the rare figures moving between shelters—are dry assemblies of flexible plates and mineral-thread muscle. They consume flakes of living growth and breathe the fine reactive dust released where new space abrades old stone. They have no beasts, no insects, no vermin, and no concept corresponding precisely to blood. A wound releases warm gray powder that tries to arrange itself into tiny replicas of the surrounding terrain.

They do not bury their dead. Burial creates depth, and depth is unstable.

Instead, the dead are seated on high platforms within mature pressure-groves. Over decades the black fans grow through the plates of the body, taking up its remaining tensions. Eventually nothing distinguishable remains except a pale branching figure embedded in the colony. Such figures serve as maps. Their limbs incline toward places where reality is becoming thin.

Those thin places are commonest along the valley’s far rim.

There the raw mountains end in smooth cross-sections, exposing not interiors but alternatives. One face shows the same landscape under fierce light, eroded into needles and ravines. Another reveals a dense green world drowned beneath an impossible ocean, though water cannot exist here. Another displays only a blank ocher color and emits the smell of iron tools not yet invented.

The faces advance by less than a nail’s thickness in a century. They are the valley’s true frontier: not edges being worn away, but surfaces behind which the rest of creation is still choosing what to become.

Nothing changes quickly. A boulder takes generations to complete its fall. A grove opens one fold during the reign of a family and closes it under their descendants. New miles insinuate themselves between old landmarks grain by grain. Even the failures of physical law proceed with patience.

Only the sound suggests urgency.

It goes on without breath or rhythm, louder than collapse, louder than weather would be if weather existed. It fills the sparse shelters, the black gardens, the unweathered summits, the stairs stranded in midair. It pours through the dry mineral flats and the downward-pointing mountain hidden beneath them.

The people sleep inside it. The vegetation feeds beside it. The cliffs are born into it.

And slowly, deafeningly, the valley becomes large enough to contain whatever it is that has not happened yet.
