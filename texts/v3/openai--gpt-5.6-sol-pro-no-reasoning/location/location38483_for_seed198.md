The city occupies the whole valley, from the riverworks on the floor to the last retaining walls beneath the cloud. Nothing resembling bare ground remains. Where the surrounding mountains once descended in scree and soil, there are now terraces of dark brick, poured concrete, riveted iron, and dressed stone, rising one behind another until distance and low weather close over them. Every surface has a purpose: roofs catch water, walls hold back slopes, stairs bridge levels, channels carry runoff, and narrow beds of imported earth support trees. Even the cliffs have been faced, bolted, drained, and fitted with galleries.

It is difficult to say where the city ends. From any high landing, districts spread for miles through the long basin, packed so tightly that individual buildings disappear into a single articulated mass. Chimneys and cooling towers stand among steep slate roofs. Warehouses shoulder against viaducts. Apartment blocks fill the spaces between foundries, pumping stations, reservoirs, schools, substations, and covered markets. Rail lines pass through upper stories and emerge on iron bridges, then vanish again into tunnels. Pipes cross streets overhead in insulated bundles. Beneath all of it are older streets, culverts, conduits, buried workshops, and foundations thick enough to have become a second geology.

The valley is cool throughout the year. Moist air gathers here, held by the ridges, and the sun is usually no more than a pale area behind cloud. Rain comes often but seldom with force. It beads on black railings, darkens masonry, gathers under cables, and runs from cornice to cornice in fine silver threads. Afterward the entire city seems to weep. Water issues from drain mouths set into retaining walls, seeps through joints in tunnel arches, and trembles on the undersides of gantries. Moss fills the lettering on old factories. Ferns root in rain gutters. Lichens have spread across concrete in broad, subdued maps.

There is plenty of water, though almost none of it is free. The original river has been divided into a hierarchy of engineered courses. In the lowest quarter it moves through a broad stepped canal between stone embankments, turning slow turbines and receiving the clear discharge of settling basins. Farther uphill, it appears as millraces, cisterns, feeder channels, pressure mains, ornamental reservoirs, and narrow open gutters beside the stairs. During wet weeks, excess water slides over tiled spillways and drops between districts in controlled falls. Their continuous pale movement can be seen at a distance, but they make less noise than expected; the sound is absorbed by fog, masonry, vegetation, and the immense depth of the built valley.

Plant life has colonized every permitted recess and many that were never intended for it. Plane trees grow from reinforced courtyards. Alders line the canals, their roots enclosed in iron cages beneath the water. Ivy binds abandoned hoists to warehouse walls. Community glasshouses fill entire roofs, glowing faintly green on dark afternoons. There are orchards on the upper terraces, dense allotments beside filtration beds, and long strips of cabbages and herbs planted beneath transmission pylons. In some districts, buildings can scarcely be seen behind layered growth: vines over brick, shrubs along balconies, moss on tiles, reeds in runoff basins, and trees lifting through old factory floors whose roofs were removed decades ago.

This lushness does not make the place feel wild. It is cultivated, pruned, harvested, surveyed. Soil arrives by freight. Roots are directed away from pipes. Fallen leaves are collected for municipal composters. Even the reeds belong to the water-treatment system. Nature survives abundantly, but within gauges, schedules, load limits, and retaining walls.

Animals are uncommon. A few small birds inhabit the eaves and glasshouse rafters, appearing as quick movements rather than a constant population. Fish live in the lower settling canals, where the water is slow and oxygenated. Cats are occasionally seen in boiler yards, and there are rumors of foxes using the disused service tunnels above the eastern works. Insects return briefly during the mildest weeks, hovering over warm cisterns. Their scarcity is not sinister. The valley is simply too enclosed, too elevated, too cool, and too thoroughly occupied to offer much unassigned habitat.

At first the city seems quiet. There is no broad traffic roar, no surf, no wind moving through open fields. Most transport runs on rails or electric cables, and the largest machinery is housed behind thick walls. What reaches the streets is a restrained industrial murmur: pumps turning beneath gratings, transformers humming in rain, belts ticking inside distant works, a tram flange sounding once around a wet curve. Every so often a pressure valve exhales from somewhere overhead. A chain strikes a mast. Water taps steadily into a cistern. Then the silence closes again.

Yet nearly everything is moving.

Freight lifts rise within brick towers. Funiculars ascend the steeper quarters. Lock gates open and shut along the canal. Conveyor galleries carry aggregate, fuel, scrap metal, timber, produce, and finished goods from one district to another. Cranes rotate above redevelopment sites. Trains cross the valley at several elevations, their windows briefly visible through the mist. Streets are continually diverted by repairs. Scaffolds spread across facades and come down elsewhere. A row of workshops may be dismantled in a month, its brick cleaned and reused in a retaining wall farther uphill. Old reservoirs become housing courts; housing courts become substations; substations are wrapped inside new public halls without ever ceasing operation.

The turbulence here is not loud disorder but unending rearrangement. The valley has reached a mature shape, weathered and stable as a whole, while its parts are replaced incessantly. It is like an old machine maintained while running. No district is ever complete. Masonry bears marks from former roofs; bridges contain spans from several centuries; pipes divide, merge, and change diameter as they pass through successive eras of construction. New ceramic conduits lie beside corroded iron mains still kept as emergency reserves. Fresh concrete braces stone arches polished by generations of damp.

Storms reveal how active the place truly is. Clouds sink until the upper terraces disappear. Runoff rises in every channel. Automatic gates redirect water across the stepped basin, and entire streets alter function: plazas become retention ponds, rail cuttings become spillways, greenhouse tanks fill, turbines engage, and pumping halls wake one after another down the valley. Warning lamps glow amber through the rain. Crews in dark waterproof clothing move along catwalks, opening access panels and clearing screens of leaves. The city flexes around the water rather than resisting it. By morning, most surfaces are merely wet, and the stored excess is being released through turbines in the lower works.

Erosion has long since been incorporated into design. Corners are rounded by rain and touch. Steps sag at their centers. Brickwork beneath overflowing gutters has worn into shallow grooves. On the oldest canal stairs, stone and mortar have softened into a single rippled surface. Nothing looks pristine, but little looks ruined. Weathering is expected. Materials are chosen to lose themselves slowly and legibly. Replacement pieces are dated, not disguised. The city’s age is visible as a density of repairs.

Higher up, where the valley narrows beneath the ridgeline, the buildings become lower and the vegetation thicker. Reservoirs occupy former quarries, edged with maintenance paths and wind-stunted trees. From there the city can be seen as a dim, damp expanse of roofs, towers, bridges, steam, and greenery filling every contour below. No central monument dominates it. The eye moves instead from system to system: the silver courses of water, the red lines of rail, the dark bands of viaduct, the pale rectangles of glasshouses, the slow plumes from heat plants.

At night, illumination remains restrained. Lamps are fixed low under awnings and along steps, leaving upper walls in shadow. Factory windows shine in isolated grids. Greenhouses cast a diluted vegetal glow into the mist. Signals change beside empty-looking tracks. Reflections occupy the wet paving more strongly than the lamps themselves, so that the ground appears to hold a second, broken city.

The governing rule of the valley is that space must perform more than one task. A roof is also a garden and a reservoir. A retaining wall contains workshops and carries a railway. A canal cools machinery, moves freight, drains storms, and grows reeds. A public stair covers a pressure main. Nothing is merely vacant, decorative, or leftover. Even demolition produces sorted stone, brick, steel, glass, timber, and soil for another district.

The second rule is that change must not interrupt continuity. Water must keep flowing while channels are rebuilt. Trains must pass while bridges are strengthened. Families remain in a block as its outer walls are replaced around them. Trees are moved rather than felled where possible, lifted with boxed roots onto freight platforms and carried to newly finished terraces. The city is never permitted the luxury of stopping.

Seen from within, it can feel enclosed beyond measure: wall beside wall, bridge above bridge, roots under paving, cables under roots, rooms behind retaining structures, and more districts beyond the rain. Seen from the reservoir heights, however, it is open and immense—a whole regional landscape manufactured in layers, green with careful abundance, darkened by water and altitude, and in perpetual motion despite its deep quiet.
