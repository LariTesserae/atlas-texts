The city occupies a warm, low basin so broad that its opposite edge is usually lost in violet haze. It does not crowd the basin. Buildings rise in separated groups—slender districts, isolated towers, long terraces—leaving expanses of reed meadow, dark pavement, and unfinished ground between them. From above, it would resemble an archipelago assembled on dry land.

The ground is new.

In some places it is black and finely porous, with a faint glassy shine; elsewhere it forms pale shelves that break at the edges into sharp flakes. There are no softened hills, no old gullies, no patient curves. Embankments retain the facets left by their making. Channels turn abruptly. Fresh mineral ridges protrude through parks and plazas as if the basin floor had crystallized only a few seasons ago. Roots have begun to enter these materials, but have not yet broken them down. Soil exists mostly where the city has persuaded it to exist.

Warmth sits close to the surface. It is not oppressive, but persistent: the warmth of stone after sunset, of enclosed machinery, of air that has traveled over wet leaves. Mist sometimes gathers ankle-deep in the open spaces. Rain is infrequent and brief. It comes in narrow moving curtains, darkening one neighborhood while another remains dry, and runs immediately into hairline grooves engineered throughout the ground.

Water here is less a landscape than an event.

It beads on walls overnight. It gathers beneath broad-leaved plants, condenses along suspended cables, and appears at dawn inside shallow ceramic basins set into the streets. After rain, low channels become silver for an hour. Courtyards fill to the depth of a hand and then empty without visible drains. Beneath the city, temporary rivers form in smooth conduits and disperse into storage membranes folded somewhere outside ordinary geometry. For days afterward, the vegetation grows with startling speed. Then the surfaces dry, the channels become paths again, and dust returns to the avenues.

The plants are lush without being continuous. Dense groves stand apart from one another, separated by bare plazas and long strips of mineral ground. Each grove has its own character. One is all immense, dark leaves held horizontally to harvest condensation. Another consists of red-jointed canes whose hollow stems whistle only when humidity changes. There are gardens of pale moss under bridges, orchards rooted in vertical walls, and tangles of glossy vines climbing structures that have no windows and perhaps no interiors.

Much of the vegetation was designed, but not all of it obeys its original design. Trees migrate slowly by withdrawing their roots from one patch of manufactured soil and extending them into another. Ferns rearrange their fronds to shade heat-sensitive machinery. After rainfall, thin green membranes spread over abandoned transit lanes; by midday they have opened thousands of tiny pores, and by the following evening they peel themselves loose and roll away in the breeze.

The city’s architecture is precise but difficult to interpret. Its oldest buildings may be only decades old, yet several appear to have been constructed before the ground beneath them. They are tall, narrow, and widely spaced, with skins of charcoal ceramic, translucent metal, or something resembling compressed dusk. Their corners are too sharp for weathered structures. Some towers taper downward and meet the earth at a single illuminated point. Others stop a few meters above the ground, supported by fields that make falling leaves hesitate beneath them.

Between the towers extend civic terraces, rail-lines without rails, covered markets, reservoirs, and broad roads rarely occupied by vehicles. Infrastructure is abundant, but inhabitants are not. The city seems prepared for a population many times larger than the one that uses it—or for a population whose members cannot always be seen.

Doors appear where they are needed rather than where façades imply they should be. A traveler approaching a blank wall may find a seam unfolding at walking speed. Once they pass, the seam closes and the wall resumes its seamless state. Elevators cross not only height but distance: one enters beside a garden and exits kilometers away at nearly the same elevation, with a moment between in which the body feels impossibly large.

The city does not generate power so much as negotiate differences. Heat between shadow and stone, pressure between dry air and wet leaves, the fractional pull of the moon on stored water—each gradient is collected. Dark panels turn toward events that have not yet occurred. A rainfall predicted with certainty may illuminate a district hours before the first cloud forms. At night, buried systems exchange minute distortions with objects above the atmosphere. The resulting energy arrives cool and almost silent.

Light is scarce by design. The sky is usually covered by a high, thin veil that transforms the sun into a dull pearl. Streets are lit at knee height or from beneath translucent pavement. Buildings mark their edges with soft blue-green lines. The brightest things are often living: patches of moss awakened by footsteps, suspended seedpods, wet bark carrying slow pulses of gold. No glare is tolerated. Even the displays in public squares seem to form directly inside the viewer’s vision, leaving the air dark.

In that dimness, distances become uncertain. A tower five kilometers away may seem close enough to touch, while a grove across the road recedes into green-black depth. The basin feels open, but not exposed. Its emptiness has structure. Every vacant tract contains buried lines, weather instruments, dormant foundations, or fields whose purpose can be inferred only when rain passes over them.

The city is mostly quiet. There is the soft ticking of contraction in cooling walls, the drip of condensation, and occasionally a low tone moving through the ground. Transit produces no engine noise. Machines pass as displacements of air, announced by trembling grass. Far-off construction is visible as towers altering their silhouettes, but the work itself makes only a faint sound like sand poured onto cloth.

After rain, the city becomes noisier. Water chatters through temporary channels. Leaves snap open. Pumping membranes throb below plazas. The red canes sing in uneven clusters as humid air leaves them, and for several hours the basin carries a dispersed, breathlike music.

Animals are uncommon. Small silver insects appear at dusk wherever mineral surfaces remain warm. They have no fixed wings; reflective films unfold from their bodies only during flight and dissolve upon landing. Soft-bodied grazers inhabit the condensation gardens, leaving neat semicircles in the moss. A few tall, narrow birds walk the drainage channels after storms, probing for larvae. Their calls are so low that they are felt through benches and walls before they are heard.

Nothing here seems to prey openly on anything else. Predation occurs as disappearance. Insects enter patches of shadow that are not present the following day. The tall birds occasionally stop, tilt their heads toward an empty façade, and are gone when one looks again. Whether the city consumes them, relocates them, or merely edits the observer’s access to them is unresolved.

Human life, or its descendant, gathers in scattered pockets. Evening markets occupy only a corner of enormous plazas. Apartments glow in irregular constellations across otherwise dark towers. People cultivate gardens beneath transit spans, share condensed water, and use the city’s impossible systems with the casual caution one gives to old weather. They know which walls lead home, which elevators have begun delivering passengers to obsolete districts, and which streets should not be walked while the pavement is flowering.

There are rules.

No one stores rainwater in a sealed vessel; sealed water begins to acquire tides.

No building may cast the same shadow for more than seven consecutive days.

When a grove moves across a road, the road is rerouted rather than the grove restrained.

A door that opens onto warm wind must be left open until the wind stops, even if another landscape is visible beyond it.

At the basin’s center lies the widest empty space: a shallow depression several kilometers across, covered in dark reeds and scattered ruins. Maps call it a reservoir, though it has held no standing lake within living memory. The reeds grow from cracks in fresh black stone. Here and there, fragments of streets descend beneath them, perfectly preserved, as if the district was lowered intact and the basin formed around it.

During the driest weeks, the depression is nearly silent. Warm air quivers above it. Then, on certain dim afternoons, condensation begins to form simultaneously on every surface in the city. Towers darken. Leaves bow beneath clear beads. Ceramic basins fill. Water runs uphill through the channels toward the central reeds.

The reservoir does not flood. Instead, the air above it deepens.

Reflections appear where there is no water: whole inverted neighborhoods trembling among the reed tops, lit by a brighter and less familiar sky. For several hours the city’s doors become unreliable, animals emerge from places that have no local entrances, and the low underground tone rises until every structure is faintly resonant.

By night the effect subsides. The gathered water has vanished. New mineral shelves are found around the depression’s edge, sharp and uneroded. Sometimes a foundation has appeared on them. Sometimes an entire tower stands where there was empty ground the previous evening, its systems already connected, its interior warm, its walls carrying condensation.

By morning, vines will have begun to climb it.
