The high country is entirely manufactured.

From a distance it resembles a pale mountain range under hard daylight: long ridges, steep-sided basins, a confusion of needles and shelves extending farther than the eye can comfortably order. Only on approach does its construction become clear. The peaks are assemblies of ceramic spars and riveted plates. The valleys are gaps left between structural districts. Cliffs terminate in flanges. Scree slopes resolve into millions of identical washers, poured decades ago and not yet settled.

There is no native stone beneath any of it. Dig through the dust and one finds mesh; beneath the mesh, insulating foam; beneath that, another occupied level where the inhabitants look upward at their own bright sky.

The altitude is genuine, though its cause is disputed. Air is thin and cool. Breath becomes laborious on the upper gantries, where the sun—or the apparatus performing the office of a sun—casts short, exact shadows. Yet no foundation has ever been reached. Surveyors descend through maintenance wells for months and emerge into higher districts than those they left. Elevation is therefore measured not from a base but by symptoms: boiling point, dizziness, frost thickness, the color of exposed blood.

The landscape is open in extent but crowded in substance. Towers stand almost shoulder to shoulder, linked by bridges, pipes, suspended rails, ladders, conveyor housings, and conduits bundled as thick as ancient trunks. Between them lie manufactured mesas surfaced in white refractory tile. Ventilation chimneys grow in groves. Cable fields sweep from ridge to ridge, each strand singing too faintly to hear unless one rests a tooth against the anchoring bolts.

There are roads, but most were built for machines that no longer fit them. Some are narrow paired channels polished by iron wheels. Others are broad processional avenues with threaded surfaces, as if whole districts were once screwed into position along them. Where an avenue crosses a precipice, its underside is often more elaborate than its top: densely finned, numbered, and inhabited by colonies of small animals that cling upside down to the warm metal.

Everything is new in shape and old in finish.

The edges remain sharp. Corners have not rounded, channels have barely deepened, and the immense fabricated slopes show little of the softening expected from weather. Nevertheless, oxidation blooms across them in sudden colors—turquoise, orange, violet-black—without noticeably consuming the material beneath. Rust here behaves less like corrosion than handwriting. It gathers in repeating marks, sometimes in columns, sometimes around doors that did not exist the previous season. Workers paint over these markings. By the following year the paint has rusted, while the underlying iron is clean.

Water is present but never broad. There are no lakes visible from the heights, only intermittent gleams: condensation ponds in shallow trays, narrow rivers running along seams, rainwater trapped in enormous bolt heads. Clouds rarely fill the sky. Instead they form inside the structures, pressing outward through grilles as cool white breath.

Most mornings begin dry. Toward midday, sunlight heats the eastern faces of the towers, and moisture migrates through the enclosed works. Droplets appear on shaded pipes. They swell, tremble, and travel uphill along engraved serial numbers. By evening, they have collected in cistern courts and inspection hollows. Some nights there is rain, sparse and vertical, falling only within geometric areas. A square kilometer may be wet while the neighboring span remains powdered with dust. Rain can sometimes be seen descending through six or seven superimposed districts, passing through floors without leaving holes.

The rivers are equally unreliable. They emerge from valves no one has opened and follow channels built for other fluids. A stream may cross a plaza, enter a drain, and issue moments later from a spout several hundred meters above. Fish occasionally accompany it.

Vegetation survives in thin concessions. Black moss fills engraved lettering. Tufts of silver grass root in the felt around expansion joints, bending not with the wind but toward whichever machinery is currently dormant. In condensation courts grow low, translucent ferns whose fronds are perforated like filter screens. Their spores collect in bearings and are blamed for many slow failures.

The largest plants are cable-vines. They appear botanical from afar, looping between towers in dark green ropes, but their skins are braided copper beneath a layer of living tissue. They put out small yellow leaves in cold weather and connector plugs in spring. No one knows whether they are plants colonizing abandoned cable or cables attempting to flower. Maintenance crews prune them carefully and submit the cut ends for electrical testing. They always carry a weak current, usually matching the heartbeat of the person holding the meter.

There are many animals. They make the place feel fuller than its scant greenery should permit.

Pale cliff-goats move across vertical platework, placing their hooves on rivet heads. Their horns curve into near-perfect handles, and older animals bear stamped load ratings along the horn base. Flocks of small bright birds nest in siren housings and switch boxes. When startled they rise in silence, hundreds at once, flashing white against the white sky; only after they settle does the sound of their wingbeats arrive.

Long-bodied foxes patrol the pipe galleries. Their fur accumulates metallic dust until they resemble cast statues. They feed on insulation mice, beetles with glass wing-cases, and the slow moths that gather around inactive lamps. In wet seasons, salamanders crowd the cisterns. Some have transparent organs. Some contain tiny rotating parts. Whether these parts sustain them or merely happen inside them is one of the region’s enduring biological disputes.

No creature vocalizes often. The goats communicate by knocking their horns against the structure, sending messages through kilometers of connected metal. Birds open their beaks, but their songs emerge from distant speakers days later. Predators hunt without calls. Even insects are subdued, crawling over hot plates with only a faint dry ticking.

The machinery is quiet as well, despite its scale.

There are factories everywhere: vast halls layered inside the ridges, assembly chambers open to the sky, stamping floors, kiln towers, reservoirs, crane forests. Most operate too slowly for motion to be seen. A press descends the thickness of a fingernail each week. Conveyor belts complete one circuit in a human lifetime. Crane hooks drift across valleys over generations, carrying loads whose original destinations have vanished.

One can stand beside an engine the size of a town and hear only an occasional contraction in its casing, like ice shifting on a winter lake. The turbines turn, but below the threshold of ordinary perception. Their motion reveals itself in long consequences: a bridge gradually tightening; shadows displaced from their expected positions; a neighborhood waking to discover that all its doors now open inward.

The local people live in narrow settlements fitted into the machinery rather than built upon it. Houses occupy empty mold racks and decommissioned control cabinets. Marketplaces gather beneath gantry cranes. Bright cloth awnings soften the glare, though their colors bleach rapidly and then return at night. Chimneys are used as stairwells. Pressure vessels become meeting halls. Every dwelling must retain at least one accessible original panel, because the environment may at any time require maintenance through the home.

Property is defined by function rather than area. A family may own the right to warm a certain pipe, collect water from a specific condenser, or sleep within the acoustic shadow of a flywheel. Maps are written as instructions: climb until the air tastes of pennies; follow the rail that carries empty hooks; turn left where your reflection arrives before you do.

The brightest places are the least trusted. Illumination pours from ceilingless skies and industrial lamps alike, clean and almost shadowless. Surfaces glitter with mica-like filings. Painted safety bands—yellow, red, electric blue—remain vivid for centuries. In some basins the light has density. Birds perch on horizontal beams of it. Rain strikes it and runs along its upper surface. Travelers caught beneath a descending band of brightness may find themselves pressed gently to the floor until sunset.

Elsewhere, perspective loosens.

A tower viewed from one side has twenty floors; viewed from the opposite side, it has thirty-seven. Parallel rails converge, cross, and continue without bending. There are doors painted on walls that open only when ignored. Reflections perform maintenance tasks after the workers have gone. A person may see themself in a polished plate at great distance, older, holding a tool they have not yet acquired.

These failures of reality are neither dramatic nor fast. They accumulate with industrial patience.

Every year the northern ridge gains one additional repetition: another arch, another row of windows, another identical cooling tower. No construction is observed. The copies simply become harder to deny. At the same time, the southern districts lose distinctions. Separate staircases begin arriving at the same landing. Houses share rooms without sharing walls. Eventually an entire neighborhood may become one place approached by thousands of doors.

The inhabitants have rules for this.

Never count identical objects aloud.

Do not follow a pipe after it changes temperature twice.

If rain falls upward, stand beneath something that has a serial number.

Never sleep in a room whose corners are all visible.

Animals may cross unmarked thresholds safely, but this safety does not necessarily extend to their riders.

If a machine begins producing an object, allow it to finish, even if the object is impossible or alive.

Above all, do not attempt to locate the natural world beneath the built one. There is no agreement that such a world ever existed. Those who search for bedrock return carrying samples of painted steel, bone, compressed feathers, or a hard blue material that evaporates when exposed to moonlight. A few return with soil. The soil contains tiny foundations.

At dusk the high industrial ranges cool. Condensation silver-coats the railings. Sparse grasses fold themselves into seams. Herds descend along the tower faces, and silent birds gather in immense numbers on the power lines. The manufactured sun goes down behind a ridge assembled from plates too large for any visible crane.

For several minutes, the whole landscape can be understood as one machine: peaks as housings, rivers as lubricant, animals as mobile components, towns as heat, people as impulses traveling through a circuit of impossible scale.

Then the lights come on, one by one, extending beyond the horizon and downward through the floors beneath it.

They do not form constellations.

They form instructions.
