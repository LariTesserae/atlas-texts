The range rises from the drowned coast in a succession of submerged cliffs. Only the highest summits break the surface, and even these appear and disappear behind rain. What once would have been a chain of alpine valleys is now a cold inland sea: deep, steep-sided, and open for many kilometers, with black ridges dividing one flooded basin from the next.

Underwater, the original terrain remains immense. Mountainsides descend through green light into blue, then into depths where the water is almost opaque. Their faces have been worn for so long that little loose stone remains. The cliffs are not raw or jagged but rounded, fluted, and deeply grooved, as though shaped by a single ancient current. Broad shelves interrupt the plunging walls. Old cirques form dark amphitheaters; passes have become straits; valleys run far below the surface as cold trenches filled with slow-moving water.

The most exposed rock is dense, fine-grained, and nearly black. Pale mineral bands cross it in long, bent lines. Every ledge has been polished by ice, floodwater, and sediment until it feels almost manufactured. In sheltered hollows, however, centuries of silt have accumulated in soft gray plains. Each storm lifts this material into enormous clouds. They travel along the drowned slopes like weather, obscuring whole forests before settling again.

Vegetation is abundant but vertical rather than pastoral. Kelp grows from the high shelves in crowded columns, its trunks thick as young trees. The longest fronds reach upward for tens of meters and stream horizontally when the current strengthens. Lower down are forests of smaller growth: dark red weed, pale fan-shaped algae, eelgrass in the drowned meadows, and coarse mosslike mats gripping the rock. The water is cool enough that growth is slow, but it has had a very long time. Almost every stable surface carries several layers of life.

Near the surface, the forests are olive and gold. Below them, where green light dominates, they become rust-colored and wine-dark. Deeper still, vegetation thins into low crusts and branching colonies that resemble frost. The border between plant and animal is not visually obvious. Soft corals stand among the algae; anemones occupy old root holes; colonies of mussels sheath entire boulders in blue-black shells. Fallen kelp does not lie still. It rolls down the slopes in tangled masses, collecting bones, shells, cable, and scraps of timber until it catches on another ledge.

The water is seldom calm. Wind drives hard across the exposed basins, raising short, steep waves that rebound from the mountain walls. Rain often closes the distance altogether, turning air and sea into adjoining forms of the same gray substance. Beneath the surface, tide and weather force water through the drowned passes. Currents collide in the old valleys and climb the cliffs in powerful upwellings. Kelp forests bend nearly flat. Shoals divide, reform, and flash silver in the shifting light. At the narrowest straits, the water roars continuously.

The mountains alter the sound rather than containing it. Above water there is rain on rock, surf striking the summit islands, wind in the few salt-burned shrubs, and the low concussion of waves entering sea caves. Underwater the place is louder and more crowded: the crackle of shellfish, stones knocking in surge channels, the groan of kelp stems, engines carrying over great distances, and the intermittent metallic hammer of loose structures. Whales call through the deeper basins. Their voices return from the cliffs in delayed, distorted echoes.

Animal life gathers wherever the currents lift nutrients. Seabirds occupy the remaining summits in such numbers that the rocks appear white from a distance. Gannets, gulls, auks, and cormorants wheel in the rain and descend through gaps in the waves. Seals sleep on tilted slabs or hunt between kelp columns. Schools of herring fill the upper water, followed by mackerel, tuna, porpoises, and sharks. In the cold depths, large cod move among the drowned crags. Lobsters inhabit fractures in the old stone; crabs cross the silt flats in loose migrations; octopuses den inside pipes, culverts, and collapsed masonry.

Human structures are scattered and stubborn. There are no intact drowned cities, only isolated traces from the last inhabited period of the valleys: a roofless stone station on a summit ledge, the square foundation of a signal house, retaining walls descending into green water. High on one cliff, iron rings mark an obsolete waterline. Lower down, cables vanish into the trench.

The most substantial works are industrial platforms anchored to the emergent peaks. They are compact, weathered places of steel grating, concrete, tanks, winches, and enclosed machinery. Some pump cold deep water through heat exchangers. Others maintain tidal turbines installed in the flooded passes, where the current is strongest. Their pylons descend beyond sight, furred with mussels and weed. Maintenance boats shelter behind breakwaters made from quarried summit rock. Cranes stand folded in the rain.

Nothing built here remains clean for long. Salt blooms on concrete. Bolts acquire orange halos. Paint lifts in thick scales. Nets snag on ladders; birds nest in ventilation housings; kelp winds itself around propeller guards. Every machine operates within an ecology eager to occupy it. Even active turbines must periodically be stopped and cleared of weed, driftwood, and dense clusters of shell.

The weather makes work uncertain. A clear morning may reveal the whole drowned range: scattered dark summits, distant curtains of rain, white water at every pass, and, beneath the surface, vast bands of kelp showing through like forests seen from the air. By afternoon the wind can reverse. Water piles into the eastern basins, overflows the saddles, and sends violent internal waves down into the trenches. Instruments register movement far below while the surface seems briefly ordinary. Then cold water rises, carrying deep fish, torn vegetation, and pale sediment into daylight.

There is no single shoreline. It changes hourly with surge and tide, and seasonally with storms. Caves inhale and exhale. Reefs emerge for minutes and disappear. Summit ponds fill with rain, become brackish under spray, then drain through cracks into the sea. On the largest islands, low grasses and tough shrubs survive behind stone ribs, pressed close to the ground. Their roots hold thin, black soil enriched by birds. In rare sheltered folds grow ferns, sorrel, and a few twisted rowans no taller than a person. These small terrestrial thickets seem almost exotic amid so much water.

The landscape’s age is clearest in its forms. Despite the violence of the present, the underlying mountains feel finished. Their soft profiles and immense worn surfaces belong to erosion on a scale no storm can meaningfully revise. Rockfalls occur, cliffs shed blocks, channels scour and refill, but these are surface events. The greater shape has settled into permanence: long backs, broad bowls, blunt pinnacles, and valleys descending farther than light.

Turbulence belongs instead to the water and to everything living in it. The sea is always rearranging its contents—schools, silt, weed, wreckage, larvae, heat. The mountains stand while whole temporary landscapes form around them. A kelp forest can be stripped from one shelf and heaped onto another in a night. A storm can fill a clear basin with suspended earth. Herring can turn an empty strait into a dense, flickering mass, and predators can disperse it before noon.

In calmer intervals the place becomes unexpectedly legible. Sunlight passes between showers and reaches down through the upper water in wide moving columns. The drowned ledges appear one after another. Fish cast shadows on stone. Old walls, natural ridges, turbine housings, and kelp trunks briefly belong to the same ordered scene. Then wind roughens the surface, the light breaks apart, and the range closes again beneath its cold, crowded sea.
