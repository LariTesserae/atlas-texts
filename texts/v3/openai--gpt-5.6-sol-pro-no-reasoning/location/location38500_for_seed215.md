The city stands on a high, unfinished plain.

It is not unfinished in the sense of abandoned construction. The ground itself is young. Across hundreds of kilometers, dark shelves of basalt are still settling into place, their surfaces scarcely weathered, their edges blunt rather than carved. Low ridges rise where pressure has folded the crust, and shallow basins gather between them before any permanent system of rivers has decided where to run. The landscape has drainage, but not yet a pattern.

Water is everywhere without being deep. It lies in long steel-colored pools over impermeable stone, trembles in seams, and seeps from newly opened fractures. Rain comes often: fine, cold rain that passes over the plateau in broad gray curtains. Afterward, every depression holds a temporary lake. Black rock shines. Vapor condenses on railings, towers, fur, feathers, and the undersides of bridges. On clear mornings, frost draws pale margins around the pools.

The plateau is enormously high, though its breadth makes this difficult to perceive. There are few dramatic summits nearby. Instead, the whole country has been lifted toward the thin part of the atmosphere. The horizon lies far away, interrupted by low volcanic swells and the remote white backs of mountains still rising. Air pressure is modest, wind carries little warmth, and sunlight has a hard clarity even beneath common cloud.

At first the city seems small in all this space. Then distance corrects the impression.

Its inhabited districts are scattered rather than continuous. One cluster of towers may be separated from the next by fifty kilometers of wet stone and grazing ground, yet both belong to the same municipality. Transit lines span the gaps in almost invisible arcs. Reservoirs, weather stations, animal corridors, landing grounds, power fields, and buried utilities bind the settlements into a single urban organism spread across a region the size of a country.

There are no broad suburbs. Construction gathers densely where it occurs, then stops.

The buildings stand on adjustable foundations drilled only shallowly into the raw crust. The city’s engineers do not assume the land has finished moving. Towers lean by design, correcting themselves as basalt contracts or a buried chamber fills with water. Streets are flexible plates whose joints open and close through the seasons. Bridges alter their length by a few centimeters each night. Whole districts migrate imperceptibly, keeping alignment with transport routes while the ground beneath them shifts.

The highest structures are not skyscrapers but atmospheric masts: thin, pale lines reaching into cloud. They draw moisture from passing air and lower it through capillary lattices, sorting the water by mineral content before it enters the city. From a distance, these masts appear to be making the rain. Around them, cloud condenses into slow pennants.

The technology is advanced enough to be unobtrusive. There are no smoking industrial belts, no constant engine noise, no forests of cables. Energy moves through buried superconducting loops and through engineered layers in the bedrock. Freight platforms cross the open country without wheels, following mapped gravitational gradients too weak for an unaided body to notice. Windows adjust not merely their opacity but the direction from which they admit light. Exterior walls repair frost fractures before they widen. Waste is separated molecule by molecule and returned to fabrication systems or soil.

Yet the city does not look miraculous. It looks practical: dark ceramics, pale alloys, rough local stone, translucent panels filmed with rain. Advanced engineering has not made the place ornate. The cold discourages exposed complexity, and the scale of the plateau makes display seem futile.

Vegetation is sparse. Most of the ground remains bare basalt, gravel, or thin mineral mud. Life gathers where water lingers: rust-colored moss around pool edges, short silver grasses in protected hollows, black lichens on south-facing rocks. A few low shrubs grow inside rings of windbreak stone. Their branches spread along the ground rather than upward.

There are cultivated areas, but they do not resemble fields. Greenhouses are sunk into shallow excavations and roofed with clear, load-bearing membranes level with the terrain. Algae beds occupy warm runoff channels. In sheltered urban courts, dwarf fruit trees grow beneath retractable ceilings. From above, agriculture appears as a scattering of green apertures in the black plain.

Animal life is far more abundant than the vegetation suggests.

Great herds of compact, shaggy grazers move between moss beds and managed forage stations. They travel in long dispersed fronts, never exhausting one patch. Their broad feet cross saturated ground without sinking deeply, and their coats collect beads of condensation that freeze white along the outer hairs.

Waterfowl crowd the seasonal pools. They arrive within hours of rainfall, as though each new body of water has announced itself across the entire plateau. Gray birds with scarlet bills pick through fresh mud; long-necked divers occupy the deeper basins; small black-winged flocks turn together above the city and settle on warm roofs at dusk. In drainage trenches, amphibious animals shelter beneath slotted stones placed for them by municipal crews.

Predators are present but seldom seen. Pale, long-legged hunters follow the margins of the herds. Smaller ones live among the transit pylons and culverts, feeding on burrowing mammals and nesting birds. The city has not driven them out. Its planning systems continuously revise traffic schedules, lighting, and construction boundaries around migration and breeding data. Sometimes a freight line closes for six days because a colony has chosen its embankment. Sometimes an entire district dims at night so fledglings can navigate by the stars.

The result is not wilderness and not domestication, but an active agreement. Animals learn the city’s rhythms; the city learns theirs.

Human movement is similarly measured. People travel mostly inside low, quiet vehicles or along enclosed pedestrian ways. In settled areas there are markets, schools, clinics, workshops, observatories, and public baths warmed by deep thermal wells. Streets widen around pools rather than draining them. Rainwater runs visibly through channels in plazas, where mineral sensors read the changing chemistry of the young rock.

The inhabited interiors are warmer than their appearance suggests. Entrances open through double vestibules into rooms of wood composite, woven fiber, and matte ceramic. Public halls contain gardens under spectral lamps. Coats steam gently on drying walls. Windows frame enormous portions of empty country.

Despite the urban infrastructure and abundant fauna, the plateau is mostly quiet. Wind makes a low breath around building edges. Water ticks from ledges. Far off, birds call across a temporary lake, each note separated by seconds of open air. Herds produce a muffled collective sound: hooves on wet stone, occasional grunts, the brushing of dense coats. Transit vehicles pass with little more than a pressure change and a brief hum felt in the ribs.

The loudest events are natural. Thunder rolls for minutes across the level country. New fractures sometimes announce themselves with a deep report beneath the ground. During rapid thaws, sheets of ice detach from shaded escarpments and break across the flats. The city listens for such sounds through thousands of buried instruments.

This is a landscape before erosion has become its principal author. Water has begun the work but has not had time to make canyons. There are only tentative channels, shallow spillways, and stains indicating where streams ran last season. A basin may overflow east one year and west the next. Gravel fans begin and end within a few hundred meters. Cliffs are fresh-faced, their fallen blocks still angular and uncolonized.

Construction proceeds under the same conditions. Survey maps are treated as temporary hypotheses. Neighborhoods are placed where the ground seems stable, but stability here means decades, not permanence. Buildings are made to be disassembled and moved. Foundations preserve records of every motion. No street is named after its direction, because directions of travel may change.

The weather gives the region its daily pulse. Cold fronts arrive from the western icefields, driving rain across the high plain. Pools spread, join, and divide. Then a dry wind descends, peeling vapor from their surfaces. Frost closes the shallows overnight. By noon the ice darkens and breaks into loose plates. Animals follow these transitions closely, and city systems do the same: opening channels, storing water, warming roosts, rerouting travelers.

Light is ordinary and therefore precious. Days are not illuminated by strange suns or luminous vegetation. There is gray morning, white noon, blue shadow, and the amber slant of evening. After rain, the low sun turns every wet surface reflective. For a short time the plateau becomes a broken mirror extending beyond sight, with towers, animals, clouds, and moving people repeated in fragments underfoot.

At night, illumination remains restrained. Settlements glow close to the ground. Tall buildings show only narrow vertical bands where rooms are occupied. Transit lines become faint moving beads across the emptiness. Beyond them, the land is dark enough for the stars to appear hard and numerous.

From the outer observation platforms, the city’s true form becomes clear. It is not a mass imposed on the plain but a constellation laid over it: dense nodes, long connections, vast intervals. Its most sophisticated machines are devoted not to conquering the young landscape but to remaining responsive to it.

Under rain, the rock continues cooling. Pools find new outlets. Moss extends by the width of a hand. A herd alters its route around a fresh crack. Far below, the plateau rises another fraction of a millimeter, carrying the city, its towers, its quiet rooms, and its innumerable animals higher into the cold.
