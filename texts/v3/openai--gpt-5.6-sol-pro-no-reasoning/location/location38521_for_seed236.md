The city occupies the crown of a young black mesa, though calling it a city suggests builders, tools, and intention. None of those exist here. It is a city in the way a termite mound is a tower and a rib cage is an arcade: organized construction without architect or instrument.

The mesa is small enough to cross before the sun moves perceptibly. Its edges fall away in steep, recently opened scarps, dark stone breaking into glassy planes and angular shelves. Almost nothing has had time to round. There are no valleys cut by rain, no streambeds, no fans of washed gravel below. The surrounding highland is a pale mineral waste, creased where the crust first cooled but otherwise unmarked. Water has never passed through it.

Above this blank country rises the Crown.

From a distance it resembles a dense grove of copper, soot, and greenish gold. Nearer, its vegetation resolves into forms that are not quite plants. They possess no wet sap, no soft pith, and no leaves made for drinking light through waterborne tissue. Their bodies are braided from dry protein, silicate fibers, waxes, and thin metallic salts. They grow by trapping dust and warm gases. Each filament sorts the atmosphere grain by grain, passing useful molecules inward along pressure gradients and crystallizing its own scaffolding around them.

The largest growths are columnar. They stand shoulder to shoulder across the mesa, hollow trunks swelling into buttresses, branching high overhead and meeting neighboring crowns. Where they touch, they fuse. In time these unions have produced streets, vaults, courtyards, galleries, narrow stairs, blind chambers, and clustered towers.

Nothing here was assembled. Everything germinated.

The streets are only the gaps that the growths have left between themselves. Some are broad enough for several animals abreast; others contract to warm slits between ribbed walls. Floors are paved with overlapping scales shed from the columns. Underfoot they feel like thin ceramic tiles, matte on top and mirror-bright beneath. Now and then one curls at the edge as it ages, but there is little wind to move it and no footfall heavy enough to break it. Thousands lie loose in absolute disorder, yet the streets remain almost perfectly quiet.

Overhead, interwoven boughs make the city dense but not dark. Daylight filters through translucent fins and membranous fans, becoming amber, gray-green, and bruised violet. There are no conventional leaves. Instead, the crowns carry flat mineral blades, pleated heat sails, and banks of tiny black cups. Some gather sunlight. Others open to the warm air and bind carbon, sulfur, nitrogen, or traces of rarer gases. Their active surfaces are hidden inside microscopic dry chambers where oily solvents perform the chemistry that water might perform elsewhere.

The temperature is always warm, especially within the inner courts. Heat stored in the black mesa rises slowly through the living walls. At noon the outer towers click once or twice as they expand. After dusk they give the heat back, and the whole Crown seems to breathe without moving.

Its tallest structures stand near the center. They are perhaps twenty times the height of a person, enough to feel monumental within so confined a place. Their lower hollows open like portals. Inside, spiral ridges ascend around central shafts, not built as stairs but worn into step-like intervals by generations of climbing bodies. Balconies are shelf fungi in function, though made of hard, laminated resin. Bridges are fused branches. Grilles are lattices of reproductive spines. Domes are inflated husks stiffened by dust.

The resemblance to architecture strengthens in detail. Rows of niches repeat at regular heights. Courtyards are surrounded by tiered chambers. Certain passages align exactly with sunrise. Thin conduits run through the walls, distributing warm gases from fissures in the mesa. Yet these patterns arise from appetite, crowding, heat, and inherited response. The Crown is urban because every organism here has adapted to the presence of thousands of others. Solitude would kill most of them.

The fauna is abundant, though at first the city appears deserted.

Stillness conceals it.

Small six-legged grazers press flat against the walls, their backs indistinguishable from bark scales. Paper-bodied gliders hang beneath arches with their wings folded into sharp geometric packets. In the towers, colonies of thimble-sized animals occupy honeycombed chambers, feeding on mineral bloom and exhaling gases required by the columns. Long, jointed browsers move through the upper lattice so slowly that a branch can seem to change position over the course of an afternoon. Their mouths are delicate rasps. Their feet end in soft electrostatic pads that lift dust without scraping.

Nothing carries much liquid. Blood, where there is blood, is thick oil or mobile wax. Eggs are sealed ceramic beads. Excretion emerges as dry powder, filament, or a pellet of concentrated salts. Bodies conserve every volatile compound. Even death is tidy: scavengers dismantle a carcass from within its intact skin, leaving a light shell that is eventually incorporated into a wall.

Predators are present, but pursuit is uncommon. The city does not favor speed. A hunter may wait in a niche for weeks, adjusting its posture by fractions. Some resemble twisted railings; others hang like seedpods over well-traveled lanes. They strike once, almost without sound. The streets register only a brief dry tap and then return to silence.

Sound has difficulty establishing itself here. The porous walls absorb it. Dusty membranes quench echoes. The air is thin at this elevation, and most creatures communicate through touch, color, scent, or vibrations conducted through the shared structure. To stand with a palm against one of the columns would be to discover that the silent city is full of traffic: faint pulses, feeding tremors, the granular passage of internal carriers, warnings moving from tower to tower through fused roots.

In open air there is little to hear. A soft ticking at noon. A wing creasing shut. The faint hiss of dust settling into a collector cup. At long intervals, a seed capsule cracks somewhere in the canopy, a sound as restrained and distinct as a pebble dropped in an empty cathedral.

There is no weather in the familiar sense. Clouds may cross the sky, but they bring no rain. The atmosphere alters by slow tides of chemistry. During the long warm season it carries enough reactive gas for growth, and the edges of the city acquire pale new ribs. In the lean season the sails fold, pores seal, and animals descend into the thermal chambers beneath the mesa’s surface. Whole districts become dormant while others wake, so the city’s center of life migrates gradually from quarter to quarter.

Change is measured in coatings.

A wall begins black, then becomes bronze as symbiotic films colonize it, then green with salt crystals, then pale when grazers strip it clean. A bridge thickens over decades. A doorway narrows. A chamber seals around an animal colony and becomes a warm internal organ of the city. At the outer margin, seedlings establish themselves in cracks no wider than a hair, laying down delicate fences of fiber and glass. Given centuries, these will become new streets.

But the place is young. Its towers still carry sharp growth seams. Many courts terminate suddenly at the mesa rim, where the living fabric has not yet learned the boundary. Fresh spires lean outward over the drop, testing empty air. Below them, fragments fallen during early growth remain where they landed, unburied and nearly unchanged.

The world seems to obey several quiet rules.

Nothing wastes moisture, because there is none to waste. Nothing grows alone. Every useful surface becomes inhabited. Heat is food, dust is building material, breath is currency, and shade is a habitat. What looks constructed must have grown; what looks dead may merely be waiting. Movement is expensive, so life prefers patience, disguise, and exchange.

Near the western edge stands an unfinished district no larger than a room. Slender pillars crowd it, translucent and needle-sharp, connected by threads finer than hair. Tiny animals travel those threads in both directions, carrying nothing visible. At sunset, the pillars take on the color of embers. One by one, their tips bend toward neighboring tips. The movement is so slow it cannot be watched, only noticed.

In perhaps a year they will touch. In ten, they will fuse into a roof. Dust will collect above it. Creatures will settle beneath it. Passages will emerge from the pattern of what survives.

A new building will have appeared in the silent city, though no hand will ever have been raised.
