At great altitude, where a mountain range should sharpen into peaks, the land has instead worn itself broad.

It is an old upland, warm and dim: a continent-sized table of living matter raised above the low clouds. The stone beneath it has endured so long that nearly every angle has gone. Ridges are blunt-backed and valleys shallow, as though the whole massif has been handled for ages by rain. No cliffs remain except where roots hold open ancient wounds. Even there the exposed rock is soft-edged, black with moisture, and threaded by pale mineral veins that weep continuously.

From a distance the plateau appears forested. Within it, the distinction between forest and ground disappears.

The surface is a deep, interlocked body of plants. Trunks lie upon trunks, still alive, their lower sides producing roots and their upper sides producing forests of stems. Vast creeping organisms have crossed basins and climbed hills until their braided tissues became the terrain. What looks like soil is mostly compressed leaf, bark, pollen, and old wood in various states of return. A foot placed carelessly may sink through centuries: first into moss, then a lattice of hair-roots, then warm humus, then the slick outer skin of some buried vine as thick as a river.

Nothing grows alone. Ferns carry mosses; mosses carry translucent cups of algae; the cups hold rainwater in which minute flowering plants float. Great trees support gardens on every branch, and those gardens develop their own canopies. Some trunks are so broad and low that mist pools on their bark in shallow lakes. Reeds colonize these lakes. Small trees rise among the reeds. From beneath, their roots can be seen descending through clear brown water and fastening into the living bark below.

The upland receives no hard sunlight. Moist air climbs its sides from distant lowlands, cools beneath the canopy, and becomes cloud. The cloud seldom moves quickly. It settles among the vegetation in dim layers, whitening the hollows and dissolving the farther trees. Above it, taller crowns merge into a ceiling the color of deep green glass. Light filters down through leaf after leaf until it has lost all direction. Morning is a gradual paling; evening, a deepening of green into blue-black. At noon, wet surfaces sometimes shine without casting shadows.

Rain is frequent but rarely violent. It begins as condensation: beads gathering along the hairs of leaves, then joining, bowing the leaves, and falling. Each drop strikes many levels before reaching the depths. The canopy divides storms into millions of separate taps. Long after the sky has cleared, the interior continues raining upon itself.

Water lies everywhere without forming many open rivers. It inhabits the land rather than crossing it. It runs through hollow stems, under mats of roots, along the grooves of immense fallen trunks. It collects in saddle-shaped gardens and dark, leaf-rimmed basins. Where it emerges, it does so quietly, swelling from moss in broad transparent sheets. These creep over worn stone for a few paces and vanish beneath another layer of vegetation.

The largest bodies of water are hidden under the plant mass. Whole valleys have been roofed by roots, leaving warm lakes in perpetual night. The ceiling hangs low over their black surfaces, studded with root tips that drink the vapor. Blind fish move there, pale and soft-finned, feeding on seeds that sift down through fissures. When they disturb the water, the motion travels into the root roof as a faint trembling. Trees far above sometimes release pollen in response, though no one could say whether this is coincidence or sensation.

Despite the height, the air is warm. The living plateau holds heat as it holds water. Rot is steady but unhurried, balanced by growth so closely that fallen matter seems neither dead nor fully consumed. A leaf may remain recognizable for years beneath the canopy, slowly darkening while fungal threads embroider it in silver. Dead trunks do not collapse abruptly. They soften, bow, and are drawn into the load-bearing weave of the land.

This is an ancient equilibrium, but not a still one. The landscape changes by sagging.

Over centuries, a grove grows heavier with epiphytes and captured rain. Its supporting trunks bend lower. Pools deepen on their backs. Eventually a whole district settles a few body-lengths, too slowly for any generation of animals to witness. Water finds new paths. Former hilltops become marshes; old marshes rise as roots fill them. The plateau’s geography is a breathing too slow to hear.

Erosion has reached an unusual conclusion here. Stone is no longer chiefly worn by water or wind, but by the soft pressure of life. Roots enter every seam and remain there for millennia, thickening by fractions. Lichens draw minerals grain by grain. Acids drip from leaf reservoirs. The mountain is being digested without violence. In places the bedrock has become a pale clay retaining the exact folds and fractures of stone, as if the rock’s form survived its substance. Root systems pass through it easily.

What stone does persist has the look of relic bone. Rounded monoliths protrude from vegetation, heavily pitted, each hollow filled with a different miniature wetland. Some are almost entirely perforated. Wind passes through them with a low breath, but only when weather crosses the high open margins. Deeper inside the plateau there is little wind. Leaves hang heavy. Mist stands between stems. The quiet is immense but crowded.

Animals are abundant, though seldom conspicuous. Most are small enough to live within the layered vegetation without ever reaching either sky or stone. Warm-furred climbers travel the underside of branches, their hooked feet finding paths invisible from above. Broad, slow amphibians rest in the cisterns formed by leaves; algae patterns their backs until they resemble pieces of the pools. Flights of dark insects move through the fog like drifting ash and settle all at once when the light dims.

Birds rarely call. Many communicate through wingbeats, bill-taps, or the shaking of resonant seed cases. One long-legged species walks the upper root mats and drums softly through them. Replies come from far away, transmitted through living wood: a sequence of muted knocks passing under swamps and groves. Other creatures produce sounds beneath hearing, and the broad leaves quiver though the air is still.

The loudest common noise is water falling unexpectedly into water.

At long intervals, fruit drops from the high crowns. Some fruits are larger than animals, leathery and ribbed, and descend through the lower vegetation with a succession of heavy impacts. They come to rest in hollows, crack slowly, and fill with rain. Their flesh feeds dense communities for decades. Seedlings grow from within the shell, turning it into a temporary island. By the time the rind decays, the young trees have joined the surrounding weave.

There are scattered forms that resemble structures.

On a broad rise stand several rings of upright pillars, each one hollow and fluted. They might be ruined colonnades, but they bud after rain. Their tops open into fans of leaves; their bases merge below the surface into one immense root crown. Between them lie low walls of porous, reddish material, straight for short distances before curving around old trees. In cross-section the walls show growth rings. Whether they were cultivated, coaxed, or merely interpreted as shelter by something long vanished is impossible to know.

Elsewhere, small chambers occur inside the thickened joints of trees. Their entrances are oval and consistently placed above the wettest ground. Smooth ribs arch overhead. Floors are lined with dry fibers that repel water despite the surrounding damp. Some chambers contain arrangements of seeds, shells, polished stones, or shed antler. Nothing in them is worked. No edge has been cut, no surface fired, no piece fastened to another. Yet the arrangements recur across enormous distances: seven pale objects around one dark object, spirals opening toward downhill water, paired branches laid beneath the entrance.

Animals use these places but do not seem to have made them. Or perhaps the trees make them for the animals, guided over generations by nesting, dung, pressure, and habit. Here architecture may be an agreement reached so slowly that no participant knows it is building.

The plateau has its own boundaries and, beyond them, its own weather. Along the outer escarpments, the living mat overhangs the worn mountain in curtains of root and vine. Water pours through these curtains in thousands of fine streams, dissolving into mist before reaching the lowlands. The edge is brighter, louder, and less stable. Great pieces occasionally tear free—not landslides of earth, but tangled hanging ecosystems carrying ponds, trees, nests, and sleeping animals. They descend intact through cloud, shedding water and leaves, and may lodge far below to become new forests.

Far inward, distances become difficult to measure. Repeating layers of green, fog, and rounded landforms obscure scale. A nearby trunk can be mistaken for a tower several valleys away; a pale ridge may reveal itself as the root of one tree. Travelers following apparent horizons find them receding, not because the ground is flat, but because every surface supports another surface. There are paths, though none stay open. Herds press shallow routes through the moss, and vines roof them over. Streams offer direction until they descend into the underland. Even the slope can mislead: the upper vegetation grows toward available light, while the buried terrain inclines another way.

Some regions are almost entirely floral. There, what appears to be fog is suspended pollen, faintly luminous in the green dusk. Vast flowers open on cycles longer than seasons. Their petals unfold over several years, forming warm chambers where insects breed and small grazers shelter. Nectar gathers in trenches along the petal bases and ferments weakly. For a time these regions become busier, filled with muffled wings and the rustle of bodies. Then the flowers close just as slowly, and the animals depart before the chambers seal.

Other regions have passed beyond trees into something quieter: low, ancient heaths spread across eroded domes, dense as felt. The shrubs are centuries old but no taller than a hand. Between them, warm rainwater lies in black reflective pools. Here the sky can occasionally be seen—an uncolored brightness behind cloud—and the openness feels more alien than the enclosed forests.

At night, there is almost no change in temperature. Darkness simply completes what the canopy began. Fungi become visible along the oldest wood, glowing in faint lines that trace the buried anatomy of the plateau. Pools reflect points of cold blue from aquatic larvae. Flowers signal with slow pulses. From a height, if any height rose above the crowns, the land might resemble a submerged constellation: sparse lights appearing and vanishing across an immeasurable black-green body.

The governing rule of this place is not competition alone, nor decay, nor unchecked abundance. It is occupation. Every durable surface becomes habitat; every habitat becomes substrate; every living thing is eventually lived upon. Nothing remains merely itself. A branch becomes a field, a hide becomes a garden, a pool becomes a cradle, a ruin becomes an organ of the forest. Space is not empty long enough to be called empty.

And yet the plateau does not feel frantic. Its abundance has aged past urgency. Growth continues everywhere, but slowly, under the weight of everything already grown. Water moves by seepage. Species answer one another across generations. Mountains become clay. Forests lower themselves into marshes. The scattered chambers and rings are swallowed without being erased, retained as slight habits in the living mass.

In the deepest quiet, rain gathers on a million leaves. For a long while, nothing falls. Then one drop becomes heavy enough, slips from its tip, and begins its long descent through the many-layered world.
