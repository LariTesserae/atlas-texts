The city occupies a broad, shallow basin where warm groundwater lies just beneath the ground. Nothing grows here. No grass splits the paving, no moss darkens the wells, no tree gives shade. The basin is too new for soil: its floor is a pale mineral crust laid over soft clay and beds of warm, saturated sand. When the crust is broken, clear water gathers immediately in the wound.

The city is built directly upon this unfinished ground. It spreads low and wide, a dense congregation of mud-brick courts, fired-clay vaults, limestone walls, reedless thatch made from split hides, and streets paved with flat slabs. Buildings rarely rise beyond two levels. The wet foundation will not bear towers, and the people possess no engines or elaborate joinery with which to force height from it. Instead, the settlement grows by adjacency. One room leans against another; one courtyard opens into three; alleys narrow until shoulders brush both walls. Beyond each district lies another district, and beyond that a belt of kilns, corrals, cisterns, quarries, and low causeways fading into vapor.

Nearly every surface is warm to the touch.

Water appears everywhere but seldom as a large body. It beads on ceilings. It weeps through masonry joints. It stands ankle-deep in sunken lanes and fills stone troughs from below. Shallow channels, cut with hand picks and polished by passing feet, carry it between neighborhoods. The channels are arranged in straight runs and right-angled branches, more like streets than streams. Their flow is so slight that floating grit may remain beside the same doorstep for years.

After rain—though rain is frequent and fine rather than violent—the whole city darkens. Fired bricks turn red-brown, limestone almost gold, and the clay lanes shine black. Water hangs in the dim air as condensation, making distant structures pale and uncertain. Yet the outlines of puddles, rivulets, and roof-drips scarcely alter. Gutters have formed no grooves. Corners remain sharp. The city seems recently completed, though its inhabitants speak of it as immeasurably old.

This is because almost nothing changes here.

The warm basin admits no seasons. Rain begins at the same hour each day and ends after the same interval. Springs produce the same quantity. Puddles neither spread nor contract. The clay does not slump; the soft stone does not weather; footsteps leave prints in exposed mud, but the prints are gone by the following morning, the surface restored without any visible agent. New walls can be raised, yet once finished they become as resistant to alteration as the ground. A chisel strikes sparks from yesterday’s soft limestone. A doorway cannot be widened after its lintel is set. Broken pottery remains broken, but the shards never abrade.

Construction is therefore conducted with great caution. A city block is modeled first in wet clay. Foundations are measured with knotted cords and water levels. Walls are corrected while the mortar is fresh, because the instant the final capstone is placed, the building enters the basin’s equilibrium and cannot easily be changed. The architecture is simple not from lack of skill, but from fear of irreversible mistakes.

The streets are crowded with animals.

Goats pack the higher pavements, their hooves striking an endless hard clatter. Broad-backed cattle stand half submerged in warm drainage courts. Dogs sleep in doorways and wake to bark at carts. Pigs root in mineral slurry that contains no roots and little edible matter, searching instead for dropped grain, grubs from refuse pits, and scraps from kitchens. Pigeons crowd every cornice in such numbers that the upper edges of buildings seem furred in gray. White waterbirds stalk the channels; ducks fill cistern squares; rats travel inside hollow walls. Bats occupy the vaults and pour out each dusk in black sheets.

There are insects, too, wherever people or animals provide food: flies making dark halos over the slaughter courts, mosquitoes lifting from the warm channels, beetles under hide awnings, pale termites in imported timber. The surrounding basin supports no visible vegetation, so nearly all this animal abundance depends on the city. Grain, dried fodder, fruit, oil, and timber arrive along the causeways from uplands beyond the vapor. Nothing green survives long after entering. Leaves yellow within hours. Cut flowers collapse before they can be carried across a market. Seeds remain edible but will not sprout, even in the wettest courtyard.

The city is thus an island of mouths in a land without plants.

Its loudness is overwhelming.

Hooves, wings, wheels, handbells, barking, braying, hammering, bargaining, and the slap of water against clay echo between close walls. Kiln crews beat wooden paddles against brick molds. Quarry workers strike wedges in disciplined rhythms. At the public channels, hundreds of jars knock against stone lips. Potters spin heavy wheels by foot; millers turn querns; drovers shout themselves hoarse. Pigeons clap into the air whenever a cart passes, while beneath them caged fowl maintain a constant rasping chorus.

The basin adds its own sound. Warm water rises through narrow cracks with a deep bubbling note. Air trapped below the mineral crust escapes through vents around the city, producing whistles, moans, and low hornlike blasts. These tones do not vary. Each vent holds its pitch exactly, day and night, until districts are known by chord: the Three Low Mouths, the Whistling Ward, the Quarter of the Seventh Tone. No one remembers silence. Even at the dimmest hour, when traffic dwindles, the springs and vents sustain a deafening foundation upon which wings, coughs, chains, and distant livestock continue to sound.

Speech is conducted close to the ear. Public decrees are displayed in clay impressions rather than cried aloud. Families use broad gestures across courtyards, and market traders bargain with fingers, counters, and marked boards. Children learn the basin’s fixed roar before language and sleep poorly in quiet places.

Light rarely becomes strong. The sky is a uniform lid of warm gray cloud, bright enough to cast weak shadows at noon but never blue. Moisture thickens the air; kiln smoke and cooking smoke remain trapped above the roofs in horizontal layers. Lamps burn in workshops throughout the day. Their flames shine on wet plaster and glazed tiles, multiplying amber points along the lanes. At night, the city does not become fully dark. Mineral deposits around the warm vents hold a faint pearl-colored gleam, and thousands of household lamps make the vapor luminous from within.

Beyond the outermost walls, the basin remains open but crowded with works. Causeways divide shallow flooded flats into rectangular fields that are not planted. Some are evaporation beds for salt; others settle clay by grade and color. Rows of brick kilns stand like blunt red beehives. Quarries are only waist-deep, because stone can be taken solely from fresh cuts. Once a quarry face has been exposed and squared, it hardens beyond the tools available, and workers must begin another trench. Thus the outskirts are patterned with countless shallow beginnings: first cuts, starter pits, half-defined canals, foundations laid out by pegs. The land seems perpetually at the first stage of becoming terrain.

There are no rounded boulders, no worn ravines, no dunes, no mature riverbanks. The basin’s edges are abrupt and provisional. Clay meets limestone in clean seams. Springs issue from hairline openings with no stain around them. Even where water has run since the oldest records, it has carved nothing. Sediment settles in crisp layers but never consolidates unless deliberately fired or capped into permanence.

This makes the city both fragile and indestructible. Its inhabitants can add to it, slowly, with hand tools, ropes, sledges, molds, levers, and animal labor. They cannot readily remove what has been accepted by the place. Abandoned houses remain sound and must be built around. Misaligned canals continue carrying their allotted trickle. A cart overturned in an alley generations ago cannot be dismantled because its wooden frame, once soaked and fixed into the street’s equilibrium, has become harder than stone. The alley now passes through the spokes.

Every district contains such accidents. They are not considered supernatural. They are simply civic facts.

The people build with an awareness that completion is a kind of law. They leave walls unfinished at one corner so they may later be altered. They keep a loose brick at the crown of every arch. City gates lack final thresholds, and bridges retain one rough parapet. Nothing important is permitted to become entirely finished. In a basin where finished things do not change, imperfection is the only room left for the future.

Still, viewed from the low ridge at its margin, the city appears complete. It fills the warm hollow from mist to mist: flat roofs, domes, courts, kilns, channels, animal yards, and crowded causeways layered so tightly that the underlying mineral world is visible only in narrow gleaming seams. Smoke lies over it. Birds turn above it. The fixed notes of the vents carry through the wet air, joined by an unceasing multitude of hooves, tools, wheels, wings, and voices.

The rain begins at its appointed moment.

Every creature reacts as it always has. A thousand pigeons rise. Goats press beneath the awnings. People cover baskets and uncover cisterns. The pale stone darkens in the same order, street by street. Water gathers in every established hollow and nowhere else.

Then the city continues exactly as before: crowded, unfinished, and enduring.
