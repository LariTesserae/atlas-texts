The city occupies the inner wall of an immense mountain hollow, so high that its lowest terraces float above banks of cloud. It has no streets in the ordinary sense. Districts cling to vertical limestone in ledges, shelves, and deep green galleries, each one looking across miles of empty air toward another quarter of the city on the opposite wall.

The basin is almost perfectly circular. Its far side is blue with distance, though every detail there—balconies, hanging gardens, pale arcades—seems simultaneously close enough to touch. Above, the rim cuts a hard, irregular ring through the sky. Below, cloud fills the abyss, white at noon and bruised violet beneath overhangs. No one can see the bottom. Waterfalls descend into it without dwindling.

Heat rests everywhere, even at this extreme altitude. The stone is warm underfoot. Moisture beads on walls and gathers in the seams between blocks; leaves sweat, columns weep, and shallow cisterns brim continuously though no rain is seen falling. The atmosphere feels recently washed and not yet dried. Breath comes damp. Fabric, bark, plaster, and hair would all cling to the skin, if there were skin here to feel it.

There are no animals.

No insects trouble the flowers. No birds cross the open interior. No frogs call from the green cisterns, and no fish disturb their surfaces. Even the soil lacks worms. The city’s vegetation is luxuriant but strangely incomplete: trumpet vines, broad-leaved figs, moss curtains, ferns tall as doorways, black reeds, orchids rooted in roof cracks, and pale trees with exposed, braided roots. Fruit hangs untouched and overripe without ever falling. Pollen never drifts. Seeds remain sealed in their pods. Nothing grazes, nests, hunts, rots, or swarms.

Yet the city is deafening.

Thousands of waterfalls issue from the mountain wall. Some pour from natural caves, others from carved mouths above plazas, and others simply appear in midair beneath arches. They strike stone channels, roofs, leaves, pools, and lower cascades with a continuous, crushing roar. The basin gathers the noise and returns it multiplied. Every surface speaks water: hiss from moss, drumming from canopies, thunder from shafts, metallic ringing where thin sheets strike the edges of empty bronze-colored basins that are not metal but stone.

The sound does not vary. Stand beside the greatest cataract or behind a sealed wall and it has the same force. Covering the ears would make no difference. The roar is not carried through air; it is a property of the place, as heat and height are properties of it. It fills chambers without entrances and resonates in objects too soft to resonate. Leaves tremble beneath it but never change position.

The city appears built, although no tool marks exist.

Its masonry is made from the mountain’s own pale rock, fitted without mortar into walls, towers, stairways, aqueducts, courts, and vaulted passages. Yet the distinction between construction and geology repeatedly fails. A colonnade begins with squared piers and ends as a row of stalactites. Steps broaden into natural strata. Houses have roots instead of foundations. A civic hall, open to the abyss, possesses a ceiling of living leaves whose veins continue into the ribs of the vault. What seems from afar to be a palace is, on approach, a cliff face with windows; seen from within, it is again a palace, extending room after room into rock that should be solid.

There is no evidence of inhabitants and no evidence that the city was abandoned. Nothing lies broken in a doorway. There are no beds, utensils, inscriptions, statues, graves, or hearths. Rooms are composed only of architecture: floors, apertures, niches, ramps, courtyards. They imply use but refuse to say what kind. A staircase may spiral for miles around an open shaft, its treads polished smooth by passage that never occurred. Bridges sag with the settled weight of centuries, though their vines have never advanced by a single tendril.

The oldest quarters occupy the wettest ledges. Here the rock has been weathered into soft hollows and scalloped channels. Erosion is visible everywhere but active nowhere. Pillars are rounded, faces erased from nameless reliefs, and gutters deeply incised by water; still, no grain loosens. The waterfalls cannot deepen their basins. The city has achieved a terminal condition in which everything bears the marks of immense age and nothing can become older.

Across the hollow, neighborhoods overlap incorrectly. A terrace can be seen both above and below itself. Aqueducts pass behind the mountain and return before entering it. Some towers cast shadows in directions unrelated to the sun; others are illuminated on every side. Looking down from a balcony reveals the underside of that same balcony, green with hanging moss, and a figureless doorway opening onto the place from which one is looking.

Distance obeys local custom. Two neighboring arches may require an afternoon to cross between, though afternoons do not progress. A district ten miles away can be entered by stepping through a shallow pool. The water there rises to the ankle but contains, beneath its surface, the inverted city plunging upward for thousands of feet. Touching the reflection meets warm stone.

The most open part of the city is the central absence. It is not merely empty air but an organizing void, around which architecture, water, and vegetation have arranged themselves. Bridges extend into it and end cleanly without collapse. Hanging plazas, unsupported, occupy the cloud layer. Groves grow on their undersides as well as their tops. Great stairways descend alone, switchbacking through the sky until whiteness conceals them. Now and then—or always, since there is no discernible sequence—a waterfall rises out of the cloud and joins an upper canal.

The light is ordinary in strength and impossible in origin. It resembles a hot, late morning: white on exposed stone, green under foliage, honey-colored in shallow water. The sun is visible as a pale disk above the rim. It never moves. Shadows remain exactly where they are, and plants show no preference for them. Deep inside the mountain, courtyards receive the same daylight. The cloud below glows as though another sky lay beneath it.

Every pool is full to its lip. Every leaf holds the same trembling drop. Every cascade occupies an unbroken shape, water racing visibly while the whole remains unchanged. The contradiction becomes less apparent with time, because time here is only another architectural suggestion—an empty corridor, built for traffic that does not exist.

The vegetation neither grows nor dies. Flowers are caught at full opening, but not frozen: their petals are warm and supple, their scents dense in the wet air. Moss can be pressed and springs back. Cut nothing, for nothing can be cut. Bend a branch and it yields, then occupies both its bent and unbent positions until released. Roots cross thresholds with the deliberate arrangement of ceremonial ropes. Some trees emerge trunk-first from pools while their crowns spread beneath the water; others carry small roofed courtyards within cavities in their bark.

There are rules, though they are learned only by contradiction.

Water always descends, except toward the center.

Architecture is continuous, except at doors.

No living thing moves, though all living things are alive.

Sound is everywhere equally loud, but silence exists in the shadow cast by any object that has no visible source.

Height cannot be reduced. A descent of ten thousand steps ends at a terrace higher than the one where it began.

Nothing new enters the basin. Nothing leaves. Water pours endlessly, fruit remains ripe, cloud stays full, and the same humid heat presses against the same pale stone.

At the highest accessible ledge stands a broad, roofless square. Its paving blocks are separated by thin channels of clear water. Ferns rise between arcades, and beyond them the entire circular city reveals itself: miles of terraces nested in cliffs, curtains of white water, gardens suspended in brightness, towers recurring at several scales, and the unmoving cloud ocean below.

At the square’s center is a dry fountain.

It is the only dry thing in the city. Its bowl is weathered as if by ages of flowing water, and the stone around it is deeply stained by splashing that has never happened. Within the basin lies a perfect miniature of the mountain hollow, including the city, the cloud, the gardens, and the dry fountain at its center. Leaning closer reveals another miniature within that one, and then another, each equally hot, wet, green, empty, and loud.

The roar issues from there.

Or the city issues from the roar.
