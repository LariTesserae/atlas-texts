The town lies in a shallow basin below the surrounding ground, though “lies” suggests more horizontality than it possesses. It is a compact, upright place: retaining walls, narrow courtyards, sunken lanes, cistern towers, workshops stacked against one another, all crowded into a hollow no wider than a few city blocks. From the rim, little can be seen except the crowns of trees and the uppermost chimneys. The streets and roofs are beneath them.

Water brought the town here. The basin receives runoff from the warm uplands, and rain travels slowly through porous stone before emerging along the inner walls. It beads on masonry, threads through root mats, gathers in gutters, and drops from one terrace to the next. There is no true river, only an abundance of seepage organized into channels. Every surface is damp. Mortar is dark around its edges; brick sweats; iron rails leave long orange stains. After rain, the lower alleys take on a shallow, continuous flow.

The place is old enough that drainage and growth have reached an accommodation. Roots do not merely invade the construction. They complete it.

Great figs and swamp laurels stand in the courtyards, their trunks filling spaces originally intended for houses. Buttress roots cross alleys as high-backed ridges, polished by generations of feet and cart runners. Some buildings have walls on only two sides, the others supplied by interwoven stems, packed fern rhizomes, or the dense aerial roots hanging from an overhead branch. Living arches span lanes. Roofs are made from trained boughs covered with tile, bark, moss, and a black layer of accumulated soil. In places the vegetation has grown so continuously through the masonry that it is difficult to tell whether a wall was constructed and then colonized, or cultivated from the beginning.

There is almost no bare ground. The paving stones carry a green nap. Drain channels are fringed with liverwort and small water plants. Vines occupy cables, pipes, balconies, derricks, and disused cranes. Where leaves cannot spread horizontally, they layer vertically: broad leaves below, narrower leaves above, creepers across both, then moss over the creepers. Even the water tanks have small trees rooted in their sediment. The whole town appears less overgrown than fully allotted, every available surface claimed by some form of plant life.

No animals live here. There are no birds in the canopy, no rats in the mills, no insects above the standing water. Fruit swells, ripens, and drops untouched. Flowers open without visitors. Pollination belongs to wind, water, and human hands; many cultivated plants are propagated by cuttings, grafting, or division. The absence is most apparent not as silence—the town is far from silent—but as a lack of interruption. Nothing darts through peripheral vision. Nothing scratches behind a wall. Puddles remain smooth unless struck by rain or machinery.

The machinery supplies the town’s constant roar.

At the basin’s lower end, pumps work day and night in brick chambers sunk below the streets. Their flywheels turn slowly, transmitting a deep, repetitive concussion through foundations and root systems. Belt-driven mills press oil from seeds, pulp fibrous stems, saw wet timber, and grind bark for dyes and medicine. Turbines turn beneath iron grilles where collected water falls into old quarry shafts. Ventilation fans labor in the workshops, their bearings whining above the heavier pulse of the pumps.

Because the basin traps sound, no machine is ever heard alone. Each becomes part of a close, layered industrial weather: the thud of pistons, the slap of belts, water hammer in pipes, the rumble of carts over root-broken paving, the continual splash from elevated channels. Rain adds a higher hiss over everything. Speech is conducted at short distance and in raised voices. Workshops use bells, lamps, and vibrating cables to signal between floors.

Yet nothing moves quickly. The engines are large, old, and deliberately underdriven. Their wheels make patient revolutions. Hoists lift bundles of timber or wet fiber at walking pace. Water advances through the town by increments—cistern to header tank, header tank to flume, flume to settling bed, settling bed to underground reservoir. Gates open on schedules measured in hours. Moss grows over pipe brackets faster than they are replaced, but still slowly enough that workers mark its progress from season to season.

The light is green, brown, and indirect. The high canopy closes above most of the basin, and buildings narrow what remains of the sky. Daylight descends through layers of leaves, steam, and suspended droplets. Workshops glow through broad, misted panes. Low electric lamps burn behind wire guards along the main routes, their light reflected from wet surfaces and multiplied in shallow channels. Even at midday the lower lanes retain an evening quality.

Heat settles with the moisture. Air in the streets is warm and heavy, smelling of sap, soaked wood, clay, fermenting leaves, machine oil, and iron. Condensation forms on the cooler pipes that descend from upland springs. Where these lines enter pumping houses, they are furred with moss and constantly dripping. Workers wrap handrails in woven bark because bare metal stays slick.

The town’s architecture follows the basin in tight concentric terraces. The upper ring contains reservoirs, nurseries, and intake works. Below them stand houses and small factories, often sharing walls and root foundations. The lowest district is devoted to pumping, settling, and storage: vaulted cisterns, sluice halls, reed beds, and sunken tanks crossed by narrow walkways. Nothing is monumental in isolation. The impression of a city comes from accumulation—dozens of chambers, bridges, stairs, galleries, ducts, and planted courts compressed into a small enclosure.

Erosion has rounded the town without destroying it. Stair treads bow at their centers. Brick corners have softened. Channels have cut smooth runnels into stone sills. Some lanes have sunk gradually as water carried fine soil downward, and their doorways are now reached by little bridges. Walls lean into roots that have thickened to receive them. Repairs are visible everywhere, but few are recent in appearance; damp and growth quickly draw new work into the same dark, settled complexion as the old.

There is an established rule here: water may be delayed, lifted, divided, filtered, heated, or used for power, but it must never be permanently stopped. Sealed foundations crack. Closed drains erupt through floors. Abandoned rooms become cisterns and then gardens. Builders leave weep holes in every retaining wall and plant thirsty trees beside vulnerable structures. Pipes are fitted with overflow mouths shaped like simple stone spouts. The town survives not by keeping water out but by giving it innumerable acceptable paths.

Vegetation is governed similarly. It is cut, coppiced, woven, grafted, and loaded with the weight of buildings, but rarely cleared. A wall removed from the living network must be replaced by another support at once. Roots serve as footings; trunks carry platforms; reed bundles filter factory water; vines bind scaffolds; leaves cool roofs and condense moisture. Industry consumes plants and is itself housed by them. Waste pulp becomes board, fuel, soil, insulation, or packing for pipe joints. Ash is returned to the growing terraces.

From above, the basin looks almost uninhabited—a dense, rain-dark grove with several smoking vents. Only after descending the rim stair does the settlement reveal its depth. Railings appear inside roots. Windows shine under leaves. A freight platform moves slowly behind a curtain of vines. Water runs beside every step, and the ground begins to vibrate with hidden engines.

At the bottom, beneath the broadest canopy and the oldest masonry, the sound is strongest. The pumps beat through the warm dimness. Water shines black in the channels. Plants press from walls, roofs, cracks, tanks, and balconies until the lanes seem tunneled through living matter. Nothing flies, swims, or scurries. Nothing is vacant. The town breathes only through leaves, steam, and the slow mechanical lungs below.
