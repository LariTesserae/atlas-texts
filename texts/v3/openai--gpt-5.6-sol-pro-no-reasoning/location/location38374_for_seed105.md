The drowned heights begin far below the surface.

There is an ocean overhead—perhaps a true ocean, perhaps the final state of an atmosphere that grew too heavy to remain air. Its upper reaches are lost beyond miles of black, cold water. Nothing like daylight penetrates this deep. The only illumination comes from the land itself: dim mineral seams, bruised blue glows in the vegetation, and infrequent sheets of pale radiance passing through the water as though enormous storms were flashing on the other side of the world.

The terrain is mountainous. Long ridges rise from unseen trenches, their slopes carved into shelves, gullies, and blunt escarpments. These are not young peaks abruptly submerged. Water has lived here long enough to soften every exposed face. Cliffs have slumped into ramps of broken slate. Channels have found stable courses through the high valleys. Stone towers remain where the surrounding rock has dissolved, but even these are rounded and furred with growth. The whole region has the settled appearance of an old range under an old sea: altered beyond catastrophe and now balanced within its strangeness.

Pressure gives the water substance. It does not feel like empty space between things but like a dark material laid over the country, dense enough to lean against walls and drag steadily through passes. The cold is profound without being perfectly still. Currents spill over saddles like invisible rivers. They descend the slopes in slow, crushing cascades, lifting curtains of black silt before letting them fall again. Where opposing flows meet, the water twists into standing columns. Stones tremble in their sockets. Long strands of vegetation incline, recover, and incline again.

There are no animals.

No fish pass through the dark. No shell closes beneath a footfall. No worm turns the sediment, and no distant call answers the noise of the currents. The absence is not immediately obvious because the place is active: leaves writhe, spores stream upward, gravel rattles, and entire meadows bow under pressure fronts. Yet after a while the distinction becomes oppressive. Every movement is water, plant, stone, or mechanism. Nothing startles. Nothing flees.

Vegetation flourishes by unfamiliar means.

Across the gentler slopes grow sparse forests of glassy stalks, each separated from the next by broad tracts of bare sediment. They resemble trees only at a distance. Their trunks are bundles of translucent tubes rooted in cracks, and their crowns divide into hundreds of flat, dark vanes. The vanes turn toward mineral warmth rather than light. Some forests face downward, hanging from overhangs with their roots sunk into the ceilings of drowned caverns. Others form parallel rows along fault lines, feeding on what rises through the stone.

Between them lie fields of ribbon growth: maroon, indigo, and nearly black. The ribbons may reach the length of a house but are only as wide as a hand. They stream over ancient terraces and knot around standing stones. When a cold current passes, they stiffen all at once, converting the force of the water into a faint internal gleam. Whole hillsides pulse in delayed sequence—first blue, then gray-green, then darkness—so that the landscape appears briefly to breathe.

The plants do not rot. With no animals and no visible decomposers, dead growth pales, mineralizes, and becomes part of the terrain. Fallen stalks collect in ravines and slowly turn to porous stone. Layer upon layer has raised low dams across the valleys. Behind them, fine silt settles into level plains. New growth roots in the petrified remains of the old, making the forests both organism and geology.

Here and there, structures stand on the high shelves.

They are small, widely spaced, and built with an economy suited to hands, rope, wedges, and stone blades. Low huts of fitted slabs crouch beneath ledges. Towers no taller than three people occupy narrow spurs. Along one ridge, a line of cairns disappears into the dark, each bound with braided vegetable fiber that should have frayed long ago. The masonry contains no mortar. Blocks are locked together by weight and careful shaping, with broad gaps through which the current may pass.

These places were made under water. Their entrances open from above rather than from the side. Roof stones carry handholds. Interior floors are weighted with flat slabs, and the walls are covered in shallow grooves used to secure tools against drifting away. There are bowls chipped from basalt, bone-shaped implements made not of bone but compressed woodstone, and knives flaked from smoky volcanic glass. Copper appears rarely: green rivets, a warped hook, a strip hammered thin enough to wrap around a broken haft.

No remains identify the builders. There are no skeletons, no carved bodies, no representations of eyes, mouths, paws, or wings. Their marks consist of currents and topography: branching lines, nested arcs, repeated notches like records of pressure or depth. On the tallest tower, a map has been pecked into every face. It shows the surrounding valleys with remarkable accuracy, but also depicts channels running vertically into the water above.

Those channels are real.

At intervals, the sea opens.

Not into air, but into a different condition of water: narrow shafts in which buoyancy reverses. Silt falls upward through them. Loose stones lift from the ground and vanish into darkness. The shafts wander slowly across the region, bending forests flat as they pass and plucking mineralized trunks from their roots. Their arrival is announced by a deep, mounting roar. Cavities in the mountains begin to resonate; terraces hum; stone tools jump against the walls of abandoned huts. Then the upward torrent crosses the ridge, immense and unseen, and everything not secured rises.

This is why the old structures are low. This is why their roofs are heavier than their foundations. This is why tethering grooves cover every interior surface.

The noise rarely ceases. Water thunders through distant ravines. Loose scree clacks down slopes, though “down” is not always reliable. Stalk forests knock together with hollow, wooden reports. Deep within the mountains, thermal fractures boom like doors closing in a buried hall. Some sounds travel for hours through the dense medium, distorted until a small collapse may seem continental and a great upheaval no more than a murmur at one’s shoulder.

In sheltered basins the water becomes almost still. There the darkness feels larger. Fine sediment hangs motionless, and the luminous plants extinguish themselves one by one. A traveler could cross an entire plain seeing only the weak green outline of a distant ridge and the occasional geometric silhouette of a roof. Yet even these basins are not dead. Gas wells through circular pits. Salt needles grow from the mud, fracture, and regrow. Pale fungal-looking mats creep by extending mineral threads a finger’s width each year. They are plants only by courtesy; they consume gradients, fastening one edge to cold stone and the other to warmer water.

Farther upslope, the range culminates in a plateau split by a canyon. The plateau is the highest known ground, although the ocean continues immeasurably above it. Its rim supports the densest growth in the region: groves of branched white columns whose tips produce dark sacs. When pressure falls, the sacs rupture together. Clouds of silver dust erupt into the current, shining briefly with stored chemical light. The clouds do not carry seeds in the ordinary sense. They abrade exposed stone, preparing it. Years later, black films appear on those scoured surfaces; decades after that, the first translucent stalks rise.

At the canyon’s bottom runs a horizontal cataract. Water enters through the eastern wall and vanishes into the western one without visibly filling the space between. Anything crossing it is seized and drawn sideways. Ancient ropes span the flow, braided thicker than a person’s torso and hardened to stone. Several have snapped, their ends streaming rigidly into the torrent. A narrow bridge of fitted slabs follows the canyon wall beside them. It leads to the largest construction in the drowned heights.

Even this is modest: a cluster of nine chambers around a central shaft.

The chambers contain racks of primitive tools, bundles of mineralized cord, sealed jars, and flat tablets incised with the same branching notation found on the ridge tower. Everything is arranged as if abandonment were temporary. Stone weights rest on lids. Copper chisels lie beneath retaining bars. In one room, hundreds of vanes cut from black plant tissue are stacked by size.

The central shaft descends into the mountain and emits a slow blue light.

At regular intervals, the shaft inhales.

Water rushes into it from every doorway. The building groans; cords strain; vegetation outside flattens toward the entrance. Then the flow stops, and for several moments there is perfect quiet across the canyon. Even the horizontal cataract falters. In that suspension, the old carvings on the walls brighten, though no pigment lies within them.

Then the mountain exhales.

A column of warmer water erupts from the shaft, carrying mineral grains and fragments of something transparent and angular. The discharge pours up through the roof opening and spreads across the plateau. Forest vanes swivel toward it. Ribbons uncoil from the terraces. Every plant within sight wakes into cold blue luminescence. The light travels outward along buried root systems until ridge after ridge emerges from darkness: peaks, ruined cairns, empty valleys, towers, terraces, and the skeletal outlines of distant forests.

For a minute, the entire drowned country is visible.

It is larger than expected. The inhabited ridge is only one among many. Other lines of small structures stand on remote heights. Roads—or channels, or boundaries—cross the slopes in vast deliberate curves. On the far side of the basin, something like a stairway ascends into open water, each step anchored to nothing visible, climbing until the glow fails.

Then the mineral warmth disperses. The groves dim. The blue map of the region contracts toward the central shaft and disappears.

Darkness returns, along with the roar of moving water.

The landscape continues its long labor: currents carving, plants building stone, shafts migrating, structures waiting under impossible pressure. Nothing hunts here, nothing grazes, nothing cries out. Yet the drowned heights are not empty. They are maintained by processes too slow to resemble intention and too regular to dismiss as chance.

Above them lies only more ocean.

Below them, the mountains breathe.
