The place is called the **High Mill**, though nothing here has moved within any surviving measure of time.

It occupies the crown of a narrow mountain whose original stone cannot be reached. From rim to rim, every surface has been covered, replaced, or hidden beneath construction. The Mill is not large: a person could cross it in an hour if there were a straight passage, and could stand at its center and know—by the pressure of enclosing walls—that the whole world of it was near. Yet there is no straight passage. Every handspan is occupied.

Walls lean against walls. Buttresses brace buttresses. Stairs ascend into blocked arches, then double back beneath themselves. Niches have been packed with tablets; gaps between tablets have been stopped with wedges; the wedges bear carvings too fine to read in the low light. Columns crowd chambers intended for fewer columns, their capitals interlocking overhead like knuckles. Bridges have been fitted under older bridges until the space between floor and ceiling is a layered mesh of ramps, braces, shelves, ducts, and ladders. Some passages are only shoulder-wide. Others narrow gradually until they become masonry seams.

There is no soil. Beneath the pavement is older pavement, and beneath that, brickwork, dry-set slabs, hammered ceramic, blocks of dark glass, and foundations descending farther than any shaft reveals. Even the mountain may be an assumption. Where the outer walls break against the sky, their courses continue downward beyond sight, suggesting that the summit itself is only the uppermost portion of a buried or suspended structure.

Everything was made with simple means.

Tool marks remain everywhere: adze-bites in vitrified beams, grooves from stone drills, dimples where copper punches once struck, thumbprints baked into clay. No component is large enough to have required a crane. The High Mill was assembled from pieces a pair of hands could lift, repeated in such numbers that repetition becomes geological. It has the immensity of labor rather than of machinery. Millions of small decisions are fitted together so tightly that no empty place survives between them.

The materials have aged past ruin into stability. Soft stone has long since powdered away; what remains is flint, fired clay, basalt tile, fused ash, mineral pitch, and a pale alloy that neither tarnishes nor gleams. Corners have been rounded by an erosion so old that it seems less like weathering than completion. Reliefs are worn smooth. Inscriptions have become shallow currents beneath the fingers. Steps sag gently in their centers, although no foot now climbs them.

There is almost no moisture. The air is warm and extraordinarily dry, with a taste of lime, old metal, and heated dust. Water would be an intrusion here, a destructive novelty. The channels along the floors did not carry liquid. Their insides are scorched and lined with dull amber crust, remnants of some resinous vapor or molten salt once guided through the structure. Small sealed jars stand in wall recesses, each stoppered with wax turned hard as stone. When cracked, they contain only a breath of sharp-smelling gas.

Nothing grows. No lichen films the masonry, no root pries at a joint, no pale insect shelters beneath a tile. Dust is present, but it is sterile dust: ceramic flour and powdered mortar that has lain in the same hollows for ages. There are no droppings, husks, webs, bones, pollen, spores, or skins. The High Mill has the cleanliness of a tomb before burial—or after all burial has been forgotten.

The light comes from openings too small to be called windows. They are needle-holes cut through many thicknesses of wall, aligned with a sky that remains a muted bronze. Thin, exhausted shafts enter at slants and vanish into the crowded interior. Elsewhere, porous blocks give off a weak red warmth, not quite luminous enough to illuminate themselves. The result is a perpetual dimness in which nearby edges are clear and everything beyond a few paces merges into one dense architecture.

Yet the place is deafening.

The sound does not rise or fall. It has no source that can be approached, no rhythm, no interruption. It is a continuous stone-deep roar, as though a vast cataract were falling on the other side of every wall—though there is no water anywhere—or as though the summit stood inside a wind too immense to produce any felt movement. The air is perfectly still. Loose dust does not tremble. Hanging plumb-bobs remain vertical. Frayed cords, preserved by dryness, do not stir.

Nevertheless, the roar fills teeth, ribs, and skull. It is not merely heard. Different materials divide it into layers: clay chambers hum, glass blocks keen, basalt growls, and the pale metal emits a pressure like a sustained struck bell. In narrow passages these tones crowd together until thought becomes difficult. In larger rooms they separate into immense, unmoving chords.

No echo follows a shout. The fixed sound consumes it immediately.

At the center of the Mill is a chamber scarcely larger than a shrine. Reaching it requires turning sideways between load-bearing screens and crawling beneath a ceiling of fitted tablets. The room contains the densest construction of all: thousands of palm-sized wheels, vanes, toothed disks, clay diaphragms, pins, reeds, and resonating bowls arranged around an empty cubic space. None of them turns. Many could never have turned; their teeth are fused together, and their axles were built directly into solid sockets. The apparatus resembles a machine made by people who understood tools but conceived machinery as a form of masonry.

The roar is loudest here.

At the exact center, the empty cube is bounded by six slabs that do not touch. Between them are hair-thin gaps, uniformly dark. Put a hand near one and warmth presses against the skin, though no current passes. The noise seems to issue from the emptiness, but so does the peculiar impression of silence beneath it: a silence deeper than the roar, as if the sound were a solid shell built around an absence.

Old carved scenes appear throughout the outer galleries. Their figures are not animals, and perhaps not living things. They show upright bundles of tools—hands without bodies, faces assembled from wedges, cords wearing masks—adding one block after another to the High Mill. Later scenes depict fewer figures and more walls. In the final panels there are no builders, only completed surfaces packed edge to edge.

The last surviving inscription, repeated on lintels near the summit rim, can be read despite its age:

**WHEN EVERY PLACE IS FILLED, NOTHING CAN HAPPEN.**

That appears to be the governing principle of the High Mill. It has achieved a state beyond maintenance and beyond decay. No stone falls because there is nowhere for it to fall. No wall leans further because another wall already receives it. Heat does not flow; each chamber is equally warm. Air does not circulate; every pressure is balanced. The structure supports itself from all directions at once.

Even erosion has stopped. What could be removed has been removed. What could settle has settled. The remaining dust occupies hollows shaped exactly to contain it. The High Mill is not preserved against time. It has used time up.

Beyond the parapets lies a sheer dimness. Whether it is sky, distance, or an unbuilt remainder cannot be determined. Looking outward produces vertigo, but no wind. The bronze light never reddens or pales. There are no stars and no moving shadows by which to reckon an hour.

Inside, all passages eventually return to the central chamber, though their slopes and turns insist otherwise. One may climb continually and arrive below the place one began. One may descend and emerge at the highest rim. Chalk marks remain where they are placed, but maps become crowded with contradictions until their lines resemble the construction itself.

The Mill does not rearrange. It has never rearranged.

Its geometry was simply finished before direction became necessary.

And through every fitted stone, every stopped vent, every sealed jar and hand-cut tooth, the same impossible, changeless thunder continues—not warning, not labor, not collapse, but the permanent sound of a completed world.
