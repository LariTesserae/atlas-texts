Beneath a plain of pale stone lies a warm chamber no larger than a village square.

There is no entrance in the ordinary sense. Hairline faults descend toward it, but none are wide enough for a body, and the chamber’s builders—if builders they were—left no stair, shaft, or door. It is embedded whole in the crust: an enclosed pocket of heat, dimness, and dry life. Above it, the world may have seasons. Here there are none.

The chamber is almost circular. Its floor slopes inward by scarcely a hand’s breadth, toward a shallow basin filled not with water but with fine black beads. The beads are smooth, warm, and lighter than sand. When disturbed they roll from one another, exposing an underlayer of white salt and needle-sharp crystals. Yet nothing disturbs them now. Their arrangement appears random until one watches long enough to understand that they occupy precisely the same positions at every glance.

The surrounding terrain is young. Black mineral ridges thrust from the floor with unworn edges, glossy as fresh fractures. Fans of amber glass lie beneath vents in the walls, still shaped like arrested spills. Thin shelves of sulfur-colored stone project over hollows, their margins delicate and serrated. They look as though they hardened yesterday, though there is no dustfall, dripping, or abrasion to age them.

Everything bears the freshness of a place newly made.

Everything has borne it forever.

Heat enters without moving. The floor is warm enough to sting bare feet, while the air remains thick but breathable, scented with resin, hot clay, animal musk, and a metallic bitterness. Moisture is absent. No condensation gathers on the ceiling. No sap runs. No blood spills. Whatever chemistry sustains the chamber has no use for liquid.

Its vegetation rises sparsely but luxuriantly, each plant occupying a generous territory of bare mineral. At first the growth resembles a garden planted too far apart: a crown here, a column there, a low spiral of leaves isolated on fifty paces of dark floor. Up close, every specimen is extravagant.

The largest are bell-trees. Their trunks are hollow cones of woven fiber, tawny outside and violet within. From each rim hangs a downward canopy of broad blades, plush and folded, their surfaces crowded with silver pores. The blades do not droop from thirst. They draw nourishment from warm gases released through microscopic cracks, fixing sulfur, carbon, and traces of iron into tough aromatic flesh. Instead of sap, their inner channels carry chains of dry powder, passed grain by grain through contracting cells.

Nothing in them flows.

Their flowers are rigid cups of translucent chitin. Stamens touch pistils and remain touching. Pollen hangs between them in golden bridges, never falling. Seed cases stand open, displaying rows of black kernels which are both ripe and not yet released.

Between the bell-trees grow smaller forms: fans of green crystal threaded with living membranes; red cushions soft as fur but rooted through stone; antlered shrubs whose branches are jointed shell; pale ribbons that climb nothing, holding themselves upright in loose, unsupported coils. Their colors are deep but difficult to see in the chamber’s dim red light. Much of the foliage appears black until one comes close enough to discover emerald, indigo, rust, and waxy blue.

The light comes from narrow mineral seams in the walls. They glow like banked embers, without flickering or cooling. Every seam gives exactly the same illumination at every moment. Shadows are therefore permanent objects. They stripe the floor, divide leaves, and fill the undersides of ledges with unchanging dark. Some animals have lived their entire existence within a particular shadow and have no organs for light.

There are animals everywhere.

They do not make the chamber crowded. Like the plants, they are scattered—one hanging under a shelf, three among the black beads, a dozen occupying the distant wall—but nearly every available way of living has found an inhabitant.

Six-legged grazers stand beside the red cushions, their mouthparts pressed into the same shallow bites. Their skins are ceramic plates separated by rings of white down. Long, hollow creatures bridge cracks, feeding on mineral vapor with comb-shaped mouths. Mothlike things cling to the bell-trees with wings spread: blue-gray, heavy-bodied, and as large as hands. Flights of tiny glass-backed insects are suspended above the basin. They form arcs and spirals, each body separated from the next by an exact interval.

The fauna is abundant, but none of it travels.

This does not mean the animals are dead. Beneath translucent shells, organs labor. Muscles hold tension. Compound eyes register the chamber. Heat is consumed and transformed; gases are breathed; dry nutrients pass through elaborate guts as chains of granules. A predator near the eastern wall has its jaws closed around the flank of a grazer. Both continue to metabolize. The prey is always being bitten. The hunter is always feeding. Neither wound nor meal advances.

Time here permits condition but denies transition.

The chamber’s tremendous noise comes from this rule.

Every wing that would beat produces the sound of beating without changing position. Every throat that would call holds one note. Every foot that would strike stone creates an impact that never decays. The air is packed with the sustained consequences of interrupted actions: chirrs, bellows, scraping shells, seedpods cracking, claws tapping, jaws grinding. Thousands of small sounds layer into a continuous roar.

Closest to the bell-trees, the din is almost architectural. Their taut canopies resonate with the chamber’s heat and issue low brass tones. The moths add a steady rasp. Buried creatures pulse against the floor, creating deep knocks that are heard through the bones more than the ears. From a niche high in the western wall comes a needle-thin shriek, unwavering and intolerably pure.

Nothing begins. Nothing ends. The noise simply is.

Scattered among the life are signs of hands.

A low wall of fitted stones encloses half of one bell-tree, though there is no reason for the wall and no matching half. Beside the black basin stands a three-legged frame made of bone poles lashed with dry tendon. Stone blades rest on a mat of plaited leaf fiber. Their edges are exceptionally sharp and show little wear. Small bowls have been pressed from clay and fired in the chamber’s hot vents; most are empty, while one contains an orderly pile of red seeds.

At the northern edge is a hut.

It is scarcely taller than a crouching person, constructed from rib bones, mineral slabs, and overlapping plates cut from some enormous insect. Its doorway faces the permanent glow. Inside are two sleeping hollows lined with fine plant down, a hand drill, a coil of cord, and a wall marked by charcoal strokes. The strokes may be a tally. There are seventeen vertical lines, a gap, and then a spiral drawn around a dark thumbprint.

No people are visible.

There is, however, a figure behind the hut: small, broad-shouldered, wrapped in a cape of stitched wings. One hand holds a chipped stone hammer against a crystal outcrop. The other raises a second stone to strike it. The posture is balanced and purposeful. Fine muscles stand out along the forearms. The figure breathes, but neither stone descends.

Perhaps the chamber caught its inhabitants in a single instant.

Perhaps there was never any other instant to catch.

The strangest evidence lies in the hut’s far corner. There, under a lattice of sticks, sits a smooth oval object of green stone. It has been polished by labor, unlike the raw, youthful geology around it. A narrow groove circles its middle. Along that groove are carved tiny scenes: the hut unfinished; the hut complete; the hut collapsed; the hut buried under dust. The final image shows the chamber split open to a sky crowded with stars.

Such change is unknown here. Dust does not accumulate. Buildings do not collapse. Stone does not weather. The toolmaker’s lifted arm never falls.

And yet someone carved a history.

The chamber admits no gradients. Heat is equal from hour to hour because there are no hours. Populations neither rise nor diminish. The predator cannot finish its prey, and the seeds cannot leave their pods. There is no starvation, because hunger does not deepen; no abundance becomes excess, because nothing reproduces to completion. Life occupies an eternal metabolic brink, sustained by reactions that proceed inwardly while all visible consequence remains fixed.

Even thought may be held this way.

The figure behind the hut might contain one unending intention: strike.

The animals might experience a perpetual alarm, appetite, or mating call. The moths may forever feel the first contraction of flight. If the plants possess any dim awareness, they know only the instant before release—the pollen about to separate, the seed about to fall, the leaf about to turn toward light.

At the chamber’s center, the black beads wait in their shallow basin. Between them rise several tiny green shoots, each curled at the tip. Their roots penetrate the dry salt below, where they draw energy from a chemical boundary that never depletes. They are the youngest things in the chamber. Their tissues are soft, bright, and crowded with potential.

They have just emerged.

They will always have just emerged.
