The plateau stands nearly three thousand meters above the lowlands, yet it is hot enough that distance trembles. Its rim rises in long, weather-rounded escarpments: dark basalt softened by moss, fern, and a mineral glaze left by centuries of rain. From below it resembles a green continent lifted into the sky. From its summit, it is less a table than a broad, broken country of shallow basins, wooded ridges, open marshes, and black shelves of stone.

Water is everywhere without gathering into any single dominant river. Rain has just passed, though rain is rarely entirely finished here. It remains on leaf tips, lies silver in bromeliad cups, crawls down trunks, and perspires from the porous rock. Underfoot, the soil gives slightly. Thin films of water move through the grass in no obvious channel, joining tea-colored pools fringed by sedge. Along the escarpments, springs emerge from seams halfway down sheer walls and spread into veils rather than falls. Wind catches them and sends their droplets upward, returning a portion of the plateau’s drainage to the plateau as warm mist.

The heat has an enclosing quality. Clouds form close to the ground, born from the wet forest itself, and their shadows cross the uplands slowly. In sunlight, exposed rock becomes too hot to touch; ten paces away beneath the canopy, the air is green, humid, and cool enough to raise gooseflesh on wet skin. Condensation pearls on every shaded surface. Nothing dries completely. Even old leaves rot while still hanging.

Vegetation occupies almost every available layer. On the open flats grow shoulder-high grasses, waxy herbs, and stands of broad leaves that turn their pale undersides before a storm. Low trees gather on better-drained rises, their crowns braided with vines. Larger forest fills the gullies, where trunks descend through hanging roots into dark water. Orchids and ferns colonize branches; moss colonizes the orchids; algae tint the permanent droplets held in the moss. Fallen limbs are rapidly covered, but not rapidly erased. They remain as green, upholstered ridges, slowly returning to soil.

The plateau’s antiquity is visible in its forms. There are no raw cuts or young ravines. Water has worked for so long that the channels fit their flow closely, polished at the bends and furred with vegetation wherever the current weakens. Basalt outcrops have been reduced to rounded backs, fluted margins, and deep bowls where rainwater revolves. The cliffs are immense but settled. Their collapses occurred long ago, and the blocks below have become islands of forest. Erosion continues everywhere, yet it feels less like destruction than respiration: grains loosen, roots catch them, pools overflow, mineral-rich water lays down another thin skin.

Across the central wetlands stand peculiar groves of ironwood. Their bark contains so much metal that old trunks ring faintly when struck by falling branches. Lightning favors them. During storms, blue discharges pass between adjacent crowns, and buried root systems conduct the charge across whole marshes. The groves are natural dynamos: sap rises and falls, conductive tissues pulse, and static accumulates in flower heads as a low electrical pressure.

This is the landscape’s industry.

No structures interrupt the plateau. There are no roads, pylons, walls, blades, pipes, or discarded objects. Yet production occurs at a scale that feels mechanized. In the ironwood marshes, electrical gradients drive colonies of metallic bacteria through branching mineral veins. They concentrate dissolved ores, plate submerged roots, and extrude delicate coppery filaments into the mud. Thermal vents beneath the western basins force water through these living deposits. Valves made of cartilage-like fungal tissue open and close with changes in pressure. Chambers fill, compress, and discharge. Entire bogs throb in slow cycles.

On still afternoons, one may hear this hidden system working: a soft click under the ground, followed minutes later by a distant exhalation as a spring releases steam. Reeds tremble in orderly bands. Gas bubbles rise along straight mineral seams. In certain pools, mats of black algae rotate with almost deliberate regularity, harvesting sunlight by day and feeding chemical reactions below at night. Nothing has designed these processes, but they have been refined by deep time into something resembling an immense wet factory—distributed, self-repairing, and inseparable from the ecology.

The soundscape remains surprisingly spare. Water murmurs under vegetation more often than it splashes in the open. Insects are plentiful, but many communicate through vibration in stems and soil rather than through the air. The loudest creatures call only at intervals. A canopy bird gives one hollow note, waits long enough for silence to reclaim the forest, then answers itself from another ridge. Frogs sound from flooded hollows at dusk, each population beginning and ending together as atmospheric pressure changes. Far below, an unseen fall makes a continuous bass note that can be mistaken for blood moving in the ear.

Animals are abundant without being conspicuous. Small deerlike browsers move through the reed beds on wide, splayed feet. Bright lizards bask on basalt until clouds cover the sun, then vanish into cracks wet with condensation. Gliding mammals cross narrow ravines at dawn. In the pools live armored fish able to creep through saturated moss when waterways shrink. Their migrations draw long-legged hunters, and those in turn draw heavy, solitary cats whose paths can be recognized by the sudden silence of birds.

The escarpment has its own fauna. Swifts nest behind the water veils, climbing columns of warm air that rise from the lowlands. Pale crabs inhabit springs hundreds of meters above any continuous stream, feeding on larvae and mineral films. Along inaccessible ledges, colonies of heat-loving bees build dark combs directly against sun-warmed rock. Their honey tastes faintly of iron and smoke, though there has never been a fire there.

Weather keeps the plateau active. Mornings begin with mist lying in the basins and clear air on the ridges. By noon, heat draws vapor upward; towers of cloud assemble over the forest, casting blue-gray shadows across the marshes. Rain arrives locally and heavily. One valley may disappear behind silver water while the next remains in hard sunlight. In the evening, runoff reaches the cliffs, springs strengthen, frogs begin, flowers open, and clouds loosen into streamers that drift below the plateau rim.

At night, the industrial life beneath the wetlands becomes faintly visible. Mineral bacteria emit a dull amber glow where groundwater rises. The light traces buried channels, not in grids but in branching systems like nerves or watersheds. It brightens when pressure builds, dims after a vent releases, and sometimes travels across a basin in a slow wave. Lightning stored in the ironwood roots leaks upward through fungi, outlining rings of mushrooms in cold blue.

These phenomena are unusual but not theatrical. The place does not present them. They occur whether observed or not, absorbed into the steady exchange of heat, water, minerals, and living tissue.

There is one prevailing rule: nothing here operates alone. Cliffs make mist; mist feeds trees; trees gather charge; charge drives subterranean chemistry; mineral deposits redirect water; water opens habitat; animals carry spores between the new pools. Even apparent stillness is crowded with transfer. The plateau’s machines are forests, its furnaces are buried vents, its wiring is root and ore, and its waste becomes the raw material of another process.

From the highest ridge, the country extends to the curved horizon in alternating bands of green, black stone, pewter water, and white cloud. The lowlands are mostly hidden. This gives the plateau the impression not of being above a world, but of being the world: an elevated, rain-soaked province under a hot sun, ancient enough to have evolved its own industry and quiet enough that the turning of that industry can be heard.
