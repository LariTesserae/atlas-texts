The city lies across a young plain of black stone, low enough that the horizon seems only a few steps higher than the ground. Nothing has had time to soften here. Ridges keep the sharp lips with which they cooled; drainage cuts are narrow, bright-edged incisions; slabs lie fitted together along clean fractures. Even the soil appears recently invented: a thin rust-red powder gathered in seams, mixed with translucent husks and the green dust of spores.

From the air, the place might be mistaken for wilderness. Vegetation covers nearly everything. Broad-leaved vines quilt the basalt. Fern towers stand in dense emerald islands, their crowns open like hands around pockets of shadow. Long grass pours through the streets, silver on one side and nearly black on the other. Yet the lushness is deceptive. Plants cluster in immense colonies separated by vacant reaches hundreds of meters wide. Each colony is a single organism, or perhaps a single decision: one fern grove, one raft of breathing moss, one thicket whose millions of stems share a pulse. Between them is emptiness.

The city follows the same pattern.

There are avenues, reservoirs, tram lines, plazas, and neighborhoods, but they are spaced as though built for beings who required a great deal of silence around them. Towers rise alone from the plain, kilometers apart. Bridges cross dry air between no visible destinations. Doorways open in isolated walls. A public square the size of a small lake contains one bench and a black column of rain. Along the avenues, lamps stand at intervals so wide that each pool of light is a separate country.

This should make the landscape quiet.

It is not.

The sound is nearly unbearable.

A continuous note comes from beneath the plain: too deep to be heard cleanly, but powerful enough to trouble the lungs and make loose seeds walk in circles. Over it passes a vast orchestration of animal calls. Glass-winged insects scream from the vine mats. Pale, long-bodied creatures in the drainage fissures strike their ribbed jaws together with the report of splitting timber. Flocks of sail-backed birds wheel in the dim sky, each turn producing a low metallic roar as air crosses the cavities in their bones. Far away, one of the solitary towers answers in a voice like a ship’s horn dragged through stone.

The loudest structures have no apparent inhabitants. They stand open to weather and roots, performing operations no one now supervises. Their walls are assembled from a gray substance that is neither metal nor masonry. It beads with condensation even in dry heat. When touched, it rearranges its grain around the pressure of a finger and sends a ripple upward through the entire building. Balconies rotate slowly toward events that have not yet happened. Elevators travel sideways into rooms larger than their towers. Cables emerge from the ground, rise in loops, and vanish into patches of dark sky.

The technology does not seem abandoned. It seems emancipated.

The streets repair themselves in narrow waves. Cracks unzip ahead of root tips and seal behind them, allowing vegetation to pass without breaking the pavement. Small machines—if machines is the word—move through the undergrowth as alterations in color. A leaf becomes chrome-bright, then the brightness runs down its stem, crosses the soil, and enters another leaf. Wherever it passes, mites gather and hold still, receiving some instruction. By evening they disperse to pollinate flowers that will not bloom for several days.

Nothing here rusts. Nothing remains entirely itself.

The light is dim despite the warmth. A ceiling of smoke-colored cloud hangs low and steady, illuminated from within by slow violet flickers. The sun is usually visible only as a brass stain. Shadows are broad and uncertain, often pointing in different directions around the same object. At intervals the clouds thin over a particular tower or grove, admitting a vertical shaft of amber light. Animals rush toward these openings. Leaves turn their pale undersides upward. The city’s surfaces wake and expose arrays of minute lenses. Then the opening closes, and everything continues under the greenish dusk.

Rain is rare enough to be an event, but moisture is everywhere.

At dawn, the buildings sweat. Water gathers on the cool gray walls, flows along channels finer than hair, and drips into ceramic basins at street level. Vines drink directly from the masonry. Fog settles in the transit trenches. Beneath certain plazas are pockets of warm water, sealed under transparent membranes that flex with the deep vibration of the ground. Fish graze upside down on the undersides of these membranes, their shadows passing through the paving stones.

The only open river runs through the western quarter. It is narrow, clear, and astonishingly fast, confined between banks of newly lifted rock. It appears for nine kilometers, beginning at a blank wall and ending beneath a stand of scarlet reeds. No source or continuation has been found. During dry weeks it shrinks to a chain of basins. During rain, it rises without widening, becoming a standing blade of water several meters tall. Schools of small black fish climb within it. Predators wait on either side, leaping horizontally through the vertical current.

Rain itself arrives in organized columns. One can see it approaching across the plain: a dark moving pillar, perhaps twenty meters wide, dragging a fringe of insects and birds. It may cross an entire district without spilling a drop outside its boundary. Some columns follow old boulevards. Others circle particular trees. One has rained continuously into the central square for as long as records persist, though the sky above it is sometimes clear. Its water strikes the pavement with such force that the plaza is enclosed by sound. Inside the column, visibility is reduced to a few inches and gravity becomes unreliable. Fallen droplets have occasionally been seen rising again.

Around water, life crowds in until every surface moves.

Blue-throated grazing animals crop the moss in herds, communicating through flashes under the skin rather than voices. Six-legged browsers step delicately among the fern towers, their narrow heads high above the fronds. In the open spaces, membrane-bodied scavengers roll with the wind, collapsing flat when a predator’s shadow crosses them. Burrowing colonies turn strips of the red soil overnight, leaving immaculate geometric fields. Tiny amphibians live inside the condensation channels of the buildings. Their calls are amplified through the walls, so a creature no larger than a seed can bellow over several blocks.

Predation is constant but rarely hurried. Hunters often wait beside the isolated lamps, where migrating insects arrive after dark. One species resembles a bundle of wet branches until it unfolds into a low, many-jointed runner. Another is visible only by the silence surrounding it: when it approaches, the city’s endless mechanical and animal thunder dims in a moving sphere. Birds fall out of the air at the sphere’s boundary. Leaves stop trembling. Even the subterranean note weakens. Then the quiet passes, and something is missing.

Human habitation, if that is what it is, concentrates in a handful of low complexes near the river. The dwellings are not dense. Courtyards, empty halls, gardens, and acoustic barriers occupy more space than rooms. Walkways are suspended above the vegetation so that migration routes remain unobstructed. Windows are thick and liquid-filled, trembling visibly under the pressure of the noise. Residents travel in enclosed capsules whose interiors cancel vibration by generating a precise local counterworld.

Few people are seen outside.

Those who emerge wear translucent mantles that crawl over the body and stiffen when struck by sound. They tend reservoirs, exchange sealed baskets, or stand beneath the sweating walls to collect water. Speech is considered unsafe in the open. Certain frequencies provoke the architecture; others attract animals from great distances. People communicate by hand signs, skin displays, or devices that place words directly into one another’s sense of balance. A greeting feels like leaning slightly toward sunlight. A warning feels like missing a stair.

The city has rules, though no visible authority enforces them.

Do not cross an avenue while its surface is flowing north.

Do not shelter under a tower that has turned all its balconies inward.

If rain falls upward, release anything you are holding.

Never answer a voice that arrives through the ground, even when it uses your own memories.

The rules are posted on tall ceramic plaques, each surrounded by a wide margin of vacant land. Moss avoids the writing. Animals do not.

Beyond the inhabited complexes, the city continues for scores of kilometers, though calling it one city may be misleading. Its parts are linked less by roads than by synchronized behavior. At certain hours, distant towers lean by fractions of a degree. Bridges tense. The river brightens. Every solitary lamp flashes once, and vast root systems contract beneath the streets. A pressure wave crosses the plain, flattening the grass in a ring too large to see whole.

Then construction begins.

Bare ground opens in clean polygons. Material rises from below without excavation, extruded as warm, seamless ribs. Walls knit themselves between these ribs, initially transparent, then darkening as airborne spores adhere to them. Rooms appear, connect, and sometimes disappear before completion. No builders attend. No plan can be inferred. A new district may consist of a transit station with no entrances, a kilometer of covered walkway, and one small chamber containing artificial night.

The plain is young because it is still being made. The sharp basalt, the drainage channels, perhaps even the river are by-products of the city’s deeper work. Under the ground, impossible engines fold heat, mass, and distance into arrangements that briefly resemble geology. Small earthquakes pass beneath the vegetation. New ridges push up overnight, splitting vine colonies and exposing stone that glows dull red before cooling. Plants advance within hours. Animals arrive before there should be food. By the following dusk, the ridge has its own ecology and a tower standing at its highest point.

The emptiness remains.

It is the defining presence of the landscape: the enormous interval between each grove, creature, building, and human life. The city is urban in organization but not in crowding. It has every apparatus of density—transport, utilities, civic space, layered infrastructure—spread across distances vast enough to make each element lonely. From a tower balcony, one can see an entire regional system operating beneath the dim sky: green colonies pulsing, rain columns wandering, isolated structures exchanging signals, herds threading through open stone, the narrow river flashing like a cut wire.

Nothing touches, yet everything is connected.

As night comes, the clouds descend until their underside catches on the tallest towers. Lights awaken below the vegetation, tracing buried streets and foundations that extend far beyond the visible city. The lamps do not brighten; darkness simply becomes more articulate around them. Creatures begin their loudest calls. Walls resonate. The ground-note rises until the horizon seems to vibrate.

Then, across the immense and nearly empty plain, every building opens a single door.

Warm air pours out, smelling of rain on new stone.

For a few minutes the city waits—lush, deafening, unfinished—for something large enough to enter.
