The country is called the High Interior, though no one has found an exterior.

It is a warm, dim world of enormous rooms.

Each room is hundreds of valleys across. Their ceilings cannot usually be seen, only inferred from the rain that gathers there and falls hours later in sparse, heavy drops. The floor is not earth. It is a weave of living roots laid over older roots, those over still older courses of timber: beams blackened by age, wicker vaults compacted into peat, gigantic mats stitched together with thorn and tendon. Cut deeply enough and one finds no rock. There is only construction descending into darkness—platform below platform, forgotten galleries, collapsed trellises, sealed cisterns, the floors of previous worlds.

Nothing here is wholly natural, but almost everything is alive.

The principal terrain consists of vast suspended terraces. They hang at different heights in the interior gloom, some separated by a handspan, others by abysses through which clouds travel. Each is supported by trunks planted upside down into the air. Their roots grip emptiness and hold. Their crowns have been bent, braided, grafted, and pegged into plains broad enough to generate their own weather.

The oldest terraces no longer look made. Their joinery has swollen closed. Wooden pins have sprouted. Lashings have rooted into what they bind. Rain has softened straight edges into hummocks and channels, and centuries of feet have polished bark roads to a dark shine. Yet in certain ravines one can still see the original workmanship: ribs laid in careful parallel, walls of woven cane, drains lined with overlapping leaves, retaining slopes stitched with cords of living fiber.

The builders possessed no machines more complicated than the lever, the wedge, the basket, and the knotted line.

This is not considered remarkable here.

A city may take nine generations to raise a single gate. A bridge begins as two saplings trained toward one another across a gulf. Children inherit their grandparents’ half-completed curves. People climb out along scaffolds of reed and bone, carrying stone-edged knives, clay pots of grafting paste, and lengths of cord. They do not assemble dead materials. They persuade growth. A wall is pruned into thickness. A stair is taught to spiral. A reservoir is grown from a bladder-fruit and enlarged one season at a time. The High Interior is total architecture executed at the speed of forests.

And the forests fill everything.

Vegetation is not a covering upon the terrain; it is the terrain, the weather, and much of the distance. The terraces are surfaced in low mosses that retain the shape of every passing pressure. Pale columns rise from them—trunks so widely spaced that one may walk for a day between two, yet each is large enough to have villages in the folds of its bark. Their crowns disappear into the upper dimness, where they interlace with the unseen ceiling. From those crowns descend curtains of root, some hair-fine and glittering with condensation, others thick enough to carry roads.

The emptiness is vertical.

Seen from a ledge, the world appears almost vacant: a few colossal trunks, islands of foliage afloat in brown-black air, distant bridges reduced to threads. The distances between surfaces are immense. Birds cross them as slowly as drifting ash. Rain falls singly, each drop visible for a long time. A person may watch one descend from the gloom, swelling as it collects mist, and step aside before it strikes the floor with the weight of a thrown fruit.

Yet wherever there is a surface, plants occupy it completely.

Leaves overlap like roof tiles. Vines grow over vines until they become beams. Small translucent ferns inhabit the undersides of larger leaves, and finer mosses inhabit those ferns. Dust itself is mostly seed. Even the open air is webbed by sparse, nearly invisible filaments that draw moisture from passing clouds. On warm nights these tighten, shifting whole hanging gardens by several paces. Their movement is too gradual to see but can be felt in the soles: a prolonged, almost tidal change of inclination.

Water is everywhere nearby and seldom gathered in abundance. It sweats from bark, weeps through woven retaining walls, and gathers beneath leaves in trembling lenses. Shallow channels traverse the terraces, made narrow to discourage evaporation. They carry tea-colored water over polished wooden beds, vanish into root-filters, and emerge many leagues away without having descended.

There are marshes at the edges of the great platforms. Here the supporting weave sags under centuries of retained rain. Reed forests stand in warm black pools, their roots threaded through the floor. When the marsh becomes too heavy, it lowers itself. Whole wetlands descend soundlessly through the interior, suspended from lengthening vines, until they settle upon some older terrace below. People mark these migrations on bark charts, though the charts are not maps so much as genealogies: which marsh was divided from which, which bridge has become a riverbank, which village now hangs beneath what used to be its fields.

The air is warm but never bright. Light enters from no discernible source. It exists as a brown-green dilution in the atmosphere, enough to reveal nearby textures while swallowing distance. At irregular intervals the whole interior pales, not as dawn but as if some vast surface has turned elsewhere. Leaves lift. Condensation beads fluoresce faintly. Animals become still. Then the pallor subsides and the High Interior returns to its perpetual late-evening color.

Fire is possible, but flame behaves badly.

A flame kindled on one terrace may appear on another several miles away, leaving the original wick unburned. For this reason people use fire only in deep clay vessels with lids. Most heat comes from compost chambers, sunless blossoms, and the slow fever of certain trees. Light is cultivated: cups of luminous fungus; moths kept in perforated gourds; pale fruits that glow for three days after being twisted from their stems. The light is weak and cherished. No one carries more than is needed to see a handhold.

The fauna is plentiful but dispersed through the immensity.

Long-legged browsers cross the moss plains in herds of thousands, yet the plains are so broad that the animals resemble moving punctuation. They have split hooves adapted to the yielding weave and narrow heads for reaching between layers of leaves. Their coats support gardens of algae and tiny flowering plants. When they sleep, pollinators visit them.

There are hollow-boned gliders that nest inside fruits. There are blind, velvet-skinned things that swim through loose root mass as fish swim through water. Finger-sized antelope graze on lichen along vertical bark. Pale apes live beneath the terraces and climb upside down, never seeing the lands whose weight they carry overhead. In the marshes, amphibians inflate transparent throat sacs and drift from pool to pool on rising warm air.

Predators are rare in sight but not in consequence. The most feared are the interval cats. One sees the front of such a creature before seeing its hindquarters, sometimes separated by several hundred paces. It moves through breaks in space as an ordinary animal moves through grass. Its prey may collapse with wounds before the predator visibly arrives. Hunters never follow its tracks, because the tracks often lead backward into the hunter’s own footprints.

Sound does not travel freely.

Leaves absorb it. Root walls draw vibration downward. A shout becomes a faint warmth in the nearest trunk. Thunder can occur without noise, visible only as a shiver passing through distant foliage. Rain speaks in isolated taps, and water in the channels makes almost no murmur. Even the animals are quiet. Wings are downy, hooves broad, calls pitched below hearing or transmitted through the living floor.

To listen, inhabitants kneel.

By pressing the forehead to a root road, one can feel migrations, storms, construction, falling water, and sometimes speech from settlements days away. Messages are knocked into particular species of timber with wooden mallets. Different trees carry different meanings. A braided ash transmits warnings; the red hollow-vine carries names; old pillarwood carries only questions. Answers sent through pillarwood emerge elsewhere altered but, according to custom, more accurate.

This is one of the rules of the place: distance revises intention.

A road followed long enough does not necessarily reach where it points. It reaches the destination most consistent with the traveler’s preparations. A person carrying fishing gear will eventually come to water, even if they climb continuously. Funeral parties always descend, regardless of the slope beneath their feet. Thieves take circular routes and often return with objects stolen from houses not yet built.

The people account for these effects in their craft. Builders do not measure only length, load, and curvature; they measure expectation. Before raising a bridge, they spend a season establishing what lies on the opposite side. Witnesses are stationed there. Songs are memorized. Identical baskets are exchanged between the two ends until the route agrees to remain singular. Even then, a bridge sometimes acquires a third end during the rains.

There are ruins, though in a world where everything is grown and regrown, ruin does not mean abandonment. It means that a construction has ceased obeying its purpose.

A staircase may become steep with age until it stands vertical, then continue coiling into open air. Reservoirs forget water and fill with warm wind. Doorways migrate into the trunks around them. Entire districts turn their inner surfaces outward, exposing bedrooms, corridors, and courtyards on the bark of a vast sphere of woven wood. These settled impossibilities are rarely repaired. They are weathered, stabilized, and inhabited cautiously. Ferns soften their angles. Nesting animals learn their loops. Erosion makes even contradictions comfortable.

Far below the inhabited terraces are the Underhangs: inverted forests growing from the undersides of the world. Their leaves point downward toward an immeasurable vacancy. Water beads at their tips but does not fall; it rises as mist and returns through the roots above. Villages there are built with floors overhead and roofs underfoot. Their inhabitants tie themselves loosely to walls while sleeping in case gravity changes allegiance.

Far above are the Looms, where young terraces are made. No one knows who began them. Miles of growing fiber pass through arrangements of branch forks, weighted stones, and rotating seedpods. Vines braid themselves according to tensions set centuries ago. New land emerges continuously from these mechanisms—a green fabric advancing into empty air at the rate of a fingernail’s growth. Crews travel its edge, correcting snarls with flint blades and bone combs. Behind them come sowers, water-guides, animal drovers, and finally settlements.

Sometimes the fabric produces an error: a valley on both sides of itself, a knot containing more interior volume than the thread surrounding it, or a patch of ground on which all seeds germinate into the plants their planters remember rather than the seeds’ own species. These places are fenced with red cord. The fences are warnings, not barriers.

No sky has been confirmed. No foundation has been reached.

The oldest surviving story claims that the entire High Interior is a basket made to carry a garden across a flood. Another says it is the interior of a seed that has not yet opened. A third maintains that there was once bare stone here, but the first builders covered it so completely that stone lost the habit of existing.

This last belief is considered dangerous. Stone tools are used, but no one asks where the stone comes from. Smooth blades and heavy gray wedges are sometimes found embedded in old trunks after storms. They are collected without ceremony. If two pieces are struck together, they make a sound much louder than anything else in the world.

That sound attracts rain.

It also makes the distant pillars seem, for an instant, like bars.

Most days pass quietly. Warm mist moves through the vast intervals. Marshes settle by imperceptible degrees. Root bridges tighten. Grazing herds inscribe dark routes through luminous moss. Builders sit on high scaffolds, cutting a notch that their descendants will deepen. Far away, a drop of water falls through miles of green dusk.

It strikes a leaf.

Across the High Interior, every leaf turns once toward the sound.
