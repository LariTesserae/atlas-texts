Below the old sea floor lies a chamber called the Bright Sump.

It is not far underground—perhaps forty meters beneath a plain of salt grass and pale mud—but it feels deeper because no daylight enters by any ordinary route. The chamber is roughly oval, the size of a small cathedral laid on its side. Its roof hangs low at the margins and rises toward a fractured central dome. Everything is visible. A white, sourceless brilliance fills the air, reflected from wet stone until shadows become thin blue stains beneath ledges.

The light comes from the water, though not by luminescence. Wherever a drop forms, its curved surface opens briefly onto an intense sky. Tiny horizons tremble inside beads along the ceiling. Rivulets shine like torn strips of noon. The broad pool occupying the lowest half of the chamber contains a whole field of moving brightness, as though open air lay on the other side of its skin.

The water is warm. So is the rock.

Condensation gathers constantly on walls of weathered limestone and on darker seams of mineral glass. It travels through old channels polished to the softness of bone. Long erosion has rounded every projection that the chamber permits to remain: terraces curl along the pool; pillars have narrowed into waists; scalloped hollows overlap one another in patient generations. Nothing looks newly broken, though breakage is continuous. Fallen pieces lose their sharpness within hours. By the following day they appear ancient, furred with pale mineral deposits and seated neatly in beds of silt.

The place has reached an equilibrium that includes collapse.

Water pours from perhaps a dozen openings, but none flows steadily. A fissure high in the eastern wall may emit a silver sheet, stop at once, and begin inhaling mist. Another spits warm droplets across twenty meters with the regularity of machinery. The pool heaves without tides. Sudden mounds rise on its surface, migrate against the current, and flatten beneath the stone terraces. Narrow whirlpools open and close in dry ground. Sometimes the water climbs the walls in branching tracks, pauses under the roof, then rains upward into cracks.

This activity makes the chamber deafening.

There is the continuous hammer of falls against hollow stone, the bass concussion of water shifting through cavities below, and a shrill, glassy singing from mineral ribs along the ceiling. At irregular intervals the entire sump produces a deep report, more felt in the sternum than heard. Loose droplets leap. The pool flashes white. Then a rapid sequence of clicks passes around the walls, as if some immense mechanism were counting its own echoes.

Yet there is little in the chamber.

The open surfaces are broad, bare, and clean. Vegetation survives only in isolated patches: dark red fans rooted where warm seepage crosses old metal; threadlike moss tracing three or four sheltered cracks; translucent reeds standing ankle-high on a silt bar near the western edge. The reeds possess no leaves. Their hollow stems collect the chamber’s noise and release it as low humming after the water briefly quiets. Each patch stands far from the others, separated by wet stone and luminous water.

Animals are rarer still. Colorless skaters sometimes move across the pool, each supported not by surface tension but a finger’s breadth above it. They leave six fine distortions in the light without touching the water. In deep crevices live soft-bodied shell creatures that emerge after a concussion and feed on fresh mineral deposits. Nothing appears to hunt them. Once in a long while, a narrow black shape can be seen crossing beneath the bright surface of the pool. It is always larger than the pool should permit.

Signs of construction occur around the chamber, modest and widely separated. A waist-high arch stands alone on a terrace, made from fitted blocks of the local limestone. It encloses only rock. On the opposite shore, seven ceramic stakes mark a curve whose continuation would pass through the water. Near the southern wall is a shelter barely large enough for two people: three vitreous panels leaned together, a grated floor, and a corroded handrail descending into a sealed fissure.

These remnants are simple. What maintains the chamber is not.

Embedded throughout the rock is an engineered medium finer than veins and older-looking than the erosion around it. It does not resemble wiring. Under close inspection, the darker seams prove to be arrangements of absence: hair-thin gaps that remain empty even when submerged, branching through the limestone in patterns too complex to hold in the eye. They alter pressure, gravity, and the permissible direction of flow. Their operations are visible only through the behavior of other things.

A falling drop may strike one of these invisible boundaries and divide into a hundred equal drops. A hundred may become one. Water disappears into a shallow groove and returns from the ceiling carrying different dissolved minerals. Heat passes through the chamber in coherent fronts, bending the reeds all at once. Light pools in corners, then pours uphill over the floor like thin milk.

The technology does not seem to be malfunctioning. Nor does it seem concerned with stability. Its purpose may be to accelerate the chamber’s natural processes until erosion becomes a form of computation. Every channel is both a watercourse and a decision. Every collapsing shelf changes the subterranean pressures, which redirect the falls, which carve new hollows, which alter the bright medium in the pool. The stone calculates slowly; the water revises it violently.

For this reason no map of the Bright Sump remains accurate for long. Its outer boundary holds—the same oval chamber, the same dome, the same low shores—but the interior remakes itself by the hour. Terraces appear where pools had been. A pillar may thicken rather than wear away. The solitary arch is sometimes on the eastern side and sometimes on the west, although its footing is never disturbed. Distances change subtly. Walking around the pool can take six minutes in one direction and nearly an hour in the other.

There are rules.

A person may touch dry stone safely, provided it has remained dry for several breaths. Water can be crossed only where it reflects the chamber roof. Where it reflects sky, it has no reliable depth and may not belong to this place. One must not stand beneath a drop containing a cloud. Echoes that return in one’s own voice should not be answered. After the deepest concussion, all moving things must remain still until the clicking has completed its circuit of the walls.

These rules survive as scratches on the shelter panels, along with tally marks and a diagram of the seven ceramic stakes. The scratches are recent compared with the stone, but no equipment, clothing, or remains identify their makers. Occasionally the handrail beside the shelter is found warm, with beads of water trembling upward along its underside.

The atmosphere smells of rain on hot pavement, though neither rain nor pavement is present. It tastes faintly metallic. Mist forms waist-high over the pool, shredded continuously by pressure waves. Above it the air is startlingly clear. The dome shines in perfect detail: cream limestone, black seams, hanging droplets, and old circular scars where something once attached to the roof.

The chamber’s brightest moments come without warning. Every water surface turns simultaneously toward that impossible exterior. The pool becomes a disk of blazing blue. Drops on the ceiling show suns, thousands of them, each at a slightly different hour. Warm wind erupts from nowhere, flattening the sparse reeds and driving mist through the lone arch. For several seconds the roar ceases—not fades, but is removed—and in the absolute quiet one can hear small stones rearranging themselves under the water.

Then the chamber shuts again.

The falls return with a single overwhelming crash. Light scatters over wet terraces. Fresh channels begin to deepen, already smooth at their edges, already old. The sparse life straightens. The skaters resume their passage above the pool. Somewhere below, in a volume larger than the ground can contain, water turns over and begins another calculation.
