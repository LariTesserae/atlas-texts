The city occupies the upper walls of a caldera too deep to see across clearly and too high for the surrounding atmosphere to feel substantial. It is not built on a plateau so much as fastened to a circumference: a belt of foundries, pressure halls, lift stations, dwellings, and condensation works clamped to black cliffs several kilometers above the basin floor.

The stone is young. In many places it has not yet become convincing terrain. Ridges terminate in blunt, glassy folds. Escarpments retain the smooth torsion of material that cooled while moving. There are few gullies, no mature valleys, none of the soft debris that ordinarily gathers beneath mountains. The slopes are slabs of basalt, sulfur-pale crusts, and dark metallic foam, interrupted by terraces cut for construction. Even these cuts look provisional. Drill marks remain sharp for centuries in the thin, dry air.

Heat comes upward.

Far below the city, the caldera floor glows only occasionally: a red seam opening between plates, a dim pulse under translucent rock. More often the heat is invisible, carried through buried veins and immense ceramic pipes. It rises into the urban belt until handrails are warm, walls breathe furnace air through expansion joints, and the soles of boots soften if left too long on unshaded decking. The highest neighborhoods are cooler by altitude, but never cold. Their towers sweat.

Water exists here chiefly as an event of surfaces. The air passing over the caldera contains little moisture, yet the city’s thermal machinery repeatedly compresses, cools, and releases it. Condensation pearls on the shaded sides of pipes. It gathers beneath heat exchangers and runs along gutters no wider than a finger. At dawn, cistern roofs become briefly silver. Every few weeks, clouds rise from the basin and collide with the upper rim, producing a hard, localized rain that may soak three districts while leaving the next one dry enough for sparks to crawl across the pavement.

The rain does not last. It strikes hot stone with a smell like extinguished iron, gathers in construction hollows, and vanishes by afternoon. Some pockets persist in sealed courtyards or deep beneath stacked infrastructure: black reservoirs with mineral skins, maintenance sumps full of warm, faintly conductive water, condensation chambers where droplets fall with ceremonial slowness into tiled vaults. These are among the city's most carefully guarded spaces. Its wealth is counted not only in heat and metal, which are abundant, but in cool surfaces.

Nothing green grows.

There is no scrub in the cracks, no moss under leaking valves, no lichen discoloring old facades. Seen from a distance, the absence is difficult to notice because the city is richly colored: oxide red, sulfur yellow, furnace white, violet heat stain, blue-black glass. Up close, the sterility becomes oppressive. Every patch of damp mineral remains bare. Every abandoned gutter holds only scale, crystalline salts, and a brittle lace of precipitated metal.

Where life might be expected, stranger processes have taken its place. In the condensation tunnels, thin mineral membranes assemble along electrical gradients. They spread across wet ceramic in branching geometries, conduct charge for a season, then fragment into glittering powder when the current changes. Engineers classify them as fouling. Some residents insist they anticipate maintenance failures. Elsewhere, silica blisters inflate and collapse beside steam leaks, leaving nested cups resembling the shells of creatures that never evolved.

The city is dense because habitable structure is easier to extend than habitable ground. Buildings do not stand separately. They brace one another. A residence may rest on the roof of a refinery whose lower walls are also part of a public reservoir, while a freight incline passes through all three. Streets become corridors, then bridges, then elevator shafts without clear transition. There are plazas, but most are vertical: tall cavities crossed by walkways at dozens of levels, with lifts crawling along the walls and vents opening into the depths.

The oldest structures are scarcely older than the mountains. Their masonry is angular and unweathered, though repeatedly modified. New districts appear as pale frameworks bolted directly into cliffs that still emit heat. Entire blocks hang from tension cables anchored beyond the rim. Beneath them are older attempts: warped platforms, vitrified scaffolds, machine housings slowly incorporated into newer foundations. The city is perpetually under construction, yet construction is not rapid. Each expansion must wait for the stone to settle, for a heat front to migrate, for stresses to become legible. A foundation may be monitored for thirty years before the first occupied chamber is attached to it.

Industry proceeds with the same patience. There are engines large enough to be mistaken for neighborhoods, but they turn at almost imperceptible rates. Deep pistons complete one stroke over several days. Ore kilns remain closed for years at a time. Cable drums rotate so slowly that dust forms transient lines on their rims. The city does not manufacture quickly; it manufactures continuously, using pressure, altitude, and geothermal gradients that would destroy more hurried mechanisms.

This slowness gives the place its quiet.

There are no endless rows of hammering presses, no constant roar from the vents. Machinery speaks in isolated sounds: a relay cracking somewhere across a chasm; the low flex of a cooling pipe; three warning knocks traveling through the cliff; a turbine beginning to sing and then falling below hearing. Freight carriages move on magnetic rails with a faint granular hiss. The loudest common sound is wind crossing unfinished frameworks, though even the wind is thin at this elevation. It plucks at cables rather than howling.

Voices do not travel far. People use wall transmitters, light codes, and contact bells fixed to the handrails. At great distance, whole districts communicate by changing the color of their exhaust towers.

The usual sky is clear, bright but not dazzling. Sunlight arrives hard enough to define every bolt and seam, yet suspended mineral haze softens the farther reaches. Across the caldera, the opposite city wall appears as a layered gray band crowded with towers. Above it rises the rim: raw peaks, broken crescents, unfinished-looking spires of volcanic glass. Shadows are blue and surprisingly deep. At noon the inhabited belt seems almost ordinary in illumination, which makes its impossible position more apparent. One can look from a laundry balcony through two kilometers of open air to a molten fracture below.

At night, the city does not blaze. Light is expensive wherever it adds heat. Windows remain narrow; pathways are marked by low amber strips; factories reveal themselves as dull red apertures and slow-moving constellations of inspection lamps. The brightest objects are often natural: a heat bloom under the basin crust, sheet lightning in a dry cloud, or the pale corona that sometimes forms around the highest aerials.

The city’s rules are architectural because mistakes become geological.

No room may possess only one anchor.

No water line may cross an active heat line without a sacrificial chamber between them.

No new load may be attached to cooling rock until its contraction has been observed through three rain cycles.

Doors open inward on the cliffward side and outward on the voidward side.

A red-painted floor is not necessarily hot, but a floor without paint must be assumed to be.

If the contact bells ring twice and then remain silent, no one moves until the silence has lasted an hour.

The last rule is poorly explained to visitors.

Fauna is rare, though not absent. Small translucent fliers sometimes appear after rain. They emerge from inaccessible pressure cavities, ride the warm updrafts, and vanish before their anatomy can be studied properly. They have no obvious wings; their bodies are taut envelopes around filaments that contract in heat. Workers call them gauges because their height above the vents corresponds loosely to pressure below.

Farther out, along the outer slopes, there are reports of heavy six-limbed animals crossing the ash at night. Their tracks end on bare rock. Nothing edible grows there, and no one knows what could sustain them. Some believe they are inspection machines from an earlier phase of the city, their casings rounded by repeated thermal cycling until they resemble living bodies. Others point out that the tracks sometimes contain moisture.

The city is broad enough to contain climates. On the eastern arc, steam accumulates beneath overhanging cliffs, and whole neighborhoods are slick with condensation. In the north, exposed bridges cross expanses so arid that spilled water snaps into vapor. The southern industrial terraces lie behind mineral haze and smell of ozone, ceramic dust, and hot copper. On the western heights stand the wind towers, narrow structures extending beyond the comfortable atmosphere, each crowded with vanes and static collectors.

Travel between these regions may take days, not because the distances are enormous, but because horizontal routes are rare. One descends, crosses through pressure halls and refinery galleries, then climbs again. Maps depict elevation more prominently than direction. Addresses are given by anchor, temperature band, and depth.

Beyond the city belt, the caldera walls continue upward into the unoccupied rim and downward toward the basin. Both are considered wilderness, though neither contains much that could live. Above are unstable glass ridges, electrical storms, and the skeletal remnants of survey towers. Below are heat, pressure, and expanses of nearly level black material that may be crust or merely the upper surface of something more fluid.

The caldera itself is still forming in a sense perceptible to instruments but not to a human lifetime. Its walls spread by fractions. New fissures develop beneath inhabited sectors, first detectable as changes in resonance. Mineral deposits thicken in pipework. The basin rises and sinks over centuries. Every structure records these movements through calibrated cracks: narrow channels filled with contrasting ceramic, measured from generation to generation.

Thus the city’s ornament is often diagnostic. Bright lines run across public walls to show strain. Towers are wrapped in numbered rings that reveal subsidence. Bridges carry strings of polished weights whose alignment records minute changes in gravity. What appears decorative is usually a warning system; what appears damaged may be functioning exactly as intended.

From a high platform, the whole place seems both immense and temporary. Dense layers of construction follow the dark curvature of the caldera, crossing vents and cliffs in a continuous, intricate band. Thin rain hangs under one district. Elsewhere, sunlight flashes on thousands of condenser plates. Freight lights move almost too slowly to notice. Steam rises in straight columns until the upper wind bends it toward the rim.

Nothing is overgrown, because nothing grows. Little is ruined, because erosion has scarcely begun. The landscape has not had time to soften its edges or conceal the city’s decisions.

Stone, infrastructure, heat, and sparse water remain exposed together, each still learning the shape of the others.
