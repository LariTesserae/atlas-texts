The country begins above the weather.

Its floor is a high, black plateau folded between even higher ridges, a region broad enough to contain valleys, watersheds, and migrations, yet so elevated that the lower atmosphere lies beneath it like a second earth. Clouds fill the distant basins. Peaks rise from them as islands, while the plateau itself remains under a permanent astronomical dusk. The sun passes somewhere behind a ceiling of particulate haze, showing only as a pale distortion in the dark. At noon, surfaces acquire edges. At night, even those disappear.

The cold is dry in the open and wet in sheltered places. Snow does not lie evenly. It is combed from the plateaus by thin, persistent winds and gathered in blue seams against escarpments. Most exposed stone is bare: basalt, iron-dark schist, and old lava with a dull metallic skin. The land has been weathered for an immense time, but not ruined. Its cliffs have withdrawn into stable faces; its screes have sorted themselves by size; its ravines follow deep, settled lines. Nothing looks freshly broken, though everything is slowly moving.

Water is scarce enough to organize the living world.

It arrives irregularly from condensation rather than rain. Air rising from the buried cloud sea cools against the high ridges, and moisture plates itself onto stone, fur, leaf, and shell. For several hours the cliffs may sweat. Droplets bead beneath overhangs and run through grooves polished into the rock. Then the air changes, the flow stops, and the wet lines freeze.

In the broadest valleys, these brief drainages converge into narrow rivers beneath lids of clear ice. They do not fill their channels. A black current runs through the middle while shelves of ice extend from either bank, widening and narrowing with each pressure change. Elsewhere, isolated pools occupy old volcanic hollows. Their surfaces are dark and perfectly still, but their depths are warmed by the mountain’s remaining heat. Moss grows around them in thick collars, and pale animals gather there without ever entering the exposed center.

Seen from a distance, the vegetation looks impossible. Great portions of the plateau appear empty—kilometers of dark stone, dim snow, and open air—then a ravine turns toward the hidden source of moisture and becomes extravagantly alive.

The plants grow downward as much as upward. Forests hang inside fissures, rooted in cracks and fed by condensation. Their trunks are black, flexible columns with no branches on the windward side. On the sheltered side they carry fans of densely pleated foliage, nearly colorless except where the tissue folds, where it shines a deep petroleum green. The tallest trees rise only a few meters above the ravine, but their root systems descend hundreds of meters through the mountain. They are less a collection of individuals than the visible tips of something buried.

Across gentler slopes spread low jungles of cushion plants. They form rounded masses large enough to redirect streams. Their outer leaves are tough and frozen; beneath this rind lies a warm, decomposing interior full of roots, larvae, fungal threads, and trapped water. The oldest cushions have split open under their own weight, creating narrow, steaming canyons. Tiny flowering structures open inside these cracks, out of the wind. Their blossoms are black and faintly luminous, pollinated not by flying insects but by long-legged mites that travel in glittering processions.

There are also the filament fields.

From a distance they resemble frost-coated grass, but each stalk is a hollow mineral tube grown by a colonial organism in partnership with metal-eating bacteria. The colonies draw iron and sulfur from the rock, leaving honeycombed pits beneath them. Condensation enters the tubes, reacts with the living films inside, and emerges lower on the slope as weakly acidic water. Whole hillsides tick almost inaudibly as the filaments expand, contract, and fracture with the temperature.

This is where the industry of the place resides.

There are no buildings, roads, tools, or ruins. Nothing has hands. Yet the plateau behaves as though occupied by a vast, dispersed manufacturing system.

In deep cracks, microbial mats refine ore into layered metallic nodules. Root colonies pump heated brine upward through stone. Forests regulate pressure in subterranean chambers by opening and closing millions of leaf-valves in sequence. The filament fields collect electrical charge from the wind and conduct it down toward buried fungal networks. During periods of high atmospheric tension, blue current passes silently through entire valleys. It is not lightning: there is no flash or thunder, only a dim ignition moving from ridge to ridge like thought.

The products of these processes are obscure. The landscape extrudes perfect ceramic rings from fumaroles, grows sheets of conductive crystal between roots, and assembles delicate lattices in the mud beneath pools. None appear designed for use. Most are eventually dissolved and fed back into the cycle. A lattice may take forty years to form and three minutes to be consumed by a migrating swarm. The mountain manufactures conditions rather than objects: warmth, salinity, electrical potential, brief corridors of breathable air.

Its engines are organisms, and its organisms are engines.

The largest animals inhabit the open spaces between green ravines. They are tall, spare creatures whose bodies are suspended between four or six jointed legs. Their hides carry dense mats of lichen, making them resemble pieces of the terrain that have risen and begun to walk. They move slowly until threatened. Then compressed gas is released through vents along their flanks, and they bound across the stone in nearly soundless leaps.

Herds may number in the hundreds, but the country is so broad that they seldom make it feel inhabited. They follow condensation fronts rather than rivers, walking beneath invisible changes in pressure. Where they pass, the lichen on their backs releases spores. Their tracks become temporary gardens, then vanish under frost.

Smaller life is abundant wherever shelter permits it. Glass-winged crawlers cling beneath leaves. Blind fish move through warm underground currents, sometimes visible through clear ice. Soft, many-jointed grazers hollow tunnels inside cushion plants without killing them. In the cliff forests, colonies of furred gliders sleep packed into root chambers, emerging during the darkest hours to drift across chasms. They do not beat their wings. They alter their body heat in narrow bands, steering themselves on rising currents of air.

Predators are fewer and almost never seen directly. The most common sign is interruption: a migration route suddenly bending, a herd standing motionless, a ravine emptied of its usual clicking life. Some predators live beneath the scree and sense electrical activity through the ground. Others may not be animals at all. Certain root systems close around warm bodies. Certain pools produce currents that run against gravity when approached.

The ecology is active but nearly silent. Cold suppresses voices, and the thin air carries little force. There is no birdsong. Winged things glide. Hooves are padded. Leaves are too heavy to rustle. Water moves under ice, stone shifts beneath moss, and organisms communicate by current, vibration, temperature, and chemical stain.

To a listener lying with one ear against the ground, however, the silence opens.

There is the deep pulse of brine being forced through capillaries. The faint granular rush of roots enlarging cracks. A periodic metallic knock from far below, repeated at intervals too regular to be geological and too slow to be deliberate. Filament fields murmur when charged. Ice makes small internal sounds like distant glassware being handled carefully. Once or twice each night, all these noises stop together.

During those pauses, the plateau appears to hold itself still.

Then a pressure front arrives.

Mist climbs from the cloud sea and pours over the lowest saddles, not as a storm but as a shallow, advancing wall. Leaves unfold. Vents open in the soil. Animals turn their bodies broadside to the moisture. The black trunks in the ravines flex and draw water down into the mountain. Fungal films brighten beneath translucent stone. Warm pools overflow for a few minutes and send silver threads across the frost.

Activity passes across the region in waves. Grazers begin to move. Buried hunters follow. Pollinators crawl up from the steaming interiors of plants. Electrical blooms spread over the filament fields. The old rivers swell under their ice and lift it without breaking it. For perhaps an hour, the landscape is running at full capacity.

Then the mist thins. Water retracts into roots, bodies, hollows, and stone. The plateau empties again.

Because the light is so weak, distance behaves strangely. Nearby forms have little color, while remote organisms sometimes reveal themselves by their own dim emissions. A herd fifty kilometers away may appear as a string of green sparks crossing the dark. A forest hidden in a gorge may illuminate the underside of vapor. The highest summits are visible only when they occult the stars.

Orientation is further confused by the mountain’s electrical weather. Compass directions would wander here. Charge flows along living corridors that migrate from season to season, and small iron particles align themselves with roots instead of north. Even gravity seems locally uncertain near the oldest pools, where droplets occasionally rise from the surface and collect on the stone above.

There may be an order to the whole system. Valleys exchange pulses. Herds transport colonies. Rivers carry dissolved metals into warm depths. Forests vent gases into the high atmosphere. Predators regulate not only prey but conductivity, thinning migrations before too many charged bodies cross a ridge at once. What appears to be wilderness may be one machine distributed across a mountain range, or one organism whose thoughts take centuries.

It has no center anyone could find.

Its great spaces remain almost bare: dark flats under dim heavens, separated by sudden seams of overwhelming life. No path crosses them permanently. No mark stands apart from natural process. Even the strange rings and lattices rising from mud do not persist long enough to become monuments.

The country builds continuously and leaves nothing built.

Above it, stars tremble in the thin air. Below it, clouds conceal the lower world. Between them the cold plateau condenses, conducts, hunts, flowers, and refines its ancient stone, working in near-total darkness with the patience of a factory that has never needed walls.
