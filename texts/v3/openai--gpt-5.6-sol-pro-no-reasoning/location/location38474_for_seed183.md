The plateau begins above the weather.

From below it appears to be a mountain range: a pale, many-pointed wall occupying half the sky, too bright to study for long. From its own surface, however, it is almost level—a high mineral country extending from horizon to horizon, broken by shallow basins, crowded fields of stone, and abrupt trenches whose bottoms cannot always be seen. There is no grass to soften distance, no moss on the wet rocks, no birds making scale intelligible. Everything stands naked in the light.

The altitude should make the place dry. Instead, water is everywhere.

It does not gather in rivers so much as inhabit the stone. Clear beads swell from cracks and hang there trembling. Cliffs weep from every layer. Smooth black slabs lie under films of water scarcely thicker than breath, yet these films creep uphill in branching sheets. After the frequent white storms, every hollow becomes a mirror. Pools persist on slopes too steep to hold them, fastened to the rock as though surface tension were stronger here than gravity.

The air is cool and piercingly lucid. Moisture condenses continually, not as fog but as glittering points: thousands of suspended droplets, each perfectly round, each flashing in the severe daylight. They fill some valleys densely enough to resemble glass dust. A traveler passing through them becomes wet without ever seeing rain fall.

There is no vegetation. Nothing green, gray-green, or lichenous interrupts the mineral palette. The land is made of chalk-white crusts, blue-black glass, rustless iron, translucent salt, and a porous yellow stone that rings when struck. Where runoff has cut into the plateau, it exposes hundreds of narrow strata folded through one another like cloth. Some layers contain regular impressions—hexagons, spirals, rows of tiny parallel cuts—but none resemble fossils. They may be old crystal habits, the traces of vanished mechanisms, or marks made by physical laws no longer operating.

Erosion has worked here for an immense time. Corners are rounded. Channels are polished. Isolated pillars have been reduced to thin waists and broad caps, and fields of pebble-sized prisms have sorted themselves into bands by tone. Yet the country never reaches ruin. For every surface worn away, another seems slowly to emerge from inside it. At noon, hairline seams open in the exposed bedrock. By evening, new ridges stand along them: low at first, glossy and sharp, then whitening as water touches them. The plateau erodes and extrudes at nearly equal rates. Its old age is not decline but equilibrium.

The most crowded districts are forests only in arrangement. Thousands of stone blades stand shoulder to shoulder, from knee height to the height of towers. They lean in several directions at once. Their bases are submerged in shallow, intensely blue water, while their upper edges are dry and dazzling. Passage through them is possible along worn lanes, though the lanes shift. A gap entered in the morning may narrow behind the traveler until even a hand cannot pass between the blades.

Despite the absence of life, the plateau is loud.

Water knocks in buried chambers. Wind drives droplets against stone with the hiss of sand. The black slabs boom at irregular intervals, a deep percussive sound felt through the feet. From the trenches comes continuous grinding, as if millstones were turning far below. In bright weather, the tall mineral blades hum. Each has its own thin pitch, but together they make a dense, wavering chord that travels for kilometers.

Echoes behave badly. A hammer strike may return before the hammer lands. Shouted words come back altered in pitch, divided among distant cliffs, or repeated hours later from somewhere underfoot. At certain basin rims, every sound is answered by the noise of breaking surf, though there is no sea within sight.

Scattered through this terrain are small works of deliberate construction. They are made without mortar and seldom contain metal. Flat stones have been fitted into low shelters, retaining walls, stairways, and circular enclosures no wider than a room. Most structures crouch beneath overhangs or cling to trench walls where water continuously runs through them. The workmanship is patient but simple: chipped edges, drilled holes, wedges of bone-colored ceramic, cord impressions baked into clay.

There are no bones.

Inside some shelters lie hammerstones, shallow bowls, flaked black-glass knives, and racks assembled from mineral rods. Everything suggests hands, weight, leverage, repetition. Nothing suggests who used them. Dust has not accumulated. Some bowls remain full of clear water that trembles even when the air is still.

The builders appear to have understood at least one rule of the plateau: enclosed water keeps a place locally stable. Nearly every surviving structure centers on a cistern or basin. Around these held waters, distances remain dependable, stones fall downward, and shadows attach correctly to their objects. Outside them, the country is less disciplined.

A cliff may show its sunlit face on both sides. A trench crossed in three strides may take half a day to walk around. Two neighboring pools can reflect different skies: one the current hard blue, the other an overcast violet dusk full of motionless lightning. In places, the horizon passes in front of nearby objects, cutting pillars off at the middle and allowing their tops to be seen far away. There are slopes where dropped stones accelerate sideways. There are basins where depth accumulates without descent.

The brightest region is called, by the marks left on old waystones, the White Confluence. Here seven shallow valleys meet beneath an unshadowed brilliance. No visible sun occupies the sky. Light rises from wet surfaces, from pale rock, from the suspended droplets, and from the spaces between objects. Shadows are faint and numerous. A solitary pillar may cast twelve, each pointing toward a different hour.

At the Confluence, water climbs from basin to basin in silver steps. It ascends silently, then pours downward elsewhere with tremendous noise. The two motions do not balance from moment to moment, but the pools never empty or overflow. Every few hours, a cool pressure passes across the valley. The hanging droplets flatten into discs; the stone blades bow without bending; and all reflected images turn to look in the same direction.

There is nothing there.

No animal crosses the flats. No insect is trapped in the pools. No microbial stain clouds the water. Even decay is absent because there is almost nothing organic to decay. The leatherlike straps found beside tools are in fact thin laminations of silicate, flexible while wet and brittle when dry. What resembles woven cloth proves to be a mesh of crystalline fibers grown through perforated frames.

If this country ever possessed an ecology, it was an ecology of processes rather than organisms. Water migrates. Salt blooms and vanishes. Cavities breathe. Stone fields tune themselves to storms. Droplets gather into floating chains, break apart, and reassemble farther downslope. Pale disks form overnight on sheltered walls, spread in branching colonies, and dissolve under direct light; whether they are mineral reactions, primitive life, or flaws in perception cannot be settled.

Weather arrives from below. Dark vapor wells out of trenches and spreads ankle-deep over the plateau, filling valleys from the ground upward. It smells of cold metal and rain on dust. Thunder travels through the bedrock before the vapor erupts into vertical showers—columns of water flung hundreds of meters into the brilliant air. The droplets pause at the top of their ascent, sometimes for minutes, before falling as warm rain through the cool atmosphere.

Afterward the landscape rearranges itself modestly but unmistakably. Channels lead elsewhere. Stairs acquire additional steps. A shelter once on the west side of a basin stands on the east, though its foundation remains where it was. New tool marks appear on exposed rock, fresh-edged and wet.

Travelers, if any still come, would learn not to navigate by peaks. Peaks exchange positions when hidden by cloud. Nor should they count footsteps, trust echoes, or sleep beside an empty vessel. The old cairns imply a different method: carry water from a known pool in a covered bowl, and let one drop fall at every turning. The droplets do not remain where they land. They crawl slowly across the stone, making a bright thread back toward their source.

That thread is the nearest thing the plateau offers to a path.

Even it is not entirely safe. Occasionally the droplets lead not to the pool from which they were taken, but to an older version of it: a deeper basin under a darker sky, surrounded by intact shelters and unworn stairs. Figures may seem to stand there, just beyond the reach of the light, striking stone against stone in patient rhythm.

They cannot be approached. The nearer one comes, the farther down the basin lies.

Still, their hammering joins the weather. It passes through the crowded pillars and the dripping cliffs, through the bright suspended rain, through every abandoned room. The whole high country answers—wet stone, hollow stone, newborn stone, and stone already ground almost to nothing—until the plateau sounds less like a dead world than a vast instrument attempting, without breath or living hand, to remember how it was played.
