The valley has no ground.

What appears from a distance to be a warm, forested cleft between mountains is a construction suspended among towers. Its ridges are load-bearing arches; its cliffs are tiled retaining walls; its deep green slopes are plantations rooted in stacked beds of brick, slag glass, and rust-stained aggregate. Even the mountains, pale in the fierce morning light, are hollow.

They rise several kilometers above the lower districts in broad, eroded-looking masses. Generations of engineers softened their original geometry. Cornices have rounded beneath runoff. Terraces have sagged into long, convincing contours. Mineral salts bloom through masonry and turn concrete faces white, amber, and green. Drainage channels have cut dark seams down the cladding, and repair crews have repeatedly followed these seams with mismatched stone. At this age, maintenance resembles geology.

No one living remembers the frame being bare.

The inhabited landscape occupies the interior and exterior of an immense viaduct complex. Parallel bridgeworks cross and recross a chasm whose bottom is hidden by foliage, vapor, and machinery. Some spans support neighborhoods; others carry reservoirs, orchards, foundries, or rail lines. Vertical districts descend from their undersides in tiers of balconies and cable-braced platforms. Seen across the valley, the whole place has the openness of a mountain region and the density of a single crowded building.

The highest ridges are bright enough to hurt the eyes. Their surfaces are clad in cream ceramic and silvered weather-metal, both chosen long ago to shed heat. Sunlight ricochets from face to face, entering the valley indirectly in broad luminous planes. Shade is rarely dark. It is green beneath leaves, copper beneath pipes, blue beneath suspended walkways. The warm air seems full of reflected daylight.

At noon, hundreds of thousands of small shutters rotate almost imperceptibly to reduce glare. Their movement passes across the mountainside as a slow dimming, district by district. It takes nearly an hour.

Water is present everywhere but plentiful nowhere. Rain comes in brief, slanting showers that may wet one side of the valley while the other remains sunlit. Every surface has been shaped to receive it. Roofs incline toward ceramic throats. Handrails are grooved into gutters. Facades carry chains down which droplets travel without scattering. Beneath the gardens, condensation webs collect moisture from warm industrial air meeting the cool interiors of old structural chambers.

After rain, the landscape speaks in drips.

Water taps from leaf to leaf, clicks into metal cups, runs through glazed channels, and disappears behind walls. For a few hours the stairways smell of wet dust, hot brick, oil, and flowers. Shallow cistern courts fill ankle-deep. Children wade there among floating seed husks until the water is pumped away. By evening, most exposed surfaces are dry again, but damp persists in shaded joints, root galleries, cooling chambers, and the deep pockets where old bridges meet older towers.

There are no natural streams, though several waterways convincingly imitate them. The largest is the Long Course, an open aqueduct descending by thousands of shallow steps from the eastern condensers. It twists around pylons, vanishes into neighborhoods, and reappears beneath gardens. Its bed is assembled from dark vitrified blocks worn smooth by centuries of modest flow. Mosses occupy its margins. Tiny silver fish live in the wider settling basins, feeding on insect larvae and industrial biofilm.

The Long Course is regulated, but not precisely. Gates swell in humid weather. Channels settle. Roots enter cracks. A spillway blocked by leaves may redirect water down a stairwell for a season before anyone decides whether this is a failure or a useful new tributary. The valley’s water system has become too old and entangled to distinguish entirely between plumbing and ecology.

Vegetation fills almost every available interval.

Trees grow from deep communal beds on bridge decks, their roots spreading through chambers designed as both soil vaults and structural ballast. Figs clasp ventilation stacks. Broad-leaved vines hang from freight gantries in green curtains, trembling when trains pass overhead. Citrus orchards occupy sun-facing terraces; their small glossy crowns are kept low against the wind. In warm recesses grow bananas, tree ferns, peppers, medicinal shrubs, and pale orchids cultivated for the dye works.

Much of this lushness is edible or useful, but not all. Centuries of escaped planting have produced dense volunteer thickets in inaccessible parts of the structure. Seedlings emerge from drain mouths. Ferns colonize wet insulation. Aerial roots descend through broken grilles and find purchase three levels below. Certain abandoned service towers are green from crown to foundation, their windows obscured by foliage and their interior stairs occupied by trees.

The landscape is not being reclaimed by nature; there is nothing underneath it for nature to reclaim. Instead, living things have entered into the construction and made a second kind of fabric. Roots bind loosened masonry. Fungal mats seal damp seams. Reed beds clean factory discharge. Trees shade machinery housings, reducing thermal strain. Removal would often be more dangerous than growth.

The fauna is equally interwoven.

Swifts nest in expansion joints and pour from the cliffs each morning in black, flickering clouds. Bright parakeets raid the orchards. Pigeons, doves, mynas, and small rust-colored songbirds crowd the cable lines. Lizards bask on sun-warmed tile, while geckos inhabit the lamp housings and hunt moths after dusk. In garden districts, bees are kept in wall boxes whose wax combs extend into specially heated cavities.

The lower greenways support heavier animals. Goats move along maintenance ledges with colored ownership bells. Lean pigs forage beneath market platforms, eating fallen fruit and vegetable waste. Civets and small foxes inhabit disused conduits. Monkeys travel certain vine-thick corridors and have learned the timing of freight lifts. They wait on the upper beams until a loaded platform begins its slow descent, then leap aboard to reach the fruit terraces below.

Animals know pathways people have forgotten.

The air is loud, though rarely frantic. Machinery establishes the underlying note: the heavy pulse of pumps, the continuous breath of ventilation shafts, the irregular hammering of mills, and the far-reaching hum of electrical substations. Cable trams complain around turns. Elevators ring at landings. Workshops spill out the sounds of saws, presses, grinders, looms, and riveters.

Because the valley is built as a series of resonant shells, sound travels unpredictably. A forge two kilometers away may seem to operate behind a nearby wall. Voices dropped into certain shafts emerge faintly in courtyards far below. Thunder rolls within the hollow mountains long after the visible storm has crossed.

Yet the noise changes slowly. Factories follow long shifts; pumps run for months without stopping; ventilation fans turn at nearly constant speed. Even the bells marking work, meals, water release, and rail passage have broad, patient intervals. The place is loud in the manner of a waterfall or a city heard from afar: persistent enough to become a kind of silence.

Industry occupies the valley’s colder interiors and its broadest decks. Foundries remelt salvaged metal. Kilns fire replacement tiles and pipe sections. Mills process fiber from terrace-grown plants. Machine halls produce bearings, valves, rail shoes, pump impellers, and the thousands of standardized clamps with which the landscape fastens itself together.

Nothing mined here is native, because there is no native rock. Material arrives from elsewhere or is recovered from the structure itself. Old beams become new brackets. Cracked ceramics are crushed into growing medium. Corroded cable is smelted and drawn again. Districts are sometimes dismantled not because they have failed, but because another district needs their substance more urgently.

This produces a peculiar rule: the valley cannot expand without becoming thinner somewhere else.

Construction is therefore less an act of addition than migration. A bridge may slowly disappear over fifty years, its railing removed first, then its roofing, then one pedestrian lane, while on the opposite side a hospital, orchard deck, or cooling tower gradually takes shape from the transferred parts. Maps are printed with dates because routes alter over generations. Elderly residents remember plazas where younger people know only open air.

The architecture accepts this slowness. Important structures are never considered finished. Walls include sockets for uses not yet imagined. Foundations contain planted voids where future loads may be anchored. Houses have doors opening onto spaces where walkways might someday arrive. Some of these doors remain above empty drops for centuries, painted and maintained by families who inherit the expectation of a bridge.

Height governs custom. Objects are tethered in upper districts. Market produce sits in netted stalls. Roof tiles are wired down individually. People wear belts with short clips when crossing exposed maintenance ways. Falling is treated less as an accident than as a category of weather: always possible, locally predictable, reduced by collective attention.

Below the sunlit residential terraces, the valley deepens through layers of older construction. Light enters via mirrored shafts and enormous white-painted wells. Gardens become shade gardens, rich in moss, ginger, fungi, and broad leaves. Condensation increases. Pipes bead continuously. The temperature remains warm, but the air grows close and mineral-tasting.

Still farther down are the hanging districts, where buildings are suspended from the undersides of spans. Their streets are narrow grated galleries with foliage visible through the floors. Here, bright lamps burn throughout the day, powered by the valley’s water descents, waste heat, and wind turbines mounted on the upper crowns. The lamps attract dense clouds of insects, which in turn feed bats and nesting swallows. Guano is collected from screens and carried to the orchards above.

No one agrees on the lowest level. Freight workers speak of a mesh floor spanning the original abyss. Pump crews insist there are more towers below it. Surveyors have charts, but the charts contain blanks marked as inaccessible rather than unknown. Warm updrafts rise from these depths, smelling of wet iron and vegetation. Occasionally they carry a scattering of pale leaves from a plant not grown in any mapped garden.

This is considered curious, but not alarming.

The valley’s greatest changes are measured in stains, lean, and shade. A tower bends a few centimeters in a decade. A fig root opens a joint over three generations. A reservoir wall develops a green tide mark, and fifty years later that mark is used to set the height of a new footbridge. Repair is continuous, but urgency is rare. The settlement has survived by allowing stresses to travel, by yielding in many small places rather than resisting in one.

At sunset, heat rises from the pale artificial mountains. The upper windmills begin turning more steadily, their long vanes flashing in orange light. Birds cross between towers in vast numbers. Water released from daytime industrial use enters the garden channels, and the lower terraces fill with the soft rush of evening irrigation.

Lights appear gradually: factory lamps first, then homes, tram stations, shrine niches, stair rails, and the green-white glow of algae tanks. They trace the construction’s true shape more clearly than daylight does. One sees that the apparent valley is a lattice, that each mountain is a crowded vertical city, and that every forested slope hangs over another slope beneath it.

In darkness the scale becomes difficult to judge. Near lamps illuminate leaves, cables, faces, wet brick, and moth wings with intimate clarity. Beyond them, thousands of other lights recede through warm haze, climbing and descending until they resemble constellations caught in foliage.

The engines continue. The waterways descend. The gardens breathe moisture into the night.

Nothing here is natural, but nearly everything is alive.
