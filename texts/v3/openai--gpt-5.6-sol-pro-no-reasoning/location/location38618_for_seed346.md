The city occupies a shallow basin no larger than an old cathedral precinct. From one rim, if the streets agree to remain straight, the opposite rim can be seen: a low embankment of black stone, braced with silver pylons and furred by frost. Yet there are thousands of addresses inside. The city has achieved density not by building upward, exactly, but by placing rooms beside one another in directions unavailable to the surrounding plain.

It is called the Near Quarter, though there is no Far Quarter.

Outside its boundary lies a cold, level waste of recently consolidated ground. The rock is young and undecided. In some places it resembles basalt; in others it is fine gray powder pressed into temporary slabs. Crystals still migrate through it, choosing lattices. Pebbles give off small clicks as they finish becoming solid. There are no mature valleys, no rounded stones, no soil horizons—only shallow wrinkles where intermittent meltwater has begun rehearsing the idea of drainage.

The city stands low in this unfinished landscape, sunk slightly below the horizon. Its oldest foundations may be three centuries old or may have been installed next winter. Records differ, and the disagreement is structural rather than historical.

Along the perimeter, the wall is scarcely higher than a person. It consists of densely jointed plates, each dull as lead and thin as paper. Condensation collects between them. Once every few days, when the air warms almost to freezing, clear water squeezes from the seams and runs through gutters into narrow cisterns beneath the streets. Rain is uncommon and delicate: brief vertical threads that evaporate from the ground upward, leaving the roofs wet before the clouds have formed.

Water is precious but not scarce in the usual sense. It appears in pockets. A stairwell may contain a knee-deep pool for a month, its surface perfectly still despite footsteps nearby; then the water will be discovered folded into the plumbing of a building across the lane. Pipes are labeled not by destination but by probability. Blue means *usually potable*. White means *has arrived from somewhere*. Black means *do not listen through this pipe*.

The visible city is a tight construction of narrow facades, ducts, covered passages, cooling fins, balconies, ladders, cables, and small courtyards. Materials recur without repetition: smoked glass, ceramic darker than the sky, pallid metal with a texture like bone, poured stone containing minute moving lights. Buildings nearly touch. In some alleys a person must turn sideways, and even then must negotiate with protruding doorframes. Windows overlap other windows. A room on the second floor may open into the basement of its neighbor. Roofs pass through one another without damage, producing interior wedges where frost forms in geometrically impossible patterns.

Nothing is ruined. Nothing is entirely complete.

Construction proceeds continuously, but so slowly that it resembles mineral growth. Autonomous tools—flat, many-legged things the size of coins—move in clusters over unfinished surfaces. They deposit one transparent layer each winter. Their work is inaudible except at very close range, where it can be heard as a soft counting. Scaffolds stand for generations. A bridge spanning a lane of less than two meters has been under assembly for eighty-one years, although pedestrians have used it since before the first support was installed.

The engineers say the city is not being built in time order.

They have learned to design accordingly. Every structure includes cavities for components that have not yet been invented and braces for loads that have already occurred. Doors are hung before their walls arrive. Foundations are excavated beneath occupied rooms without disturbing the floor, because the floor is temporarily assigned to another version of gravity. Building codes specify acceptable contradictions. A dwelling may contain no more than two mutually exclusive kitchens. No public stair may terminate on the same landing more than three times. Reflections must be registered with the municipal surveyor if they persist after the object has moved.

The rules are printed on metal plaques at every intersection. The plaques change rarely, usually during condensation.

Despite its size, the Near Quarter cannot be crossed quickly. Distance accumulates here in dense layers. A lane twenty paces long can take an afternoon if approached from the east, while the same lane, walked westward, may end before it begins. Residents navigate by sequence rather than position: pass the warm wall, descend until the bells stop being visible, turn at the smell of iron, ignore the first courtyard containing your own footprints.

Maps are grown rather than drawn. They are kept in shallow dishes under civic lamps, where branching films of engineered frost reproduce the current arrangement of streets. Each map remains accurate for several hours. When it ceases to correspond to the city, it melts into drinking water.

Daylight is ordinary, which makes the rest of the place less so. The sky is a pale, cold blue. Clouds cross at believable speeds. Shadows generally fall away from the sun. Under that honest illumination, the city’s impossibilities look practical and almost modest: a balcony with three undersides; a tower shorter than the doorway at its base; a row of apartments visible edge-on as a dark line no thicker than thread. At noon, metal roofs give back a clean white glare. At dusk, windows illuminate one by one, though some glow with mornings from other climates.

The air smells of frost, wet stone, ozone, and occasionally salt.

Vegetation survives in small, deliberate concessions. Gray moss grows around condensation outlets, flattened close to the masonry. In sheltered gutters there are threads of black reed, jointed like antennae, which uncurl only when no one is watching. Courtyards may hold one or two low shrubs enclosed in glass. Their leaves are transparent and contain slow green sparks. They are watered by capillary tubes and pruned according to diagrams that account for branches appearing in adjacent rooms.

Beyond the wall, pale lichens form isolated circles on the raw stone. They are the first living things to test the new ground. Each circle expands by less than a finger’s width in a year. Where meltwater lingers in shallow depressions, there are films of rust-colored microbes beneath skins of ice. No grassland waits beneath the cold. Ecology here is tentative, local, almost architectural.

Animals are rare enough to be noticed individually. A handful of white, six-winged moths shelter in the heat exchangers. They do not fly so much as change which surface they occupy. Small blind crustaceans inhabit certain cisterns, feeding on mineral deposits and the fine dust of forgotten data. Their shells bear patterns identical to street plans, though never the current one.

There is also a bird.

Residents disagree about its species. It is seen perhaps twice a year, perched on aerials or on the rim wall, a compact dark body against the snow-colored plain. Its call has never been heard directly. Instead, sometime after it departs, one person somewhere in the Quarter remembers a note that no one else recalls hearing. For several days that person may whistle it unconsciously. Doors nearby then open onto outdoor places.

For all its machinery, the city is quiet. Engines have no exhaust and little vibration. Freight moves through buried channels as shifts in local mass. Elevators announce themselves by a pressure in the teeth. The loudest common sounds are boots on metal grating, meltwater ticking into reservoirs, fabric brushing walls in narrow passages, and the occasional brittle report of young rock settling outside the perimeter.

Conversation is subdued. People do not shout directions, because the city may interpret them as instructions.

At night, the quiet deepens rather than becoming absolute. One can hear faint processes within the walls: molecular assemblers repairing stress fractures; clocks negotiating a common second; pipes carrying water past rooms that have not been constructed; the almost subsonic flexing of space held in an urban shape by pylons around the basin. From far below comes a periodic sound like ice cracking on a vast lake. Municipal instruments insist there is no cavity beneath the city. The instruments also insist the sound is moving upward at one millimeter per century.

The central square is the largest open place, though “open” is relative. It is crowded with columns, kiosks, ventilation trees, public condensers, benches, memorial slabs, and slow-moving civic machines. Above it hangs a volume of absent architecture called the Vacancy. Snow falls into this volume and disappears at its boundary. Birds—when there were more birds—refused to cross beneath it.

The Vacancy is not empty. Empty things can be measured.

Its outline resembles a building removed so completely that the possibility of having occupied its space was removed as well. Streets bend around it. Nearby residents dream of corridors they cannot describe upon waking. The city’s advanced systems were originally built to stabilize the phenomenon, or perhaps the phenomenon was installed to stabilize the systems. No surviving document makes the distinction.

Every twelve years, technicians enter the square wearing suits lined with nested horizons. They inspect the perimeter and add one more railing. The railings have accumulated into a dense metal thicket, each exactly parallel to the boundary and none parallel to the others.

Change in the Near Quarter is patient. Frost thickens. A facade gains a centimeter. A lane develops an additional afternoon. Water finds a new low point and remains there for decades. The raw plain outside begins, almost imperceptibly, to erode: a hairline rill deepens beside the western wall; sharp rubble loses its first edges; a basin catches three successive seasons of thaw and becomes eligible for a name.

Meanwhile, the city grows inward.

New houses appear between adjoining walls where no gap was visible. Population remains stable, but the number of occupied rooms increases. Each year the census discovers several residents who have apparently always lived there. They possess documents, neighbors, childhood memories, and municipal debts. They are welcomed without ceremony. Space is found for them because space, here, is not a quantity but a habit.

From the plain at evening, the Near Quarter seems almost comprehensible: a compact dark settlement in a shallow hollow, silvered with condensation, a few warm windows burning under a clear cold sky. Its towers are low. Its wall is modest. No traffic roars, no smoke rises, no forest obscures its shape. A traveler might estimate that crossing it would take ten minutes.

Then one notices that the windows do not all face outward.

Some look deeper into the city. Some look down on the traveler from behind. One, high in a narrow facade that cannot contain a room, shows the same plain after long erosion: channels cut deep into mature stone, the basin widened, the wall gone, sparse black vegetation moving in a warmer rain.

In that window the city is still present.

It has become smaller than a hand and infinitely more crowded, held beneath glass on a table in a room whose walls have only just begun to form.
