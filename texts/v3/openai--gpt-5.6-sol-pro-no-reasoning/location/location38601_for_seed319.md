The city occupies a high basin whose floor has been worn nearly level by water that no longer gathers deeply enough to make a lake. From the surrounding rim it appears broad and unfinished: districts of dark masonry, rust-colored machinery, and pale mineral ground separated by immense vacant spaces. Nothing crowds. Streets run for kilometers between isolated blocks. Factories stand apart like forts, connected by elevated pipes and rail lines. Beyond them, the basin continues into haze, bounded by long escarpments whose profiles have been softened over ages into terraces, gutters, and sloping shelves.

It is warm here despite the altitude. The air presses lightly on the lungs and carries a perpetual trace of metal, rain, and heated stone. Moisture is everywhere without becoming abundance. It beads on handrails before dawn, sweats from shaded walls at noon, and emerges from seams in the cliffs in thin, patient threads. Shallow channels border most roads. Their water is amber or milky with mineral sediment, moving too slowly to ripple unless a machine passes nearby.

There are no young landforms. Every exposed surface seems to have reached agreement with weather. The cliffs do not look freshly broken; they are rounded, stained, and fluted, their harder layers projecting as long brows above recessed bands. Rain has carved the city’s stonework in the same manner. Cornices sag into smooth lips. Steps have shallow bowls where generations of feet and dripping roofs have met. Concrete pylons are striped black, ocher, and green-gray by seepage. Even steel has lost the sharp character of manufacture, its rust arranged in stable scales that no longer appear to spread.

The basin’s water travels through an ancient drainage system beneath the city. Open canals feed grated shafts, which descend into cisterns and pressure tunnels cut through the bedrock. Pumps raise part of the flow to cooling towers and industrial reservoirs on the upper terraces. The remainder is allowed to wander through buried galleries before returning at the basin’s lowest edge, where it forms a chain of reedless marshes.

The marshes are wide but austere. Sheets of ankle-deep water lie between ribs of salt and black mud. Vegetation occurs in isolated clumps: stiff gray rushes, waxy ground plants, and wiry shrubs rooted wherever mineral crust has fractured. A single shrub may command fifty meters of empty shore. After rain, small copper-colored films spread across the pools. In dry intervals they contract into iridescent seams.

The city seems overbuilt for its population. Vast plazas separate civic halls from warehouses. Housing rises in narrow towers, but many windows are sealed with ceramic tile or corrugated metal. Covered walkways cross empty ground at the second and third stories, connecting buildings whose lower doors have long since become inconvenient because the basin floor shifts by a few centimeters each century. The streets preserve older alignments beneath newer ones: stone gutters under concrete culverts, tram rails beneath rubberized paving, brick conduits incorporated into steel pumping mains.

Construction here is never demolished if it can instead be absorbed. A failed mill becomes the foundation of a condenser station. An abandoned reservoir receives a roof and becomes a market. Old smokestacks serve as radio masts, storm drains, or anchor points for suspended pipes. Whole neighborhoods are organized around machinery whose original purpose has been forgotten but whose continued operation is considered essential.

This is partly because of the sound.

It is immense and nearly continuous. It comes less as a collection of noises than as a property of the basin, like heat or gravity. Deep turbines turn beneath the streets. Pumping stations beat at intervals too slow to be mistaken for engines and too regular to be natural. Cooling fans in the western works generate a broad roar that moves through the city with the wind. Suspended pipelines resonate in several pitches, and the cliffs return each pitch altered—lower, stretched, and multiplied.

Conversation outdoors is conducted close to the ear or through gestures. Buildings have double walls and heavy felt curtains. Public rooms display lights that brighten and dim with announcements. Factory shifts are signaled by changes in vibration rather than sirens: three long pulses through the pavement for morning, two for evening, one continuous tremor when the lower tunnels must be cleared.

No one agrees on whether the city produces the noise or merely gives it form. The oldest records describe the basin as loud before the first foundations were laid. Early surveyors wrote of a subterranean thunder audible through bedrolls. The city may have been built here not despite that resonance, but to harness it. Its engines are bolted directly to particular strata. Turbine chambers align with natural caverns. Some machines have no visible drive shaft or fuel intake; they consist only of enormous iron frames pressed against wet rock, humming as if the mountain turns them from within.

For all the violence of the sound, very little happens quickly.

The turbines rotate at a stately pace. Freight trains cross the basin no faster than a walking person, pulling long chains of ore carts and water tanks. Crane arms take hours to swing from one side of a yard to the other. Gates in the flood channels open over days, controlled by counterweights that descend almost imperceptibly into wells. Industrial processes are measured in seasons: vats fill during the rains, settle through the warm dry months, and are emptied when the mineral layers have separated.

Smoke, where it exists, rises in slow columns and spreads beneath a ceiling of humid air. More often the factories release steam. It hangs over the roofs as a luminous gray veil, condensing on cables and dripping steadily into the road channels. At certain hours the whole eastern district appears to be raining upward and downward at once—steam ascending from vents, warm droplets falling from elevated pipes, low cloud pressing against the upper towers.

Vegetation survives mainly in neglected joins. Pale moss grows inside culverts but nowhere else. Threadlike vines hang from condensation towers, too sparse to conceal the riveted shells beneath them. Small trees occur only where water mains have leaked for decades. Their trunks are thin, their crowns flat and widely spaced, and their leaves have a dark lacquered surface that sheds mineral rain.

Animals are uncommon enough to be individually noticed. Long-winged birds sometimes circle above the exhaust thermals without calling. Mud-colored lizards live in the warm seams of retaining walls. In the lower marshes, something occasionally moves beneath the opaque water, producing a straight wake that may continue for several hundred meters before vanishing. There are almost no insects in the open air. The few that emerge after rain are large, slow, and drawn not to lamps but to vibrating metal.

Light is ordinary here, which makes the rest of the place stranger. Days are neither permanently dim nor theatrically bright. Sunlight falls warmly across pale stone and standing water. Clouds cast recognizable shadows. Rust is rust-colored; wet concrete darkens; steam catches gold near evening. Yet the city’s distances distort under humid air. Towers that appear an hour away may require a full day to reach, while a far escarpment sometimes seems close enough to show individual drainage scars.

At night, illumination is practical and incomplete. Sodium lamps mark canals and loading platforms. White arcs burn above the rail junctions. Most of the basin remains dark, though its machinery continues. Red maintenance lights climb isolated stacks in vertical rows, blinking so slowly that a visitor may not realize they blink at all. Reflections repeat them in roadside water, making downward towers beneath the ground.

There are rules peculiar to the place.

No tunnel is sealed completely; every wall must include a narrow water passage. No machine may be synchronized perfectly with another, because matched rhythms are believed to wake destructive harmonics in the basin. Streets crossing the oldest fault lines are paved with loose plates rather than poured slabs. Residents do not build bedrooms directly above pipes, even disused ones. During the warmest weeks, when condensation is heaviest, people tie ceramic chimes to exterior rails—not to make music, but to reveal changes in the background vibration. If a chime falls silent while the basin still roars, the adjoining building is evacuated.

The city’s engineers speak of erosion as a partner rather than an enemy. Water is given sacrificial routes through structures. Foundations contain layers meant to dissolve in sequence. Canals are allowed to wander within broad stone beds. Buildings are designed to lean by degrees and then be braced into their new posture. Maps include not only present streets but their anticipated positions centuries from now.

Thus the city changes, but with such patience that change resembles permanence. Mineral curtains thicken beneath aqueducts. Marshes migrate from one abandoned yard to the next. A factory wall becomes a cliff; a cliff is pierced into a machine hall. The settlement does not conquer the basin, nor decay into it. City and land wear one another into compatible shapes.

From the southern rim, especially after rain, the entire region seems half submerged—not in water, but in sound. Pipes gleam across the open ground. Scattered towers rise through steam. The shallow marshes hold pieces of the sky. Every surface drips, resonates, settles, and endures. Nothing hurries. The roar goes on.
