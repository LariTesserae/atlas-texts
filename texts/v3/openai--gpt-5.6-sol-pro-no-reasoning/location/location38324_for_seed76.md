The high country begins above the clouds and does not end.

It is a continent lifted into thin, brilliant air: an immense tableland of pale stone, salt, and old volcanic glass, weathered until every horizon has the calm profile of something finished. Ranges rise from it only gradually. Their summits are broad-backed rather than sharp, worn through ages of wind into terraces and shallow bowls. Cliffs occur at the margins, but even these have lost their violence. They stand in long, fluted walls, shedding occasional scales of rock into aprons already ancient.

There are no rivers. No lakes occupy the basins, and no snow lies permanently on the heights. Water is almost absent from the system—not merely scarce, but ecologically unimportant. The air carries only traces of vapor. Clouds form reluctantly and pass as thin white veils, often evaporating before their shadows have crossed the ground.

Yet the plateau is green.

Its vegetation does not drink. It feeds on light, stone, and the slow chemistry of the atmosphere.

Across the open country grow widely separated colonies of mineral foliage: low fans, branching mats, and tall, hollow fronds whose surfaces are the green-black of oxidized copper. Their tissues contain no sap. Inside them, fine lattices of silica hold reactive gases drawn from the wind. By day, sunlight drives those gases through a chain of transformations; by night, the plants stiffen and release the stored products into their roots. Their growth is slow at the center and surprisingly quick along the edges, so each colony advances as a loose ring while its interior becomes brittle, collapses, and returns to powder.

From a distance these rings appear lush, like dark islands scattered over a bright sea. Up close, there is ample bare ground between every stem. The abundance is in their vigor and complexity, not their coverage. A single grove may contain a hundred interwoven forms, while the next grove lies a day’s journey away.

The largest growths occupy old erosion basins where the wind has sorted the ground into layers of salt, pumice, and metallic dust. Here stand the sailwoods. Their trunks are porous stone sheathed in living film, and their crowns consist of broad vertical vanes turned edgewise to the noon sun. At dawn and evening the vanes rotate—not on joints, but by swelling one crystalline layer while shrinking another. Whole forests slowly change orientation together. The motion is nearly imperceptible unless watched against the horizon.

They make no rustling sound. Their leaves are too rigid and the air too thin.

Instead, when the wind strengthens, the groves produce a faint chord. Each hollow trunk favors a different frequency. The lowest notes are felt through the soles more than heard; higher ones drift in and out at the edge of perception. Because the colonies are far apart, the plateau is mostly silent, punctuated by distant tones whose sources may lie beyond sight.

Brightness rules the place. The sky is a deep, cold blue overhead and nearly white at the horizons. Sunlight strikes the pale flats and returns upward, so shadows are sharply drawn but never entirely dark. Beneath overhangs, mineral crusts glow with reflected radiance. The living surfaces protect themselves with mirrored scales, and when a slope turns toward the sun, thousands of fronds flash at once. These signals pass from ridge to ridge like weather.

The temperature remains cool despite the glare. Thin air loses heat quickly, and every shadow has a cutting edge. During the long afternoon the exposed stone warms enough to loosen the upper layers of salt. After sunset the temperature falls at once. The vegetation contracts with tiny, dry clicks, and frostlike films of atmospheric crystals form—not ice, but pale deposits of ammonia salts and carbon compounds. By sunrise they have vanished back into the air or been absorbed by the root lattices.

Wind is the plateau’s principal weather and its chief sculptor. It comes in broad, orderly tides governed by the heating of slopes thousands of kilometers apart. Morning air descends from the central uplands; evening air returns from the continental rim. Between these tides there may be hours of perfect stillness.

In storm seasons, the sky fills with mineral dust so fine that it behaves like smoke. These storms do not roar. The sparse atmosphere cannot carry a full terrestrial howl. They approach as a dimming of the far plateaus and a low pressure in the body. Then the world turns amber. Dust streams inches above the ground, pouring around trunks and over saddles, exposing one root system while burying another. Static light crawls through the groves in violet filaments. Sailwoods close their crowns and lean fractionally into the flow.

Erosion is everywhere, but nothing here looks newly broken. The landscape has long since reached terms with its winds. Stones are faceted and seated firmly in hollows of their own making. Ridges are armored with dark crust. Loose fragments arrange themselves in patient stripes aligned with the prevailing currents. Even during a storm, change is distributed across such immensity that no single place seems disturbed. A basin may migrate northward over ten thousand years. A cliff may retreat the length of a hand over the lifetime of a species.

The plateau has its own currents of fertility. Gases exhaled by night-growing mats collect in depressions and creep downslope, invisible except where they alter the color of the ground. Other colonies feed on these low rivers of chemistry. Thus valleys exist without watercourses: branching networks of faint green stain, silver crust, and black vegetation tracing where dense gases repeatedly flow.

At some basin floors, the air itself behaves almost like a liquid. It pools, stratifies, and forms slow internal waves. Tall fronds planted at the edges bend when such a wave passes, though the air above remains still. Seeds—thin mineral flakes with living rims—float upon these invisible surfaces. They gather along chemical shorelines and germinate in curving bands.

Animal life is rare, and much of it might be mistaken for moving vegetation.

Small glass-backed grazers cross the flats on six or eight hair-fine legs. They visit the outer edges of colonies, shaving off fresh films with ceramic mouthparts, then spend days motionless while light restores their internal chemistry. Their bodies are nearly transparent except for a branching green organ spread beneath the shell. When threatened, they flatten against the ground and become another shard among millions.

Higher in the sailwoods live pale kite-creatures with no true wings. They climb the rigid fronds during the calm hours and release themselves when the evening wind begins. Gas-filled chambers open along their bodies, carrying them from grove to grove. Because vegetation islands are so widely spaced, many disappear into the bright distance. A successful landing may occur hundreds of kilometers away. Their journeys distribute spores, parasites, and the minute dormant forms of other species caught in the grooves of their skins.

There are predators, but few. Long-bodied hunters wait in gas channels where grazers migrate. They sense pressure changes through combs along their flanks and strike only once every several weeks. Their metabolism is so measured that a hunting animal may remain in the same posture long enough for dust to form streamlines around it. Nothing chases here. Pursuit is too expensive in the thin air. Life relies on waiting, drifting, or being overlooked.

Most activity happens on scales that conceal it. A mirrored heath may seem perfectly still, though each of its plates is turning by a fraction of a degree. A bare salt flat may contain billions of dormant membranes beneath its crust, awakening briefly when a particular gas blows down from the uplands. A hillside may change from silver to green over the course of an hour as organisms emerge from microscopic pores to harvest morning light, then withdraw before noon.

From the highest swells of land, the immensity becomes difficult to understand. Ridge follows basin, basin follows ridge, each clear in the dry air, until distance erases contrast rather than detail. Dark living archipelagos stipple the pale continent. Cloud shadows travel over them without producing rain. Farther away, atmospheric refraction lifts plateaus above the horizon, so they appear as suspended countries: green lines and white cliffs floating in brightness.

There are no paths. Nothing has ever cut or built one. Repeated migrations leave only subtle alignments of polished stones, erased by the next dust tide. There are no ruins, markers, walls, or signs of a shaping intelligence. The sailwoods’ regular spacing can look intentional, as can the concentric growth rings of the basin colonies, but these are agreements between wind, chemistry, and light—not designs.

The world’s primary law is exposure. Almost everything is visible, yet distances make visibility a kind of concealment. A black grove on the horizon may be ten kilometers across. A threadlike moving speck may be a herd whose individuals stand taller than a person. Brightness removes intimacy. It reveals surfaces while flattening scale.

Its second law is economy. Matter circulates without liquid to gather and carry it. Dust serves as blood; wind serves as river; sunlight provides the great daily pulse. Life must catch what passes, hold it briefly, and surrender it in another form. Nothing decays into damp soil. Dead growths delaminate into flakes, fibers, salts, and reactive powders. These blow onward to nourish distant colonies, sometimes on the other side of the continent.

At night, the place becomes colder but not truly dark. The ground releases stored light in a low green afterglow. Living colonies mark the land like dim constellations, each isolated by miles of black mineral plain. Above them the stars are hard and numerous, unsoftened by moisture. Meteors burn briefly in the thin upper air, often reaching the plateau as warm stones.

Then comes the deepest silence. Winds settle. Gas currents sink into the basins. The few animals fold themselves beneath ledges or anchor to the sailwood crowns. Across a land large enough to generate its own weather, countless chemical exchanges continue without a sound.

Before dawn, the forests begin to turn.

A dry clicking passes slowly through the groves. Fronds align toward the hidden sun. Mirrored scales open. Dark mats spread their reactive surfaces over cold stone. One by one, the high ridges ignite in white light, and the brightness descends into the basins.

For a few minutes every living thing faces east.
