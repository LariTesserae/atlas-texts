The city lies one hundred meters below the old ground, inside a chamber no larger than a cathedral close.

It was not excavated. The surrounding earth was persuaded apart.

Walls of young black stone rise almost vertically around the settlement, still sharp where they fractured, their faces glossy with pressure and threaded by pale mineral seams. There are few ledges and no deep channels yet. Water has only begun its work. It beads on the rock, gathers along new cracks, and descends in wavering silver lines. Here and there a whole wall seems to perspire.

The chamber is conspicuously empty. Its buildings occupy only a narrow band around the perimeter, leaving the center as a broad, dim court of shallow water. A person standing at one edge can see nearly everything: the distant doors, the bridges, the thin towers, the black mirror between. Yet the vacant distances make the place seem larger than it is. Nothing crowds the eye. Every pillar stands alone. Every light has darkness around it.

The central water is seldom deeper than an ankle, though its depth is unreliable. Its floor is made of fitted hexagonal plates, dark ceramic with a faint metallic grain. Rain falls upward from them.

At intervals the plates chill, and moisture condenses in the warm chamber air. Droplets form close to the floor, tremble, then stream upward in soft vertical showers. They rise toward a porous crown suspended beneath the ceiling, where the water vanishes into machinery that has no obvious housings, pipes, or moving parts. Later it returns through the stone walls as seepage. Thus the city maintains its weather: floor to ceiling, ceiling to rock, rock to floor.

The warm air smells of wet basalt, leaf mold, and the clean mineral sharpness that follows lightning. Condensation settles constantly on skin and fabric. Even when no upward rain is running, the atmosphere is thick enough to make distant architecture blur at its edges.

Most buildings are grown from a pale structural substance resembling bone porcelain. It curves without joints around doorways and under bridges, but breaks into precise facets at roofs and corners. Narrow houses are set into the chamber wall in three ascending tiers. Their fronts are only a few meters wide. Behind them, rooms extend into volumes folded through dimensions the surrounding geology does not possess. A doorway may open onto a corridor longer than the entire cavern. Windows overlooking the central court sometimes show other windows from impossible angles.

From outside, however, the city remains small.

No streets cross the court. Instead, isolated stepping platforms rise from the water and move slowly from place to place, following routes that are not marked. They recognize intention rather than passengers: when someone wishes strongly enough to reach a particular door, a platform begins drifting toward them. Hesitation causes it to stop. Contradictory desires can leave several platforms stranded in the open water for hours.

The city was constructed for a population that is absent.

Doors dilate at a passerby’s approach. Empty lifts travel between the tiers. Warm rooms brighten in anticipation and dim again when no one enters. Workshops continue manufacturing immaculate, inexplicable objects—rings without openings, transparent sheets that cast solid shadows, vessels colder inside than outside—and disassemble them before the next cycle. The infrastructure behaves not like an abandoned mechanism but like a host waiting with inexhaustible patience.

Its activity makes the chamber loud.

Water drums upward against the ceiling crown. Hidden pumps—or whatever occupies the functional role of pumps—send bass pulses through the rock. Bridges occasionally retension themselves with a report like a struck bronze gong. Beneath the court, ceramic plates tick, snap, and grind as the city corrects its foundations against geological pressure. Every few minutes a resonant tone passes around the chamber, door to door, too regular to be thunder and too rich to be an alarm. Moss shivers when it sounds.

There are no animals. No insects hover above the water, no worms turn the saturated soil, no birds or bats occupy the dark height. Nothing has gnawed the pale railings or nested in the dry recesses. The absence is especially noticeable because the place otherwise seems capable of sustaining life.

Plants have come, though perhaps they were invited.

Moss forms thick green-black cushions where the walls weep. Ferns grow from gutters and cracks, uncurling translucent fronds beneath the dim amber guide lights. Mats of liverwort spread over abandoned thresholds. Around the lower buildings stand small groves of thin, smooth-barked trees whose roots do not penetrate the floor; instead, each root divides into hairlike filaments and floats just above it, drinking condensation directly from the air.

Their foliage is sparse enough not to fill the cavern. Long oval leaves cluster at branch tips, leaving generous darkness between crowns. When the upward rain begins, the trees turn their leaves edge-on, and the droplets slip past them toward the ceiling. During the return seepage they flatten their leaves to catch the falling threads from the rock.

Without grazers, pollinators, or decomposers, the vegetation follows peculiar closed cycles. Flowers open only beside active electrical fields. Pollen crawls along conductive veins in the architecture. Dead leaves are collected by the moving platforms and lowered into warm slots in the floor, where they are reduced to water, salts, and a little fragrant vapor. Nothing rots in the ordinary way. There is no buzzing, scurrying underside to the greenery. The plants stand in profound biological solitude.

Light comes mainly from narrow amber lines embedded in steps and lintels. They never illuminate whole surfaces. They indicate edges, depths, and permitted directions, turning the city into a diagram suspended in darkness. The water doubles these marks until there seem to be two settlements: one around the walls and another descending beneath the court.

The ceiling is almost invisible. When the machinery above receives an upward shower, brief blue glimmers travel through its porous mass, revealing something like an inverted root system—or the neural tissue of an enormous mineral brain. The glimmers vanish before the eye can assemble them into a stable form.

There is one rule the city enforces absolutely: nothing may remain in the center when the low tone sounds three times.

After the first tone, the stepping platforms turn toward shore. After the second, every door closes and the trees fold their leaves. The water across the court becomes perfectly still despite the dripping walls. At the third, distance changes.

The central court does not expand visibly. Its opposite edge remains where it was, perhaps forty meters away. But anything left on the water must cross an immeasurable interval to return. A dropped tool may be seen lying only a few steps from shore, clear in every detail, while retrieval instruments extend kilometers of cable without reaching it. Reflections show stars unknown to the surface world. Sometimes the dark water reflects no ceiling at all.

The interval lasts less than a minute. Then the court becomes shallow and local again, and stranded objects tap gently against the nearest platform as if they had never been gone.

No one knows whether this is the purpose of the city or merely a side effect of how its empty interior is made. The buildings appear to brace themselves during each occurrence. Mineral seams flare faintly in the fresh-cut walls. New cracks open with sharp, gunshot sounds, and warm groundwater presses immediately into them. The cavern is geologically young; every cycle alters it. Moss colonizes fractures that did not exist the day before. Stairs shift by the width of a fingernail. Bridges wake and adjust their spans.

The place is therefore finished but never settled.

Its precise construction stands inside raw stone. Its gardens are lush but incomplete. Its weather circulates without a sky, and its city services continue without citizens. Warm water is everywhere—underfoot, in the air, inside the walls—yet the great court preserves an impression of vacancy, as though emptiness itself were the principal resource being gathered here.

Above, far beyond the black ceiling and its roots of light, ordinary rain may be falling on the surface.

None of it reaches this place.

This place makes its own.
