Far below the weather, beneath a cold sea broad enough to erase the idea of shore, an old plain lies under black water.

It is not a level plain in the clean sense. The seabed has been worked for ages by pressure, currents, glacial sediment, and slow collapses until every sharp feature has been worn down. Ridges survive only as long backs of stone. Valleys are shallow troughs filled with clay. Ancient escarpments have slumped into broad inclines over which the water passes without interruption. Nothing rises high, yet the region is not empty. It extends outward in low folds, shelves, channels, and fields of rubble until darkness prevents any greater view.

The bottom is packed almost everywhere. There is scarcely a patch of exposed bedrock or unmarked mud. Stones lie fitted against stones, their edges rounded, their hollows filled by smaller gravel, shell fragments, and compacted silt. In some places the sediment is crowded with pale nodules, each grown around the remains of something older. Elsewhere, mats of tube worms and crusts of cold-water sponge occupy every firm surface. They are not lush. Their colors are ash, rust, bone, and weak yellow. What makes the place dense is not abundance in the usual fertile sense, but accumulation: layer upon layer of mineral deposit, dead shell, burrow, pebble, collapsed burrow, and new deposit. The sea has had a very long time to fill every vacancy.

The water is near freezing. It carries the clean, metallic cold of the deep ocean, a cold that penetrates rather than bites. There is no seasonal warmth here and little distinction between day and night. Above, thousands of tons of dark water press downward. Far overhead, storms may travel and ice may form or break, but their light does not reach this depth. At most, a dim blue-gray dilution sometimes appears in the upper water, too weak to cast a shadow. Most of the landscape is revealed only in fragments by living sparks.

Yet the water is violently active.

A deep current comes across the plain in irregular pulses, forced between distant banks and accelerated through the old channels. It rolls close to the bottom, dragging curtains of silt behind it. Where two flows meet, they twist upward into immense, slow vortices. The eye would call them slow; anything caught inside them would not. Gravel ticks and hops. Dead shells tumble until they lodge in cracks. Whole fields of mud alternately vanish beneath suspended sediment and emerge again scoured smooth.

The turbulence never produces true disorder for long. It is ancient turbulence, confined to paths worked out over geological time. The same hollows collect the same drifting matter. The same saddles split the current. The same stone backs bear the force of the flow and have been polished into dark, blunt forms. Change is constant, but its possibilities are old.

Sound is scarce. The current itself is nearly silent at this depth: a pressure felt along the body, a sustained movement without a voice. Close to the bottom there are faint noises—the dry clicking of gravel underwater, the scrape of one rounded rock against another, the minute patter of falling particles. Occasionally a distant collapse passes through the plain as a low concussion. It arrives through stone before it arrives through water, shaking loose little clouds from the seabed. Then quiet closes over it.

Life exists in isolated economies.

Low films of bacteria stain certain stones brown and cream. Sparse thickets of branching sponge grow where the current reliably brings food, all leaning in the same direction. Their branches are stiff, irregular, and brittle-looking, more like mineral frost than vegetation. A few translucent anemones open in sheltered lee pockets. They may remain contracted for weeks, no more conspicuous than lumps of gristle, before spreading their tentacles into a favorable stream.

There are fish, but not many. One may appear at the edge of visibility as a long dark body held motionless against the current. It moves only when necessary, spending no energy on display. Blind crustaceans probe among the packed stones. Thin starfish cross the mud so slowly that their trails are erased before they seem to have advanced. From time to time, a larger animal passes overhead: a sharklike silhouette, a pale ray, something without a clear outline. Its wake briefly rearranges the suspended silt, and then it is gone.

Points of blue-green light flare when the current disturbs small drifting organisms. These flashes do not illuminate the sea so much as mark its motion. A vortex becomes visible as a scattered spiral. The wake behind a passing body writes itself in sparks. A stone dislodged from a ridge rolls downhill through brief constellations. Each light dies almost at once, leaving the darkness seemingly denser for having been interrupted.

No built thing stands here. There are no walls, roads, stakes, vessels, or ruins. Even objects that might resemble artifacts resolve, on approach, into natural forms: a row of split shells set by the current, a flaked slab of shale, a hollow stone with an edge sharp enough to cut flesh. Such edges are the nearest the landscape comes to tools. If a hand could work here, it could select hammerstones from the gravel, pry shell from stone, sharpen bone against basalt. The place offers the materials of the earliest craft but no evidence that anyone has claimed them. Pressure, cold, and darkness keep all making brief.

The world here obeys simple rules. Anything light is carried. Anything soft is buried. Anything raised is worn down. Anything hollow fills.

To persist, a living thing must anchor itself, enter the sediment, or move only when movement pays for itself. Color has little use except as chemistry. Heat is lost immediately. Waste becomes food somewhere downstream. Death is seldom dramatic; a body settles, is opened by small mouths, and joins the densely written bottom.

Across the region, the old plain divides into several distinct grounds. There are stone fields where the current runs hardest, pale mud basins where it weakens, and narrow ravines crowded with shells. There are slopes carpeted in manganese-dark pebbles and broad fans of silt spread beneath unseen clefts. At the center lies a depression deeper than the surrounding seabed. Water pours into it from several channels, collides, and rises in a permanent column of turbulence. Fine sediment revolves there without settling. Fragments of shell and sponge circle for months, perhaps years, ground smaller on every passage.

Around this restless hollow, the bottom has reached a kind of final form. It cannot become much lower without becoming flat; it cannot become empty because the currents continually deliver material; it cannot become still because the shape of the land concentrates the moving sea. It is both exhausted and ceaseless.

The landscape does not transform into anything new. It rearranges itself with tremendous energy inside boundaries established before most living lineages began. Every moment is turbulent. Every age looks much like the last.

In the darkness, that is what stability means.
