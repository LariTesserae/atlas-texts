The town occupies a shallow saddle high in a range of rounded, weather-worn mountains. It is not large: from either gate, the opposite wall can be seen beyond the roofs, and a person walking without haste can cross it before the light changes appreciably. Yet nearly every piece of the saddle has been worked. Retaining walls divide the slopes into narrow levels. Stairs pass beneath houses and reappear in courtyards. Drains run under lanes paved with gray stone. The settlement feels urban not through size but through completeness: nothing within its boundary is quite untouched.

The surrounding heights are cool and mostly bare. Low ridges rise beyond the walls, their rock softened by ages of frost and thin rain. There are no sharp new scars, no active screes. Every exposed face has settled into a stable inclination; every fallen block seems to have found its final hollow. Pale lichen covers the oldest surfaces in round, slow-growing maps. In protected seams, dark moss gathers where condensation lingers.

Water is present without abundance. It arrives as mist, as beads under stone eaves, as a fine rain that may pass for an hour and leave the ground merely darkened. The town was built to keep every portion of it. Roofs tilt toward clay gutters. Gutters empty into covered channels cut down the center of the lanes, and these feed several deep, stone-lined cisterns. In each neighborhood there is a small square containing a public basin. Most are only half full. Their water is cold, clear, and almost perfectly still.

A narrow seasonal stream passes below the eastern wall. Even in wet weather it is modest, flowing between shelves of smooth bedrock and disappearing at intervals beneath old masonry. Small pools remain in its deeper cups. Around them grow the settlement’s densest plants: rushes no higher than a hand, bitter cress, mint, and a dark-leaved herb used in broth. Elsewhere the earth supports little beyond tough grass, cushion plants, and scattered thorn shrubs bent close to the ground.

Trees are rare enough to serve as landmarks. One crooked mountain ash stands in the upper market, protected by a circular wall and fed with water left after washing vegetables. Three dwarf junipers grow near the western gate. A few household courtyards contain espaliered plum trees, their branches tied flat against sun-warmed walls. The fruit is small and tart. Fallen leaves are gathered rather than allowed to decay where they land.

The buildings are of local stone, stacked in thick courses and packed with clay. Their walls lean subtly inward, giving the streets the sheltered feeling of channels cut through rock. Roofs are made from overlapping slabs supported by short timber beams brought up from lower valleys. Because wood is costly, doors are low, rooms are narrow, and beams are reused until they are polished and black. Metal is present only in small, valued forms: knife edges, needles, a few hinges, hoops around buckets. Most daily objects are stone, fired clay, horn, leather, woven grass, or carved wood.

The town’s apparent permanence comes from maintenance rather than grandeur. Every wall bears repairs, but the repairs follow the old pattern so exactly that they do not read as change. Stones loosened by winter are set back into their places. Clay render washed from a facade is applied again in the same muted tone. A worn stair tread is turned over. Roof slabs are exchanged between buildings so that no house ever appears wholly new or wholly ruined.

The streets are steep but orderly. Main lanes follow the contour of the saddle, while stairways climb between them. There are no wheeled vehicles; the corners are too tight, and the inclines too severe. Loads are carried on backs or on narrow sledges dragged over smooth paving. Stone bollards at intervals offer places to rest a basket or brace a burden. The largest open space is scarcely more than a broad courtyard, edged by workshops, storehouses, and a roofed platform where grain is measured.

Industry is quiet and close to the hand. Potters work in shallow sheds near a communal kiln. Masons shape soft gray blocks with simple iron picks and harder stones. Weavers use upright looms weighted with pierced disks. Grain is ground in small rotary querns or in a water mill below the eastern wall when the stream is strong enough, which is not often. On dry days the idle millwheel rests exactly where the last water left it.

Even when inhabited, the town is hushed. Thick masonry absorbs voices. Felted doors close without slamming. The principal sounds are footsteps on stone, the scrape of a broom, quernstones turning, and water dripping at regular intervals into covered jars. In rain, the gutters produce a subdued network of trickles beneath the streets. Bells are too costly for common use. Time is marked by a wooden clapper struck from the central roof: a flat, carrying knock that crosses the whole saddle and fades against the hills.

Animals are uncommon. A few small goats are kept outside the southern wall, tethered among the thorn scrub where they cannot strip the cistern margins. Chickens live in enclosed courtyards. Now and then a raven crosses the town, its wingbeats audible overhead; less often, a pale mountain fox is seen at the stream after dusk. There are tiny black beetles under damp stones and threadlike insects above the basins in summer. Nothing forms great herds or noisy colonies. The altitude and thin vegetation keep life dispersed.

The air smells of cool mineral surfaces, woodsmoke, damp wool, and clay. After rain, the entire town darkens by several shades. Water outlines the paving joints and hangs from roof edges, but mud is scarce because almost every traveled surface is stone. When the clouds lift, ordinary daylight returns: neither brilliant nor dim, filtered by the altitude into a clean, neutral clarity. Shadows lie sharply enough to show steps and ledges, yet the gray materials keep contrast gentle.

Beyond the gates, footpaths lead to quarry shelves, pasture pockets, and the lower valleys. They are old paths, sunken slightly by use, edged with stones placed generations ago. No road approaches directly. Visitors must arrive on foot, and the final ascent gives the town the appearance of having been fitted into the mountain rather than constructed upon it.

There is little sense of expansion. The walls define the useful ground, and the useful ground has long been apportioned. New households subdivide existing rooms. New workshops inherit old thresholds. The burial terraces beyond the north gate resemble the living town in miniature: low stone enclosures, gravel paths, sparse grass, names cut shallowly into slabs.

Nothing here is truly motionless, but change has been reduced to recurrence. Rain enters the cisterns and is drawn out again. Walls shed stones and receive them back. Children learn the same stairs by touch that their grandparents climbed. Moss advances over one edge while being worn away from another. The town persists at a point of balance—between mountain cold and sheltered habitation, between dryness and collected water, between bare rock and the few living things that can root in its seams.

Seen from the ridge above, it is compact and quiet: a dense knot of gray masonry in a broad emptiness, holding its small supply of water, warmth, soil, and human sound against the cool, settled mountains.
