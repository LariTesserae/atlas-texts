Far beneath the surface lies a hollow so broad that distance restores the illusion of a world.

No stone roof can be seen from the floor. It is hidden beyond a white atmosphere of drifting spores, ice dust, and vapor, though now and then its presence is betrayed by a distant concussion: a crack so deep it is felt through the roots before it becomes sound. Nor is there much stone underfoot. Nearly everything in the hollow is alive. The ground is an interlocked fabric of trunks and rhizomes, layered over immeasurable depth—an entire buried continent grown rather than laid down.

It is called the Underbloom.

Its light does not come from a sun. Across the unseen ceiling, immense colonies of pale leaves cling upside down, turning their broad faces toward mineral seams. They extract heat and chemical energy from the rock and release it as a severe, milk-white radiance. Their combined glow fills the cavern without obvious direction. There are few true shadows. Even the undersides of branches retain a blue-green visibility, and the horizon, where one exists, shines like frosted glass.

The brightness suggests warmth, but there is almost none. Sap thickens here. Breath ghosts immediately. Fine rime traces every vein and thorn. Water appears only in intervals: condensation trembling on waxy surfaces, brief rains shaken loose by movements in the roof, narrow rivers that run for a season and then withdraw into thirsty root beds. Most moisture is held inside living tissue. To cut even a modest vine is to release a stream.

The land has no ordinary soil. What seems at first to be a plain is the taut upper skin of a colossal leaf, many leagues across, its surface furred with silver hairs tall enough to resemble grass. Low ridges are leaf veins. Escarpments are folds where the blade buckled while growing. Beyond it may stand a forest of only seven trees, but each trunk is wider than a city and separated from the next by days of travel. Their crowns vanish upward into the glowing haze. Between these presences lie tremendous open reaches: smooth membranes, bare tangles of supporting fiber, and yawning cavities where nothing grows because the local plant body has not yet decided what that space will become.

This emptiness is deceptive. The Underbloom is nearly total vegetation, but its structures are so large that life becomes landscape, sparse at the scale of a traveler. One may cross a hundred kilometers seeing only a single root, while remaining inside one organism the entire time.

The hollow is young.

Its immense plants are not settled into the patience of forests. They are in a phase of violent construction. Ridges rise overnight as new vascular bundles inflate beneath the ground-skin. Forests unfold in hours, pale shoots driving upward with the slow force of glaciers and then opening explosively into transparent fans. Whole plains sag when their supporting bladders empty. Canyons split with edges as crisp as torn fruit. They have not yet been softened by decay, because decay itself is uncommon in the cold. Broken surfaces remain sharp and wet-looking, often for centuries.

The weather comes from growth.

When the ceiling colonies photosurge, their waste heat loosens curtains of accumulated frost. Rain descends in isolated pillars several kilometers wide, leaving the country around them dry. Droplets strike leaves like thrown pebbles, drumming for a day or a week before stopping at once. Elsewhere, the sudden respiration of a new grove produces warm updrafts, which become horizontal gales beneath the roof. These storms carry spores in luminous clouds. Electrical charge flickers through them, not as lightning bolts but as sprawling blue veins, briefly sketching the nervous architecture of the air.

The most dangerous event is a turning.

The great leaf-plains slowly incline toward favorable light and moisture. For generations a settlement may stand on level ground, then one season cups of sap along the horizon begin to slide. The plain tilts by degrees. Rivers alter direction. Houses creep from their lashings. Eventually the entire surface may rear into a wall, folding along its central vein and spilling forests, pools, and villages into the gulf between leaves. Such motions are almost silent at first. Only near the end does the world roar.

Sound otherwise travels strangely. The vast chamber should echo, but the vegetation absorbs most noise. Footsteps vanish into resilient tissue. Wind passes over velvet surfaces without a hiss. There is no continuous birdsong, no insect drone, no surf. Silence returns immediately after each disturbance, as though the landscape closes over sound.

What can be heard is intimate: the ticking of frost; the faint suction of a root drawing moisture; fibers tightening beneath one’s feet; a seedpod opening somewhere beyond sight with the remote report of split timber. During rapid growth, trunks groan in registers too low for the ear, producing nausea and trembling in the teeth. Experienced travelers lie flat and place a cheek against the ground. Through it they hear the approach of upheaval long before the air reveals anything.

Animal life is rare and conservative. There are white, six-legged browsing creatures that cross the open membranes in narrow files. Their feet end in soft clamps so they do not pierce the surface. They drink almost nothing, shaving frost from luminous stems and storing it in sacs along the spine. When threatened, they fold into seedlike shapes and let the next tilting carry them away.

Higher up live the sailbacks: blind animals with skin stretched between elongated ribs. They do not truly fly. They launch into the enormous voids between branches and steer through convection currents, sometimes remaining aloft for months. Their calls are soft clicks used to measure the distances to moving trunks. A sailback may vanish into brightness and return years later dusted with pollen from some unknown province.

The most abundant mobile beings are arguably plants. Thread-vines unhook themselves when shade falls over them and crawl, knot by knot, toward light. Cup flowers migrate in rings around sources of dripping water. Needle groves uproot themselves before a turning and advance in communal steps, each tree leaning, rooting, and pulling the grove behind it. From a distance their progress resembles a dark weather front.

People, where they exist, build lightly.

Their dwellings occur as solitary clusters at vein junctions or beneath the shelter of leaf folds: huts woven from shed fibers, platforms pinned into tough outer bark, bone-white bridges hung across vascular channels. They avoid stone because it is scarce, and metal because exposed ore lies far above in the unreachable roof. Their blades are chipped from frozen resin, hard as glass in the cold. Needles, hooks, and drill points are made from thorns. Rope comes from spiral creepers that contract when chilled, allowing loads to be lifted by packing snow around the line.

Nothing is nailed deeply. To wound the substrate invites sap floods, infections, or defensive growth. Construction rests in loops, cradles, and living grafts. A village is less a set of buildings than an agreement with the plant beneath it. Gardeners coax ribs upward as walls. Roofs are persuaded from broad leaves. Paths are made by stroking growth buds with mixtures of salt and ash until they turn aside.

Fire is both precious and feared. Dry fuel is almost nonexistent, and open flame can trigger violent chemical responses in the air. People keep small coals inside double-walled ceramic pods, feeding them scraps of dead pith. The light of fire is unnecessary; its heat is treated as medicine, tool, and sacrament. A household may possess a coal inherited across generations. To let it die is a misfortune. To let it escape is a crime.

Ceramic is made in rare hot vents where roots pull back from poisonous mineral breath. Around these vents stand the closest things to permanent structures: squat kilns of mud and powdered shell, storage chambers, and shrines marked with handprints. Yet even these sites are temporary on the landscape’s timescale. A vent cools, a root discovers it, and green tissue swallows the masonry. Travelers sometimes find a lone fired arch protruding from a branch kilometers above the current ground, proof that the branch was once a plain.

Water determines most routes. Condensation wells are mapped by songs because physical maps quickly become obsolete. A well may be a cavity in a flower where cold vapor gathers, a dependable drip from a scar, or a bladder that can be tapped once without killing it. The laws surrounding water are strict. No vessel touches a communal pool directly. No traveler takes the final clear layer above the bitter sap. No wound may be opened upstream from a settlement.

There are also rain chasers who follow darkening patches in the ceiling glow. Their possessions fit into woven sleds. They raise cones of cloth to catch brief downpours, then depart before the newly watered country erupts into growth. Behind a rain, the ground becomes dangerous: shoots spear upward, pods burst, and blind roots sweep across the surface looking for purchase. Within a day, an empty white plain may become a thicket several hundred meters high. Within another, the thicket may exhaust its stored water and collapse into brittle arches.

The world seems chaotic, but it has rules.

Light is food, and every surface slowly tries to face it.

Warmth means motion. Anything newly warm will grow, hatch, ferment, or wake.

Deep cuts summon a response. The surrounding vegetation tightens toward the wound, often crushing whatever made it.

Water never remains ownerless for long. Roots detect it, cups turn toward it, and migratory groves alter course.

Nothing dead falls quickly. The cold preserves abandoned husks and fallen limbs, leaving them suspended in vine nets or lodged across chasms. Thus the scattered ruins of both plants and people remain visible long after their purpose has vanished.

And direction is not fixed. “Down” is governed less by stone than by the tension of the living surfaces. When a continent-sized leaf changes its posture, pools crawl sideways, hanging forests become upright, and old cliff villages find themselves on level ground. The people distinguish several downs: falling-down, root-down, rain-down, and the remembered down of one’s birthplace.

At great distances, beyond the inhabited leaves, stand new formations still emerging from the void. They appear first as thin green lines in empty brightness. Over decades they thicken into cables, and the cables braid into bridges hundreds of kilometers long. Membranes spread between them, translucent and veined, dividing the cavern into new horizons. Sometimes two growing structures meet with enough force to buckle continents. Sometimes they recognize chemical kinship and graft together, exchanging waters, diseases, and whole ecologies. Sometimes they reject one another. Then pale defensive forests rise along the contact line, and for generations the border thrashes with thorns.

From afar these upheavals are beautiful. White storms circle luminous crowns. Vast leaves turn like slow mirrors. Blue electrical patterns pulse through pollen clouds. Rain falls in isolated columns through hundreds of kilometers of brilliant emptiness. A solitary sailback crosses the glow, no larger than a fleck of soot.

Up close, the same world is cold sap, tight rope, numb hands, and the constant need to listen through one’s feet.

No one knows whether the Underbloom is a cavern overtaken by plants, or the interior of a plant mistaken for a world. Excavators who cut downward find older mats of vegetation, then voids, then other shining layers far below. Those who climb return with reports of root systems descending from still higher countries. The few fragments of exposed rock are fresh-faced and angular, as if the stone itself has only recently cracked open to make room.

Perhaps the whole buried immensity has just germinated.

Perhaps its present turbulence is no storm, but the first unfolding of a leaf.
