The city occupies a high basin where heat should not have gathered, yet does.

From the western rim, the whole settlement can be seen at once: pale terraces descending through miles of open mineral ground, interrupted by cistern towers, low refinery halls, and black lines of pipe. There are broad vacancies between districts. Streets cross flats where no buildings stand. Rail spurs travel alone for long distances, shining in the sun before disappearing behind shelves of weathered stone. Nothing crowds except the water.

It comes from within the mountain.

Every exposed face weeps. Clear threads issue from seams in the rock, gather under ledges, and run through grooves worn smooth over centuries. The basin floor is riddled with warm springs and shallow, brilliant pools. After the daily condensation, pavement turns mirror-black and rooftops drain for hours. Steam hangs ankle-high in the lower wards, though overhead the air is painfully bright.

There is no vegetation. No moss rims the channels, no reeds soften the reservoirs, no stain of algae troubles the water. The mineral surfaces remain bare: white carbonate, rust-red iron, blue-gray basalt, green crusts of copper salts. In places the springs have built terraces of their own, rounded lips and folded aprons that resemble frozen waterfalls. Elsewhere, acid runoff has bitten the stone into honeycomb. These forms are old and stable. New deposits gather upon them so slowly that painted survey marks from vanished administrations remain visible beneath transparent skins of mineral.

The heat has several sources. Some rises geologically from the deep fractures. Some is manufactured. The industrial quarter stands over the largest vents, drawing superheated brine into turbines and separation towers. Its chimneys are short and wide, not intended for smoke but for pressure. Distillation sheds, salt works, pumping stations, ceramic foundries, and condenser fields occupy separate shelves around the basin. Their machinery is large, but it moves with unnerving restraint.

Nothing clangs.

Gears turn behind insulated walls. Freight platforms glide on magnetic tracks without wheels. Pumps pulse belowground, felt through the soles rather than heard. Steam is captured before it can shriek. Even the electrical grid seems muted: no hum at the substations, only heat shimmer around dark ceramic vanes. The people who designed the city treated noise as leakage, and leakage as failure.

The result is not true silence. Water ticks from railings. Condensate whispers along gutters. Pressure doors exhale softly. Far out on the flats, a cooling sheet may contract with a single porcelain click, startling in the immense quiet. One can sometimes hear a shadow cross the ground—the faint dry flutter of membrane above.

The fauna is everywhere, though at first the basin appears empty.

Most creatures do not resemble inhabitants of a living world. They have no relation to plants because there have never been plants here. The foundation of the ecology is chemical: warm water meeting air, dissolved metals meeting porous stone, sulfur rising into light. Invisible films inside the springs consume these gradients. They are not green and do not make leaves or visible mats. Their work reveals itself only in subtle changes of color and in the mineral shells abandoned after their colonies move on.

Grazers follow them.

Small glass-backed things cling under submerged ledges, scraping clear paths through new deposits. Their many feet are transparent; their internal channels shine amber with sulfur compounds. Coin-sized sieve animals drift in cisterns, opening and folding like delicate mechanical flowers. Along drainage cuts, pale runners move in groups, each supported on four needle legs, dipping hinged mouths into cracks where warm brine evaporates.

Above them sail the basin’s largest animals: broad, nearly weightless kites whose bodies are stretched between jointed spars. They feed on airborne spores, mineral dust, and the minute ballooning stages of spring fauna. In the noon updrafts, hundreds may circle over the condenser fields. Their skins reflect so much light that they vanish when seen from below, leaving only moving patches of coolness.

Predators are quieter still. They wait in hot pools disguised as rafts of pumice, or flatten themselves into the geometric shadows of pipes. A long-bodied species haunts the aqueducts. It senses pressure changes and can strike through water without disturbing the surface until the final instant. The city’s cistern keepers leave marked basins for it, because it controls the glass-backs that would otherwise clog intake screens.

Thus the urban works and the ecology are not separate. Reservoir gates open according to migrations. Spillways are roughened to provide attachment sites. Waste heat is routed through breeding chambers beneath public baths. Certain factories shut down for three hours at dawn when the membrane kites descend to drink condensate from their roofs. The city’s engineering codes include diagrams of animals beside diagrams of valves.

The buildings are made chiefly of fired stone, dense glass, and metals chosen to grow protective tarnishes. Wood is absent, as are fibers, paper, oils derived from forests, and all the inherited shapes those materials once encouraged elsewhere. Doors pivot on mineral bearings. Awnings are thin plates of mica composite. Furniture is cast ceramic or woven from metallic filament. Records are etched onto reusable sheets of black glass.

Most structures are low, adapted to fierce light and violent thermal strain. Their outer walls are pale and thick, cut with deep apertures. Between them stand skeletal towers supporting condensation meshes hundreds of meters across. At night these meshes cool under the open sky. Moisture gathers on every strand, then falls in disciplined rows into stone catchments. By morning, the elevated districts are briefly veiled in rain made by architecture.

The rainfall rarely reaches beyond the city. From the rim it looks like a gray curtain suspended over terraces of white and red, while the distant flats remain bright.

Human movement is sparse. A figure crossing a plaza may be the only upright form visible for half a kilometer. Workers travel beneath reflective mantles and carry dark faceplates against the glare. Habitations are clustered underground around cool shafts, so the apparent city is far larger than its visible population. Many of its avenues were built for transport machines that no longer run, or perhaps have not yet been installed. Their emptiness gives the settlement the quality of a diagram laid directly onto the earth.

There are signs of great age, but little ruin. Erosion and repair have reached an understanding. Corners are rounded by warm rain; then maintenance crews fit them with sacrificial tiles. Aqueducts have sagged into graceful curves without breaking. Mineral deposition slowly seals old cracks and blocks abandoned doors. Some neighborhoods have been engulfed waist-deep in pale travertine, their lower windows preserved behind translucent stone. Rather than excavate them, the city has raised its walkways.

On the northern side of the basin, where the altitude is greatest, the ground breaks into long shelves. Water pours down them in broad films too thin to roar. Under the white sun, the descending sheets are almost invisible except where they bend the view of the plains beyond. Animals pass through them. Pipes pass behind them. There are chambers carved into the cliff, each fronted by a veil of flowing water, and within those chambers the temperature is cool enough for breath to condense.

No one agrees on whether the basin is naturally wet.

The oldest channels run uphill.

Survey instruments show that several springs emerge under less pressure than their elevation requires. Condenser towers collect more water than the atmosphere appears to contain. At the center of the city is a circular reservoir whose level never changes, although it feeds half the districts and has no measurable inlet. Its surface is perfectly still, untouched by evaporation despite the heat. Flyers will not cross it. Aquatic grazers placed in it are later found in distant springs, alive and coated with minerals unknown in the basin.

The reservoir is fenced only by a low curb.

In this place, danger is not announced by walls or noise. It is marked by accommodation: a route bending gently around an empty pool, a factory timed to an unseen migration, a stair raised one additional step above the wet pavement. The city has survived by learning which processes may be harnessed, which may be diverted, and which must simply be given space.

Toward evening, brightness becomes more severe before it fades. The western terraces flare gold. Every wet surface doubles the sky. The mineral plains beyond the basin turn white, then violet at their farthest edge. Condensation begins on pipes, window glass, animal shells, and exposed skin.

The first drops fall without wind.

Across the open city, thousands of shallow channels begin to fill. Silent machinery adjusts beneath the streets. Pale runners emerge from cracks. Kites descend from the cooling air. Predators loosen themselves from the stones. Warm water enters ancient grooves and follows them through industrial wards, abandoned plazas, inhabited vaults, and terraces built by springs before there was a city.

Nothing grows here.

Everything flows.
