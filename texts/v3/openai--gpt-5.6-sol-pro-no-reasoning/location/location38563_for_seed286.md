The ocean stands high above the world.

It is not suspended in a basin or held behind walls. It has grown there: a continent of water borne aloft by vegetation so immense and old that roots, trunks, and leaves have ceased to be separate categories. From far below, the whole formation resembles a dark-green weather system fixed around the shoulders of the planet. Its underside hangs in veils of root fiber. Its upper surface lies beneath an open, cool sky, level enough to make a horizon.

Within it, there is no firm ground.

What seems at first to be seafloor is densely woven plant tissue: pale root-felt, leathery rhizomes, and broad overlapping fronds pressed flat by the water above them. Yet this living floor is often kilometers down. Between it and the upper light extends a clear, cold-green interior ocean. Trunks rise through that depth like widely spaced towers, but none can be called an individual tree. Each is a temporary thickening of the same continuous organism. Their bark is ribbed, black, and slick with small translucent leaves. Their crowns break apart into innumerable floating blades near the surface, where they spread so broadly that storms and seasons should form beneath them.

They do not.

Nothing here changes.

The water is always in motion, but its motion never develops. Vast currents pass in permanent circuits around the trunks, returning with precisely the temperature, speed, and burden of silt with which they departed. Waves lift and fall without traveling. Rain occupies the air above in fixed silver columns: every drop descends, joins the high ocean, is drawn into leaf and root, rises invisibly through the living mass, and appears again at the same point in the sky. There is no first drop and no final one. The weather is an engine at equilibrium.

The light is ordinary daylight—neither mystical nor dim—but the distances make it strange. Sunbeams enter through apertures in the floating canopy and remain at fixed inclinations, broad white shafts extending through blue-green water. Dust, spores, and minute swimmers circulate inside them. At any moment they appear to be falling, though their constellations never alter. Beyond the beams lies ample emptiness. Despite the overwhelming quantity of vegetation, the open water between major growths is spacious, sometimes hundreds of kilometers across. Plant life constitutes every boundary and substrate, yet leaves enormous chambers within itself.

These chambers have their own geography.

There are high pelagic plains where buoyant leaves, each several kilometers long, float edge-on in orderly ranks. Their tissues are inflated with cool gases and metallic-smelling oils. Beneath them lie the trunk provinces, sparse forests of vertical columns disappearing both upward and downward. Deeper still, the root escarpments begin: terraces worn into living fiber by currents that have followed the same routes for ages. They have the settled contours of ancient stone—gullies, arches, bowls, overhangs—but they heal exactly as quickly as they erode. Every torn filament is replaced; every replacement is worn away. The cliffs keep their weathered faces forever.

At the edges of the high ocean, water pours outward in continuous falls. These falls are taller than mountains and loud enough to be heard through the entire organism. Yet the ocean never empties. The descending water frays into mist, is caught by hanging roots, and is pumped upward through capillary channels. From below, the margins look like a continent surrounded not by coast but by an unending white mane.

There are no paths, walls, vessels, pylons, ruins, or tools. Nothing has been constructed.

Nevertheless, the place is industrial.

The industry belongs to the plants.

Deep in the root floor, cylindrical tissues turn inside fixed chambers. Rings of lignin and cartilage contract in sequence, driving water through vascular mains as wide as valleys. Membranes separate salts with monotonous efficiency. Mineral nodules are dissolved, graded, and conveyed upward in sap streams. Broad electrocyte fronds discharge in synchronized waves, maintaining chemical differences between the upper and lower waters. Gases are compressed inside bladder groves and released through thousands of identical valves. Seed casings move along peristaltic canals as if on assembly lines, though none ever reaches a destination it has not reached before.

The sound is enormous.

One hears the groan of trunks under static loads, the turbine roar of arterial currents, the regular concussion of gas bladders, the crackle of biological electricity, and everywhere the deep continuous thunder of the peripheral falls. These noises overlap until they resemble heavy machinery operating beyond sight. Close to the root floor, the pumping rhythm can be felt through the water more strongly than it can be heard: a pressure passing through the body in vast, perfectly spaced strokes.

No mechanism accelerates, jams, or wears out. Nothing is consumed beyond replacement. There is production without accumulation and labor without progress. Leaves emerge from the growth seams already bearing the scars they will always have. Old bark peels away at the same rate that new bark forms beneath it, so the falling strips hang perpetually halfway detached. Fruit ripens but does not rot. Spores are released, carried, captured, and returned to their parent tissues in closed migrations.

Animal life inhabits these cycles in abundance, though it too is thinly distributed across the immensity.

Long-bodied grazers move over the submerged leaves, cropping lanes of green tissue that close behind them. Shoals of silver animals gather around electrical organs, feeding on microbes stunned by each discharge. Blind, soft-winged creatures circle the pump mouths and are swept through labyrinths of vascular water, emerging elsewhere unharmed. Between the trunks drift immense filter feeders, nearly transparent except for their cold blue circulatory nets. Their tails never beat. They occupy permanent circuits in the current, mouths open, sieving the same replenished cloud of plankton.

Predators exist, but pursuit has become geography. A black, ribbonlike hunter hangs across a narrow current pass. The same shoal enters its reach; the same scattering occurs; the same number are swallowed and restored by breeding elsewhere. On the root terraces, shelled colonies raise forests of feeding limbs whenever the pressure pulse arrives. They withdraw between pulses. This has happened without variation for longer than any living tissue can record, though all the tissue carries the record.

There are no seasons. The coolness is universal and gentle, approaching cold without becoming hostile. Near the surface the water has the chill of shaded rain. At the deepest root mat it is almost exactly the same. Heat made by muscular pumping, decay, and electrical discharge is drawn into pale thermal vines, moved outward, and shed into the permanent waterfall mist. No warm vent forms. No cold pocket deepens. The ecosystem permits local difference only as part of a balanced circuit.

Even disturbance is included.

Far out in one open chamber, a trunk has split from crown to root. Its two halves lean apart, exposing amber interior wood. The split looks recent: loose fibers stream from it, animals flee the wound, and clouds of sap darken the water. But the fibers never tear free. The animals never quite leave. The sap cloud neither spreads nor settles. The catastrophe is a stable organ of the landscape, as permanent as a canyon.

Elsewhere, a bloom occupies thousands of square kilometers. White flowers open under the water, each large enough to enclose a hill. Their petals bend in the current but cannot be damaged. Inside them, heavy-bodied pollinators swim between anthers with a droning vibration that joins the deeper mechanical roar. Pollen pours from every contact in golden smoke. It never runs out, and the surrounding water never becomes opaque. Seen from above, the bloom is a pale geometry beneath the ocean surface, separated by broad intervals of dark, empty water.

Height is difficult to comprehend here. A swimmer may descend for days and still see the upper canopy shining plainly overhead, while the root floor remains a vague green shadow below. This is not because light behaves impossibly, though it sometimes seems to. The water itself is maintained at extraordinary clarity by innumerable filtering veils. They strain every suspended particle, sort it, and return exactly enough to sustain the fixed beams and permanent hazes. Visibility is managed as carefully as salinity.

Direction is marked not by landmarks but by pressure and sound. Toward the rim, the waterfall thunder thickens. Toward the central pump basins, a slow bass rhythm dominates. Upward, the light grows whiter and the foliage broader. Downward, the trunks divide into buttressed root systems whose surfaces bear the polished erosion of immeasurable flow.

There is no evidence that anything has ever begun here.

No sapling stands beside an elder, although narrow trunks and broad trunks both exist. No bones gather, though animals feed and are fed upon. No sediment thickens. No lineage advances toward a new form. Birth, growth, predation, repair, and death occur everywhere, but together they make a single stationary event. The high ocean is not frozen; it is furiously, deafeningly alive. Its stillness exists at a scale larger than motion.

From the open sky above, wind presses the floating leaves into shallow valleys. Rain rattles across them. The ocean heaves. Winged animals emerge through pores in the canopy and skim the wet green surface, calling sharply in the cool air. At the horizon, the upper sea curves away beneath ordinary daylight, vast enough to possess distant blues and false continents of haze.

Beneath them, unseen, the living machinery continues: roots pumping, membranes sorting, animals circling, cliffs eroding and regrowing, water falling off the edge and climbing back through the veins.

Everywhere there is motion.

Nothing, anywhere, happens.
