The mountains are being made in the dark.

They rise across a broad, high country where no river has ever run and no rain has taught the ground to soften. There are no valleys in the mature sense, only distances between upheavals: long mineral floors tilted toward fresh escarpments, basins lifted before they can settle, ridges breaking through other ridges. The land stands at an extreme altitude, though there is no sea from which to measure it. Beneath a low black sky, it feels less like a summit than the exposed upper surface of a world still forcing itself outward.

Heat comes from below. It is steady in the open flats, seeping through seams and warming the thin, acrid air, but intense along the newborn faults. There the stone shines a muted red beneath translucent crusts. Nothing flows as freely as lava. The planet’s interior extrudes dense pastes of sulfur, iron salts, and soft silicate glass that bulge upward, wrinkle, and harden in place. Whole slopes advance by swelling. A cliff may appear over several hours without any rock falling: first a line on the plain, then a raised lip, then a wall pushed vertically from beneath.

There has not yet been time for erosion. Every surface bears the clean grammar of its making. Sheets of black glass preserve the drag marks of subsurface pressure. Pale mineral terraces retain sharp corners and thin, brittle rims. Cones stand unscored, their flanks immaculate except where they split from within. Nothing is rounded. There is no sand in the ordinary sense, because grinding has scarcely begun. The loose ground consists of angular splinters, metallic flakes, and porous clinker newly shed by cooling crusts.

The darkness is not complete. It contains weak sources rather than illumination.

Hairline fissures open and close across the plain, glowing amber for a few breaths at a time. Farther off, heat halos outline the undersides of overhangs. Some peaks carry cold mineral phosphorescence: wan blue stains that brighten when the rock is strained, tracing stress through the mountain before it moves. Thus the country reveals itself by episodes. A ridge flashes into visibility, tall and knife-thin, and disappears. A field of hexagonal plates glimmers underfoot. For an instant, a distant wall shows tier above tier of fresh stone, climbing beyond sight; then the blue stress-light goes out and depth returns to guesswork.

The sky contributes almost nothing. Dust and heavy volcanic gases make a ceiling without features. Occasionally the upper dark reddens, not with dawn but with the reflection of an eruption occurring beyond the horizon. Shadows have no fixed direction. Near the vents, mineral vapors drift close to the ground in dim copper veils, but these are dry exhalations—chlorides, sulfur compounds, powdered crystal. They leave no dew. They deposit delicate collars around cracks, building yellow and white structures that resemble frost and perish at a touch.

There is no vegetation. No buried seed waits for a gentler era. The only things that suggest growth are inorganic: branching crystals, expanding blisters of glass, and slender mineral chimneys that accrete where hot gases escape. Some chimneys stand no higher than a hand; others rise like empty trunks along the faults. Their interiors are lined with red needles. In certain sheltered clefts, black films spread slowly across warm stone. Whether they are carbon precipitates or a chemistry approaching life is impossible to tell. They neither feed nor reproduce in any recognizable way. They simply appear where the temperature and pressure remain briefly exact, then curl away when the ground changes.

Nothing walks here. Nothing flies through the fumes. There are no tracks, burrows, droppings, shells, bones, calls, or feeding marks. The absence of animals is more profound than silence: the landscape contains no scale shaped by bodies. Openings are not entrances. Ledges are not perches. Cavities have never sheltered anything. The land makes forms that seem usable only because an observer brings the idea of use.

Scattered across the high plain are structures that almost contradict this.

They are low, severe shapes assembled from slabs unlike the surrounding rock: three upright stones supporting a fourth, a narrow ring half sunk in clinker, a staircase of seven steps ending against an unbroken wall. They stand many kilometers apart and never gather into settlements. None contains joints, ornament, metal, or any sign of tools. Some may be accidents of extrusion, slabs pushed together by converging faults. Others appear too exact. One thin arch spans a crack that repeatedly widens and closes beneath it, yet the arch remains standing. Elsewhere, a roof of pale stone rests over a shallow chamber, its underside polished to a darkness deeper than the sky.

There are no devices in these places, no mechanisms awaiting power. If they were built, construction here belonged to the geology itself. Perhaps the world once learned a few architectural gestures from pressures now extinct. Perhaps form precedes intention.

The country is mostly quiet. Its violence is too large or too deep to produce continuous noise. For long intervals there is only the faint hiss of gas passing through pores and the dry ticking of hot glass cooling. Sound travels strangely in the dense lower air. A small fracture nearby may be swallowed at once, while a break fifty kilometers away arrives as a single enormous knock, clean and hollow, as though someone struck the underside of the plateau.

Then the upheavals come.

The ground does not shake so much as alter its decisions. Slopes lean in new directions. Parallel cracks race across a basin, each opening with a brief orange line. A dark dome inflates on the horizon and splits soundlessly into petals, exposing an incandescent interior before sealing beneath ash. Stone fins thrust from a plain in sequence, one after another, fast enough to follow with the eye. A mountain loses no material, yet changes profile as its western face folds inward and emerges along the north as a serrated ridge.

During the greatest episodes, the sparse structures and mineral towers seem momentarily numerous because the land creates repetitions everywhere. Arches form and collapse. Terraces stack themselves. Columns rise in ranks before twisting back into the crust. The whole region becomes busy without becoming inhabited—an immense, blind construction site with no workers, plans, or finished state.

Its governing rule is not decay but revision. Nothing has lasted long enough to become ancient. Even the tallest ranges are young in their substance, lifted so quickly that heat remains trapped in their cores. Their summits pierce the higher atmosphere, exposing glassy crests to a near-vacuum where gases stream from the rock in pale banners. Yet their faces are pristine, without gullies or scree fans. They possess the altitude of old mountains and the surfaces of objects made tonight.

Navigation is therefore unreliable. Landmarks persist, but the ground between them may lengthen, buckle, or rise into barriers. A flat crossing can become a stair of fault blocks. A pass can close without collapse, its walls simply pressing together. The safest surfaces are not the coolest or the oldest, but those that emit a low violet glimmer: rock under tension announces itself before changing. Complete darkness is more dangerous, though sometimes a black plain remains untouched while every shining ridge around it is remade.

Above everything stands the sense of arrested beginning. The world is not ruined, because ruin requires a former wholeness. It is not barren in the way of a place that has lost its life, because nothing here remembers abundance. It is raw, warm, elevated, and unfinished. Its emptiness is the emptiness of a mold before filling, of a word before meaning gathers around it.

Across the open distances, another ridge begins to rise.

First comes a faint straightness against the dark. Then the stress-light kindles along it, a blue-white thread hundreds of kilometers long. The thread lifts. Black plates tilt on either side, exposing red beneath. Gas pours upward in silent curtains. For a few minutes the new range resembles a wall; then buttresses emerge, peaks separate, and enormous saddles deepen between them. No debris falls from their faces. No dust softens their edges.

When the movement stops, the mountains stand hot and flawless beneath the sealed sky.

The quiet returns before they have finished cooling.
