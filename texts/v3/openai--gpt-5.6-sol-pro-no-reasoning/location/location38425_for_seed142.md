The city lies across the summit of an old plateau, although *lies* suggests an ease it does not possess. It has been pressed into the stone: streets cut as narrow grooves, factories sunk to their roofs, towers reduced by ages of abrasion to black, waist-high stumps. From a distance the whole settlement resembles a diagram engraved on an anvil.

There is no visible horizon. Darkness begins a few kilometres beyond the city and continues without distinction, not as night or weather but as the ordinary substance of the world. A low vault may exist overhead. Instruments insist upon it, returning measurements from thirty-seven kilometres above the plateau, yet no eye has ever picked out its surface. There are no stars, no moon, no change of illumination. The scant light comes from the city itself: furnace seams, dull sodium apertures, and lines of buried lamps marking roads no traffic uses.

Everything is warm to the touch.

The plateau is one of the highest known places, rising through the dark above a basin whose floor has never been surveyed. Its edges descend in immense, shallow steps. These were once cliffs, then escarpments, then slopes; erosion has labored so long that even the rubble has lost its angles. Dust is rare. Whatever could be loosened was loosened ages ago, and whatever could be carried away is gone. The remaining surface is a close-grained mineral, brown-black and faintly metallic, polished by processes no longer active.

Nothing blows across it. Nothing falls.

There is no water in the city or beneath it. Pipes are plentiful—more plentiful than rooms—but they transport dry gases, powdered salts, electrical charge, and, in the oldest lines, nothing at all. Reservoirs hold dense amber vapor under pressure. Cooling systems use phase-changing ceramics that circulate as beads through iron conduits. Industrial grease is made from sulfurous compounds and leaves pale crystalline flowers around the joints of machines. The city’s inhabitants, when there were inhabitants, seem to have regarded liquid water as hypothetical. In technical inscriptions it is represented by a warning glyph: a branching crack enclosed in a circle.

The oldest structures are scarcely distinguishable from geology. Their walls have the rounded patience of pebbles, though no river shaped them. Doorways have become low arches; staircases have subsided into ramps. Rows of residential blocks stand far apart, separated by plazas large enough for armies. Their windows are sealed with slabs of smoky glass. Behind them are rooms without furniture, each containing a shallow square recess in the floor and a metal loop in the ceiling. No two interpretations of these arrangements agree.

Later districts are more legible. Rail lines converge on foundries. Gantries crouch over loading courts. Chimneys rise in clustered bundles, braced against winds that never came. Vast plants occupy terraces below the central city, their machinery organized into repeating sequences: crusher, separator, kiln, press, rack. The sequence is so consistent that the factories may be read like a sentence repeated across the plateau.

But they manufacture no recognizable object.

Material enters the works as hard, dark blocks quarried from the plateau. It is milled, heated, magnetized, demagnetized, ground to powder, compressed, and finally fused back into hard, dark blocks. These are conveyed to the quarries and fitted precisely into the cuts from which the original stone was taken. The operation is almost perfectly conservative. A little heat escapes. A trace of mineral accumulates in the filters. Otherwise, after perhaps several centuries of processing, the plateau is restored to its previous arrangement.

Some of the factories still perform this cycle.

They do so quietly. There are no roaring engines, no shriek of belts or hammers. Machinery here was built on an extravagant scale but tuned to move slowly: pistons completing one stroke in nine days, rail carts progressing a hand’s breadth each hour, kilns warming and cooling over decades. If one stands beside an active plant, the first impression is of total stillness. Only after prolonged attention does the structure disclose its motion. A chain changes the angle of its sag. A flywheel’s corroded mark approaches the floor. Somewhere inside the stone, a bearing gives a low, prolonged note.

The city’s loudest sound occurs every eighty-one hours.

A pressure gate opens beneath the eastern works, and a column of hot gas passes through the subterranean network. The sound is less like an exhalation than like a distant building remembering it was struck. It moves under the streets in a deep pulse, causing iron covers to tremble and pale dust to leap from cracks. Then the gate closes. The city resumes its quiet, altered in no observable way.

No vegetation has ever rooted here. There is no soil, only machined mineral, fused slag, compacted salt, and the smooth native rock. Green appears nowhere. Even pigments tend toward iron-black, ash-white, sulfur-yellow, and the deep bruised red of heat-treated stone.

Life, if that term applies, survives in isolated industrial niches. Inside certain warm conduits are colonies of thin silicate wafers arranged edge to edge. They grow by accreting exhaust crystals and splitting when mechanical stress reaches a precise threshold. Their metabolism may be no more than fracture. Yet they turn gradually toward electrical fields and repair gaps in their formations, suggesting an intention too slow to demonstrate.

There are also the wire animals.

They are seldom seen, perhaps because there are very few. Each consists of six or seven jointed lengths of tarnished metal surrounding a ceramic core. They emerge from cable trenches and cross open ground without sound, placing their feet only on seams in the paving. They avoid light. They do not feed in any witnessed manner, though sometimes one stands for years beside a transformer, touching the casing with a single limb. When the transformer eventually fails, the animal departs.

Their tracks are shallow paired scratches, found throughout districts older than the machines themselves.

At the plateau’s center is the city’s principal elevation: a broad mound only ninety metres high, worn almost flat. The administrative quarter is built around it in concentric avenues. At the summit stands no palace, antenna, or temple, but a circular shaft descending into warmth. Light placed over the opening fails to reveal its sides after the first few metres. Dropped objects do not strike bottom. They return, sometimes much later, resting in designated collection trays in factories around the city.

A piece of iron dropped into the shaft may emerge after three years as a piece of iron of identical mass but different age. Its corrosion vanishes. Tool marks appear that no tool in the city can make. Occasionally an object returns before it is dropped, obliging the custodians—when custodians existed—to keep it until the appropriate date and complete the event.

This may explain the city’s changelessness.

Decay occurs, but never accumulates beyond a narrow tolerance. Walls erode until they reach their present rounded forms and then erode no farther. Machines lose flakes of rust, yet maintain the same dimensions. Lamps fail, remain dark for years, and then resume without repair. Survey marks cut thousands of years apart coincide to the width of a grain. The city is not preserved in the ordinary sense. It is permitted small motions, local losses, and brief irregularities, but every process eventually curves back toward a fixed arrangement.

The factories may not be factories at all. They may be the mechanism by which this arrangement is enforced.

Beyond the western blocks, the city thins into scattered infrastructure. A switching tower. Three empty depots. Parallel roads dividing and rejoining across open stone. Lamp posts stand kilometres apart, each casting a weak ochre pool. Between them the darkness feels architectural, as if the illuminated places are chambers and the unlit distances are walls.

Farther still, at the plateau’s edge, the surface bends gently downward. There is no dramatic precipice, only an increasingly steep plane descending into absolute black. Heat rises from below, steady and dry. Old rails follow the incline until their lights become too small to distinguish. According to maps, there are lower suburbs on the terraces: districts of storage vaults and worker barracks, then extraction fields, then a ring road almost two hundred kilometres long.

The maps may be correct. Signals still arrive from those places.

Every signal is identical: a short electrical tone repeated at long intervals. It contains no words. Its timing never varies. Attempts to answer produce no response, though several hours later the central factories make tiny compensating movements, as if the transmitted energy has been entered into an account.

Standing at the edge, one senses the breadth of the city without seeing it. Widely separated lamps define fragments of roads. Furnace mouths glow far below like covered coals. A lift tower shows a single vertical thread of red, descending farther than perspective should allow. Between these marks is a warm darkness so complete that scale becomes uncertain. A nearby railing and a distant industrial complex may appear equally close. The mind begins assembling streets where none exist and erasing buildings that do.

There is an old rule engraved above many entrances:

**NOTHING MAY BE ADDED.  
NOTHING MAY BE REMOVED.  
PASSAGE IS PERMITTED.**

The inscription occurs in factories, dwellings, substations, and at the mouths of abandoned tunnels. On the eastern road, it has been carved directly into the plateau in letters fifty metres long. Erosion has softened the cuts, but not erased them.

No one knows who the rule addresses.

The city waits at its great altitude, warm and nearly lightless, with all its avenues open and all its machinery engaged in the endless production of equivalence. It has reached an age beyond ruin. Ruin would be a development, and development is one of the few things this place cannot sustain.

Here, even abandonment has become infrastructure.
