At first it seems to be a plain after rain.

The ground is dark, shallowly flooded, and warm enough to blur its own horizon. Dim reflections lie between long bars of black material. Here and there, a drop falls into the water, though there is no sky directly overhead and nothing resembling a cloud. The rings spread too far. Some pass under distant structures and emerge on the other side; others return to their point of origin and close upward into wet, trembling spheres.

Nothing here is natural, but very little looks manufactured in any familiar sense.

The plain is an assembly of fired slabs laid edge to edge across a hollow world. Each slab is several kilometers wide, though their surfaces preserve the irregular fingerprints of whoever pressed them into shape. Their material resembles a mixture of ceramic, charcoal, salt, and hammered glass. The joints between them are stuffed with braided ropes of clay. These ropes sweat constantly. Beads of mineral-heavy water gather along the seams, run for a while, then boil away into a low white vapor.

There is no underlying rock. Beneath any cracked plate is only scaffolding: stakes, pegs, lintels, woven mats of baked cord, vast arches held together by wedges. Under those, more scaffolding descends into hot darkness. Everything supports something else, and nothing rests on a foundation.

The tools that built it remain embedded everywhere. Mallets the size of districts lean against unfinished walls. Chipped wedges protrude from faults. Plumb lines hang from points too distant to see, their cords vanishing into the dimness above. There are no engines, no cables carrying power, no evidence of precision machinery. The whole immeasurable place has been cut, beaten, braided, stacked, smeared, fired, and pinned by hand—or by some process that leaves the grammar of handwork behind.

No workers remain.

Perhaps no workers ever existed. The tools sometimes act by themselves, but slowly and without intention. A stone-headed hammer rises over several hours, pauses, then falls. Its impact passes through the plain as a soft subterranean concussion. Fresh cracks race outward, glowing dull red. Almost immediately, wet clay wells up from the joints and fills them. Pegs twist tighter. Frames rearrange their bracing. The land is continuously being built, though it is already larger than any continent.

Its lowlands are broad basins of fitted tile. Rainwater collects there to the depth of an ankle or a sea, depending not on topography but on attention. Looking directly at a basin makes it shallow. Turning away permits it to deepen. Travelers—if travelers were possible here—would hear the quiet pressure of an ocean gathering behind them and find, upon looking back, only a film of water over black clay.

The heat comes from below and from within the materials. It is not the dry violence of a desert. It is kiln heat softened by steam: enclosing, damp, mineral-rich. Water condenses on every vertical surface. Walls weep. Obelisks bead with moisture. The enormous hanging cords grow slick and dark. In sheltered depressions the air is almost breathable until a wave of furnace wind crosses the plain, carrying the odor of wet ash, hot flint, and newly opened pottery.

There are no plants. Nothing roots in the cracks, no scum gathers on standing water, no spores cloud the air. Even decay is absent. Broken materials do not rot or weather; their edges remain sharp, and the shallow floods leave no stain. The place is too new for erosion. Water runs, steam abrades, and heat expands every joint, but none has persisted long enough to make a channel. Rivers begin everywhere and have not yet learned where to go.

They travel in raw, indecisive sheets.

One may see a silver line appear across a hundred kilometers of level flooring. It thickens, branches, and briefly acquires banks made from tiles tipping upward on their hinges. Then the river forgets its direction. It climbs a wall, divides into suspended threads, or pours into the impression of a door that was never cut. Minutes later the whole drainage pattern is dismantled. The banks flatten. The water withdraws into seams. A different watershed assembles elsewhere with a series of distant wooden knocks.

The sparse structures rising from the plain are monumental but incomplete. There are roofless enclosures with walls hundreds of meters thick; isolated staircases whose steps are made of unfired brick; circular palisades enclosing nothing but warmer darkness. Towers taper downward instead of upward, broad at their tops and balanced on single ceramic pins. A few have already sunk through the flooring and hang below it, still counted by the world as standing.

Distance behaves poorly around them.

Two towers separated by a day’s apparent journey may share a shadow. A doorway visible on the horizon may be reached in ten steps, yet remain horizon-sized when approached. Some structures possess an interior but no exterior. Their rooms open directly from the flat ground through rectangular changes in temperature. Others have facades, thresholds, buttresses, and rain gutters, but behind their doors is the same plain under the same dim vault.

The light has no source. It is a bruised gray-red radiance trapped in the vapor, just sufficient to distinguish wet black surfaces from dry ones. At irregular intervals the tiles below become translucent. Then the framework beneath is revealed in immense depth: layer after layer of crude joinery, glowing clay, hanging bridges, baskets of cooling slag, and half-formed platforms being shoved sideways by unseen levers.

Far beneath, something like weather moves through the construction.

It arrives as a distortion rather than a sound. Uprights bend without breaking. The horizon wrinkles. Entire provinces lift the width of a hand and settle at a different angle. Water races toward the new low places, hissing over hot seams. Towers exchange positions. An arch may suddenly discover that it has always been a well; a reservoir turns inside out and becomes a dome beaded with rain on its inner surface.

These upheavals are enormous, but almost quiet.

Most of the time there is only the ticking of cooling ceramic, the soft run of water, and the occasional hollow report of a mallet falling beyond sight. Steam murmurs at the cracks. Loose plumb bobs tap against walls. During the largest rearrangements, sound seems to be stored rather than released. Silence tightens across the plain while structures buckle and horizons fold. Hours later, when everything appears still, the delayed noise arrives as a warm gust: a low roar containing splintering, thunder, surf, and thousands of hands striking clay.

Then quiet returns.

The absence of life is complete enough to become a presence. There are no tracks except those impressed into the slabs during manufacture. Some resemble bare feet, but their toes branch like river deltas. Others are long parallel grooves, as if heavy sledges were dragged across the wet material before firing. They begin abruptly and end at walls. Rain pools in them, making chains of narrow mirrors.

In these mirrors, the landscape is not reflected as it stands. The water shows later stages of construction.

A puddle may contain the image of a completed roof spanning the whole plain, patterned with smoke holes and painted beams. Another shows the tiles dismantled and stacked neatly beside an immeasurable pit. In some, the scaffold has become a maze of chambers occupied by moving points of heat, though nothing resembling a body can be discerned. The images do not predict consistently. They may be plans, discarded possibilities, or memories of versions that were built and unbuilt before time settled into its present direction.

Time is one of the unfinished materials.

Near the joints, droplets sometimes fall upward before condensing. Freshly broken wedges may be found already rounded by centuries of handling, while ancient-looking walls split open to reveal clay still soft from shaping. A hammer can fall twice without rising between blows. Shadows arrive early and wait beside objects that have not yet been erected.

Even causality here is pegged together rather than continuous. Remove one wedge and a tower elsewhere may become incomplete. Pour water into a basin and rain begins beneath the floor. Make a mark in wet clay and the same mark appears on every surface large enough to contain it, scaled to fit: on tiles, distant walls, the underside of the dim air.

Yet the place is not ruinous. It is embryonic.

Its turbulence is the violence of formation, not collapse. The vast apparatus is trying configurations. It raises a wall, tests the burden of distance against it, then kneads it back into flooring. It invents rivers and revokes them. It experiments with inside and outside, with weight, sequence, reflection, and support. The crude construction is not evidence of a primitive civilization but of a young reality using the simplest tools available to teach matter how to stay made.

Water is its measuring substance. Wherever the world is uncertain, moisture gathers. Condensation outlines invisible beams. Pools reveal uncommitted depths. Thin streams trace temporary laws of downwardness. Steam marks those places where two incompatible versions overlap.

On the farthest visible horizon stands the only structure that seems nearly finished: a low ring of dark blocks encircling a reddish haze. It may be a kiln, a city wall, a collar around a wound, or the rim of the mechanism manufacturing the plain. Its circumference is too large to perceive as curved. At intervals, crude gates open in it, each fitted with a lifted slab of stone and a wooden prop.

Through one gate comes hot rain.

Through another comes darkness in slow folds.

Through a third, the horizon itself is periodically pulled, wet and shining, like a length of newly made cloth.

The gates open and shut without rhythm. Each change sends new conditions across the lowlands. A season may last nine breaths: first condensation, then shallow flooding, then a furnace gust, then a sudden rearrangement of every vertical thing. Nothing has time to erode. Nothing has time to colonize. The plain remains perpetually fresh—scored, soaked, fired, and unfinished.

Above it all, beyond the hanging cords and invisible supports, something scrapes softly at the ceiling of the world.

Powder falls.

It is not ash, dust, pollen, or snow, but pale granular matter with no stable scale. On the ground it appears fine enough to float on water. Held close, each grain is an angular building block containing tiny fitted plains, tiny dark towers, tiny sweating seams. For a few moments the powder accumulates in dunes. Then each grain unfolds, claiming more room than it occupied. New platforms spread over old ones. Shallow seas are pinched into being between them. Tool marks widen into ravines before the world remembers that erosion has not begun.

The result is more landscape: raw, vast, hot, wet, and almost empty.

Construction continues in silence, extending in every direction—not toward completion, perhaps, but toward the first stable definition of a place.
