The landscape is hidden inside a mountain.

Not in a cavern, exactly, but within the narrow, incandescent seam where two masses of rock press against one another. The seam is scarcely wider than a hand in most places. It rises for kilometers through the range, folded and branching, sometimes closing to the thickness of foil, elsewhere opening into chambers no larger than a sleeping animal. No daylight has entered since the mountain began to lift.

Everything here is crowded.

Black crystals jut across the gaps in interlocking fans. Sulfur-yellow crusts occupy the hollows between them. Metallic nodules push outward from the walls, touching, merging, and pushing again. There are no floors or open passages in the usual sense—only packed surfaces, fissures between surfaces, and still finer fissures within those. Even the air is compressed into hot, dry pockets: carbon dioxide, sulfur vapor, methane, and traces of electrically active gases trapped under crushing pressure.

There is almost no water. What little hydrogen and oxygen occur here are fixed inside minerals, unavailable as liquid. The seam’s chemistry proceeds by heat, pressure, abrasion, and the slow migration of ions through solid rock. Salt does not lie in beds; it flowers directly from stone in hard, opaque blooms.

The mountain is young. Its innards have not acquired the softened contours of age. Broken faces remain sharp enough to divide a grain of dust. Crystal needles grow straight into neighboring crystal needles, each halted by collision rather than weather. Along the seam, the rock is continually being born from deformation: pale feldspar recrystallizing into dense mosaics, mica aligning itself in glossy scales, dark veins intruding and cooling by fractions of a degree over centuries. Erosion has barely begun. There is only pressure-fracturing, microscopic grinding, and the first powder accumulating in protected pits.

The heat comes from below and from compression. In the deeper pockets, exposed mineral faces glow a faint dull red, though most of the seam remains utterly black. Light arrives in brief, local events: the blue snap of a crystal failing; an orange pulse where gases oxidize against a newly opened surface; a greenish corona crawling over metallic whiskers. These flashes illuminate no panorama. They reveal a few centimeters—a serrated wall, a compact swarm of bodies, a glitter of mineral dust—then disappear.

The sound, however, is immense.

The entire seam behaves like the sounding board of a machine. Tectonic stress travels through it as continuous low thunder. Rock grains click and chatter under load. Crystal lattices fail with reports magnified by confinement until a fracture the length of a fingernail cracks like artillery. Deeper down, magma moving through hidden conduits produces a slow mechanical pounding: not regular enough to be an engine, but too forceful and repetitive to feel accidental.

Nothing has been built here. The industrial quality belongs to geology itself.

Ore is crushed, heated, sorted, plated, and fused without conveyors or furnaces. Pressure acts as the press. Thermal gradients act as the refinery. Electric currents run through conductive bands of graphite and sulfide. Piezoelectric crystals charge as the mountain strains, releasing their accumulated voltage through branching filaments. Metallic vapor condenses on cooler faces in bright skins, is scraped away by motion, and condenses again. It is a factory with no architecture, no operators, and no product that ever leaves.

Yet it is crowded with animals.

They are very small.

The largest would fit beneath a fingernail, and most are scarcely visible without magnification. They have no need for open water and little resemblance to the fauna of the surface. Flat, jointed creatures move between mineral plates by anchoring their many hooked limbs and contracting their bodies in slow waves. Their shells are ceramic composites grown from silica, iron sulfide, and carbon. Some are black enough to vanish against the walls; others shine like scraps of copper.

Dense colonies cluster around electrical discharge points. There, thimble-shaped grazers scrape films of mineral-feeding microbes from crystal surfaces. The microbes do not photosynthesize and form nothing that could be called vegetation. They live as thin chemical stains, drawing energy from gradients between fresh rock and hot gas. In brighter flashes the stains show as gray, violet, or oily bronze, but in ordinary darkness they are known mainly by texture and smell.

Predators inhabit the junctions of cracks. They are narrow, armored things that brace themselves across a passage and detect vibration through taut sensory hairs. When prey approaches, the hairs trigger a springlike strike. The movement is one of the few genuinely quick events in the seam. Afterward the hunter may spend months digesting its meal, warming itself against a conductive vein.

Other animals feed directly on electricity. They fasten chains of conductive spines between differently charged minerals and let weak currents pass through layered organs in their bodies. Colonies of them resemble tangles of wire or iron filings gathered around a magnet. Individually they are nearly inert. Collectively they redirect discharges, making lightning crawl along the living colony instead of leaping across the gap. Their breeding cycles follow the mountain’s strain: a major pressure pulse can cause millions of minute eggs to split open at once.

Space is the principal resource. Every stable pocket is occupied. Shells are used after death, though not as tools: scavengers settle inside them; microbes coat them; smaller animals burrow into their porous layers. Nothing remains empty for long. A newly opened fracture releases hot gas with a shriek, then receives its first colonists within hours—dust-sized drifters carried on the pressure change. Over decades the walls become plated with life. Over centuries mineral growth narrows the fracture again, compressing its inhabitants into smaller and smaller refuges.

There is no broad migration, no weather, no season. Change comes through geologic rhythms. A corridor widens by a millimeter. A gas pocket cools. A vein changes polarity. A section of wall becomes chemically edible after ten thousand years of remaining inert. Entire ecosystems develop around such slight alterations and vanish when the rocks touch.

At the seam’s highest reaches, where the mountain approaches the cold surface, the fauna grows sparse and pale. Far below, heat sterilizes all but tough dormant capsules lodged inside crystal defects. Between these limits lies the packed living band: kilometers tall, centimeters wide, folded many times through the mountain’s body.

Its inhabitants do not know height as an open view or a drop beneath the feet. To them, height is a pressure gradient, a direction in the stone. Upward means cooler rock, thinner gas, and longer intervals between electrical storms. Downward means heat, metallic vapor, and the deep hammering that never stops.

The whole hidden country changes slowly, but it is never silent. The mountain grinds upward. Minerals crowd the seam. Tiny shells rasp against crystal. Charges gather in darkness.

Then, somewhere in the packed blackness, a fracture begins.

For several years it is only a tension in the lattice. Then comes a white flash, a noise like a factory collapsing, and a hairline territory opens where no territory existed before. Hot gas enters. Dust turns in the gap. The first animals arrive.

The raw walls are still sharp when feeding begins.
