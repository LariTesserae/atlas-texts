The place is hidden inside a drowned tree.

From outside, the tree is only one trunk among thousands on the floor of a cold inland sea: a black column leaning beneath forty meters of water, its crown long since dissolved into silt. Pale mosses fur its bark. The drowned forest stands so closely packed that even the water seems forced to squeeze sideways between the trunks. Nothing large swims there. There is scarcely room.

A split near the roots opens into the landscape.

It is narrow enough to enter sideways, through curtains of fibrous wood that have gone soft without rotting. Beyond them, the trunk is hollow—but its interior is larger than the tree could contain. Not vastly larger. The impossibility remains intimate: a chamber the size of a cottage folded inside a trunk no wider than an embrace.

Cold water fills it completely.

The chamber descends only a little. Its floor is low, uneven, and newly made, ridged with fresh splinters and shelves of amber heartwood. There are sharp edges everywhere. They have not yet been rounded by currents, though the tree has stood submerged longer than the lake above remembers being a valley. Pale sediment gathers in cracks but never thickens. When disturbed, it rises in slow clouds, then settles upward onto the ceiling.

Space is almost absent. Rootlets crowd the chamber in dense red-brown masses. Water moss packs every recess. Translucent leaves overlap like scales, growing without light and without any apparent source of nourishment. Some are green only at their veins; others are colorless, as though the idea of vegetation persists after color has become unnecessary. Fungal fans press from the walls in tiers. Their soft margins touch across the passages, forming doors that part reluctantly and close behind whatever passes.

Everything is wet, and then wetter beneath that. Water beads within the water: cold, perfectly round droplets hanging motionless in the flood. Each contains a minute reflection of open sky. If touched, a droplet breaks with the dry sound of a twig snapping and releases a brief downward fall of rain, though there is no air here for rain to fall through.

The darkness is nearly complete. A dimness comes from no single source. It lies on certain surfaces—a mineral blue along roots, a bruised violet in the cups of moss—and vanishes when approached directly. Light behaves like a shy animal. It is most visible at the edge of sight.

There are structures, or things that resemble them.

Small arches of woven root stand against the inner wall. Beneath some are shallow basins cut into the wood, each holding water distinguishable from the surrounding water by its heavier darkness. A stair of five steps climbs one root and ends against solid bark. Elsewhere, three upright splinters support a roof made from a single curled leaf. The shelter is no larger than a clenched hand. Nothing lives beneath it.

These constructions bear no tool marks. They may be accidents of growth, but they repeat too carefully for comfort. The arches always number seven. The stairs always have five steps, even after one is broken. If a fragment is removed, the chamber takes several seasons to correct itself. New wood swells from the wound. Moss retreats. Slowly, without motion that can be witnessed, the pattern returns.

The place has almost no current. The lake’s pressure lies over it like a great sleeping body. Movement happens by patient displacement: a root thickens over a month; a veil of moss shifts the width of a fingernail in winter; suspended grains migrate in loose constellations. At long intervals the whole chamber seems to inhale. Its walls draw apart by a fraction. The packed vegetation relaxes. Then the space closes again, gently enough that nothing tears.

The sound of this breathing is less than sound—a pressure in the teeth, a low wooden thought.

Otherwise there is quiet. Bubbles do not rise here. Water does not lap. Occasionally something clicks from deep in the heartwood, followed hours later by an answering click from a root that appears far away. The chamber complicates distance. A noise made beside the entrance may return from beneath the floor. A touch against the ceiling may stir leaves behind one’s back.

Fauna is rare and uncertain. Thin white larvae sleep in knots in the wood, waking perhaps once in a decade to consume a ring of growth. A single crustacean, blind and blue as old ice, travels the chamber’s perimeter. It is always encountered alone, always moving counterclockwise, and always missing its left forelimb—even after it molts. There may be many such animals obeying the same description, or one animal passing through folds in the room.

More elusive are the root-fish. They look like pointed leaves until they turn. Their bodies are flat, veined, and attached by hair-thin stems to the walls. Once detached, they swim for only a few minutes, seeking another place to root. During that interval they possess eyes. Once anchored, the eyes close, green over, and become buds.

Nothing hunts them. Nothing needs to. The ecology is less a chain than a closed sentence. Moss feeds on softened wood; wood grows from the drowned roots; roots draw sustenance from moss. The larvae consume years, not cellulose. The crustacean grazes on discarded directions. Pale fungi digest small pockets of distance, which is why the chamber remains larger within than without.

Near the lowest point is a circular hollow covered by overlapping leaves. Under them lies the coldest water in the chamber. It has the density of stone, though a hand can pass through it. Below its surface, if surface is the word, the drowned forest appears upside down: thousands of trunks descending toward a distant silver plain. This is not a reflection of the forest outside. Some trunks are thicker; some branch into roots at both ends. Among them stand trees that have not yet fallen into the lake.

From time to time, one rises through the hollow.

It comes as a black shoot no thicker than thread, pushing upward against gravity, pressure, and sequence. Over years it grows into the chamber, adding itself to the already impossible density. Moss immediately covers it. Root arches accommodate it. The walls edge outward by the smallest measure.

The landscape is young in this way. It is ancient wood, but newly becoming a place. Its ledges remain sharp because they have only just arrived. Its sediments have not yet learned where down is. The little stairs and shelters may be first attempts at form, rehearsals for inhabitants that do not exist. Every surface seems provisional, wet from creation.

There are rules here, although they are local and slow.

No loose thing can leave by the same opening through which it entered.

A living thing that remains through one complete inhalation of the chamber acquires a growth ring somewhere in its body.

Count the seven arches, and there will be eight. Count them again, and there will be six. Stop counting, and seven return.

Any flame imagined clearly enough becomes a patch of warm darkness. The moss avoids it.

The entrance remains behind the visitor only while it is not sought.

And water, here, is not the medium filling the landscape. Water is the landscape’s memory. It remembers rain, sap, blood, lake, cloud, and melt. In one narrow fold it is salty. In another it tastes faintly of iron or summer soil. Press an ear to certain knots and one can hear rain falling on leaves that existed before the flood.

The chamber changes little, but it is not still. Over centuries, the root-fish migrate from wall to wall. The inner forest rises thread by thread through the covered hollow. The stairs soften, then reappear sharp. The blind crustacean completes circuits no observer can count. Above, beyond the bark, the drowned trunks gradually collapse into the silt.

Inside, the tree continues constructing the forest that drowned it.

There may come a time when the chamber is packed completely—every interval occupied by root, leaf, fungus, arch, step, and cold suspended droplet. Yet this will not close it. The space will simply fold smaller between each presence, making room where room has ceased to be possible.

At the very center, perhaps, a final pocket will remain: dark, cool, no larger than a seed.

And inside that seed-sized water, another drowned tree will be waiting, split at its roots.
