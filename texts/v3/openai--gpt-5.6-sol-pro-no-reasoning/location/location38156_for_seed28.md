The city is hidden inside a horn.

Not a horn fashioned by any hand, but the shed resonating organ of something that once crossed the mineral flats: a chalk-white spiral half-buried beneath centuries of windblown salt. From outside it seems small, no more than a ribbed hill with a narrow crack along one side. The surrounding country is hot, level, and almost vacant. Pale dust runs to the horizon. Nothing casts shade except the horn, and nothing moves except veils of grit skipping over the ground.

Inside, the spiral is much larger than its outer dimensions permit.

A whole city occupies its coils.

The passage opens abruptly into warm brilliance. Every inner surface shines with a dry, nacreous glare: ivory, apricot, sulfur-yellow, flashes of pink where the walls have been polished by generations of shoulders and palms. Sunlight enters through pinholes in the shell and is gathered by dishes of beaten mica, then passed from mirror to mirror until even the deepest chambers blaze. There are few shadows, and those that remain are sharp enough to resemble cuts.

The city has no broad ground and no conventional streets. It clings to the spiral wall in narrow bands. Terraces, stairways, dwellings, storehouses, kilns, gardens, and public courts follow the long inward curve, one above another, all facing the central hollow. Seen across that hollow, the opposite neighborhoods look close enough to touch, but reaching them may require half a day’s walk around the coil.

Construction is dense, though habitation is not. Thousands of little rooms have been carved into the horn’s softer layers, yet most stand empty. Their doors are low arches closed with woven screens. Roofs are made from shell flakes pinned together with wooden pegs; bridges are braided from fiber and tendon; retaining walls are fitted from blocks of saltstone without mortar. There is no machinery more elaborate than a pulley, a treadle, a counterweight, or a wheel turned by hand. Nevertheless, the place feels thoroughly urban. Every ledge has been measured. Every dangerous slope has a rail. Marks scratched into the walls identify districts, workshops, wind rights, mirror routes, and family alcoves.

Only a few people are ever visible at once.

A gardener passes carrying a hooked knife. Three children crouch around a game played with black seeds. High above, someone tightens a bridge cable. The buildings around them suggest a population a hundred times larger, but the city has always been sparsely inhabited. Its planners build for echoes, ancestors, and possible descendants as readily as for the living. An unused room is not considered abandoned. It is a chamber waiting for its proper sound.

The vegetation is lush by the standards of this waterless world. Thick gardens spill from cracks and balconies, but nothing here is soft with moisture. The plants live on mineral vapor, heat, and charged dust.

There are forests of ribbon-leaf, whose copper-green blades unfurl from ceramic troughs of powdered limestone. Their leaves are thin as foil and edged with translucent hairs that comb nutrients from the air. Knotted shrubs push pale roots directly into the horn wall, dissolving it grain by grain with weak acids and drawing up stored salts. Cushion plants crowd the stairs in red and saffron mounds. Tall bladder trees rise in the larger courts, each bearing dozens of taut, luminous sacs filled with buoyant gas. Their trunks are hollow lattices rather than solid wood. In the heat, the sacs lift the branches toward the ceiling; after dark they contract, and the entire crown lowers with a dry sigh.

No rain falls inside the horn. No cisterns exist. There are no wells, streams, fountains, damp cellars, or cups. The inhabitants do not drink. Their bodies and crops share an older, non-aqueous chemistry based on warm oils, volatile salts, and sulfurous compounds. Cooking is the art of melting, smoking, fermenting, and crystallizing. Street vendors sell hot resin in folded leaves, brittle cakes of seed glass, and curls of spiced fungus that dissolve on the tongue into aromatic fumes.

Open flame is forbidden except in sealed kilns. Many living tissues here burn too eagerly.

The city’s defining fact is its noise.

Wind crosses the salt flats almost continuously. When it finds the crack in the buried shell, the entire structure plays.

The outer aperture produces the lowest note, a pressure more felt than heard. Deeper chambers divide it into immense harmonic bands. Galleries roar. Stairwells whistle. Thin partitions chatter against their pegs. The bridges hum beneath the feet. Every alcove has its own pitch, and the central hollow braids them together into a deafening, ceaseless chord.

Conversation at a distance is impossible. Speech survives only at arm’s length, mouth near ear, or through touch-signs tapped against wrist and shoulder. Public announcements are made with boards pressed to the shell wall: a sequence of mallet blows enters the city’s existing resonance and travels farther than any voice. Doors are tuned. Market awnings are tuned. Even clothing bears little reeds and rattling scales so that people can identify one another through the roar.

The inhabitants do not ask whether the wind is blowing. They ask which wind is blowing.

The hot eastern wind deepens the lower coil and makes the inner gardens release pollen. The high north wind excites the narrow chambers, driving insects into shelter and causing old masonry to creep. A rare wind from directly overhead finds every aperture at once. On those days the city becomes almost unbearable: railings leap beneath the hand, bladder trees bow and rise, loose salt skitters uphill in vibrating patterns, and the residents retreat into padded rooms with plugs of waxy fiber pressed into their hearing folds.

Yet the sound is not merely endured. It is the city’s calendar, power source, warning system, and law.

Treadle workshops operate during the loud hours, when shaking floors help drive saws and polishing stones. Seeds are set out in shallow trays to sort themselves by resonance. Healers diagnose fractures by listening to the patient against a singing wall. Court disputes are held in a chamber where lies are believed to introduce a faint discord—though whether the chamber truly detects deceit or merely terrifies witnesses into honesty is not settled.

Erosion proceeds everywhere, but slowly and as part of the city’s order. The horn is ancient, weathered into equilibrium with the winds that play it. Its ridges have softened; its lip has been scalloped; the deepest coil is polished like old bone. Dust pours constantly through the upper apertures and settles in drifts along quiet ledges. Workers sweep it into gardens or press it into bricks. Acid-rooted plants widen cracks, and masons follow behind them, deciding which openings should be braced and which should become new houses.

Thus the city is always being destroyed into its next form.

Its maps are temporary. A staircase may last six generations, then thin to translucence and be closed. A wall may begin to sing unexpectedly, earning preservation as a shrine. Whole districts migrate by a few handspans each century as the inner layers flake away. There are strict prohibitions against cutting across certain pale bands in the shell. These are called memory strata. When struck, they answer with voices unlike the wind—short, blurred cries, as though sounds made here long ago were trapped in the mineral and are only now working free.

Fauna is scarce. Tiny sail mites drift in the central hollow, steering on the acoustic pressure. They alight on ribbon leaves to feed on mineral pollen and are prized as omens because no one knows where they breed. Blind clicking lizards inhabit hot crevices near the kilns; each claims a resonant cavity and defends it by snapping its jaw in complex rhythms. Once or twice a year, a long, translucent animal descends from the outer crack. It has too many legs and a body light enough to billow. The city closes its screens and waits for it to pass inward, toward the final coil.

No one follows it.

At the center of the spiral lies a circular door, seamless and black, set into the shell where the passage should narrow to nothing. It is the only object in the city made of metal. No forge can soften it. No tool leaves a mark. During ordinary winds it remains silent, but in certain summer harmonics it vibrates against the palm with the rhythm of something breathing.

The innermost residents tend empty plazas around this door. Their houses are low and sparse. They cultivate forests of silver filament that bend toward the metal but never touch it. By tradition, nothing is built directly before the threshold, and no mirror may cast sunlight upon it.

This gives the center a peculiar darkness in an otherwise brilliant city: a neat, cool-looking disk surrounded by blazing shell and trembling leaves.

The oldest stories say the horn was not shed but planted. Others say the apparent city is itself an instrument, and its inhabitants—through their roads, gardens, bridges, chambers, and carefully maintained emptiness—are components placed to refine one eventual note. This is why unused spaces are preserved. This is why certain walls may not be thickened, despite the danger. This is why the population remains small enough that bodies do not dampen the resonance.

Outside, the low salt country appears lifeless under the white sun.

Inside its buried hill, terraces glow, gas trees rise and lower, dust arranges itself into figures, bridges thrum, roots dissolve ancient walls, and a nearly empty city listens amid a sound vast enough to seem like silence’s opposite.

Each year the dominant pitch drops by a fraction.

The door at the center answers a little more clearly.
