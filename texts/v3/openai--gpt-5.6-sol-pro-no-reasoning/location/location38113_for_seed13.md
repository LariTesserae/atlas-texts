The city stands high in a young range, occupying the broad floor and lower walls of a mountain basin. The peaks around it are dark, angular, and scarcely weathered. Their faces look newly broken: long planes of slate-colored stone, pale seams of quartz, and sharp crests on which almost no soil has gathered. There are few scree slopes. Fallen rock does not appear to accumulate, and the cliffs retain their fresh fractures without crumbling. From any high street, the basin can be seen almost from end to end, though its farther quarters soften into blue-gray distance.

It is a cool place, but not a harsh one. The air has the temperature of shaded stone. Breath is sometimes visible beneath arcades, and condensation pearls on roof tiles each morning, yet frost never advances and snow never settles. Clouds hang in fixed shelves against the surrounding summits. They do not visibly travel. A thin, even daylight occupies the basin from horizon to horizon, neither strengthening nor fading. Shadows are clear but mild. There is no sunrise, no noon, and no evening—only the steady illumination by which the city has always measured its work.

Water is scattered rather than plentiful. Narrow channels descend from dark openings in the cliffs, run beneath certain streets, and emerge in courtyards as clear stone-lined pools. Some channels are no wider than a hand; others feed shallow canals through the central districts. Their surfaces tremble without perceptible current. On outer terraces, dampness gathers under overhangs and among beds of moss. Rain is present in isolated places: one stair may be silvered by a fine vertical drizzle while the neighboring roof remains dry. These curtains of rain do not drift, diminish, or spread. Citizens build around them as they would build around springs or boulders.

The city’s builders have made a dense pattern from simple means. Stone is everywhere, quarried from the basin walls and set without mortar in blocks whose edges remain sharp. Buildings rise two or three stories, occasionally five where they lean into the slope. Their lower rooms are half excavated; their upper levels are timber-framed, plastered with pale clay, and roofed in split shale. Roads are paved with broad slabs. Bridges consist of single lintels or corbeled arches. Raised walkways cross garden plots and watercourses, joining neighborhoods that would otherwise be divided by ravines.

No structure depends on elaborate machinery. Doors turn on stone sockets. Water is lifted in clay jars on shoulder yokes. Wood is shaped with bronze adzes and hard stone blades; masonry is split with wedges, fire, and water. Carts are uncommon on the steep ways. Most burdens move by hand, by pack animal, or along sledges greased with moss. The largest civic works—the retaining walls, cistern courts, public stairs, and immense stepped foundations—were achieved by repetition, rope, lever, and patient labor.

Yet the city feels spacious. Construction clusters on terraces, leaving wide reaches between districts: pasture ledges, damp meadows, exposed shelves of bedrock, and groves of low, glossy trees. From a tower one sees not a continuous mass of roofs, but islands of masonry scattered through green and gray. Broad processional roads cross these open places and remain nearly empty. A person walking on them may hear the city before seeing another citizen: the faint knock of a loom, a mallet touching stone, the brief hollow note of a jar set down.

Vegetation is vigorous wherever moisture reaches. Ferns crowd the fixed rain curtains. Thick moss upholsters north-facing walls, but stops at exact boundaries where the stone becomes dry. Alder-like trees grow along the canals, their silver leaves held at unvarying angles. Between houses are gardens of bitter greens, pale beans, medicinal reeds, berry shrubs, and small fruit trees trained against warm masonry. Beyond the last terraces, tufted grass rises between black outcrops. It is lush without becoming tangled. No plant colonizes new ground, and no old growth retreats. Every grove maintains its shape.

The same permanence governs living creatures, though they still move within it. Mountain goats travel the upper streets and leap between parapets. Small deer browse in meadow districts. Long-tailed, slate-feathered birds gather on roof ridges, while bright finches occupy the gardens. Moths rest beneath eaves; beetles shine in cistern cracks; pale fish hover under bridges. There are foxes, marmots, lizards, bees, and soft-footed cats descended from no remembered arrival.

They are abundant, but their abundance does not increase or diminish. Predation occurs, eggs hatch, and berries are eaten, yet the total pattern never changes. A missing fish is somehow present again beneath the same bridge. A broken stem stands whole when no eye is upon it. Animals have habits but no seasons. The goats follow identical circuits along the cliffs; the birds leave their roosts in the same number and return after the same interval, although the light never marks an hour.

The city therefore keeps time by sequence rather than change. Bells are absent, being too loud and too dependent on metal. Instead, attendants walk prescribed routes and strike suspended boards once with padded hammers. One district wakes when the white goats cross its eastern stair. Bakers uncover their ovens when condensation forms on a particular bronze plate, though the droplets never seem to grow. Market stalls open after the canal birds make their circuit of the central pool. These events do not vary. The city’s calendar is a loop of observed arrangements.

Nothing ages here in the ordinary sense. Timber does not rot beyond its present stains. Tool edges become neither sharper nor duller. The skin of an elder remains as it is; children remain children. Wounds can be suffered but settle back into their former state. A wall dismantled for new construction is found intact when attention returns to its old site, while the newly laid stones remain where they were placed. This makes building possible but unsettling: the city can gain structures without losing materials, and demolition is only temporary.

Over immeasurable repetition, this rule has produced urban layers that do not bury one another. New stairs branch from old stairs. Annexes occupy courtyards while the courtyards persist around them. Rooms overlap in ways that should be geometrically impossible. A doorway may open onto an ancient lane at one hour of the civic sequence and a recently built granary at another, although neither place changes. Residents navigate by memorized conditions: enter after the finches settle; cross while the canal is reflecting the cloud shelf; do not use the third arch when the goats are below it.

The strangeness is accepted as a property of stone and distance. Surveyors carry knotted cords and wax tablets, recording not merely length but circumstance. Maps resemble woven nets, each route tied to an animal movement, a patch of condensation, or a communal task. There are no compasses. Direction is given toward a peak, a rain curtain, a quarry face, or one of the motionless clouds.

Despite its size and its many animals, the basin is mostly quiet. Water makes almost no sound. Wind is too weak to stir the groves. Voices are kept low because sound travels cleanly between the fresh mountain walls; a shout in the upper wards can cross the entire basin and return unchanged. The people dislike echoes. An echo is one of the few things that seems to multiply here, and old custom holds that too many repetitions might make a word permanent.

So workshops are muffled with felt. Markets use hand signs alongside speech. Hooves are wrapped when animals cross ceremonial paving. Even festivals are quiet: colored cords hung between roofs, aromatic leaves crushed underfoot, masked walkers passing through the fixed rain. Music is made with breathy flutes, gut strings, and clay vessels tapped by fingers. It blends with wingbeats and the occasional small crack of stone from the quarries—a crack that sounds newly made, though the mountain face afterward is exactly as sharp and whole as before.

Beyond the outermost wall, paths climb toward the passes. They cross damp pockets filled with moss, then long dry ledges where scattered flowers grow from fresh fissures. The passes can be reached, but they never lead out. A traveler descending the far side eventually sees the same basin from another summit: the same cloud shelves, the same islands of roofs, the same pale channels and angular peaks. The city may be enclosed by mountains, or it may be the only valley repeated around itself.

No one has proved which. The expeditions return with identical stones, familiar seeds, and reports of distant districts whose streets correspond to streets at home. Their pack animals resume their old circuits. Their footprints remain briefly in the damp ground and then become the footprints that were already there.

The place is not dead, and it is not frozen. It is full of movement, labor, appetite, craft, and life. But all movement occurs inside a complete arrangement. Nothing tends toward ruin or renewal. The young mountains stay young. The city becomes more intricate without becoming older. The rain continues falling in its narrow, patient columns, and beneath the unwavering daylight, every bright droplet occupies the whole of time.
