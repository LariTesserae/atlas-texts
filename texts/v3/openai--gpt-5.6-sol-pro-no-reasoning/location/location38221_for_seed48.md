The city lies on a low, hot coast where the land has never quite decided whether it is solid ground or tidal flat. From a distance it appears large: several districts of brick mills, concrete warehouses, storage tanks, rail yards, and workers’ blocks spread across the basin. Yet almost every structure stands alone. Broad tracts of mud, shallow water, and stained paving separate one cluster from the next. Roads cross these vacancies on embankments, and pipelines travel for miles on rusted trestles, casting thin shadows over the flats.

There are no trees. No weeds rise through the cracked asphalt, no moss softens the culverts, and no grass holds the soil along the drainage channels. The absence is complete enough to alter one’s sense of scale. With nothing leafy to interrupt the eye, every distance is exposed: loading cranes a kilometer away, the distant stacks of a refinery, the pale rectangular faces of apartment towers on the opposite shore.

The ground consists of old industrial fill pressed over marine clay. It has settled unevenly during decades of heat and flooding. Streets dip into permanent pools. Rail lines bend gently where their beds have subsided. Concrete aprons tilt toward drains that cannot quite empty them, and along the oldest canals the retaining walls have been rounded and undercut by generations of tides. Everything is weathered but stable in its weathering. Rust has thickened around bolts and ladder rungs; bricks have lost their corners; painted warnings survive only in sheltered recesses. The city has accommodated decay rather than defeated it.

Water is nearly everywhere, though rarely deep. It lies in gutters, cooling basins, wheel ruts, abandoned foundations, and long evaporation ponds. It beads on metal each morning despite the heat, condensed from the saturated air, then runs in slow tracks down the sides of tanks. Some buildings seem to weep continuously through seams in their masonry. Beneath the pavement, brackish groundwater waits close to the surface. When heavy rain arrives, it rises through storm drains before the runoff can descend.

The water is warm. In enclosed basins it can be almost hot, carrying a skin of oil, mineral foam, or drifting insect cases. Open channels steam faintly where factory discharge enters them. Salt crusts form on the margins during clear periods and dissolve again after the next rain. The mud is black, soft, and deeply scored by tracks.

Those tracks belong to the city’s dominant population.

Animals occupy everything left unused by people. They fill the open spaces so thoroughly that the emptiness of the architecture becomes misleading. Tens of thousands of shorebirds stand on the flats, densely packed around the shallowest water. Gulls line the roofs, parapets, crane booms, cables, and lamp standards. Pigeons crowd the empty upper floors of factories. Bats sleep in expansion joints beneath bridges. Rats live in the warm galleries around steam pipes and emerge at dusk in dark, orderly streams.

In the canals there are mullet, eels, crabs, and small silver fish adapted to low oxygen and abrupt changes of salinity. They congregate near outfalls where warmth and nutrients enter the water. Herons wait along concrete ledges. Cormorants spread their wings on rusted handrails. Feral dogs travel between districts in loose packs, crossing rail bridges and service roads with the confidence of commuters. Cats inhabit the dryer substations and prey upon the birds and rats. In flooded basements, mosquitoes breed in numbers that make unprotected skin untenable after sunset.

Nothing about the animals is unnatural. Their abundance comes from ordinary opportunities multiplied across a city with few inhabitants and much wasted heat. Grain leaks from conveyors. Fish gather in warm effluent. Refuse collects behind barriers. Roofs provide cliffs without vegetation or terrestrial predators. The industrial system, still operating at reduced capacity, has become a vast accidental feeding mechanism.

The heat imposes the city’s schedule. At noon, exposed metal cannot be touched barehanded. Air above roofs and rail beds shimmers, and the wet atmosphere turns the basin into a dim gray chamber. The sun is seldom seen directly. Moisture, smoke, and fine mineral dust merge into a luminous overcast that gives weak shadows even at midday. Sodium lamps remain on beneath bridges. Red aircraft beacons blink above the stacks, their light blurred by haze.

During the hottest hours, activity contracts. Dogs sleep beneath tanker cars. Birds gather in the shade of elevated pipes. People move through enclosed corridors, service tunnels, and cooled control rooms. Pumps continue to turn, but the broader city seems paused.

The quiet is deceptive. There is rarely one loud source nearby, only distant, separated sounds: a compressor starting in another district; the metallic knock of a loose sheet in the wind; water spilling from a culvert; a train coupling somewhere beyond the tanks. Fans produce low tones too steady to attract notice. Electrical substations hum. Now and then a siren sounds across the basin, flattened by humidity until its origin is impossible to place.

The animals contribute less noise than their numbers suggest. The great bird colonies murmur, shuffle, and occasionally rise in a sudden storm of wings. Mosquitoes form a thin pressure around the ears. Crabs scrape under debris. At night the dogs call across the flooded lots, one group answering another over several kilometers. Then silence returns—not true silence, but the settled quiet of systems running far apart.

Rain changes the place rapidly. Storm fronts arrive from the coast as dark bands. Wind moves dust and feathers ahead of them, and loose plastic travels down empty avenues. The first drops strike hot roofs with separate, heavy clicks. Within minutes the sound becomes continuous. Water pours from broken gutters, runs down exterior stairwells, and fills the depressions in subsided streets. The canals rise brown against their walls. Pumps engage automatically, discharging broad arcs into channels already near capacity.

Birds lift from the flats before the deepest flooding. Rats climb cable runs and maintenance ladders. Fish pass through opened floodgates into yards and under buildings. Crabs appear on warehouse floors. The city’s boundaries become temporarily aquatic: roads turn into causeways, courtyards into lagoons, and the rail embankments into long islands.

Afterward, the heat returns. Steam rises from black paving. Every surface sweats. The animals redistribute themselves as water drains from higher ground and concentrates in the lowest basins. Shorebirds follow the retreating edges, feeding in the exposed mud. Gulls descend on whatever the storm has uncovered. Predators move behind them. The whole city rearranges around a few centimeters of changing water.

Human habitation persists in compact enclaves, mostly on upper floors and on the higher artificial ridges. The inhabited districts are connected by rail, elevated road, and covered walkways. Below them are workshops, pump stations, ice plants, repair depots, and old manufacturing halls still producing cables, machine parts, canned fish, fertilizer, and construction panels. Much of the machinery is oversized for the remaining population. A single active turbine hall may stand amid six abandoned ones; one illuminated loading dock may face acres of dark quay.

The principal rule of the place is that nothing at ground level remains dry for long. Doors open inward from raised thresholds. Electrical equipment begins well above the floor. Vehicles are parked on ramps. Warehouses keep their goods on steel racks even when the concrete beneath appears dusty. Every district maintains painted flood marks, and no one mistakes the lowest one for a guarantee.

A second rule is that the animals have right of first occupation wherever maintenance stops. A silent conveyor becomes a nesting ledge within days. An unused room fills with pigeons, rats, or bats according to its height and temperature. Standing water acquires larvae. An unlatched storehouse is entered by dogs. Human space is not defended once but continuously, with screens, shutters, netting, sealed bins, noise makers, traps, and the simple habit of never leaving food exposed.

At the outer edge of the city, the built land dissolves into tidal infrastructure. Concrete flood cells, abandoned salt pans, dredged channels, and low islands extend toward the dim sea. Refineries and port cranes recede into haze. There is no green horizon—only water, mud, mineral crust, steel, and sky.

At low tide, the flats broaden until they seem almost dry. Birds spread over them in immense stippled fields. At high tide, those fields vanish, and the flocks move onto roofs, dikes, and gantries, covering the structures so densely that from afar the city appears to have grown a restless gray surface. Then one disturbance passes through them. Thousands rise together, wheel once in the hot, wet air, and settle elsewhere among the old machines.
