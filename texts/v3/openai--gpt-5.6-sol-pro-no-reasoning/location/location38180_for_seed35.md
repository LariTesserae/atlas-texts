The chamber lies just beneath the surface, though “beneath” is misleading. The roof is not earth but a single cooled crust of black mineral, thin enough in places to transmit the pressure of the world above. Nothing passes through it—not rain, not roots, not daylight—yet the ceiling carries a dull amber translucence, as if noon has been fossilized inside the stone.

The chamber is roughly cathedral-sized and almost completely occupied.

Thousands of mineral columns rise from floor to roof. They are fresh-edged, dark, and densely set, with barely enough space between them for a person to turn sideways. Some are hexagonal, some split into blades, and some branch abruptly into angular elbows that seem machined rather than grown. There are no rounded surfaces. The place is geologically young, or at least preserves the manners of youth: fractures are crisp; fallen shards retain needle points; dust has not had time to soften anything.

Yet nothing here changes.

No crack lengthens. No powder settles. The same chips hang on the same ledges. Warm air occupies the narrow aisles without circulating, uniformly dry and uniformly pressurized. There are no cool corners, no furnace mouths, no gradients by which one could navigate. A thermometer gives the same reading beside the floor, against the ceiling, inside a machine housing, or held in an open hand. The chamber seems not merely stable but complete, every transfer already compensated.

Water has no place in its chemistry. The floor is a hard salt-metal eutectic that never sweats or deliquesces. Pale incrustations resembling lichen are actually colonies built from silicone membranes and powdered copper. They do not drink. They feed on minute differences in electrical potential between neighboring crystals—differences so small and so perfectly replenished that the colonies neither visibly spread nor die.

These growths are the chamber’s nearest equivalent to vegetation. They form thin bronze rosettes in protected angles, wiry black tufts along insulated seams, and occasional translucent fans no larger than a hand. Their fronds are stiff. Touching one produces a taste of tin at the back of the tongue, even through gloves. Some bear what look like seeds: tiny ceramic beads threaded onto conductive hairs. The beads remain attached year after year, waiting for a disturbance the chamber’s equilibrium does not permit.

Between the columns stand the scattered works of an industry without an obvious plant.

A squat compressor occupies one recess, bolted directly into the mineral. Three meters away, a flywheel turns inside a cage. Elsewhere there are switch cabinets, ceramic conduits, ribbed transformers, stamped inspection plates, and narrow troughs filled with graphite pellets. None of these pieces connect in a way a surface engineer would recognize. Pipes terminate against unpierced stone. Cables emerge from housings only to enter the same housing again. Pistons drive no shafts. Gauges have no needles, only shallow dishes in which metallic insects sit facing fixed directions.

Every machine is operating.

The compressor hammers. The flywheel shrieks at constant speed. Relays chatter in dense, disciplined bursts. Transformers produce a chest-deep electrical bellow, and hidden mechanisms strike the columns with an endless complexity of taps. The noise is immense within the confined volume. It should fluctuate as parts wear, animals interfere, or echoes overlap. It does not. Every sound returns in precisely the same form, not as a repeating recording but as a continuous condition of the chamber, like temperature or pressure.

Conversation is impossible. Shouting alters nothing. A spoken word can be felt vibrating in the speaker’s skull, but no one else hears it—not because it is drowned out, but because the chamber’s acoustic field seems already full. There is no vacant interval into which a new sound can enter.

The animals have adapted to this fullness.

They are everywhere.

Glossy, many-legged creatures pack the crevices between columns. Flat-bodied grazers cling to the undersides of ledges, scraping copper colonies with combs of glassy teeth. Pale sacs pulse inside protective wire cages, each attended by swarms of pin-sized black flyers. Long jointed animals, narrow enough to pass through gaps no wider than a wrist, stand folded around machine casings. Their limbs resemble measuring instruments: calipers, dividers, hooked probes. Larger fauna hang from the roof like bundled cables, warm and dry to the touch.

Few possess eyes. Vision has little value in the amber dimness, where columns block every view after a few paces. Instead they bear tuning forks, pressure pits, conductive whiskers, and taut membranes that quiver in exact sympathy with particular machines. Each species belongs to a frequency. When its machine sounds, the corresponding animals feed, mate, or orient themselves. Since the machines never alter their rhythm, the animals never need to improvise.

Their abundance does not produce chaos. Bodies fill almost every unused space, but they seldom collide. Each creature follows a permanent route through the chamber: around one column, under a conduit, across a warm housing, then back again. Generations occupy the same tracks. A beetlelike grazer may crawl in a tight loop over a patch of bronze film, eating at one edge while the film reforms at the other. A roof animal continually releases a litter of dry, folded young; at the opposite end of its body, adults return and are reabsorbed. The number of creatures remains constant.

Predation also occurs without consequence. Needle-mouthed hunters seize soft-bodied runners in the darker aisles. The runners are consumed; simultaneously, elsewhere in the chamber, identical runners unfold from cracks in the salt-metal floor. No one has found eggs in those cracks, or tunnels beneath them. The floor appears solid.

There are signs that people once entered, though not that they lived here. Small iron platforms have been wedged between columns. A ladder descends from a sealed hatch and stops two meters above the floor. Numbered porcelain tags mark twelve machines, but the sequence runs 4, 4, 9, 2, 4, 4, 9, 2. Near the compressor stands a narrow booth with a chair, a dead lamp, and a window facing solid rock. The chair is worn smooth at the arms. Its dust never shifts.

The chamber has boundaries. One can walk its perimeter in less than half an hour, squeezing through warm, crowded passages where the roof descends. There are no branching caves. The outer walls curve inward cleanly, enclosing the whole system like the lining of an organ. Instruments suggest only a few meters of material separate it from the surface, but drilling fails strangely: bits emerge from the wall at the point where they entered, hot and blunted, without having turned.

There is only one apparent entrance, the hatch above the useless ladder. It opens onto a shaft full of black mineral teeth. The teeth are sharp and unweathered. Climbing among them is possible, and the amber brightness increases toward the top, but the climb never brings the surface closer. After an hour, the hatch remains immediately below. After a day, it does too. Descending takes a single step.

Nothing in the chamber decays, though many things are visibly damaged. A snapped column remains snapped. A corroded panel remains corroded to exactly the same depth. The machines bear dents, scoring, and heat discoloration, all fresh-looking, none progressing. The place has the appearance of an accident that has only just happened and will have only just happened forever.

It may be a factory, though nothing leaves it.

It may manufacture the constancy that contains it: holding the warm dry air at one temperature, keeping every animal at its appointed abundance, preserving each sharp edge, maintaining the impossible wall of sound. Perhaps the machines do not make objects but prevent events. Their product is the chamber’s unchanging present.

In the darkest central aisle, where columns nearly touch, there is a small clear space. No animals enter it. No mineral colonies grow there. A waist-high pedestal rises from the floor, bearing a single red industrial switch beneath a cracked safety cover. The label is legible despite the dimness:

**BEGIN.**

The switch is already down.

It cannot be lifted.
