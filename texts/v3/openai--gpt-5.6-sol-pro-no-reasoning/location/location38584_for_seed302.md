At a high elevation, where the air should be thin and clean, a broad basin lies packed almost solid with stone.

There is no soil. Nothing roots, clings, flowers, or decays. The basin’s floor is a continuous congestion of pale mineral forms: low shelves fitted beneath leaning slabs, blunt columns crowded against fluted walls, nodules nested by the thousand in hollows of chalk-white rock. Even the open distances feel full. Ridges overlap ridges, and every gap reveals another surface behind it—faceted, pitted, glazed, or furred with crystals too inorganic and regular to mistake for life.

Water is everywhere without gathering into a river. It occupies seams and dimples, films the sloping stone, and stands in narrow black channels scarcely wider than cracks. Moisture condenses constantly from the cool air. Each exposed face weeps from countless pores, so that the entire country shines as if rain has just passed, though there are no clouds and no weather can be seen approaching.

The light is severe.

A white sky presses brilliance down into the basin, and the wet rock returns it from every angle. Smooth planes flare like mirrors; crystal-packed cavities scatter it into colorless halos. Shadows exist only deep inside fissures, where they are blue-gray and apparently bottomless. There is no soft cover for the eye, no leaf, dust, smoke, or lichen to absorb the glare. Far slopes fade not into haze but into increasing whiteness, each densely serrated horizon laid over the next.

The stone is old enough to have lost every fresh edge. Water has worked it into settled shapes: scallops, basins, ribs, and channels whose curves fit one another with the inevitability of bone joints. Yet the landscape does not seem actively eroded. Its surfaces have reached a kind of agreement with the water. The rivulets follow permanent routes; the pools hold permanent levels. Droplets swell beneath ledges, fall, and are replaced at precisely the same interval. Nothing deepens. Nothing collapses. The basin is weathered but no longer weathering.

Its sound is overwhelming.

Water alone could not account for it. The trickles are too small, the falls too short. Nevertheless, the basin roars continuously—a broad, crushing note that can be felt through teeth and sternum. Every wet cavity resonates. Every narrow channel contributes a high metallic thread; every hollow column holds a lower tone. Together they produce something like an immense cataract heard from within the rock itself.

There are no gusts in the sound, no pauses, no rhythm of increase and recession. It remains exactly as loud at dawnless noon as beneath the unchanging white sky of any other hour. Crossing the basin does not seem to bring one nearer its source. The roar belongs everywhere equally. A hand pressed to the ground feels the same fine vibration on ridge, shelf, and flooded hollow.

In places the water rises through fields of closely packed mineral chimneys. Each chimney is no taller than a knee, chalky outside and glass-dark within, and each is filled to its lip. The water does not overflow. Its surface trembles under the omnipresent vibration, breaking the bright sky into minute geometric fragments. Elsewhere, shallow terraces cover entire slopes, tens of thousands of stone rims laid one above another. Every terrace carries a skin of clear water. From a distance these slopes resemble silver scales, but there is no movement across them—only fixed brilliance.

The coolness is constant and penetrating rather than bitter. Wet stone draws heat from whatever touches it. Condensation forms even on surfaces already immersed, making beads beneath the water like lenses within lenses. There is no ice. There is no steam. The world seems held exactly between those possibilities.

Nothing green has ever marked it. No spores cloud the pools, no microbial stain darkens the runoff, and no film makes the stones slippery. Even decay is absent. The water has no taste of earth, root, or organic residue; it tastes only of dissolved stone—faintly metallic, faintly saline, clean to the point of sterility.

Yet the basin is not entirely without creatures.

Very rarely, something can be seen moving through the narrow submerged channels. It is translucent and flat, no longer than a finger, with a hard central line and lateral membranes that quiver against the current. Whether there are several such animals or only one repeatedly glimpsed is difficult to determine. They never enter the open pools. They pass beneath the ledges, turn sideways through impossible seams, and vanish into water-filled rock.

No tracks follow them. Nothing feeds on them. Nothing obvious could feed them.

High above the basin, pale specks sometimes cross the light. They might be animals circling at an enormous altitude, but they never flap and never descend. Their paths repeat so exactly that, after long observation, one begins to expect each speck at its appointed place: emerging beyond the western ridge, describing the same shallow arc, disappearing into the eastern glare. Like the droplets and trembling pools, they appear less alive than included—small motions permitted within a landscape whose total state never alters.

There are no ruins and no signs that hands have tested the stone. No path is worn more deeply than another. The shelves sometimes resemble stairs, the chimneys resemble vessels, and the long galleries between ridges resemble streets crowded with silent monuments, but these likenesses dissolve at close range. Every apparent design is only the patient grammar of water and mineral.

Beyond the central basin, the land rises into compressed ranges. Their slopes are not bare in the sense of emptiness; they are overloaded with fins, chambers, sinkholes, and overlapping stone folds. Water gleams in every depression. The heights enclose one another so densely that distance becomes difficult to judge. A wall that seems a mile away may be separated by only a few crowded ravines, while a nearby ridge may prove to be the first of fifty.

Still, there is no evidence of change. No pebble falls. No watercourse shifts. The bright sky never clouds, though moisture continues to condense. Pools neither fill nor evaporate. The rare channel-creature neither grows nor dies where it can be witnessed. Even the deafening resonance is perfectly unwavering, an endless event with no beginning and no expenditure.

This is the place’s quiet rule beneath all its noise: everything may move, but nothing may become different.

Water falls without removing stone. Creatures pass without arriving. Light strikes without warming. The basin roars without exhausting its voice.

And across the high, wet, crowded country, every surface shines in the same permanent instant.
