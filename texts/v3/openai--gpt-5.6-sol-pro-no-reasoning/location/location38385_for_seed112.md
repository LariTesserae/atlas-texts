The high country is one plant.

From a distance it resembles a continent-sized plateau of pale green felt, raised above the mineral lowlands on thousands of branching trunks. Seen from beneath, however, there is no plateau in any geological sense. The apparent ground is a woven crown: roots, runners, compressed leaf-sheaths, hollow spars and centuries of wind-packed pollen, all locked together into a living shelf several kilometers thick. Its upper surface reaches into cold, brilliant air. Its underside hangs over darkness.

There is almost no water here. No rivers score the surface, no rain gathers in hollows, and nothing in the tissue would be recognized as sap by an ordinary botanist. The plant’s circulation is an anhydrous suspension of oils, liquefied salts and volatile hydrocarbons. At the prevailing temperature it flows slowly, with the viscosity of wax. Where a stem is broken, the exposed channels do not bleed. They smoke, crystallize, and seal beneath a white scar.

Moisture is dangerous. A rare ice-bearing object from the upper atmosphere can burn a black cavity kilometers across as its meltwater triggers frantic, poisonous growth. For generations afterward, the region remains a tangled tumor of fronds before the dry winds and the plateau’s own regulatory blights bring it back into balance.

The upper country appears sparse despite being almost entirely alive. Its principal organism has become the terrain, leaving vast distances between recognizable forms. Low, silver scales pave the open reaches. Here and there stand leaf-towers: solitary blades hundreds of meters tall, translucent at their edges, spaced so widely that each commands its own horizon. Their surfaces are ribbed like cooling fins. They collect light, electrical charge, carbon dust and traces of nitrogen, but no dew.

Nothing casts a soft shadow. The sun is hard and near-seeming in the thin air, and every polished leaf throws a blade of white radiance across the plain. The sky has a saturated violet cast at noon. By late afternoon it becomes almost black behind the luminous crowns, while the vegetative floor remains glaring with stored fluorescence. Scar tissue is chalk-white; young growth is blue-green; old photosynthetic skins weather toward pewter. The brightest tracts are the deadest ones, where salt armor lies over dormant tissue and reflects the sky.

The land is established but never still.

Wind has worked upon it for ages, combing everything eastward. It has rounded the oldest ridges, hollowed chambers through the fibrous substrate and carved long grooves in the leaf-scales. Yet erosion does not simply reduce the plateau. The plant responds. A hollow opened by wind becomes a lung; a torn margin thickens into a load-bearing rib; dust caught in a groove induces a line of new blades. The weathering and the growth have negotiated so long that their struggle has become the region’s anatomy.

The result is a landscape full of mature forms: polished saddles, leaning escarpments, long galleries beneath overlapping tissues, and great circular clearings where the crown flexes around ancient wounds. Nothing looks young even when it was made that morning. New structures emerge already creased, armored and asymmetrical, grown according to stresses older than themselves.

The noise is overwhelming.

Every leaf-tower is an organ pipe. Wind enters slits along its base, accelerates through branching cavities and leaves by apertures that open and shut under pressure. A single tower can produce a note low enough to disturb the lungs. Across the plateau, tens of thousands sound at once. The fundamental roar never ceases, but inside it pass waves of pitch: continental chords moving with weather fronts, abrupt metallic shrieks when vents constrict, and deep pulses conducted through the living ground.

The smaller leaves add their own sound. They do not rustle. Their mineral edges strike one another like loose ceramic plates. Fields of them clatter in advancing bands, so that a gust can be heard coming hours before it arrives. During electrical weather the tall blades snap charge from tip to tip, and branching violet discharges run hundreds of kilometers along the horizon with a noise like continuous tearing.

There are also engines, though none were built.

They grow in the underside and at the margins where the plateau’s tissues are under greatest strain. Bulbous chambers draw in the thin atmosphere, compress it through rings of contractile fiber, separate useful gases across catalytic membranes, and exhaust the remainder in hammering jets. The earliest chambers may once have served only for respiration. Over evolutionary time they became pumps; the pumps became refineries; the refineries became a metabolism operating on an industrial scale.

There are cracking organs that split hydrocarbons under pressure, furnace-knots where mineral catalysts glow red inside insulating bark, and long conveyor veins filled with pellets of concentrated carbon. Seed components are produced in separate districts and transported for assembly. Protective wax is synthesized beneath the western reaches and pumped upslope. Nitrogen compounds are fixed in lightning belts. Waste salts are extruded as bright cliffs along the plateau rim.

No overseer directs this industry. Chemical thresholds, pressure changes and electrical signaling coordinate it. Its order is the order of an ant colony or a storm: distributed, responsive and occasionally catastrophic. A failed valve can starve a province. A surge in one vascular trunk may trigger thousands of dormant vents. Production migrates with the seasons, and an entire horizon can wake into mechanical activity without any visible cause.

When this happens, the ground trembles. Seams open in ruler-straight lines. Veins rise from the substrate as pale, flexing pipes, carry hot oils toward a growth front, and sink again after the transfer. Circular diaphragms the size of lakes inflate and collapse. Forests of temporary chimneys force powdered salts into the air, making brilliant plumes that stream for days. Then scavenger growth sweeps over the abandoned apparatus, dismantling it molecule by molecule until only a shallow depression remains.

These events give the upper country weather of its own. Heat from the metabolic districts produces towering convection in an otherwise cold atmosphere. Dry cyclones form over furnace-knots and drag veils of pollen into spirals visible from orbit. The pollen is not soft dust but electrostatically charged platelets, glittering like mica. It accumulates on the leeward sides of ridges until a shift in voltage sends whole dunes flowing uphill.

Pressure storms pass through the tissues as well as over them. During a major redistribution, the plateau rises and falls in broad swells. A traveler standing still might see the horizon lift by a hundred meters, accompanied by a deepening tone from the wind-organs. Old cracks close. New ravines split open. Far away, a section of the crown may overturn completely, exposing kilometers of ivory root-lattice to the sun before fresh skin races across it.

The fauna live by reading these changes.

Most are light-bodied and broad-winged, because walking is dangerous on a surface that may become vertical without warning. Flocks of translucent grazers drift above the scale-fields, scraping microscopic films from the leaves. Their wings are stiff membranes stretched on carbon spars, and they steer as much by electrical field as by wind. When startled they turn simultaneously, flashing white, then vanish edge-on against the sky.

Heavier animals inhabit the sheltered grooves. Six-legged browsers clamp themselves to the substrate with magnetic pads and feed on sacrificial growths produced for them. The plateau tolerates these herds because they carry catalytic metals between distant tissues. Their mouths stain red with iron-rich pollen. In calm intervals they migrate along pressure ridges in files thousands long, but when the ground begins to resonate, they drop flat and lock every joint.

Predators are mostly listeners. They crouch in hollow stems, insensitive to the general thunder but alert to minute interruptions within it. Some imitate the note of a healthy leaf-tower, luring flying grazers toward a false refuge. Others travel through the vascular spaces, pursuing animals by changes in pressure. There are burrowers that never encounter open air and airborne creatures that may never land during their entire lives.

At the highest margins, where the living shelf thins into branching promontories, immense filter animals hang beneath the continent. They resemble dark, folded sails. The exhaust of the plant’s engines keeps them aloft against the underside while they harvest spores and minute fauna from the jets. Their calls are inaudible amid the surface roar, but can be felt through the feet as slow, questioning tremors.

There are no ruins, roads or tools. Apparent artifacts repeatedly prove to be organs: smooth black cylinders are seed cases; ranked pylons are temporary heat exchangers; regular fields of hexagonal pits are the scars left by reproductive bladders. The landscape has independently arrived at valves, turbines, distillation columns, electrical grids and production lines, yet it has no category for machinery. Industry here is anatomy.

Nor is there a wilderness separate from that industry. The untouched state of the place is one of manufacture.

At intervals measured in decades, the plateau reproduces. Vast districts shut down. Migratory animals flee toward the rim as stored compounds are diverted into a few deep chambers. The living ground becomes cold enough for atmospheric gases to frost its surface. Then the chambers begin to beat.

For weeks, the whole continent rings.

At the climax, armored seed-masses are accelerated through grown barrels and fired into the upper atmosphere. Each launch flattens leaf-towers for hundreds of kilometers and sends pressure fractures racing across the crown. Most seeds fall into the dry lowlands and die. A few enter the high winds and travel around the world. Rarer still, one strikes another elevated shelf, punches into its tissues and begins a slow chemical negotiation: parasite, offspring, graft or invader according to the compatibility of their oils.

Afterward comes a quieter season, though quiet here is relative. The great engines idle. Clattering leaves settle. Animals emerge into a cool brilliance full of drifting salt. Wounds knit themselves into dark seams, and the exhausted launch organs are digested from within.

For a time, the plateau seems almost like a plain.

Then somewhere beyond the curve of the horizon, a pressure front begins. The light changes as billions of leaf-scales tilt together. A bass note enters the ground, too deep to hear at first, and the herds lock themselves down.

The note rises.

One after another, the towers answer.
