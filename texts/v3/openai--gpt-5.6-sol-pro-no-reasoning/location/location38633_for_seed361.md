High on the shoulder of an old plateau, inside a country otherwise burnt pale and level by age, there is a narrow cleft called the Choir.

It is easy to miss. From the open uplands the entrance resembles a crack between two leaning slabs, barely wide enough for a person to turn sideways. Warm wind moves over it without entering. Thorn grass and low silver shrubs grow sparsely around the rim, each plant isolated by several strides of ocher stone. There are no trees to announce water, no plume of mist, only a black seam and, if one stands close, a tremendous sound coming from below.

The cleft descends no more than thirty meters, though its folded passages make it feel deeper. Its walls are the rounded remnants of a mountain older than the plateau itself. Nothing sharp survives. The rock has been polished through eras into hollows, waists, blind bowls, and smooth ribs the color of old bone. Some surfaces are glassy where vanished torrents carried fine grit; others flake softly beneath the fingers, exposing bands of umber, green-gray, and mineral violet. The whole formation seems less broken than exhausted—as though erosion has already tried every possible shape and left the stone in its final posture.

Heat gathers in the upper passages. Lower down, warmth remains but loses its harshness. Moisture arrives intermittently and from several directions. Brief rains fall on the plateau and vanish through porous rock, reappearing days later as droplets on the cleft walls. At dawn, condensation pearls on shaded stone. Once or twice in a season, an underground pulse fills the lowest channel with knee-deep brown water, which drains before noon and leaves separate black pools in the worn basins.

Thus the Choir is not wet, exactly. It is a place where water is always either expected or remembered.

Vegetation occupies every surface that can hold dampness, but it does so in islands. Mats of emerald liverwort line one wall while the opposite wall remains bare for ten meters. Ferns unfold from seams in the rock, their fronds broad, thin, and almost translucent. There are pendant roots with no visible parent plant, small waxy creepers, cups of blue fungus, and a vine whose leaves close whenever shadows pass overhead. Higher ledges carry dry tufts and thorn fans. Lower hollows hold lush miniature thickets shoulder-high, separated by long rooms of naked stone.

The sparseness gives each growth an exaggerated presence. A single fern colony becomes a forest. One flowering stalk, pale and taller than a person, scents an entire chamber with pepper and fermenting fruit. Fallen leaves do not form litter; they are removed too quickly.

For the cleft is crowded beyond reason with animals.

They fill the place without making it look full.

Most cling to walls, hide beneath films of water, pass through holes too small to notice, or travel overhead in such rapid streams that the eye mistakes them for shifting shade. Beetles with lacquered red backs browse the moss. Pale crabs live in damp fissures far above any permanent pool. Thousands of narrow-winged insects rise at once whenever sunlight reaches the floor, their flight producing a dry electrical roar. Thumb-sized frogs call from mineral cups, each cup holding only a mouthful of water. Long-legged lizards patrol the open stone, stopping to lift their feet from its warmth. Small furred animals with folded ears leap between root curtains. They are hunted by ribbon-snakes, the ribbon-snakes by cliff birds, and the cliff birds by something seldom seen that leaves three-pointed footprints in condensation on vertical surfaces.

Nothing here is silent. Wings hiss. Frogs hammer. Beetle shells tick against rock. Hidden mammals whistle in warning, and predators answer with abrupt wooden clacks. Water falls from ledge to ledge with the amplified force of rainfall in a cavern. When the afternoon wind crosses the entrance, pressure changes inside the branching cleft and the entire formation begins to moan.

This is the origin of its name, though the sound is not only geological.

Across the deeper walls stand scattered constructions: thin arches, incomplete rings, little bridges spanning gaps no wider than a hand. Some appear to be ceramic, others tarnished metal, but none can be chipped or scratched. They have been embedded so long that mineral curtains have grown over them. There are perhaps twenty in the whole cleft, with generous emptiness between them. At first they resemble fragments of an ancient installation whose purpose has been lost.

At close range they reveal themselves to be functioning.

Each ring holds a field too subtle to see directly. Condensation curves toward it. Gnats alter course. Sounds entering one side emerge elsewhere in the cleft, delayed, enlarged, or changed in pitch. A frog calling into a ring near the lowest pool may be heard from a stone arch overhead as a bass note powerful enough to tremble dust loose. The network gathers the countless small noises of the animals and routes them through the rock.

No cables connect the structures. No source of energy is apparent. Their surfaces contain depths that do not agree with their thickness: beneath the dull outer skin move dim geometries, receding farther than the plateau is wide. Moss avoids some of them and colonizes others. One ring flowers each rainy season, producing black filaments that behave like roots until touched, when they retract into solid material.

The devices seem neither ruined nor maintained. They are settling, very slowly, toward some completed state. Their operation changes with the ecology around them. When insect populations swell, new acoustic passages open. If a pool dries, the nearest arch stops transmitting sound and begins cooling the stone instead. Predators learn to use these changes. So do prey. Certain frogs call notes that emerge from distant rings as false alarms. A hunting bird can strike one arch with its beak and flush moths from another chamber. The little folded-eared mammals sleep beside a device that bends the cries of snakes away from their nest.

The technology has entered the food web so completely that it is no longer clear whether it was built for the animals, altered by them, or merely interpreted by generations of instinct.

At midday, ordinary sunlight falls almost vertically into the upper crack. There are no supernatural colors, no permanent gloom. Stone is stone; leaves are green; shallow water reflects the white sky. This normality makes the stranger events difficult to dismiss. A bird flies into a narrow beam and its shadow exits through three different passages. A swarm sounds for several minutes before it appears. Rain droplets pause beneath an arch, not motionless but describing tiny closed circuits in the air. Then the sun shifts, and all of it becomes merely an old ravine full of noise.

The Choir is most active after brief rain. Water courses down ancient grooves. Mosses expand. Snails emerge from sealed cracks. Frogs breed in puddles that may last only hours, producing young with extraordinary speed. Flying animals pour from every cavity and predators follow them in frantic layers: lizards below, birds above, mantises between. The transmitted calls overlap until the rock emits a continuous chord, loud enough to feel through the ribs.

At such times one of the deepest structures wakes.

It is a low doorway set into a wall polished smooth by vanished floods. Beyond it lies no room, only rock, yet warm air exhales from its opening. Every animal in the cleft becomes briefly still. The doorway produces a tone at the lower edge of hearing, and loose water climbs the wall in branching lines. The lines resemble roots, then river systems, then veins. For several seconds the entire cleft appears to be mapped in wet silver upon the stone, including passages no creature has entered and chambers that cannot fit beneath the plateau.

Then the tone ceases. The water falls. The animals resume all at once, louder than before.

No one knows whether the doorway is measuring the ecosystem, preserving it, dreaming alternatives to it, or using its abundance as a form of computation. Whatever made the scattered instruments is absent, unless the absence itself is only a failure of scale. The builders may be gone for millions of years. They may be smaller than the mites grazing on the arches. They may be the pattern formed by all the animals together when their cries are routed correctly through the stone.

The plateau around the cleft remains vast, warm, and nearly empty. Weather has worn its ridges low. A few remote shelters stand on the horizon—single-room structures of stacked slab, used by travelers who may never find the hidden entrance. Near the rim of the Choir there are faint carved marks, shallow enough to be mistaken for natural pitting. They advise no fires, no blocking of pools, no imitation of calls.

The last rule is the important one.

The Choir answers imitation.

A copied frog note returns as something almost like a frog note, but with an extra interval. A whistle returns from below in an unfamiliar voice. Repeat it, and the cleft repeats again, refining the sound. Animals withdraw into cracks. The loudness ebbs. Even the insects settle.

Those who continue eventually hear their own breathing issuing from the rings ahead of them, then from behind, then from passages that do not exist. After that comes another breath, timed between theirs: warm, patient, and so immense that the whole ancient plateau seems to rise and fall around the hidden cleft.

By evening the effect passes. Heat lifts from the stone. Separate pools darken under ferns, each busy with mouths, eggs, hunters, and reflected sky. The sparse mechanisms continue their inaudible exchanges. Wings fill the upper crack. Water condenses from apparently dry air and begins its slow descent, drop by isolated drop, through a landscape no larger than a building and no less complicated than a world.
