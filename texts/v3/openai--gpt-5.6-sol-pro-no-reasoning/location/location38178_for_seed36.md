Below the surface lies a country of leaves.

It is not a cave in the ordinary sense. There is no ceiling of rock, no exposed mineral wall, no rubble underfoot. The entire hollow is held open by vegetation: colossal root arches, fused trunks, and woven membranes of pale wood that bear the weight of the ground above. What seems at first to be a subterranean forest is, with time, revealed as one continuous plant—or as many plants so thoroughly grafted together that the distinction has ceased to matter.

The hollow extends for miles. Its floor slopes so gently that distance is difficult to judge, opening into broad galleries and narrowing again through thickets of supporting roots. Vast chambers lie beyond one another like valleys enclosed beneath an unbroken canopy. There are ridges, terraces, pits, and shallow basins, but none has been carved by erosion. They look unfinished because they are unfinished: newly extruded folds of living tissue, raw contours produced by swelling, division, and pressure. Here, terrain does not wear away. It is continuously held at the instant of formation.

Yet nothing can be seen to grow.

Every curled shoot remains curled. Every bead of sap hangs where it first welled out. Split seed coats rest open around motionless white radicles. Broad leaves emerge halfway from their sheaths and stop, glossy and perfect, as if the whole country occupies one permanent developmental moment. It is embryonic without becoming young, complete without ever reaching maturity. There are no fallen leaves and no rot. Scars do not close, but neither do they worsen.

The heat is deep and bodily. It has no apparent source and varies nowhere: the same close, heavy warmth in open chambers, narrow passages, and water basins alike. The air smells of crushed stems, green fruit, wet bark, pollen, and the faint sweetness of exposed sap. Breathing feels less like taking in air than drinking something thin and aromatic.

Light is everywhere.

It comes from the vegetation itself, although nothing resembles a lamp. Leaf undersides shine white-gold. Veins glow through translucent blades. Root skins emit a steady greenish radiance, and the soft tissues inside split pods are almost painfully bright. There are no shadows sharp enough to hide in. Even beneath a root or inside the cup of a flower, light seeps through living walls. The brightness does not pulse or fade. There is no day here, and therefore no night.

The densest regions are almost impassable. Leaves overlap in tiers from floor to vault, some broad enough to serve as plazas, others fine as hairs and packed into luminous haze. Tendrils bridge gaps in taut parallel lines. Flower columns stand shoulder to shoulder, their unopened petals making hot, fragrant corridors. Pillars divide and rejoin overhead. A traveler must pass through slits between stems, crawl beneath suspended seed cases, or walk along the shallow gutters formed where two enormous leaves meet.

Everything underfoot is plant matter. The firmest paths are ribbed root backs, dry and warm as old leather. Softer ground yields like the rind of a fruit. In some places, a clear membrane covers chambers full of green pulp, and each step dimples the surface without breaking it. Elsewhere, mats of fine roots form pale, springy flats stretching beyond sight. There is no dust. There are no loose stones. The nearest thing to soil is a dark accumulation of sterile pollen caught in creases of the living floor.

Water exists in isolated pockets. It beads endlessly along cool veins and gathers beneath downward-pointing leaf tips, but the drops never quite fall. Shallow pools occupy the lowest basins, perfectly clear and perfectly still. Their bottoms are tangled with submerged roots whose white branching tips remain poised in the act of entering the water. Long channels cross the galleries, suggesting rivers, though nothing flows through them. They are narrow ribbons of water held between raised green banks, their surfaces untroubled by current or evaporation.

Condensation coats certain chambers. Bright droplets stand on every hair and thorn until whole groves appear furred with glass. Elsewhere the heat is comparatively dry, and leaves fold inward to preserve the moisture locked in their tissues. The contrast produces no breeze. No warm air rises, no cool air descends. The atmosphere has reached an equilibrium so exact that even scent does not seem to drift; one passes from the odor of resin into the odor of blossoms across an invisible, motionless boundary.

Animals are uncommon enough to seem accidental. A pale, wingless moth may cling to a luminous petal, its antennae lifted but unmoving. Tiny translucent frogs sit in water cups between leaves, their bodies showing minute green bones. Far apart in the root vaults are large, blunt-shelled creatures like tortoises formed from bark. They do not feed, call, sleep, or noticeably breathe. Whether they are animals at all is uncertain. Some are attached to the floor by fine rootlets. Some bear buds along the seams of their shells.

There are no swarms of insects despite the abundance of flowers. No birds move through the open galleries. Nothing grazes the leaves or bores into the wood. The place supports life in overwhelming quantity but little appetite. Its ecology is an arrangement rather than a contest: pollen that waits to be carried, fruit that waits to be eaten, nectar that never diminishes. Every relationship is prepared, and almost none is enacted.

The quiet is correspondingly immense. Now and then there is a soft internal creak, too deep to locate, as though a trunk miles away has remembered pressure. A taut tendril may give a single glassy note when touched. Footsteps are absorbed by the fibrous ground. In condensation chambers, one expects dripping, but hears none. The silence is not empty; it is crowded with events held just short of occurring.

Scattered through this vegetal country are structures, though it is difficult to say who built them. Low shelters stand beneath root arches, assembled from overlapping scales of bark. Narrow bridges cross water channels, made of straight stems grown side by side. In a few chambers, leaf walls have been pleated into alcoves with entrances exactly tall enough for a person. There are terraces of seed husks, circular clearings edged with thorns, and stairlike growths ascending the sides of immense trunks.

Nothing has been cut or tied. There are no tool marks, fasteners, hinges, or worked surfaces. The structures may be natural coincidences, or the plant may once have responded to inhabitants whose hands left no separate trace. Some shelters contain shallow depressions shaped like sleeping bodies. Others hold bowls grown directly from the floor, each with a motionless measure of water inside. Their arrangement suggests use. Their immaculate condition denies history.

At the center of the region—if a place without visible change can be said to have a center—the galleries open into a basin several miles wide. Root buttresses descend around it like the walls of an amphitheater. The basin is crowded with upright shoots, millions of them, each ending just below the surface of a thin translucent ceiling. Beyond that membrane lies the upper world: a bright blur of soil, or perhaps sky, impossible to distinguish through the living tissue.

Every shoot is on the verge of breaking through.

None ever does.

Their tips press upward in a dense, pale field, lifting the membrane into countless tiny domes. The landscape’s permanence is clearest here. It is not ancient stillness, not the calm left after completion. It is the stillness of imminence maintained forever. The whole buried country is prepared to become something else: a forest, a meadow, a continent of green erupting into another realm. Roots brace. Cotyledons wait folded. water hangs ready in the veins. Flowers hold their pollen. Creatures face passages they will never cross.

Nothing decays because nothing finishes forming. Nothing flows because no moment yields to the next.

The country remains hot, wet in pockets, unbearably bright, and full to the limits of space—a world not dead or sleeping, but permanently beginning.
