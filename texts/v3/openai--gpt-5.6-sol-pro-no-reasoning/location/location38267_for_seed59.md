The town occupies a high, shallow basin where the mountain briefly relents. It is not a summit, nor quite a valley, but a shelf caught between steep wooded slopes. Water gathers here before choosing one of several ravines. The entire settlement has grown around that hesitation.

From above, little ground is visible. Roofs, gardens, coppiced trees, vine trellises, reed beds, and the broad crowns of old beeches press together into one uneven green surface. Smoke rises through it in thin gray threads. Paths appear only where people, goats, and runoff continually reopen them. Walls emerge from vegetation for a few yards and disappear again beneath moss, ivy, espaliered fruit trees, and mats of pennywort. Even the buildings seem less erected than trained into shape.

The basin is small enough that a person can cross it in an hour, though never directly. Its boundaries are close and distinct: a black escarpment to the north, two forested ridges on either side, and a broken lip to the south where the collected water spills down into the low country. Within those limits, however, everything is layered. Houses stand above channels, sheds lean against houses, footbridges pass beneath balconies, and gardens grow on roofs whose eaves drain into cisterns belonging to the floor below. A street may divide into three paths around the roots of a tree and reunite at different elevations.

Stone provides the bones of the town, but living material covers almost all of them. The oldest walls are made from dark local rock laid without mortar, broad at the base and softened by centuries of rain. Their joints have filled with roots. Hazel rods, woven willow, bark shingles, split cedar, clay, and bundles of mountain reed make up the newer construction. Where a retaining wall collapses, people drive stakes into the wet earth, weave branches between them, and plant cuttings along the top. In a few seasons the repair becomes a living bank. The line between masonry and hedgerow is mostly one of age.

The air is cool even in summer. Clouds arriving from the west catch on the surrounding ridges and descend into the basin as mist or persistent fine rain. Every surface holds water. Droplets stand along fern tips, collect beneath handrails, and travel slowly down ropes of vine. The rock face north of town weeps from hundreds of narrow seams. Its water does not fall in one clean cascade; it threads through moss, liverwort, roots, and dangling curtains of saxifrage, breaking into silver beads before reaching a long communal trough.

Rain here is rarely dramatic at first. It begins as a thickening of the air. Leaves turn their pale undersides. Swallows descend. Then the canopy starts to tick, thousands of separate impacts accumulating until the whole basin sounds like grain pouring onto stretched hide. Channels brighten between paving stones. Gutters overflow. Stairways become streams, and every household brings out boards, baskets, plugs, and little wooden gates to steer the water where it is wanted.

The town’s infrastructure is largely hydraulic. Shallow rills run beside nearly every path, lined with flat stone and crossed by removable slats. Water passes through settling pools, washing basins, fish pens, dye yards, mills no larger than cupboards, and finally into the marsh terraces at the southern edge. Nothing is enclosed for long. There are no great pipes beneath the streets, only visible channels that must be cleared constantly of leaves, feathers, silt, snail shells, fruit skins, and the industrious constructions of animals.

After heavy rain the system becomes turbulent. Families upstream shout warnings. Children sprint ahead of sudden brown surges, lifting bridge planks and pulling screens from culverts. Shopkeepers stack goods on hooks. Goats are driven to upper rooms. The small waterwheels spin too fast and have to be disengaged with poles. For an hour the town seems on the verge of washing loose from the mountain, but its old terraces divide the force into a hundred manageable streams. By evening, the channels are clear again and the lowest gardens are covered in fresh black silt.

The vegetation is not ornamental. It is floor, roof, wall, fuel, forage, medicine, fiber, and boundary. Dense reed mats float over the wetter ground. Willow roots hold the canal margins together. Pollarded trees support elevated walks and storage platforms. Vines stretched from house to house shade the market lanes in warm months and shed their leaves before winter, allowing light to reach the lower rooms. Moss is deliberately encouraged on cisterns because it keeps the water cool. Broad-leaved dock and burdock are permitted near kitchens; nettles occupy dye plots behind workshops; rushes grow in every shallow basin not needed for drinking.

Old fruit trees stand in courtyards so constricted that their branches have been guided through windows and over roofs. Pears ripen above chimney vents. Small sour plums drop into lanes and are eaten by pigs before people can gather them. Apples are trained flat against south-facing stone. Higher on the slopes, chestnut, beech, alder, fir, and mountain ash form a nearly continuous forest. Timber is harvested branch by branch rather than tree by tree, leaving the canopy crowded and uneven but seldom broken.

The settlement is as full of animals as it is of plants. Domestic and wild life overlap without much ceremony. Chickens roost in the rafters of open sheds. Ducks travel the channels in noisy groups and routinely halt foot traffic. Lean pigs forage in the lower reed beds, returning at dusk with mud to their shoulders. Goats occupy roofs, steps, balconies, and any wall broad enough to accept a hoof. Dogs patrol the market for scraps, while small long-haired cattle pull sledges along the few wider lanes.

Above this ordinary livestock moves an almost oppressive abundance of smaller life. Frogs fill every ditch and cistern lip. Newts shelter beneath damp stones. Snails gather on doors after rain. Beetles shine in rotting timbers; moths sleep under bark shingles; clouds of midges hover wherever the wind drops. Spiders build between railings overnight. Earthworms surface in such numbers that people sweep them gently from busy paths before dawn.

Birds provide the loudest and most constant layer. Sparrows erupt from the eaves whenever grain is poured. Jackdaws nest in old chimneys. Thrushes work the mossy courts. Swifts scream between roofs in summer, passing close enough to stir hanging laundry. Geese announce strangers long before the watch platforms do. In the reed terraces, moorhens clatter invisibly through the stems, and herons stand above the fish ponds with the patience of officials.

The surrounding forest presses its fauna into town as well. Squirrels cross overhead on branches and trellises without touching the ground. Deer enter abandoned upper gardens. Foxes follow the poultry lanes at night. Pine martens raid roof lofts, and occasionally a black bear descends for fallen fruit, creating an uproar of bells, dogs, banging pans, and shouted advice. The bear is seldom harmed. People open a path toward the woods and harry it from behind, accepting some damaged fences as part of living on the mountain.

Silence is almost unknown. Even in calm weather there is dripping, wingbeat, water over stone, the knock of hand looms, axes splitting kindling, pestles in mortars, saws working wet wood, and voices passed from terrace to terrace. Bells hang everywhere: on goats, at spill gates, over shop doors, beside flood gauges, and in the high forest where charcoal burners work. Their different tones form a practical language. A rapid wooden clack means a blocked channel. Three strokes on a bronze handbell announce an animal loose in the grain court. A deep iron note from the southern terrace means the basin is spilling safely.

Market days intensify everything. The central space is not a paved square but a network of platforms built among six enormous beeches. Sellers arrive with baskets on their backs or narrow sledges pulled by cattle. Fish change hands beside bundles of reeds, fungal dyes, smoked cheese, carved spoons, wax, medicinal bark, salt brought up from the lowlands, and iron tools repaired so many times that little of the original metal remains. The people possess knives, adzes, nails, sickles, needles, and saw teeth, but metal is scarce. Most mechanisms are wood, rope, hide, ceramic, or stone. Hinges are leather. Water gates pivot on hardwood pins. Roofs are fastened with pegs and weighted branches.

Construction is therefore slow, collective, and continually revised. No building is wholly finished. A room may be added when a tree grows strong enough to support one, then abandoned when its floor settles toward a channel. Houses lean into one another until ownership becomes difficult to read. One family maintains the roof, another the wall below it, and a third the stair passing through both. Rights are often defined by water: who may draw from a rill, who must clear it, who receives its silt, and who is responsible when it escapes.

The oldest quarter sits against the weeping cliff. Here the lanes narrow to shoulder width and remain dim at midday. Ferns grow from roof ridges. The rock behind the houses is incorporated into bedrooms, store chambers, shrines, and cool pantries. Water can be heard inside the walls. Some rooms have shallow grooves cut across their floors so seepage passes beneath raised sleeping platforms. Clay cups are set under reliable drips. Their slow, irregular notes continue all night.

Downslope, the town loosens into marsh gardens. Narrow embankments divide dark pools crowded with watercress, taro-like mountain roots, reeds, mint, and flowering rush. Fish flicker in the irrigation trenches. Children move between beds on poles and floating boards. Here the boundary of the settlement is seasonal. During dry weeks the gardens extend southward; during storms they vanish under a single shallow sheet of moving water. Houses at the edge stand on stone piers, with boats tied beneath them and ladders that can be drawn up when the current becomes strong.

Nothing in the basin remains still for very long, but neither does it feel temporary. Its turbulence is old and practiced. Floods alter paths; roots lift floors; storms drop trees across roofs; animals dismantle careful work; walls slump and are rebuilt a little farther inward. Change is not treated as failure. The town has endured by yielding in small ways, repeatedly, instead of insisting on permanence.

After rain, when the clouds lift just above the ridges, the place seems to breathe. Mist rises from the canopy. Every leaf shines. People emerge to inspect gates and terraces, calling damage reports across the water. Ducks rush into newly flooded courts. Birds begin at once, louder than before. Hammers answer them. Channels chatter under the walkways, bearing twigs, petals, and mountain silt toward the southern lip.

For a few minutes sunlight may cross the basin at an angle and reveal its full density: hundreds of wet roofs under vines, ladders among branches, bridges over silver threads of water, smoke caught beneath foliage, livestock pushing through crowds, and the cliff behind everything streaming quietly through its green skin. Then the light moves on, the mist closes, and the town returns to its ordinary condition—not hidden, exactly, but absorbed into the living mass that supports it.
