The country begins above the clouds, though there are no clouds.

It is a high, cool tableland lifted so far above the surrounding world that the horizon seems lower than the ground. The sky occupies less than it should. One looks outward across leagues of pale mineral and sees distant ridges stacked beneath one another, descending into blue air, while the plateau itself continues almost level: an elevated waste left behind after mountains have been worn down to their roots.

Nothing here has happened recently.

The stone is not jagged. It has endured beyond jaggedness. Ancient winds, now absent, polished the exposed bedrock into broad backs and shallow hollows. Cliffs ring the tableland, but their faces are smooth and faintly concave, as though ages pressed against them with a patient thumb. There is almost no loose grit. Every particle that could move has long ago fallen, lodged, fused, or been carried over the edge. What remains is the settled residue of a world that has finished eroding.

The surface is gray-white, sometimes lavender where ironless minerals darken in the cold. Long seams of black glass cross it without branching. Salt lies in tight geometric crusts, although no water is known to have passed here. Underfoot, the crust bears weight like fired tile. Where it has cracked, the fissures are packed not with dust but with small living bodies.

They are everywhere.

At first they resemble stones, seed husks, flakes of mica. Then perspective adjusts. Every pale oval on the plain is an animal. Every narrow line is a row of animals fitted nose to tail. The darker patches in hollows are colonies layered many bodies deep. Millions occupy the tableland, perhaps billions, yet almost nothing moves.

There are shell-backed grazers no longer than a thumbnail, their shells faceted to match the salt polygons beneath them. There are six-legged creatures tall as dogs, with narrow torsos and folded limbs like bundled measuring rods. There are immense, low animals spread over the rock in films, their bodies mistaken for discoloration; a single one may cover an acre and be no thicker than leather. Beneath overhangs hang crowded ranks of dry, translucent organisms, bell-shaped and delicate, though there is neither water nor wind to stir them. Between all these forms are mites, jointed threads, blind burrowers, soft nodules, horned crawlers, and animals like empty cages walking on too many feet.

All are alive. None appears to breathe.

The place has no springs, no dew, no hidden aquifer. Life here is founded on stone, cold light, and a chemistry of brittle gases drawn directly from the air. The broad film-beasts gather sunlight across mineral skins. Smaller creatures scrape the skins away. Predators press against prey and exchange substances through contact so slow that an attack may require three centuries. Reproduction occurs by accretion: an animal builds a smaller likeness beside itself from atmospheric crystals, molecule by molecule, until some distant day the two are separate.

Nothing eats in a hurry because nothing can.

The apparent stillness is not dormancy. It is the local speed of life.

A herd crosses the plateau, but its leading edge advances less than the thickness of a fingernail in a human lifetime. Winged forms fill the air over the cliffs, fixed at various heights with wings extended. They are not hovering. Each is falling, but the fall has been divided across geological time. One may descend a handspan between the rise and extinction of a civilization below. Their bodies feed on the minute difference between upper and lower air.

Sound cannot accumulate under such conditions. Now and then the salt gives a tiny click. A shell closes with the faintness of cooling metal. If one lies with an ear against the bedrock, there is a pressure just below hearing: the massed internal friction of uncountable lives proceeding.

Sparse growths stand among them. They are called plants only because they root.

Black stalks emerge from cracks and rise to knee height, branchless and square-sided. Each bears one dull red plate angled toward the midday sun. The plate is neither leaf nor flower but a thin mineral kiln in which the organism assembles carbon around a lattice of silicon. Other growths form tight silver spirals no larger than coins. On the highest ridges are groves of hollow pillars, widely spaced, their walls made from overlapping scales. They have been dead for so long that their descendants use them as mountains.

The plateau is densely inhabited but visually bare. Life does not soften the land. It repeats its hardness.

Scattered across the open reaches are signs of another species, perhaps still present. Low shelters have been built from fitted slabs, each scarcely large enough for one crouching body. The builders used stone wedges, bone pins, braided tendon, and occasional scraps of hammered copper gone green-black without corrosion. There are no roads, but lines of upright chips lead from shelter to shelter. At intervals, shallow bowls have been ground into the bedrock. They cannot have held water. Most contain arranged shells, knucklebones, or the immobile larvae of local animals.

The shelters never stand near the cliff edges. Their doorways face inward, toward a region at the plateau’s center where the stone rises in a confusion of smooth towers.

These towers are not ruins. Nor are they natural in any reliable sense.

From a distance they look like densely clustered rock needles, thousands of them, filling a basin perhaps twenty leagues across. As one approaches, their spacing changes without any tower moving. Two that seemed miles apart come to stand nearly touching; a narrow passage opens onto a plain larger than the country outside. Their shadows point in mutually impossible directions. Walking around one may return a traveler to its front, older by no measurable interval but carrying dust from a place where dust still exists.

The animals become more numerous there. They cover every tower in fitted mosaics of shell and limb. Some pass through the stone and emerge from adjacent surfaces. Others possess only one side: seen from the east they are intricate living machines, while from the west there is nothing at all. High overhead, long vertebrate chains bridge the towers, each chain made of separate creatures sharing a single skeleton.

At the exact center stands a crude ring of hand-shaped stones.

It may be the oldest built object on the plateau, or the only new one. Within the ring, reality loses even the pretense of continuity. The ground is visible from beneath. Creatures appear nested inside their own bodies. Near objects are obscured by far ones. A person who raises a hand sees the gesture already waiting in the air, permanent and detached, one among countless prior gestures that have never faded.

This explains the stillness, or seems to.

Time does not pass across the tableland. It accumulates.

Every event remains where it occurred. The ancient flight of an animal persists as the animal itself, stretched invisibly between departure and arrival. Every footfall is still being made. Every life that has occupied the plateau is present, compressed into the same cool afternoon. The overwhelming abundance is not merely fertility; it is the refusal of anything to become past.

Death exists, but departure does not. The dead continue to occupy their final shapes while descendants form around and through them. Predators retain prey from attacks older than the cliffs. Generations coincide until species become dense braids of ancestry. What appears to be a colony may be one creature at every stage of its life, all stages inhabiting the same patch of stone.

The scattered shelters are similarly layered. Each is a hut, its ruin, its proposed reconstruction, and the untouched ground before it was built. A stone hand-axe may be found embedded in the slab from which it was chipped. Copper ornaments encircle wrists that have not yet entered the plateau. The people, if they remain, are difficult to distinguish from their traces. A crouched silhouette glimpsed beside a shelter could be a living watcher, an afterimage several thousand years old, or the expectation of someone who will arrive.

Yet the land is not chaotic. Its impossibilities have settled into equilibrium. Nothing erupts. Nothing transforms. The contradictions are ancient and stable, worn smooth like the rock. Cause and effect lie side by side without conflict. The world has had enough time to accommodate both.

Above it all, the sun gives ordinary light.

There are no dramatic colors, no perpetual eclipse, no supernatural radiance. Morning—if morning can be said to advance—rests softly on the eastern faces of stones. The sky is a clean, cool blue. Shadows are moderate and clear. This normality makes the tableland harder to comprehend. Under commonplace daylight, every shell and fissure is plainly visible, and the eye has no darkness in which to excuse what it sees.

From the rim, the plateau appears peaceful: pale open ground, smooth heights, sparse black plants, scattered stone huts. Only after long looking does one understand that almost every inch is inhabited; that the quiet is made by creatures whose cries take epochs to form; that the still wings over the abyss are in the middle of flight.

And only later comes the more troubling recognition.

The land below the plateau is moving.

Clouds travel there. Rivers flash. Forests alter with the seasons. Smoke rises from settlements and disperses. Seen from the high rim, all of it flickers with indecent haste, born and erased before it can acquire substance. The tableland alone is real in the old sense: enduring, crowded, complete.

Anything that climbs onto it begins to remain.

A footprint does not vanish. A breath stays near the mouth as a faint crystal veil. The next step stands beside the last, and eventually the walker discovers that each former self is still present, extending backward in a silent procession toward the cliff.

There is no known way to descend without leaving most of oneself behind.
