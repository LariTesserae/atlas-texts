The water has no visible surface.

It fills a constructed world so broad that distance behaves like weather. Cold currents migrate through it over decades. Pressure increases almost imperceptibly from district to district, and a traveler may pass through the equivalent of an ocean’s depth without encountering a cliff, a trench, or any clear boundary—only a long succession of shallow descents worked into the floor.

Everything here was made.

The ground consists of fitted slabs: black stone, fired clay, dense glass, and blocks of an old composite resembling shell but containing no trace of life. They lie in overlapping courses beneath a deep skin of sediment. Their joints are wide enough for roots. Their surfaces have been worn nearly level by uncounted ages of moving water, until stairs have become ramps and inscriptions have softened into shallow, unreadable hollows.

There is no bedrock below them. Beneath each pavement lies another built layer: vault roofs, packed ceramic fill, ribbed foundations, collapsed galleries, older pavements, and conduits sealed with hammered plugs. Dig far enough and one does not reach nature, only prior construction. Walls support walls. Floors rest on buried domes. The whole drowned expanse may be a structure floating in deeper water, or it may continue downward without end.

Its makers possessed no subtle machinery. Nothing is seamless. Every block bears the logic of rope, lever, wedge, chisel, kiln, and patient hands. Pillars were raised drum by drum. Great roofs were assembled from corbeled courses. Conduits were lined with thumb-smoothed clay. Where metal survives, it is simple: copper cramps turned green-black, lead seals, iron rings reduced to rust stains. The wonder of the place is not sophistication but accumulation. Generations with basic tools covered a continental area so completely that no original ground remains.

Now the architecture is submerged beneath cold, almost motionless water.

Darkness is nearly absolute. In rare places a dull gray-green radiance seeps from mineral-glazed tiles. It is not enough to illuminate chambers, only to disclose their dimensions by fragments: one edge of a column, the underside of a bridge, a row of apertures vanishing into blackness. At greater distances, light is absorbed before it can reveal anything. Space announces itself through pressure and echo rather than sight.

Vegetation grows everywhere that the masonry allows.

There are forests, though none rise freely. Pale fronds lie close against walls, following mortar lines like frost. Long black-green ribbons emerge from drainage slots and trail horizontally in the weak current, sometimes for hundreds of meters. Dense meadows of branching weed cover plazas and rooftops, their tips dusted with silt. In vaulted interiors, translucent mats hang upside down from ceilings, fed by cold seepage through the brickwork. Some are so old and thick that they have become part of the architecture: layered vegetable curtains stiffened with minerals, dividing halls into silent rooms.

The plants grow without obvious sunlight. They feed on dissolved substances leaking from ceramic reservoirs and on the faint chemical differences between one layer of water and another. Their metabolism is exceedingly slow. A frond may take a year to uncurl. A grove advances across a courtyard in the span of a human lifetime. When stems die, they do not rot quickly; they bleach, collapse, and remain as fibrous felt beneath new growth.

There are no animals.

No fish cross the corridors. No shell adheres to the stone. No worms disturb the sediment, and no small bodies flicker at the edge of sight. Leaves are never bitten. Spores drift without being eaten. Dead growth accumulates in undisturbed layers. The absence becomes most noticeable in lush places, where every surface suggests habitat but nothing watches from it.

Sound is correspondingly sparse. Water transmits the occasional noises of the structure with immense clarity: a stone settling far away; the muted fracture of a roof under sediment; the drawn-out complaint of a bronze pin shifting in its socket. Each sound may travel through halls and cisterns for many minutes, altered by arches until it resembles a distant knock. Afterward, quiet closes again.

The dominant sound, where it can be heard at all, is the low movement of water through constrictions. It murmurs in old sluices, whispers around door lintels, and pulses through vertical shafts. Some passages breathe on cycles lasting days. The water in them rises by a hand’s breadth, pauses, then withdraws. Whole regions exchange their contents this way, slowly enough that clouds of silt can remain suspended like weather fronts.

The terrain is low and anciently flattened. There are no sharp mountains, only vast architectural swells: buried temple platforms, roof fields, embankments, and broad terraces descending into deeper districts. Former towers have been reduced to rounded stumps. Walls that once divided cities now appear as long, gentle ridges under vegetation. Erosion has not destroyed the world so much as negotiated it into a final shape. Every exposed corner is smooth. Every cavity that could collapse has collapsed. What remains is the stable residue—forms too broad, too supported, or too deeply packed to fail.

Across the central expanses stand forests of columns whose roofs are lost in darkness. The columns are crowded, often only an arm’s span apart, and their repetition gives the water a dense, interior quality. Elsewhere, the construction opens into basins hundreds of kilometers wide. These are paved in tessellated stone, though sediment has obscured all but occasional geometric bands. Low causeways cross them in straight lines. At intervals, squat shelters rise from the causeways, each containing hooks, benches, ceramic vessels, and hand-operated gates.

The gates still move.

Not by engines. Their timber has long since mineralized, but counterweights descend in sealed wells, responding to minute changes in water pressure. A gate may take forty years to close. Another may stand open for centuries before accumulated plant matter makes it heavy enough to tip. In this way the drowned world continues to regulate itself with the simplest of mechanisms: balance, buoyancy, siphons, floats, and stone weights. Its slow hydrology is an artifact maintained by gravity.

When a gate finally shifts, the consequences unfold gradually. A cold layer drains from one basin into another. Nutrients follow. Vegetation darkens along the receiving channels. Far away, pressure eases against a weakened vault. Decades later, a section of pavement subsides, opening new rooms to the water. Nothing happens suddenly enough to be called an event, yet the landscape is never wholly still.

There may once have been air in the upper chambers. Evidence remains in the form of hearths, smoke-blackened ceilings, dry-land staircases, and doorways absurdly narrow for passage through water. There are gardens designed around paths and beds, now transformed into tangled beds of ribbon weed. Wells descend from places already drowned. Rain channels run along roofs beneath an ocean. Whoever constructed the world did not build it for its present condition, though they built with flooding in mind.

Perhaps the inundation came by increments. The lowest courtyards filled first. Walls were raised, then raised again. Doors were sealed with clay. New floors were laid over old flooded districts. But the water continued to rise until there was nowhere higher to build—only more ceilings, more reservoirs, more carefully fitted stones. The final inhabitants may have lived on rafts beneath the uppermost domes. Those domes are drowned now as well.

Still, the place does not feel ruined. Ruin implies interruption. Here age has completed the work. The water, masonry, sediment, and plants have settled into mutual dependence. Roots brace fractured joints. Silt loads the roofs evenly. Flooded chambers relieve pressure beneath the paving. Slow valves prevent destructive currents. The structure survives because it is submerged, and the vegetation survives because the structure continues to leak the minerals stored within it.

There is no horizon, yet the landscape is vast enough to possess regions and climates. In the northern galleries, water is almost freezing, and vegetation forms low, glassy crusts. In the long western basins, dark fronds grow thick as standing grain. Toward the deepest constructed terraces, pressure makes the water heavy and exceptionally clear; there the architecture can sometimes be glimpsed at great distance as darker darkness—arches behind arches, descending.

Travel is measured by landmarks that barely change: the Field of Leaning Piers, the Hundred Sealed Wells, the Green Ceiling, the Plain of Broken Lids. Routes pass through doors, over rooftops, beneath bridges, and along the exterior faces of buildings buried so completely that they resemble hills. A journey may remain indoors for months without encountering a chamber small enough to comprehend.

And always there is the uncertainty of what “indoors” means here. The ceiling above may be a roof, or a foundation for another flooded city. A plaza may be the lid of a reservoir. A continent-sized basin may be only one cistern among many.

In the quietest districts, the water is so still that sediment stands upright on the paving in delicate ridges. Plants grow through it without disturbance. Nothing swims past. Nothing grazes them. The dark retains every slow trace.

Then, somewhere beyond sight, a counterweight settles by the width of a finger.

A gate gives a low stone groan.

The vegetation inclines almost imperceptibly as the water begins, over the coming years, to move another way.
