Beneath the oldest quarter of the city lies a courtyard that was never paved.

Buildings enclose it on every side: warm plaster walls, shallow foundations, blind windows, and brick arches whose lower courses have sunk into the soil. It is not a ruin. Families still live above it, though no door opens directly onto the ground below. The courtyard survives inside the block like a space overlooked during construction, accessible only by a narrow stair behind a bakery and, in the rainy months, by crawling through a low drainage arch.

The place is perhaps thirty paces across, but its boundaries are difficult to judge. Balconies project overhead. Buttresses divide the walls into recesses. Fig roots enter cracks and make little rooms of shade. The city presses closely around it, yet the floor itself remains open, with wide intervals of dark earth between clumps of growth.

Nothing here appears worn.

The soil is continually being made. Fine mineral dust falls from the soft mortar. Earthworms draw down leaves from the edges. Fig pulp, pigeon droppings, beetle husks, and windblown flour accumulate in thin layers. After rain, the layers swell and merge; during dry weather they contract into new seams. There are no gullies, no rounded stones, no old channels polished by passage. Water has not had time—or sufficient freedom—to carve anything. It gathers instead in thumb-deep depressions, temporary and soft-sided, which collapse as soon as they empty.

The courtyard is therefore less a finished terrain than a place where terrain begins.

Its lowest point lies beneath a broken clay conduit projecting from one wall. Most days the conduit is dry. Condensation darkens its lip before dawn, and individual drops fall at long intervals into a shallow basin of moss and black soil. After rain, water runs through it for an hour or two, clear at first, then cloudy with lime. A narrow sheet spreads across the courtyard floor without finding a permanent course. It divides around roots, vanishes into loose ground, and leaves isolated pockets beneath tiles and in the hoof-shaped impressions of fallen plaster.

By afternoon, in warm weather, much of this water is gone. Moisture remains under leaves, inside brick cavities, and in the cool folds where roots meet masonry. Snails know these places. So do small pale frogs that emerge only when the damp reaches the surface.

Vegetation is lush without being continuous. There is room around each thing.

A fig tree rises near the center, not tall enough to clear the surrounding roofs but broad enough to hold the courtyard together. Its trunk divides low, exposing smooth gray roots that lie on the new soil rather than within it. Between these roots grow ferns, nettles, purslane, maidenhair, and several kinds of grass. Elsewhere, the ground opens into bare, nearly black earth. A single elder leans from a wall. Vines hang down from balconies but do not reach the floor, their tips turning slowly in the warm air.

Moss occupies the masonry in precise patches determined by seepage. It covers one arch completely and leaves the neighboring bricks bare. Tiny plants root in fresh mortar fractures: first algae, then a green film, then threads of liverwort. Their colonies have abrupt edges. Each is a settlement only a season old.

The built forms are simple and old-fashioned: brick, clay tile, lime plaster, timber, stone thresholds. Nothing mechanical enters the court. Water follows gravity. Shade follows the sun. Cooling comes from evaporation and from the depth of the surrounding walls. Even the city’s refuse arrives without machinery: a cabbage leaf dropped by a cook, a feather, charcoal dust, a plum stone carried by a rat.

Human life is sensed mostly through weight and warmth. Floorboards creak overhead. Once in a while, flour sifts from between joists in a faint white veil. The walls retain the day’s heat long after the courtyard has dimmed, making the enclosed air mild even at night. In summer it can become close and humid, fragrant with fig leaves and wet lime. In winter it remains sheltered, seldom cold enough for standing water to skin with ice.

The light is greenish and indirect. Sun reaches the floor only briefly, moving as a narrow bright shape across the soil before climbing a wall and disappearing. Most of the day is held in an even dusk. Under the fig canopy, water beads remain visible on spider silk long after the leaves themselves seem dry.

Despite the enclosing city, the place is full of animals.

Pigeons descend from the roofs one at a time, wary of the cats that watch from ledges. Sparrows enter in quick groups, bathe in the loose soil, and leave shallow bowls behind. Geckos inhabit the warm plaster. Beetles move beneath bark and tile fragments; ants maintain thin roads across otherwise empty ground. In the wet hours, frogs hunt midges around the basin. At dusk, bats circle in the small square of sky, seeming enormous whenever they pass close overhead.

A rat family lives beneath a collapsed threshold. A grass snake sometimes follows the wall, nearly indistinguishable from a root until the root moves. Hunting and feeding happen quietly here. A moth disappears between a frog’s jaws. A snail’s shell is opened against a brick. Fig seeds pass through birds and arrive in improbable cracks.

The court is active, but rarely loud. Its sounds are intimate and separate: a drop striking mud, claws touching tile, a wing briefly beating against leaves. There is the soft granular fall of mortar. Rain, when it comes, begins as taps on the canopy and balconies, then gathers into threads along the walls. For a few minutes the whole enclosure whispers. Afterward, dripping continues from different heights, each interval lengthening until silence returns.

Even silence is layered. Above it lie muffled footsteps, a chair dragged across a room, distant voices too blurred for words. Below it is the almost inaudible work of loose earth settling around roots.

The courtyard obeys a small set of ordinary laws. Water arrives irregularly and must be used at once. Shade preserves what sunlight would take. Every cavity becomes shelter, every fallen thing becomes soil, and nothing remains exposed for long unless it is too dry, too smooth, or too often disturbed. There are no ancient landforms here. No cliff has weathered into grace; no stream has inherited a bed. The geography is provisional.

A puddle formed in the morning may determine where gnats gather by noon and where a frog waits by evening. A fallen tile may shelter enough moisture for moss to begin. A seed may split a seam that yesterday seemed permanent.

Thus the hidden court continually constructs itself from minor events. It is urban in its enclosure, wild in its occupation, and young beneath structures much older than it. The city has given it walls but no plan. Within those walls, rain, warmth, roots, and animals are making the first draft of a landscape.
