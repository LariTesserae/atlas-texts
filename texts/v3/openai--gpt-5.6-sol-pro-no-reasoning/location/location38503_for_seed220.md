The city lies only twelve meters below the desert, though once inside it feels much farther down.

It occupies a single enormous chamber, an ovoid hollow worn through pale bedrock before anyone built here. The ceiling bows overhead like the underside of a buried hill. No lamp reveals all of it at once. Its farther curves remain black, inferred from echoes and from the condensation that occasionally loosens above and falls through the darkness.

The city is urban in arrangement but almost empty of matter. There are streets, intersections, plazas, transit lines, drainage channels, service corridors, stacked addresses, and designated districts—but most are defined by absence. A house may consist of four slender corner posts, a threshold, and a volume of subtly altered air. Towers are columns of vacancy around which stairs spiral without visible support. Bridges cross the chamber in hair-thin arcs. The builders learned to give structure to intervals rather than objects, fastening distance itself into rigid shapes. As a result, the settlement contains thousands of rooms and scarcely any walls.

The oldest districts have been here long enough to seem geological. Every exposed edge has softened. Mineral films veil the thresholds. Steps are shallow with use, though no great population appears to remain. Where the chamber walls were once cut into terraces, seepage has rounded the marks into fluted curtains and broad, patient channels. Repairs were designed to weather at the same rate as the stone, so it is no longer clear which surfaces are natural and which have been maintained for centuries.

It is warm belowground. Heat rises through narrow faults in the floor and spreads beneath the streets. Some passages are merely comfortable; others breathe furnace air in slow pulses. The stone retains warmth, and metal handrails are almost hot enough to hurt. There is no wind in the ordinary sense, but temperature differences drive a steady circulation through the hollow: hot air ascending through the central city, cooler air descending along the walls. Dust is lifted, carried upward, and sifted back down in fine, nearly invisible rain.

Moisture comes intermittently.

After storms in the desert above, water finds old fractures and enters the ceiling over hours or days. At first there is only a darkening of stone. Then droplets gather, tremble, and fall from such a height that they arrive as cold pinpricks. The streets have been shaped to catch them. Each intersection contains a shallow black basin; every threshold tilts toward a buried cistern. For a brief period the city gleams with threads of runoff. Its dry channels speak all at once, and pools form in worn depressions no larger than tables.

Within days, most of the water is gone—drunk by reservoirs, wicked into ceramic veins, or evaporated by the heat below. White mineral rings remain around the pools. Condensation persists longest on the northern wall, where beads line the rock like dim glass and feed a narrow river that flows for only a few dozen paces before descending through a grate.

Vegetation survives in these wet intervals. Pale jointed reeds emerge from cracks around the basins, grow rapidly, release a dust of black spores, and collapse as the streets dry. Rust-colored films spread across damp masonry. In two plazas, old trees stand inside transparent shafts sunk toward deeper aquifers. Their crowns are sparse and silver, each leaf turned vertically to gather the city’s negligible light. The roots are more extensive than the branches, threaded under whole neighborhoods and periodically opening valves in search of water.

Nothing here is truly illuminated. Along important routes, strips of engineered mineral give off a bruised violet glimmer when stepped upon. Signs appear only as a person approaches, their letters assembling from floating specks and fading behind them. In unused districts even these responses have ceased. Navigation depends on touch, memory, and sound.

Sound is the city’s dominant weather.

The chamber continually resonates. Pumps hammer far below the floor. Pressure gates close with detonations that travel around the curved walls and return altered seconds later. Transit capsules pass through evacuated conduits with a deep, rising howl, though one may wait hours without seeing a passenger. Thermal ducts contract and release with cannonlike reports. During periods of seepage, thousands of falling drops strike roofs, rails, pools, and tuned plates, producing a dense metallic roar.

The architecture uses this noise. Buildings announce their boundaries acoustically. A doorway may be identified by a low chord felt in the teeth; a forbidden stair returns footsteps half a beat too early. Public messages travel as sequences of vibrations through the paving. The inhabitants once read the city with their entire skeletons.

Some systems continue to behave as if crowds still fill the streets. Crossings halt nonexistent traffic. Elevators open onto empty platforms. An avenue widens itself at the traditional evening hour, shifting its anchors in the underlying geometry, then narrows before dawn. Rooms relocate overnight within fixed outer dimensions. A hospital keeps a district of air sterile, although there are no beds inside it. Beneath the central plaza, an engine exchanges two identical cubic meters of space every seventeen minutes. No surviving explanation says why.

The mechanism is beyond conventional machinery but not magical in appearance. It is old, standardized, municipal. Access panels bear scuffed numbering. Maintenance recesses contain tools fitted to forces rather than fasteners: handles that grip inertia, calibrated loops for tightening curvature, sealed jars of manufactured vacuum. The most advanced works are treated with the same civic plainness as gutters.

Animal life is scarce. Tiny translucent mites graze on mineral films after rain. Long-legged water insects hatch in temporary pools, mate beneath the violet path lights, and die before the basins empty. Blind, warm-skinned lizards live beside the heat faults; they remain motionless for weeks, then cross entire districts in a single night to feed.

Rarest are the sounding animals. They resemble thin black ribbons when seen at all, slipping through the open volumes of abandoned towers. They have no evident wings. Instead they follow pressure waves, turning sharply when pumps strike or gates slam. In silence they sink helplessly toward the floor. The city’s noise keeps them aloft.

There may be people here. Evidence of habitation appears in careful, isolated details: a warm cup resting on a sill without walls; fresh cord wound around a railing; one cistern recently swept of spores. Now and then a figure is visible at the far end of a resonant street, but darkness and acoustic refraction make distance unreliable. A person a hundred meters away may sound close enough to touch. Someone passing beside you may cast their footsteps into another district.

The city’s principal rule is that emptiness can be owned, engineered, damaged, and repaired. Its second is that no enclosed volume remains where it was unless continuously observed. Doors therefore matter even when walls do not. Closing one stabilizes the room behind it; leaving it open permits that room to drift into the municipal interior, sometimes reappearing elsewhere with its heat, moisture, and contents intact.

This is why the streets remain so bare. Objects create anchors. Too many possessions would make the city rigid, unable to adjust to the slow pressure of stone around it. The apparent abandonment may instead be stewardship: a population trained to leave almost nothing where it lives.

At the chamber’s lowest point lies a round plaza surrounded by nine unoccupied stations. Its paving has been eroded into a shallow bowl, polished by water and immense age. A black aperture opens at the center. Hot air rushes from it with a continuous thunder, loud enough to erase speech. Condensation forms along its rim and is flung upward in glittering droplets, briefly catching the violet light before vanishing into the roof-dark.

All the city’s avenues incline toward this place, though maps insist they do not.

During the wet season, runoff gathers in the bowl. The aperture’s updraft holds the water above it in a trembling, concave lens. Reeds germinate around the edge. Sounding animals circle in the pressure column. Old signs wake throughout the city, and unseen machinery changes tempo beneath the pavement.

For several hours the empty settlement becomes louder, wetter, and almost alive.

Then the lens collapses upward.

Rain falls from the ceiling onto the city for a second time.
