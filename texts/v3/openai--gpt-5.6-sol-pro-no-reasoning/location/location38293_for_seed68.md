The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a long walk, where the ground has risen recently from an older riverbed. Its boundaries are unmistakable. On three sides, raw shelves of blue-gray clay lift abruptly from the plain, their faces still sharp enough to show every slump and fracture. On the fourth, the basin opens into reed country and a narrow river that appears substantial only after rain. Most days it divides into dark channels among gravel bars, leaving isolated pools under mats of pennywort.

The settlement is called a city on maps, perhaps because it has a station, a power house, and municipal chimneys. Seen from the clay rim, it resembles a collection of workshops scattered through an overgrown garden. Buildings stand well apart. Between them lie meadows, coppices, allotments, and vacant lots so thoroughly reclaimed by vegetation that streets seem to cross open country on their way from one block to the next.

Nothing here is old enough to have softened.

The roads are poured in pale aggregate and break cleanly at their margins. Drainage cuts retain the triangular profiles left by excavators. Retaining walls consist of black, angular stone packed in wire cages, not yet dulled by moss except along their lowest courses. The railway embankment crosses the basin on fresh red ballast. Even the hills have the unfinished look of material tipped yesterday: spoil cones behind the foundry, terraces scraped for houses that were never built, a raw crescent where the river altered course and exposed a bank threaded with roots.

Yet growth is everywhere. Alder and willow have filled the damp depressions. Birch saplings rise from cracks around warehouses, their leaves flashing silver-green whenever wind moves through the basin. Nettles crowd loading yards. Ferns occupy the shaded sides of concrete tanks. Along the quieter streets, enormous rhubarb-like plants lean over drainage channels, collecting condensation until their leaves release it in sudden, heavy drops.

The climate is cool without being severe. Mornings begin with mist lodged knee-high among the buildings and clear into ordinary, generous daylight. Clouds move low and visibly, white or iron-colored, but rarely stay long enough to produce sustained rain. Moisture comes in fragments: a fifteen-minute shower; beads forming on cold pipes; water seeping through the new clay; fog caught by roofs and fed into cisterns. By afternoon the exposed gravel may already be dusty while water still gleams beneath dock leaves a few steps away.

Because water is unreliable, the town has made an industry of catching it.

Every roof slopes toward a chain, gutter, or suspended trough. Pipes cross alleys overhead in orderly bundles, feeding cylindrical reservoirs painted green. Condensing towers stand among the trees like squat metal organs, drawing damp air across refrigerated fins. Their fans turn continuously, and droplets fall into resonant pans below. Older residents can identify each tower by its note: the bass drone near the station, the rattling tenor behind the machine works, the high metallic hum at the northern clay shelf.

The town is loud almost everywhere.

Noise does not come from crowds; the streets are too empty for that. It comes from mechanisms stretched across the basin. Small factories stamp fittings and cut sheet metal. Pumps wake at scheduled hours. Compressors cough, belt drives slap, and freight wagons strike one another with reports that travel cleanly through the cool air. Saws from the timber yard rasp for minutes at a time, then fall silent so abruptly that birdsong floods the gap.

Sound accumulates strangely against the clay walls. A hammer blow at one end of town returns a moment later from another direction. The evening siren seems to arise from the ground. Rain on the broad industrial roofs is deafening, though the rain itself may be light: millions of separate impacts amplified by corrugated metal, tanks, ducts, and empty loading bays.

The animals have adapted by becoming louder still.

Starlings nest in the warm cavities around exhaust vents and gather on power lines in dense black notation. Their calls imitate reversing alarms, loose bearings, and the short double whistle of yard locomotives. Marsh birds stalk the reed pools beyond the last warehouse, barking invisibly at dusk. Green frogs occupy sumps and culverts. Their choruses travel through the drainage system, emerging from gratings far from the water in which they sit.

Foxes den in the fresh embankments. Hares feed on the open lawns between workshops, lifting their heads whenever machinery changes pitch. There are beetles beneath every fallen sheet of timber, pale moths in the condenser houses, and bright dragonflies around tanks whose water is clean enough to reflect the entire sky. Otters sometimes enter by the river channels and pass through town using covered drains. Workers recognize their prints on wet concrete but seldom see the animals themselves.

One species belongs particularly to the basin: a small slate-colored bird called the riveter. It builds nests from wire, dry grass, and flakes of rust, always on resonant structures. The male courts by dropping pebbles down lengths of unused pipe. Different pipes make different songs. At sunrise, whole abandoned yards ring with descending scales.

Despite the machinery, there is little sense of hurry. Production proceeds by long cycles. The clay pits advance a few meters each year. Saplings thicken around idle equipment. Reservoirs fill drop by drop, then discharge once every several days into the municipal cistern. Trains arrive infrequently, but when they do, the entire town seems organized around their passage: gates lower, cranes wake, doors roll upward, and engines drag their heavy sound across the basin for an hour. Afterward, everything subsides into fans, insects, and dripping water.

Buildings are rarely demolished. They are emptied, roofed again, and given another purpose. A former turbine hall houses a market beneath its gantry crane. A row of brick kilns has become warm winter gardens, each circular chamber planted with figs, mosses, and medicinal herbs. The old pumping station is now a public bath, supplied by waste heat from the foundry. Its deep pool occupies the machine floor, among tiled columns and sealed brass gauges. Condensation hangs under the rafters and falls with soft, random taps.

There is one peculiar rule to the place: no water may be allowed to run unheard.

Every cistern has a bell float. Every public pipe ends over a metal cup or stone basin chosen for its tone. Leaks are not merely repaired; they are first located by listening. Children learn the water notes of their neighborhood before they learn street names. A clear ringing means a household tank is full. A dull knock below ground means seepage has entered the storm vaults. A rapid silver patter from the municipal tower announces rain on the northern shelf, often before anyone in the basin feels it.

This custom began as practical accounting, but over time it altered the city. Water and sound became inseparable. Silent wetness is treated with suspicion. Pools discovered without a drip, inlet, or audible underground flow are marked with white stakes and left alone.

Such pools appear occasionally after the clay shifts.

They are small, often no larger than a room, perfectly still and unexpectedly deep. Plants grow densely around their edges but never lean over them. Frogs call from nearby ditches and not from the pools themselves. The water reflects daylight accurately, yet at night it retains a dim gray brightness rather than reflecting lamps or stars. Engineers blame mineral films, though none have collected a sample that behaves the same way once removed.

The town has incorporated these mute waters into its plan. Streets bend around them. Factory walls stop short. One stands in the courtyard of the electrical works, ringed by safety railings and foxgloves; another lies behind the bakery, where delivery drivers leave a respectful margin while turning their trucks. The largest occupies a vacant plot near the river. Willows grow in a circle around it, but the center remains open to the sky.

Apart from these pockets, the basin feels vigorous and comprehensible: a young place made from clay, gravel, steel, weeds, and patient utility. It smells of wet leaves, hot oil, cut timber, river mud, and ozone from the substations. Its colors are practical—oxide red, galvanized silver, municipal green—set amid a wealth of living greens.

From the rim at midday, ordinary sunlight reveals everything without drama: the scattered roofs, the reed beds, the bright rails, the narrow river, the fans turning slowly above the trees. Smoke and mist travel together at roof height. Birds orbit the chimneys. Engines pound, frogs answer from the drains, and somewhere among the buildings a cistern reaches capacity.

Its bell begins to ring.

One by one, other reservoirs take up the note, until the small, sparse city sounds much larger than it is: an immense mechanism hidden inside a grove, measuring the arrival of water one drop at a time.
