The High Basin is an ocean held far above any ground.

No one looking into it could say what supports it. There is no seabed of stone, no continental shelf, no buried mantle. The entire basin—from its black lower reaches to the dim, storm-beaten surface—is constructed. Its floor is a shallow dish of fitted slabs, and beneath those slabs lie older courses, then older ones again: masonry descending farther than sound, each layer serving as the foundation of the next. If there was ever a natural world underneath, it has been built over so completely that even the idea of raw earth feels implausible.

The workmanship is total but not technological. There are no engines in the walls, no cables, lenses, valves, or lights. Nothing moves with the precise repetition of a machine. The place appears to have been made by methods closer to patience than industry: blocks rubbed into conformity, vaults balanced by weight, ceramic ribs interlocked without mortar. Some surfaces have the faint parallel scoring of tools; others seem to have been shaped by handling, one touch at a time, over ages. Whoever built the basin possessed craft, but perhaps not devices. Or perhaps the structure is so old that all mechanisms have worn away, leaving only the passive geometry that once contained them.

The water is bitterly cold. Near the surface, it is pale iron-gray, troubled constantly by wind. Farther down it becomes blue-black and heavy, carrying a fine suspension of powdered ceramic and metal oxide. There is little light. Day arrives only as a diffuse whitening overhead, strained through clouds, rain, and a surface that never stops breaking. Storms travel across the basin without recognizable seasons. The rain falls hard enough to flatten small waves, yet beneath it larger swells cross in opposing directions, striking one another into sudden towers of water.

This turbulence does not diminish with depth as it should. Currents run through the basin like arguments. They plunge vertically along the walls, reverse in vaulted trenches, and erupt miles away as cold upwellings. Sometimes an entire district of the deep water begins to rotate, slowly at first, then with enough force to pull loose debris into a dark wheel. Elsewhere, narrow jets race through archways and along tiled avenues, scouring them clean in minutes after centuries of silt.

The basin’s interior is laid out like an inundated country.

Terraces step down from the outer walls, each broad enough to hold a town. Causeways cross the open water, though most lie submerged at different depths. Their paving stones are rounded nearly smooth. Staircases descend from nowhere to nowhere, vanishing into blue darkness. Rows of roofless chambers occupy long ridges, and their doorways are too broad and low for human proportions. In the central reaches stand sparse towers, hundreds of body-lengths apart. They are hollow, perforated from top to bottom, and currents moan faintly through them when the storms align.

Nothing remains sharp. Ancient erosion has brought every surface close to its final shape. Corners have become shoulders; inscriptions, if they were inscriptions, have faded into shallow depressions. Once-complex reliefs survive as pale bulges on walls. Thresholds have been worn into saddles. In places the masonry has been polished until it reflects a blurred, pewter-colored image of anything passing nearby.

Yet the place is not a ruin in the usual sense. A ruin implies a short interval after abandonment. The High Basin has been under water so long that flooding is no longer an event but the fundamental condition. Its architecture has settled into the sea’s vocabulary. Collapses become reefs of fitted blocks; broken roofs become low plains; fallen columns lie aligned with the current like ribs. The basin has forgotten dryness.

There is almost no vegetation. No kelp streams from the towers, no weeds gather in cracks, no green film softens the tile. The cold surfaces remain mineral and bare. What clings to them is difficult to classify: thin gray membranes that spread in polygons, chalky nodules with pinprick mouths, and translucent crusts that withdraw imperceptibly when shadow passes over them. They might be animals, colonies, or something simpler than either. They give the masonry the look of old bone dusted with frost.

Animal life, by contrast, is everywhere—but dispersed through so much water that abundance rarely becomes crowding.

Pale shoals roam the upper terraces. Each creature is no longer than a finger and almost entirely transparent, its spine marked by a row of black beads. They turn together with such speed that a school can disappear by presenting only its edges. Broad, slow swimmers patrol the open reaches below: flat bodies, white beneath and dark above, moving on undulations too subtle to see. They resemble scraps of night detached from the depths.

In the drowned galleries live jointed scavengers with stone-colored shells. They wedge themselves into sockets in the walls when currents rise, locking their limbs against the masonry. Afterward they emerge to search the newly scoured floors, tapping delicately at cracks. Their tapping is one of the basin’s few intimate sounds.

Larger animals are heard more often than seen. They travel through the deepest avenues and produce long, descending calls that seem to come from the structure itself. A note may begin under one’s feet, move outward through the slabs, and fade several minutes later against the far wall. Sometimes another answers. Sometimes every creature in the nearby water becomes still, and nothing answers at all.

The quiet is relative. Close to the storm surface, water hammers and hisses. In narrow passages, currents whistle through holes worn in the walls. Loose tiles knock together during pressure changes. But across the great drowned plazas there are long intervals when the only sound is the soft grain of suspended sediment moving over ceramic. The violence of the place is often visible rather than loud: distant clouds of silt blooming upward, shoals bending around invisible vortices, a tower inclining over several hours and then vanishing into its own foundation without a crash.

Construction and destruction continue, though no builders remain.

This is the basin’s strangest law. Its fitted pieces do not hold fixed positions. Under exceptional currents, whole walls loosen into individual blocks. The blocks roll, rise, and migrate along the floor. Days or years later they collect elsewhere, drawn into arrangements by their shapes and by the water’s pressure. An arcade may disassemble in one district and slowly reappear across a trench. Floors buckle into stairs. Stairs flatten into causeways. Circular rooms tighten around vortices as though sheltering them.

Nothing levitates, and no hidden mechanism is evident. Each motion is explicable by weight, buoyancy, current, and fit. Only the total result is inexplicable. The basin continually rebuilds itself without plan, yet it rarely produces a mere heap. It prefers corridors, courts, towers, channels, and doors.

Thus maps expire quickly. The broad regions persist—the outer terraces, the central deeps, the long northern trench—but their connections change. A passage open at dusk may be sealed by morning with a wall that looks ten thousand years old. An archway may lead into a chamber where yesterday there was open water. Fresh structures emerge already softened, already ancient, because their components carry the erosion of prior forms.

At the basin’s highest rim, the architecture rises through the surface into perpetual rain. There are parapets there, black and slick, visible only between waves. They enclose an ocean at an altitude where clouds drag across the water. Beyond them is not land but a white, depthless weather. Spray and cloud become indistinguishable. Water pours over the outer edges, yet the basin never empties; rain and upwelling replace what is lost, or the geometry below returns it by routes too deep to trace.

On rare calm intervals, the surface becomes almost flat. Then the submerged country can be glimpsed in layers: pale roads crossing beneath one another, isolated towers, immense circular courts, shoals flashing like filings in dim light. Far below all of it, at the limit of vision, something vast seems to move across the tiled floor.

The calm never lasts.

Rain stipples the surface. A current turns. Somewhere in the dark, ancient blocks begin to shift, and the whole made ocean quietly starts becoming another version of itself.
