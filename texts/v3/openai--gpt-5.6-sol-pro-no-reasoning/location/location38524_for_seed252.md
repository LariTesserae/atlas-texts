Beneath a newly split plain of basalt lies a cavity no larger than a small room.

It is not a cave in the old sense. Its walls have not been rounded by centuries of seepage, nor furred with mineral curtains. The hollow opened only recently, when the cooling crust contracted around a pocket of superheated brine. Every surface still bears the violence of that opening: black planes of volcanic glass, knife-edged shelves, pale fractures branching through stone. Loose splinters lie where they fell. Nothing has yet softened them.

The chamber is hot enough to make breathing difficult. Heat comes from all sides rather than from a single vent. The floor is a thin, dark crust over deeper rock; the walls release stored warmth; the water itself arrives almost boiling. Yet the cavity is brilliantly lit.

The brightness comes from a mineral seam exposed across the ceiling. Under pressure, its clear crystals sustain a dense electrical state that present physics would describe only incompletely. No device made it. The seam grew this way in a rare arrangement of cooling rock, salt, and planetary field. It gathers scattered thermal motion and releases it as steady white radiance, without flame and almost without waste. Each crystal shines through its own depth, so there are few true shadows. Black glass gleams blue at the edges. Droplets burn like tiny lenses.

The effect is advanced in mechanism but entirely natural: a geological phenomenon that happens to perform feats beyond any known engineering.

Water is everywhere without quite filling the chamber. It beads on the ceiling, runs along fresh cracks, and collects in several shallow depressions. Along the lowest wall, a narrow ribbon of brine repeatedly appears and disappears. When pressure rises below, it wells up clear and trembling. Minutes later it drains back through pores too fine to see. Condensation forms continuously in the hot, saturated air. Large drops gather, hesitate, and fall with isolated clicks.

Those drops are the loudest regular sound.

Otherwise the chamber keeps a close silence. There is a faint granular ticking as glass contracts. At intervals, a submerged bubble reaches one of the pools and breaks with a soft, thick pop. Now and then the floor gives a minute shudder, dislodging a sliver from an overhang. It strikes the stone with a startling brightness of sound, then the quiet closes again.

Nothing green grows here. There is no soil, no moss, no root hair exploring the cracks. Even the usual cave films are absent from most surfaces. The mineral world is too young, too hot, and chemically unstable. Rust-colored stains have only begun to bloom where water touches iron. Around one pool, white salts outline the changing water level in narrow, immaculate rings. Elsewhere, sulfur condenses in sparse yellow grains, each grain separated from the next by broad reaches of bare black rock.

Life is present only at the threshold of notice.

In the brine dwell rare translucent bodies smaller than fingernail clippings. They have no visible limbs and scarcely resemble animals: flattened, flexible commas that cling beneath submerged ledges. They release their grip only when the pool cools by a few degrees, drifting upward to graze on even simpler heat-feeding cells suspended in the water. When the next pulse of hot brine arrives, they fold into glossy capsules and sink into cracks. A patient observer might wait hours to see one move. Most visitors would call the chamber lifeless.

The place changes nevertheless.

Moisture gathers and falls. Pools swell, steam, and contract. Salts travel outward one crystal at a time. Heat opens hairline fractures; cooling seals others with newly deposited silica. The bright seam fluctuates subtly as pressure shifts through it. Sometimes its white light acquires a faint violet cast, and the droplets on the ceiling respond by trembling in place, held for several seconds against gravity by a local electrical field. Then the color returns to white, and rain falls all at once—brief, heavy, and over before it can become a storm.

These cycles are rapidly remaking the pocket. The youngest fractures are transparent and sharp. Older ones, perhaps only weeks old, have already become clouded by mineral deposition. A shallow channel has begun to cut across the floor, though it is not yet smooth enough to deserve the name. Water follows it uncertainly, dividing around shards and rejoining in bright threads. Each pulse chooses almost the same route, changing it by fractions.

There is no entrance wide enough for a person. The cavity exists inside the rock as a sealed brightness: an intimate weather system with no sky, a wet and incandescent room that no one constructed and perhaps no eye will ever see. Above it lies a dark thickness of cooling basalt. Below it, hotter waters circulate through a maze of new fractures. Its boundaries are temporary.

Eventually the ceiling seam will dim. The brine will either drain or rise. Silica will blunt the black edges, salts will crowd the bare floor, and the chamber will become an ordinary mineral pocket—or collapse before age can reach it.

For now it remains open, sharp, sparse, and dazzling: a hidden place at the beginning of its history, raining into itself.
