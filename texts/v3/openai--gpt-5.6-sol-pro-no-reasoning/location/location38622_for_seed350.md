The flood is not over. It has become the climate, the ground, and perhaps the substance from which the region is continually made.

Warm black water fills the basin from the buried lowlands to a horizon rarely visible through rain. There is no dependable surface. At times the flood lies twenty meters deep over drowned soil; elsewhere it rises without transition into vertical reaches—columns and hanging walls of water that stand unsupported in the air. Rain crosses these formations sideways, upward, and in slow revolving sheets. The distinction between atmosphere and ocean survives only locally.

What land exists is recent. Black shelves of glassy slag push from the flood with knife-bright edges, steaming in the warm downpour. Mud islands inflate overnight around submerged machinery, clot with roots, then rupture and sink before noon. Ridges appear where opposing currents collide and harden: pale mineral seams, lattices of salt, quick-set concrete extruded from no visible nozzle. They have not yet had time to round. Every projection is raw, fractured, chemically vivid. Erosion is weak not because the water is gentle, but because nothing remains in one shape long enough to be worn smooth.

Vegetation occupies nearly every available interval.

Broad floating leaves overlap like roofing plates, dark green above and violet beneath. Rope-thick stems descend through the water, knotting around pipes, beams, and one another. Mangrove-like groves stand on no soil at all; their roots enclose pockets of warmer water and hold them as translucent bulbs. Curtains of filamentous weed hang in the upright water walls. Ferns grow from rust. Pale reeds emerge from turbine housings. Moss seals electrical cabinets so completely that their warning lights shine through it as blurred red stars.

There are no animals. No insects visit the waxy flowers. No fish disturb the submerged avenues. No birds cross the rain. Even the innumerable small motions among the leaves are caused by pressure waves, machinery, or the plants themselves. Pollination occurs through industrial vibration. Seedpods open only at particular frequencies, firing their contents into turbulent currents when the buried engines reach full load. Some flowers have grown cup-shaped around sirens and appear to drink the sound.

The machinery is everywhere, though buildings are sparse.

A pumping station leans above the flood on six concrete legs, its lower floors full of green water. Farther out, sections of refinery walkway connect nothing to nothing. Chimneys rise singly from drowned foundations, exhaling bubbles rather than smoke; the bubbles fall into the sky and gather beneath the cloud ceiling. Conveyor towers migrate slowly across the basin on concealed tracks. Their belts carry wet clay, leaves, shards of ceramic, and occasionally perfect cubes of compressed rain. No operator is visible. Control rooms stand open, lit by failing amber panels, their chairs rooted to the floor by fibrous growth.

Underwater, larger systems continue their work. Rows of impellers churn beneath the drowned fields. Intake mouths dilate and close. Kilometers of insulated cable twitch in the silt as though current were a muscular force. Vast dies force mud upward into hexagonal columns, manufacturing temporary ground. Other machines break that ground apart moments later and feed it back into the flood.

The noise is beyond loudness. It is structural.

Rain strikes water, leaves, metal, and other rain with a continuous crushing roar. Pumps hammer at mismatched rhythms. Turbines howl below the hearing range and make the vegetation shudder. Pressure vents scream from unseen depths. Steel gantries twist with sounds like ships splitting in winter ice. Every few minutes an enormous concussion crosses the basin, flattening the floating gardens in expanding rings. The plants rise again behind it, streaming warm water.

Speech would be useless here. Even thought seems to require shelter.

The few surviving structures have been adapted accordingly. Scattered through the region are small windowless chambers of layered concrete, rubber, cork, and root fiber. Their doors open inward against water pressure, though the water level may be lower inside—or may occupy the ceiling while the floor remains dry. Within, the noise diminishes to a dense vibration in the teeth. Maps are bolted to walls, but none agree. Handwritten marks recur in several shelters:

**DO NOT FOLLOW THE SHORE.**

**COUNT THE THUNDER BETWEEN ROOMS.**

**IF THE WATER SHOWS YOUR REFLECTION, WAIT UNTIL IT FORGETS.**

The warnings are practical. Distance does not remain constant in the basin. A refinery stack glimpsed through rain may be a kilometer away, then stand close enough to touch when lightning illuminates it. A submerged road can descend for days while never reaching greater depth. Two neighboring islands may share the same roots but experience different hours. Water sometimes remembers former structures: currents bend around factories demolished decades ago, and algae grow in the shapes of absent staircases. Entering one of these green outlines can place a traveler briefly inside the vanished building. The walls are felt as pressure, the rooms as colder volumes. Then the memory collapses and becomes water again.

Lightning occurs mostly below the surface. Blue fractures travel through the flood, branching around roots and corroded pylons. For an instant they reveal the basin’s impossible density: drowned roofs stacked beneath newer roofs; forests growing upside down from suspended silt; pipes entering themselves; tanks larger inside than the districts containing them. The light never reaches far. Beyond each illuminated layer waits another, close and crowded.

The warmth accelerates everything. Metal flowers into rust while watched. Shoots lengthen visibly in the rain. Fresh concrete sweats and grows soft. A green island can form around a floating bolt, spread into a grove, acquire a humming generator and three meters of rail, and fold underwater within a single afternoon. Destruction is equally fertile. When a tower falls, its impact throws up ridges of mud already seeded with ferns. When a boiler bursts, the escaping steam condenses into a hovering pond. Roots enter the pond from all directions and construct a thicket in midair.

Nothing decomposes in the ordinary way. Without animals, decay belongs to plants, chemicals, and machines. Leaves are dissolved by acidic mist, drawn into filtration beds, and pressed into dark fuel bricks. Dead trunks mineralize around hot pipes. Rust creeps across steel in branching patterns and sprouts metallic fronds. Some surfaces cannot be classified: half bark, half cast iron, warm to the touch and pulsing with periodic current.

The region’s apparent center is a low circular factory almost entirely submerged. Only its roof and twelve exhaust cowls rise above the flood. Around it the rain rotates in a broad, permanent vortex. Vines descend into its open vents; cables emerge from them and climb the vines. At irregular intervals, the factory sounds a horn so powerful that the nearby water becomes momentarily solid. Waves freeze into dark, curved walls. Raindrops hang like beads in glass. Floating leaves are trapped halfway through folding.

During these intervals of hardened water, the landscape can be crossed on foot.

Then the horn ends.

The flood liquefies all at once, and anything caught inside resumes every interrupted fall, current, collapse, and collision simultaneously. Forests plunge through themselves. Walkways snap. Great volumes of water trade places. The world roars back into motion with enough force to tear new channels through the air.

No one knows what the factory produces. Occasionally a loading door opens beneath the surface and releases hundreds of identical objects: ceramic stair treads, empty green bottles, warm bricks stamped with dates that have not occurred, or small sealed drums containing only the sound of rain. Conveyors collect most of them and return them to the building. The rest become nuclei for islands.

There is no sunrise, though brightness changes. Day is a gray-green diffusion through layered cloud and suspended water. Night is nearly black except for submerged lightning, instrument lamps, and the slow chemical glow of certain flowers. Those flowers open toward the deepest water rather than the sky. Their radiance shows no moving creatures—only roots, cables, weeds, drowned machines, and farther down, another trembling surface with rain falling upward from it.

The basin may have a bottom. The machinery behaves as if it does.

The plants behave as if it does not.
