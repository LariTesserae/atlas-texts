Below the surface lies a cavern so broad that its opposite walls are usually lost in grayness. It is not deep in the crushing sense. The ceiling hangs low enough to remain present—an uneven plane of newly fractured limestone—but the hollow extends laterally for many leagues, a buried plain rather than a chamber. Long shelves step down toward shallow channels. Fresh cracks divide the floor into angular plates, their edges still sharp, their exposed faces pale as split bone.

The place is cool and damp without being wet. Water enters as condensation, gathering on the ceiling until each drop grows heavy enough to fall. Occasional rains on the land above awaken narrow seams, but there are no lasting floods. Moisture collects instead in scattered basins: clear pools fitted tightly into fissures, chains of puddles along the lowest shelves, and thin streams that travel a short distance before disappearing through gravel. Between them lie broad tracts of dry stone dust and loose chips.

Nothing appears to age here. The fractured ledges do not round. Fallen flakes remain where they first landed. The channels keep their exact courses, never deepening, while each pool holds the same dark waterline against the rock. Drops fall steadily enough to seem numerous, yet no basin fills. Water vanishes at precisely the rate it arrives. The cavern persists in a young state—not recently made, perhaps, but held forever at the moment after breaking.

Dimness occupies it evenly. There is no visible source of illumination. A cold, diffuse gray clings to stone and water, sufficient for silhouettes, edges, and movement but rarely for color. Pale mineral faces shine faintly. Wet surfaces carry dull reflections. Distance fades not into darkness but into layer after layer of the same ash-colored air.

Vegetation survives only where condensation is most dependable. Low films of moss darken a few ledges. Colorless fungal threads bind pockets of silt beneath overhangs, producing small, blunt fruiting bodies that animals crop as soon as they appear. Along the pools grow scattered mats of liverwort and translucent algae. There are no groves, no vines, no continuous ground cover. Each patch stands apart, an island of softness on an otherwise mineral plain.

The animals are another matter.

They fill the cavern.

Vast herds of pale, long-legged grazers move over the shelves, browsing invisible films from the rock with rasping mouths. They are lean and compact, no larger than hares, but number in hundreds of thousands. Their hides range from chalk white to the gray of old water. Eyes are reduced or absent; broad ears and trembling whiskers turn constantly toward dripping water and the movement of their neighbors. From a distance, a herd resembles a tide of pebbles flowing over the floor.

Among them run smaller creatures: blind beetles with polished shells, silver springtails lifting like spray, translucent crickets, soft-bodied scavengers, and quick six-legged things that vanish into cracks at the pressure of a footstep. Dense rafts of tiny crustaceans patrol the pools. Cave fish lie in orderly layers beneath them, mouths opening and closing in the same unhurried rhythm. Flat worms cling under stones. Pale eels knot themselves in the deeper fissures.

Above, the ceiling is furred with animals.

Bats cover much of it, though not in chaotic colonies. They hang at regular intervals, each body separated from the next by a narrow hand’s width, forming immense constellations that continue beyond sight. At any moment a fixed portion of them is airborne. Their paths braid through the cavern’s open reaches: departure, feeding circuit, water, return. They do not migrate, breed in sudden seasons, or erupt in alarm. Their population neither increases nor dwindles. Newborns appear as the old die, and scavengers dismantle every carcass before its warmth has left it.

The loudness is continuous and layered. Wings beat overhead like distant rain. Claws tick across limestone. Grazers rasp mineral films from the floor with the sound of files drawn softly over wood. Crickets pulse from crevices. Water drops strike pools with hollow, amplified knocks. Bats pour out high cries that the ear catches only as pressure, while lower animal calls roll across the shelves in long, throaty waves. There are no silent intervals. Even when every nearby animal is still, the regional multitude remains audible—an immense living weather enclosed in stone.

Predators are common because prey is inexhaustibly common. Pale cats, low at the shoulder and heavy in the forelimbs, wait among angular blocks. Long-snouted reptiles lie beside the warm bodies of crowded herds, cool-skinned and patient. Packs of small, nearly hairless hunters trail the edges of animal rivers. They kill, feed, and withdraw, yet never seem to alter the number or behavior of the prey. Panic spreads only a measured distance. The herd parts, closes, and resumes grazing.

Everything follows durable routes. Hooves and padded feet cross the same slabs without wearing them smooth. Bats repeat the same circuits through the same cold air. Predators hold territories whose boundaries never shift. Pools contain stable populations, neither clouded by waste nor stripped by hunger. Fungi consume what falls; grazers consume the fungi; hunters consume the grazers; minute scavengers clean the hunters’ remains. The balance is neither peaceful nor merciful. It is simply exact.

There is no construction anywhere. No wall has been stacked, no path cleared, no mark deliberately cut. Yet primitive intelligence flickers among the fauna. Certain long-fingered animals pry shellfish from pool margins with sharp flakes of limestone. They do not shape the flakes, but select them carefully and carry them for a time. Small apes—or something near enough—use fallen stalactite tips to turn stones and expose grubs. One population of broad-jawed rodents wedges bone splinters into cracks to draw out larvae. Tools exist only as borrowed fragments of the cavern. They are never collected into stores, never improved, and never leave artifacts that outlast their immediate use. When released, each tool becomes indistinguishable from debris.

The terrain opens into several distinct regions while remaining part of the same buried plain. To one side lies a field of sharp limestone fins, crowded with roosting animals and threaded by narrow runways. Farther on, the floor descends to a chain of shallow black lakes, their margins alive with breathing, drinking, spawning bodies. Beyond those rise broken terraces where the ceiling nearly meets the ground, forcing herds into broad, slow-moving columns. Elsewhere, immense flat expanses hold almost nothing but dust, scattered moss, and passing life. These empty reaches make the concentrations of fauna at water and shelter seem even more impossible.

There is no wind, though wings push local currents across the skin. The air smells of wet mineral, fur, guano, fungus, and cold animal breath. It should become foul, but it does not. It should grow warmer under so many bodies, but it remains cool. It should darken with disturbed dust, yet visibility never changes. Like the pools and the unworn stone, the atmosphere is held at equilibrium.

A visitor could travel for days and encounter no evidence that any earlier moment differed from the present one. No abandoned nest decays. No colony expands into a new chamber. No channel shifts after rain. There are births, hunts, wounds, feedings, and deaths, but no history accumulates from them. Events occur without producing change.

The cavern is not frozen. It is furiously alive.

That is what makes its permanence unsettling: not stillness, but motion repeated so completely that, at the scale of the landscape, it amounts to stillness. The animals surge, flutter, drink, rasp, stalk, and cry beneath the dim stone ceiling. Water falls. Pools receive it. Fresh fractures keep their edges. The underground plain extends into gray distance, loud with innumerable lives, forever maintaining the shape of its first day.
