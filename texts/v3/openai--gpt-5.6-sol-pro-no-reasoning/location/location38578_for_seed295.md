The valley is built all the way down.

There is no bedrock beneath it, no original mountain under the mountain, no soil that was not laid by deliberate mouths and feet. Its high ridges are walls of fitted stone, each block carried, rolled, or shouldered into place so long ago that lichens have erased the joints. The cliffs are retaining faces. The ravines are drains. Terraces descend from the crest in immense, shallow steps, holding forests, reed beds, grazing lawns, and dark reservoirs. Even the distant peaks, softened by age and warm mist, have the faint regularity of roofs.

Nothing here suggests machinery. There are no cables, engines, lamps, hinges, or wheels. Construction belongs instead to the innumerable animals that occupy the valley. It is not a wilderness, though it looks like one from afar. It is a city made without tools.

The air is warm despite the altitude. Dampness collects rather than flows: condensation beads on leaves, slicks stone, and gathers in hollows lined with clay. Rain comes irregularly, usually at night, striking the upper terraces and vanishing into porous masonry. For days afterward, water reappears below as drips and thin curtains, feeding pockets of fern and deep cistern ponds. There is one river, but it is less a continuous stream than a procession of basins. Water spills from one constructed pool to another, disappears through gravel galleries, then emerges hundreds of paces away beneath roots braided into an arch.

Every surface has been occupied. Moss seals the retaining walls. Trees rise from square courts filled with compost, their roots carefully enclosed by stone. Vines knit neighboring terraces together until the architecture appears to sag under green weight. Bromeliads crowd ledges; liverworts glaze stairways; broad leaves overlap like tiles. In the darker lower galleries, pale fungi consume old nests and fallen timber. Their warmth draws clouds of tiny flies.

The vegetation is lush but not unconstrained. Browsing mouths keep passages open. Hooves maintain the sloping roads. Burrowing colonies aerate the garden beds. Birds sow fruit trees along parapets by habit, always choosing the same defecation posts. What resembles horticulture is the accumulated consequence of appetite, migration, nesting, and territorial custom.

The valley is almost entirely dark.

Its terraces face away from the strongest sun, and the upper walls carry dense crowns of foliage that close above the lower levels. Day arrives as a diluted brown-green glow. Pools reflect narrow pieces of sky. At noon, a few shafts reach the deepest courts and reveal drifting insects, wet fur, feathers, horns, and steam rising from crowded bodies. Night is darker but not quieter. The place has evolved no dependence on sight alone. Paths are read by scent, touch, echo, and the pressure of familiar neighbors.

The sound is overwhelming.

Tree frogs pulse from every cistern. Rookeries occupy whole cliff faces, tens of thousands of nests packed into cavities between blocks. Their cries merge into a continuous abrasive weather. Monkeys bark across the canopy. Hoofed animals clatter over stone ramps in slow-moving herds. Wings beat in enclosed galleries with the concussion of rugs being shaken. Cicadas produce a high metallic pressure that makes the teeth ache. Beneath it all is the low, ceaseless labor of the valley itself: chewing, scratching, breathing, dripping, rumination, the shifting of bodies against bodies.

There are so many animals that emptiness seems unnatural. A bare step is crossed within moments. A branch bends under several species at once. Small birds ride the backs of large browsers; beetles inhabit the birds’ nests; mites inhabit the beetles. Sleeping colonies hang beneath bridges in layered curtains. Tortoises crowd warm plazas paved with flat black stones. Rodents live in wall conduits, maintaining them simply through passage. Great slow herbivores move along the broadest avenues, polishing the paving with their feet and trimming the foliage to the height of their mouths.

Predators are present, but they do not bring silence. Their arrival changes the composition of the noise: warning barks propagate terrace by terrace; wings lift in successive dark explosions; hooves strike the roads. Then the valley closes again behind them.

Its laws are ordinary laws, enforced by repetition. Water descends. Dung accumulates. Roots widen joints. Walls settle. Animals repair what benefits them and abandon what does not. A breached cistern becomes a marsh; a marsh fills with reeds; reeds trap debris; debris becomes a new terrace. Fallen trees are buried beneath nests and mud, strengthening the slope. Structures persist not because any creature understands the whole, but because thousands of species maintain fragments of it in pursuit of food, shelter, heat, or mates.

The oldest works have reached equilibrium with erosion. Corners are rounded. Stairways have become ramps. Relief carvings—if they were carvings—have softened into niches where swallows nest. Some walls bow outward without collapsing, held by roots and the compacted generations of burrowers. Nothing looks newly made. Construction proceeds at the pace of colonies: a grain carried, a twig placed, a stone nudged aside, a tunnel lengthened by the width of a claw.

From the highest ridge, the true scale becomes visible. The inhabited terraces extend across several adjoining basins, linked by saddle roads and forested causeways. Dome-shaped hills prove to be ancient mounds. Long escarpments meet at careful angles. Reservoirs recur at measured intervals, black among the green. Warm clouds hide the farther reaches, but the noise continues through them, implying more courts, more galleries, more crowded slopes beyond sight.

No maker stands apart from what is made. The valley has no ruins because nothing has ceased being used; it has only structures whose users have changed. A hall becomes a rookery, a stair becomes a streambed, a tower becomes a strangler fig’s hollow core. The entire high country is architecture passing so slowly through ecology that the two can no longer be separated.

It is damp, warm, ancient, and almost lightless: an animal-built mountain range, densely alive, roaring without pause, changing by increments too small to witness.
