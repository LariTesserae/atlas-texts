The city occupies a hollow near the summit of a mountain, though from within it does not feel high. Its boundary is close and unmistakable: a broken ring of pale cliffs, no more than a few hundred paces across, enclosing a bowl of grass, stone, water, and unfinished streets. Beyond the ring there is only sky. Clouds pass beneath the gaps in the cliffs, and on clear days the distant lowlands appear as blue-gray stains, too far below to possess detail.

Everything here seems newly made.

The rock has scarcely been weathered. It rises in blunt shelves and swollen folds, pale green, charcoal, and chalk-white, with none of the deep channels or delicate pinnacles that age might carve. Cracks are short and uncertain. Some end halfway across a slab, as if the stone changed its mind while cooling. After rain, water beads on the smooth faces rather than finding established courses. It gathers wherever it can—in hoof-sized hollows, between fitted paving stones, along the shallow foundations of houses—and then spills over in thin, wandering films.

The water has not yet learned the mountain.

There is no river, only a multitude of beginnings. Springs press silently from seams in the cliff. Condensation pearls beneath overhangs. Every roof drips for hours after the clouds have gone. Narrow trenches cross the settlement, but the water frequently abandons them, slipping into fresh cracks or appearing unexpectedly in courtyards. One stone basin may overflow for a month, then remain dry for years while another begins to weep.

The people call these changes *the choosing*.

Their city is elaborate despite its size. It contains gates, plazas, cisterns, retaining walls, stairs, workshops, gardens, shrines, and even the suggestion of districts, all compressed into the mountain bowl. Yet it is nearly empty. A broad avenue, paved in fitted slabs, may serve three occupied houses. Rows of roofless chambers ascend the inner cliff like seats in an amphitheater, but no one remembers them ever being full. Staircases divide and climb toward blank walls. Bridges span fissures only a person could step across. Stone channels branch with the complexity of veins, feeding terraces where nothing has yet been planted.

It is urban space awaiting an urban population.

No ruins lie beneath it. The city is not old enough to be abandoned. Instead, the builders construct in anticipation. Each generation adds homes for descendants not yet born, granaries for harvests the small summit cannot produce, meeting halls for assemblies that would require every living person several times over. They work slowly with stone hammers, antler picks, hardwood levers, fiber ropes, fire, and water. Copper is known but scarce; it is reserved for awls, fishhooks, ritual blades, and the thin shining plumb bobs used by master builders. Most shaping is done patiently, one bruise at a time.

The masonry is remarkable not because the tools are sophisticated, but because time is abundant. A mason may spend eighteen years preparing a single lintel. Walls are assembled from large, softly angular blocks fitted without mortar. Fresh-cut surfaces show a dim waxen sheen. Moss enters only later, gathering in seams as fine green handwriting.

Vegetation fills what people do not.

The central hollow is lush with knee-high sedges, mountain sorrel, broad-leaved herbs, and dark grasses heavy with water. Small groves stand in sheltered pockets: crooked rowans, low silver-barked trees, dense shrubs with purple stems. Ferns unfold beneath stairs and inside unused houses. Moss pads the reservoirs and climbs the lower walls, but the upper stone remains bare, too new and too frequently washed. Gardens merge into marsh at their edges. Beans and bitter greens grow beside wild orchids; medicinal plants escape their beds and take root in gutters.

Because the soil is shallow, roots sometimes lift whole mats of earth. These drift a finger’s breadth above the underlying stone during the wettest season, buoyed by trapped water. Walking across them produces a deep, soft drumming. Children test the floating turf with poles and cut it into squares, towing the pieces to bare courtyards where new gardens are wanted.

The air is cool without being bitter. Even at midday it holds the chill of stone chambers and cloud shade. Sunlight, when it arrives, is clear and ordinary: neither magical nor dim, simply bright enough to reveal every wet surface. The city then glitters in a modest, scattered way. Drops shine under eaves. Pale pavements reflect the blue of the sky. Thin steam rises from dark soil, though the air never grows warm.

Clouds enter through the western gate.

They do not merely descend upon the settlement; they move through its streets like slow crowds. Walls emerge and vanish. Empty doorways become rectangles of white. The nearest roofs drip loudly while farther buildings dissolve, leaving only their hammering echoes. Moisture condenses on hair, woven cloaks, tools, and the faces of statues. When the cloud passes, the whole city seems newly taken from water.

For such an empty place, it is astonishingly loud.

The cliffs collect and multiply every sound. Stone hammers strike in uneven choruses from distant terraces. Mallets thud. Wedges crack. Water plinks into hundreds of bowls, gutters, jars, and cisterns, each with its own pitch. Wind crosses the mouths of unused drainage pipes and raises long notes, low as horns or sharp as reed whistles. A single conversation in the upper square can be heard, distorted but distinct, in the marsh below. Footsteps return from several directions.

The builders have learned to listen before speaking. Certain sounds travel farther than others, and some words should not be said loudly. A name called near the eastern cistern may emerge hours later from a cliff seam on the opposite side of the bowl. Short sounds return quickly. Long sounds sometimes remain in the rock.

This is the place’s principal strangeness: the mountain is acoustically unfinished.

The new stone has open chambers within it—bubbles, cooling ducts, blind fissures, and taut mineral sheets. Sound enters these spaces and moves slowly. At dusk the city releases fragments of the day: half a hammer stroke, a cough, three syllables of a work song, the bark of a dog that died the previous winter. After heavy rain, the buried echoes are flushed out all at once. The settlement then roars with old labor. Invisible crews pound beneath the plazas; vanished arguments mutter from wells; ceremonial drums roll from the uncut cliff.

No one considers this haunting. It is geology.

The people map echoes as carefully as springs. Small clay marks are fixed to walls: spirals for places that return sound, bars for places that hold it, open circles for places that swallow it entirely. The silent zones are treated with caution. Moss grows especially thick there, and water crossing into them makes no audible splash. Houses are not built in silence, although storehouses sometimes are.

Animals are scarce. The height and enclosed terrain offer little forage, and the wet rock provides few burrows. Tiny black snails graze on the cistern walls. Pale moths gather around lamps of rendered seed oil. Now and then a pair of mountain crows settles on the ring cliffs, watching the city with a proprietary patience. Their calls ricochet for minutes.

There are also long-legged amphibians in the marshy center, no larger than a thumb, colored like wet slate. They are rarely seen. Before rainfall they climb walls and cling beneath stone ledges, where their throats pulse without sound. Only when the first drops fall do they cry out—a startling clatter like pebbles shaken in a pot. Thousands may be present, but the chorus lasts less than a minute, and afterward the city seems briefly quiet.

Human habitation reveals itself in small concentrations. Smoke rises from perhaps twenty roofs. Woolen cloaks hang beneath awnings. Baskets stand beside doorways. On dry afternoons, people sit in the open to twist cord, scrape hides, grind grain, or sharpen wooden stakes. Their lives occupy only a fraction of what has been built. They may walk an entire street before encountering another person.

Still, they maintain the whole city.

Leaves are swept from empty plazas. Channels are cleared where no garden depends on them. Roof beams are replaced over vacant chambers. Each unfinished district has a keeper who inspects it after rain, marking movement in the walls with lines of red ocher. Construction is not driven by urgency. It is the settlement’s central obligation, continued at the pace of lichen and inheritance.

Plans are not drawn. They are sung.

A building song describes each wall by rhythm: heavy beats for large stones, quick beats for small fillers, held notes for lintels and openings. Because the cliffs preserve sound, the oldest plans can still be recovered from certain resonant shafts. A master builder lowers an apprentice by rope into darkness, and the apprentice sings a prompt. Sometimes the mountain answers with a pattern composed generations earlier.

Errors are left in place. A stair that rises too steeply is not demolished; another stair is built beside it. A channel that flows uphill remains as a record of misjudged level. In this way the city becomes layered without becoming ancient. Its complexity is accumulated intention rather than accumulated decay.

The ground itself continues to settle. Not violently, but visibly across a lifetime. A plaza develops a shallow tilt. A spring appears beneath a bedroom. Two walls once separated by an alley lean together until their roofs touch. The inhabitants accept that architecture here is collaboration with stone still becoming stone. They never build closed circles, because the mountain must have room to expand. Every wall contains a deliberate gap. Every roof has one unfastened beam. Every paved street leaves a strip of living earth down its center.

Nothing may be completed.

That is both engineering principle and sacred law. To finish a structure—to seal its last seam, smooth its final roughness, declare it fixed—is believed to make it brittle against the mountain’s slow emergence. Finished things crack. Open things adjust.

Thus the city stands high above the world: small, green, wet, ringing, and largely unpeopled. It is neither a ruin nor a beginning, but a beginning prolonged into a way of life. Water searches for its courses. Plants test the shallow soil. Echoes wander through chambers that erosion has not yet opened. Streets wait for feet. Houses wait for families. The mountain waits to discover its own shape.

And each morning, before the first tools strike, the builders walk the ring of pale cliffs and listen for any sound the stone has invented in the night.
