The city occupies the upper faces of a mountain range so high that the lower world cannot be seen.

There is no ascent to it. Beneath the outermost terraces, cliffs narrow into darkness and then into an absence without weather, horizon, or reflected light. Sometimes the mountains appear rooted in that depth; sometimes they hang over it. This changes according to where one stands. From a northern parapet, the peaks seem to rise from a black plain far below. From the next parapet, no plain exists, and the same peaks descend upside down beside themselves.

The air is cool, thin, and perfectly dry. Nothing beads upon stone. Nothing drips. The basins in the plazas have never held water, though their interiors have been polished into pale bowls by something that moves through them on moonless intervals. Salt lies everywhere in narrow seams: white, gray, violet, occasionally clear as glass. It is not the residue of vanished seas. Under the salt are older layers of salt, and under those, intricate crystals that split into warm dust when exposed.

The city has no builders.

Its streets, stairs, courts, arches, retaining walls, and close ranks of narrow towers are made from the mountain’s own banded rock. There are no tool marks. Houses appear to have weathered out of solid ridges, their rooms produced by exfoliation, their windows by fractures, their doorways by the shedding of rectangular slabs. The distinction between geology and architecture is persuasive at a distance and impossible up close. A cliff becomes a district by degrees. A scree slope resolves into courtyards. A row of dwellings turns, over several miles, into a natural colonnade of basalt.

No mechanism operates here. No hinges, wheels, lamps, pipes, or cables have ever been found. Yet the city is organized. Broad avenues follow the pressure-lines of the massif. Tall chambers stand where the wind is weakest. Flights of stairs link ledges that do not remain vertically aligned. Walls surround open squares, although beyond them lies only more emptiness. The place possesses infrastructure without technology: channels for substances that are not liquids, vents for winds that do not enter from outside, bridges spanning distances that occasionally cease to exist.

There are thousands of buildings, but almost nothing inhabits them.

The emptiness is not ruin. Floors are clean. Corners hold no litter. Doorways remain sharp beneath their long weathering. It is as if vacancy is the city’s intended population, carefully housed in halls, vaulted granaries, silent amphitheaters, and neighborhoods of single-cell rooms. Space is parceled here with civic precision. Some courtyards contain more absence than their dimensions should permit. Looking into one gives the sensation of overlooking a continent at night.

Light arrives without a visible sun. It is a diluted mineral glow, strongest along exposed edges and weakest in open sky. At what might be noon, the city is the color of ash under cloud. At what might be evening, the salt veins awaken with a dim internal radiance, tracing old faults through walls and pavements. Shadows seldom point in the same direction. They gather beneath arches, detach, and cross the plazas slowly, as though returning to structures that no longer stand.

There is weather, though almost no atmosphere seems available to make it.

Dry fronts move across the heights. They announce themselves by a darkening of distant ridges and a faint tightening in the cavities of the skull. Then a transparent pressure passes through the city. Dust lifts in flat sheets, never swirling, and slides uphill. Loose stones tremble but do not roll. For several minutes every arch produces a hair-thin tone, too quiet to be called music. Afterward, new cracks appear in the salt crust, branching in patterns resembling street plans.

At longer intervals comes the inversion.

The void below brightens to dull silver, while the sky becomes granular and close. Gravity weakens along the eastern wards. Pebbles rise from roofs and arrange themselves in sparse constellations several body-lengths above the streets. Whole staircases can be climbed sideways. The towers lean—not by moving, but by altering the direction in which their height is understood. These events may last an hour or a season. Their passing leaves no damage. The city is old enough to have weathered not merely wind and cold, but repeated changes in the rules that define a wall, a slope, a distance, or a fall.

Erosion has reached equilibrium with impossibility. Every surface is worn, but none is diminishing. The cliffs continually shed flakes as thin as paper. They descend a short way, pause, and join the mountain elsewhere. Gargoyle-like protrusions blunt under the dry fronts and sharpen again during inversions. Some districts are visibly ancient—rounded stairs, hollowed thresholds, pillars narrowed to stone cords—yet casts of those lost surfaces can be found in adjacent empty rooms, formed from compacted darkness.

There is almost no life in any familiar sense.

In shaded fractures, colonies of black film spread one grain per year. They consume neither light nor mineral. Instead, they seem to feed on small errors of position. Where the film grows, mismatched corners align and cracked lintels remember how to bear weight. A patch scraped away—if scraping were possible without tools—would leave architecture subtly less coherent. This may be the city’s only vegetation: not plants, but a slow corrective skin tending the geometry.

Elsewhere, especially beneath overhangs, stand brittle fans of translucent mineral fiber. They look grown rather than deposited. They open during the dry fronts and close when the air is still. Each is rooted in a seam of salt and casts a shadow shaped like an unfamiliar animal. Broken fans release no spores. Their fragments crawl very gradually toward the nearest vertical surface and fuse there.

The animals themselves are rare.

Small six-legged things, pale and jointless, cross the broad avenues at enormous intervals. They make no sound and leave rows of shallow depressions ahead of themselves rather than behind. A traveler may see tracks appearing across a plaza and, hours later, the creature arriving to fit them. They graze upon the translucent fans, though no portion of the fan is removed. After feeding, they become slightly more opaque.

Higher still, among the needle peaks above the main city, solitary floaters pass through the dimness. They resemble folded membranes stretched around empty centers. They ride the pressure fronts, turning without wingbeats, and sometimes enter the upper towers through windows too narrow for them. No one has seen one emerge. Their low calls cannot be heard directly; instead, loose salt gathers into concentric rings on nearby floors.

The only loud event is extremely distant.

Now and then, beyond the western escarpment, a section of mountain detaches and falls into the lower absence. There is no impact. Days later, the city gives one deep contraction. Doors become narrower. Plazas shorten. The horizon jerks nearer by an imperceptible degree. A muted thud passes through the soles, more felt than heard, and every rare creature freezes in place. Then the fallen mountain appears overhead as a dark island, eroded on sides that did not exist before.

Time is unreliable but not chaotic. The city follows several overlapping cycles. Salt brightens. Shadows migrate. Pressure fronts scour the high wards. Inversions release stones into the air. Black films reconcile the resulting discrepancies. Mineral fans open, the small animals cross their preexisting tracks, and the floaters vanish into towers. These processes fit together like an ecosystem, though matter, distance, and causality occupy the roles normally held by soil, water, and sunlight.

There are rules.

Nothing wet can exist here. Any liquid introduced into the city would divide instantly into dry components: powder, vaporless cold, and the memory of flow. A sealed vessel would become, upon entry, a solid inclusion in the rock. The city does not permit containment to contradict its dryness.

Nothing falls indefinitely. A falling object is eventually reclassified as part of another altitude.

No room remains entered when unobserved. Turn away from a doorway and the chamber behind it may become a cliff cavity, a neighboring street, or the interior of a tower several miles off. Routes persist only while traveled.

Sound cannot cross an open square. It goes around, entering the buildings and emerging from windows on the far side. Thus the plazas are profoundly silent even during the dry fronts, while empty houses sometimes whisper with noises making long detours.

And no structure is abandoned. Abandonment would imply former occupants. The city’s vacancy is older than its stone.

From the highest ridge, when the dimness thins, one can see the whole inhabited-looking expanse: terraces descending over dozens of summits, bridges reaching from peak to peak, square districts set into vertical cliffs, avenues pale with salt, towers puncturing layers of skewed horizon. It is vast enough to suggest a nation and empty enough to seem like a single unoccupied room.

Far beyond, other ranges hover in the near-void. Some carry cities of their own. Some may be reflections cast by no light. During inversions they draw closer, and for a little while their streets align with these streets, forming an urban plan that extends across the gaps.

Then the pressure changes.

The distant districts rotate out of correspondence. The bridges once again end over nothing. Dust ascends in level sheets. Along every wall, the salt begins to glow.

And in the sealed silence of the central plaza, footprints appear one by one, approaching from a direction for which the city has no gate.
