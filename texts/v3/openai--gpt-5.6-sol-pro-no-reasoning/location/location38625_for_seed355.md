The city lies low enough that the horizon has difficulty clearing it.

It occupies a broad depression of black clay and weather-softened stone, a basin shallow as a thumbprint and many miles across. Water is everywhere but rarely deep. It films the streets, gathers in courtyards, trembles inside old foundations, and moves beneath the paving with the slow pressure of blood under skin. After rain—which may have happened yesterday or centuries ago—the walls continue to weep. Drops swell from undecorated stone, descend halfway, then hesitate as though reconsidering gravity.

There is almost no light. The sky is a close, heated darkness, not clouded so much as unfinished. At its lowest reaches it condenses against the rooftops. Warm mist pours over parapets and down stairways in translucent folds. Nothing shines directly, yet surfaces can be distinguished: wet basalt from dry basalt, brick from mud, open door from sealed wall. The illumination seems to come from the act of looking. Whatever receives attention acquires a dim gray edge, while everything behind the observer closes again into perfect black.

Heat remains trapped in the basin. It is heavy, mineral, and intimate. Standing still brings sweat immediately, though the moving air is almost cool. The stones radiate accumulated warmth through the soles of the feet. Shallow pools are hotter than the air and sometimes steam without boiling. In the deepest plazas, condensation falls upward from the water and disappears into the sky.

The city has no builders.

It is nevertheless extensive and unmistakably urban. Long avenues cross at deliberate intervals. Houses stand wall to wall in some districts, while elsewhere single facades rise from acres of flooded emptiness. There are arcades, plazas, cisterns, terraces, narrow service passages, processional stairs, drainage channels, and broad vaulted structures resembling markets. All are made without timber, metal, glass, plaster, or any sign of tools. Stone meets stone in weathered seams. Bricks appear to have grown rectangular. Columns descend into the ground without bases and emerge elsewhere as curbs or thresholds.

No inscription survives, if any was ever made. Ornament is limited to erosion: scallops cut by runoff, mineral veils, rounded corners, and branching grooves where water has found the same route for ages. The city is not ruined. Ruin implies a prior completeness. This place appears to have been eroded into architecture.

Its oldest streets are the most precise. Water has worn their centers into smooth, shallow canals, leaving raised margins like sidewalks. At intersections, centuries of converging flow have opened circular wells. Their rims are polished and warm. Looking into one reveals not water below but another intersection beneath, with its own well at the center, and beneath that another. Occasionally a drop falls past all of them. Its sound never returns.

Vegetation survives sparsely and without color. Pale reeds grow one at a time from cracks, separated by whole streets. Their stems are soft, nearly transparent, and bend toward enclosed spaces rather than toward any source of light. Flat black lichens occupy shaded walls, although shade has little meaning here. In flooded courts, strands of rootless moss drift just beneath the surface. They form rectilinear mats that align with absent buildings. When disturbed by rain, the mats break apart and slowly assemble into a different plan.

There are no animals. No insects stipple the water. No birds cross the unseen sky. There are no shells in the mud, no tracks, webs, nests, bones, droppings, calls, or startled motion at the edge of sight. Even the vegetation produces no apparent seed and bears no evidence of having been grazed. Decay occurs without scavengers. A fallen reed softens into clear pulp, enters the nearest gutter, and is gone.

This absence makes the city’s movement difficult to accept.

Doors open, but only when no wall contains them. An arch may detach from one building during the humid night and stand across a road by morning. Alleys narrow while traversed, gently at first, their opposing walls sweating as they approach. They never crush anyone; they simply become too narrow for the traveler who entered them, forcing that traveler to emerge somewhere incompatible with the route taken.

Rain does not come from above. It begins on surfaces.

First, beads appear across the paving. Then walls pearl over. Roofs darken from within, releasing warm sheets down their sides. For several hours the whole city exudes water, filling channels and low rooms. The water streams toward whichever district is currently oldest. Age is not fixed here. A newly encountered house may bear ten thousand years of erosion, while a tower seen repeatedly across the basin can become sharper-edged each time it is approached.

At the height of these rains, streets loosen from geography. A canal may flow east and arrive from the west. Courtyards exchange their contents: one loses its knee-deep water and receives another’s staircase; another wakes holding a section of avenue, complete with curbs and doorways that lead sideways into submerged rooms. The transformations are silent except for the quiet rubbing of stone beneath stone.

This is the city’s loudest sound.

Most of the time there are only drops, distant runoff, and the low movement of warm water under pavement. Thunder occurs underground. It rolls slowly beneath entire districts, too deep to vibrate the walls, and wells rise by a finger’s width as it passes. Sometimes a single splash is heard from a dry chamber. Sometimes footsteps continue after the walker has stopped, but they are not imitations and do not approach. They are the steps that would have been taken had another turning been chosen.

The farther quarters open into broad salt flats where the city becomes sparse. Here, isolated staircases descend through crusted clay. Walls stand miles apart, facing one another across warm, mirror-thin water. Rows of empty foundations mark neighborhoods never raised above ankle height. During the darkest hours—though darkness never meaningfully changes—whole streets can be sensed beneath the flats. Their shapes appear as faint currents, water following buried gutters. A person may walk over roofs without realizing it until a chimney opens at ground level and exhales damp air.

Beyond the flats are low ridges of marl and porous limestone, heavily weathered despite their gentle relief. Water seeps from them continuously. It does not form springs so much as unveil itself, covering the rock in clear skins. The ridges resemble immense collapsed buildings; the buildings resemble exposed geology. Between them lie marsh margins without a central marsh: mud shelves, reedless shallows, mineral scum, channels that branch and end blindly. The city may be expanding into these formations, or the formations may be eroding toward urbanity.

Nothing here is constructed, yet construction continues.

A wall begins as a line where condensation refuses to cross. Silt accumulates along it. Mineral deposits harden in the silt, making a curb, then a low barrier, then a facade. Openings remain where water passes through. With enough seasons, the flow changes course, leaving doors and windows. Rooms develop around persistent pockets of dry air. Roofs condense out of the mist as thin stone membranes, thickening under their own runoff. Thus a house can take centuries to appear, though occasionally one arrives between two glances, already ancient.

Demolition follows the same law in reverse. A district is not shattered but becomes progressively less architectural. Corners round. Rooms widen into hollows. Corridors become streambeds. Domes sag into hills. Streets separate into natural channels until no distinction remains between civic plan and watershed.

The process never finishes. The basin has reached an equilibrium between city and landscape, but equilibrium here is not stillness. It is continuous exchange. Every plaza lost becomes a pool elsewhere. Every sealed room causes a spring to open on a distant ridge. When a tower sinks, a well rises. Water carries no rubble, only shape.

There is one rule that can be learned: the city resists witnesses to completion. Follow the construction of a wall and it will remain forever knee-high. Turn away, and it may roof itself. Watch a doorway erode and the last stone will persist until attention lapses. Maps fare worse. Any representation accurate enough to guide movement begins to collect moisture. Lines spread, blocks soften, and the map eventually depicts the room in which it is being read, including the reader as an empty, water-filled outline.

There is another rule, less certain: dry places are dangerous.

They are rare—small chambers under stairs, sealed alcoves, the centers of broad floors where no condensation gathers. Sound disappears inside them. Heat intensifies. Their walls are unnaturally crisp, spared the balancing labor of water. If left open, they begin to draw moisture from surrounding streets. Pools shrink toward them. Gutters reverse. Mist streams through doorways and vanishes. A dry room may drink an entire neighborhood until the foundations crack and the buried channels lie exposed.

Then, without warning, the room is found full to the ceiling.

No flood is heard. No door releases it. The water simply becomes one of the room’s properties, like dimension or darkness.

Those who enter such water—if anyone enters, if anyone besides the observer has ever existed here—would find it breathable and hotter than blood. They would sink past the floor into an inverted district suspended beneath the basin. There the buildings hang downward from the underside of streets, their towers narrowing into black depth. Water occupies the open air, while rooms hold pockets of dense, dry darkness. Rain rises from bottomless plazas. Erosion sharpens edges instead of rounding them.

No animals live there either.

No citizens wait in the houses above or below. The city has avenues but no traffic, drains but no waste, thresholds but no inhabitants. Its emptiness does not feel abandoned. Rather, habitation seems to be a category the place has omitted. The structures organize water, heat, darkness, and attention instead of bodies. A square is where mist gathers to exchange direction. A house is a device by which stone dreams of interiority. A road is the temporary agreement of many surfaces to lead toward the same mistake.

At intervals, all water in the basin becomes still.

Drops halt on walls. Steam freezes in the air. Underground flow ceases so completely that the absence of sound feels like pressure against the ears. In that pause, the city can be perceived at its full extent—not seen, but apprehended as a single connected form reaching beneath the flats, through the ridges, and perhaps far beyond the horizon. Every room is then part of one enormous chamber. Every well is the same well. Every isolated wall is an interior partition of a structure too broad for curvature to reveal.

The pause lasts until something notices the observer.

Then water resumes everywhere at once.

A warm rain rises from the streets. The old stones soften at their edges. Far across the basin, a row of arches quietly lowers itself into the marsh-dark ground, while beside the observer a new doorway condenses in a wall that was solid a moment before.

Beyond it is the same city, wetter and older.

Beyond that, the same city is being born.
