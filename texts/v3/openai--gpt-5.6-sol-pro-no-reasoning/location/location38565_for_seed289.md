Beneath a plain of pale, compacted clay lies a chamber no larger than a sleeping animal.

It is not a cave in the usual sense. Nothing here has been hollowed by a river, widened by frost, or worn smooth by long passage. The chamber appears to have opened all at once around a crowded knot of stone, leaving every surface raw and newly parted. Its walls still possess the granular delicacy of an interior never before exposed: crystals with unbroken corners, seams of chalk as fine as sifted flour, black mineral membranes stretched between slabs. There are no channels, no scallops, no mature formations. Even the cracks seem embryonic. They begin, hesitate, and stop.

The space should be dark. Instead it is fiercely bright.

Light comes from no lamp, opening, or flame. It occupies the chamber as pressure occupies deep water—uniformly, without direction. Every grain casts a tiny shadow, yet there is no source toward which the shadows agree. White stone shines like salt. Damp surfaces carry a hard silver glare. Transparent crystals glow from within, exposing suspended motes and hair-thin faults. Nothing can hide, though almost everything is concealed behind something else.

The chamber is packed nearly solid.

Pebbles nest in the gaps between larger stones; calcite needles fill the gaps between pebbles; pale roots thread the spaces between needles. Lichens sheath the roots, translucent fungi sheath the lichens, and colonies of minute shell-bearing animals fasten themselves to the fungal films. There is hardly enough open air to insert a finger. Passage is possible only through a network of capillary corridors: narrow, bright interstices winding among mineral faces and living bodies. To enter would not be to step into a room, but to be admitted between its components.

Moisture is present everywhere, though there is almost no standing water. Condensation beads cover the ceiling and remain there in perfect hemispheres. Silver droplets cling beneath downward-pointing crystals without falling. A few pockets between stones contain clear water, each one smaller than a cupped hand. Their surfaces are flat and immobile even when touched by the crowded animals along their rims. The surrounding clay is damp, cool, and darkened to the color of old bone.

Vegetation survives in small, stubborn forms. There are no leaves broad enough to stir. Colorless rootlets enter from the earth above and divide repeatedly, ending in brushes finer than fur. Low green plates grow where the roots cross luminous stone, each plate crowded by hundreds of upright stalks tipped with amber capsules. A moss resembling miniature reeds fills one fissure. Elsewhere, brittle red filaments emerge directly from the rock and bend around obstacles without displacing them. Nothing spreads. Nothing withers. The sparse plants hold precisely the territory available to them, and no more.

The animals are far more numerous.

White mites stand shoulder to shoulder on wet mineral skins. Flatworms occupy the undersides of droplets, their bodies arranged like petals around trapped bubbles. Transparent beetles wedge themselves headfirst into chalk seams, while pale, many-legged creatures bridge cracks too narrow for even a root hair. Pinhead snails cover certain stones so densely that the rock beneath them cannot be seen. Threadworms knot the water pockets into slowly curving tangles. Small wingless flies cling to the ceiling, each in the same attitude of imminent departure.

None of them depart.

The chamber is full of sound: an enormous, continuous clamor disproportionate to its size. It is made of tiny noises multiplied beyond counting—the rasp of mouthparts on crystal, the click of shells settling against shells, the taut hum of fungal membranes, the almost metallic vibration of roots under pressure. Droplets ring faintly where they touch stone. Beetle cases tick. Mineral needles sing against one another in high, unwavering tones.

Together these form something like a waterfall heard through a wall, though no water falls. The sound never swells or diminishes. There are no beats, pauses, echoes, or accidents. Each creature contributes its minute portion forever, producing a roar that is not an event but a property of the place, as fixed as brightness or depth.

That fixity is the chamber’s governing law.

Nothing changes here.

The moisture neither evaporates nor gathers. Condensation does not slide. Animals feed without consuming, breathe without exhausting the air, and press past one another without altering their arrangement. The transparent beetles appear to scrape at the stone, but no mark deepens beneath them. Roots exert force without advancing. Fungal threads seem caught at the instant of branching, alive yet forever midway through the gesture. Even decay has found an equilibrium: scattered among the living are tiny husks, split shells, and pieces of hollow stem, but they neither rot nor dry.

Near the center lies the chamber’s only obvious construction: a low ring assembled from seven chips of black shale. It surrounds a shallow depression in the clay. The stones are too evenly spaced to be accidental and too small to serve any known purpose. No creature enters the ring. Roots curve around it. Mites crowd its outer edge but do not cross. Within, the clay remains bare beneath the sourceless light.

Farther back, three pale sticks lean together in the form of a roof, sheltering nothing. A row of snail shells has been set mouth-down along a crystal ledge. On one wall, short parallel scratches form a rectangle that was never completed. These are not ruins exactly. They are gestures toward building, made perhaps by an intelligence that had not yet separated itself from instinct—or by the chamber itself, testing the idea of arrangement.

No tools remain. No tool could function here for long; there is no free space in which to swing, cut, carry, or assemble. Whatever made the ring used pressure, patience, or the imperceptible cooperation of many small bodies. Yet even patience is difficult to imagine, because patience requires a future.

There is no future in the chamber.

Nor is there a past in any ordinary sense. Its geology is newborn but never becomes old. Every fracture is perpetually fresh. The chamber is always at the instant just after opening, though it has never been closed. Its creatures are always feeding, always hatching, always poised to fly. The black shale ring is always newly placed and already ancient. One can inspect the walls for centuries and find no erosion, only the immaculate suggestion that erosion is about to begin.

Above it, seasons may pass. Rain may darken the plain, grasses may bend, animals may migrate over the buried ceiling. None of that enters. The thin roots connecting the chamber to the surface transmit neither growth nor weather. They are less like roots than representations of roots: exact, living statements held in cool clay.

Because the place is so small, its invariance feels immense. A mountain frozen in time would still allow distance, perspective, ignorance. Here every detail is close enough to touch. Every bright grain insists on its permanence. The roar arrives from all sides at once. The packed inhabitants expose the terrible completeness of their positions: each mite fixed beside its droplet, each shell against its neighbor, each root between two crystals, all alive, all active, none proceeding.

At the chamber’s narrowest margin, where the walls nearly meet, there is a final slit full of brightness. Looking into it reveals another concentration of bodies and stone, then another beyond that—not larger rooms, but ever smaller interstices, each apparently as crowded and brilliantly illuminated as the first. Water pockets shine among them like buried eyes. Minute rings of black fragments surround minute patches of bare clay.

The landscape may end after a few handspans.

It may also continue inward without limit, its true depth concealed not below the earth but between adjacent things: an inexhaustible country compressed into spaces too narrow to change.
