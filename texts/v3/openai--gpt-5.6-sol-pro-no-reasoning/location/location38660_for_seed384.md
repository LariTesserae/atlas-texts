The town lies entirely beneath black water.

Not drowned—not recently. Nothing here preserves the posture of disaster. Doors do not gape as if abandoned in haste; streets do not trail loose belongings or drowned trees. The town was built for this pressure, or has endured it long enough that the distinction no longer matters. Its masonry has rounded at the corners. Iron rails have swollen into mineral-heavy ribs. Every surface bears the polish of slow currents, and every cavity has been tested, enlarged, packed, or sealed by centuries of moving silt.

It occupies a shallow basin in the deep, no larger than a walled hamlet. Around it, the seabed rises in a low rim of dark shale, defining the place as neatly as a crater defines its pool. Beyond the rim there is only descending water: cold, depthless, and almost entirely without light. The town seems small because its boundaries are close, but within those boundaries everything is crowded. Buildings lean over narrow lanes. Pipes cross between roofs at several levels. Cables descend in black festoons. Gantries, pressure tanks, valve trees, chimneys, ladders, and sealed conduits fill every interval that might otherwise have opened onto water.

There is scarcely room for the current.

The buildings are squat and heavy, constructed from black brick, riveted steel, leaded glass, and blocks of pale stone that have acquired the dull sheen of bone. Their walls are thicker at the bottom. Windows are few and circular. Some are blind plates of metal; others hold an amber darkness in which a gauge needle or suspended chain may occasionally move. Roofs are domed or steeply pitched, not to shed rain but to divide pressure and turn drifting sediment. Many are linked by enclosed bridges. These bridges make the upper streets: narrow tubes with flanged joints and small observation ports, layered over the original alleys until the settlement resembles a knot of plumbing wrapped around a handful of houses.

At its center stands the pumping hall.

It is not much larger than a chapel, but every other structure appears to defer to it. Four intake pipes enter its foundation from beneath the seabed, each broad enough for a person to walk through. Three exhaust stacks rise from its roof and end in cages of corroded bronze. No smoke comes from them. At long intervals, one releases a column of bubbles so fine and dense that it looks almost solid—a silver pillar climbing into the dark. The bubbles vanish overhead without illuminating anything.

The machinery inside cannot be heard in the ordinary sense. Water and masonry swallow sharp noises. Yet the town carries a constant, intimate vibration. Put a hand against a wall and there is a pulse under the mineral crust: three slow beats, a pause, then three again. Loose screws tremble in their housings. Chains lift a fraction from the ground. Silt leaps in tiny halos from pipe seams. The whole basin seems to breathe by mechanical means.

Otherwise it is profoundly quiet.

There is no surf, no birdsong, no rain striking roofs. Even collapse occurs softly here. A tile loosens, turns once, and settles into mud without impact. From time to time a pressure door opens somewhere out of sight, and the displaced water travels through the alleys as a gentle shove. Hanging lamps sway together. Curtains of rust particles bow. Then stillness returns, altered by a few degrees.

Light is rationed. Small blue lamps burn at intersections, enclosed in thick cages and spaced just far enough apart that each reveals the absence of the next. Their glow reaches only a few arm lengths. It catches on suspended grit and makes the water seem packed with ash. Along the pumping hall, strips of greenish glass shine from within, too dim to show what moves behind them. Elsewhere, illumination comes from colonies of cold bacteria inhabiting cracked insulation. They trace cables and leaking joints in faint white lines, outlining the town’s hidden circulation as frost outlines the veins of a leaf.

The upper water remains black. Now and then, however, a remote brilliance passes over the basin—broad, colorless, and diffuse. For several seconds the streets acquire shadows. Every pipe and railing becomes sharply present. Then the brightness moves on. Nothing visible casts it. The town’s lamps dim during these passages, as if conserving themselves or hiding.

Vegetation is scarce and unconvincing. There are patches of hard, translucent weed where warm seepage reaches the stones, but it grows in individual blades rather than meadows. Each blade is stiff as glass and angled into the prevailing current. In sheltered gutters, pale filamentous mats spread across brickwork, feeding on iron and the minute heat lost through the walls. They retreat whenever a pump cycle changes. Around one disused courtyard stands a grove of seven tube-shaped organisms, thin as saplings and taller than the surrounding roofs. They have no branches. Their white skins contract slowly, filtering the water, and once every few days they close all at once. No one sees what causes this.

Animal life is rarer still. Tiny crustaceans inhabit the gauge boxes, their shells nearly transparent except for a red point at the center. Flat worms fold themselves into pipe threads. A blind fish may sometimes be found hovering beneath a streetlamp, perfectly vertical, its jaws opening and closing with the rhythm of the central pumps. Larger creatures avoid the basin. When something passes beyond the shale rim, it is known only by the disappearance of distant particles, as though a piece of the darkness has moved in front of another piece.

There are tracks in the mud, though nothing is seen making them.

Some are narrow grooves left by maintenance crawlers: low, many-legged machines that emerge from wall ports when the lamps go dark. They scrape mineral buildup from valves, tighten bolts, and carry flakes of corrosion into square receptacles. Their work is methodical but not entirely reliable. One crawler has spent years polishing the same brass plaque at the end of a flooded stair. Another continuously lays a cable behind itself while a second machine, following closely, coils the cable and returns it to storage.

Other tracks are less explicable. Sets of round impressions cross streets and stop at sealed walls. Silt is sometimes brushed from door handles at a height suggesting human use. Frost forms on the inside of certain windows even though both sides are submerged. At irregular intervals, one of the observation bridges develops footprints—not in sediment, but in condensation beneath its glass.

The town remains active according to rules whose purpose has worn away.

At the first deep pulse of the pumping hall, shutters close over every eastern-facing window. At the second, valves along the lowest street vent black water, warmer than the sea and smelling faintly of copper. At the third, the basin current reverses. Debris rises from gutters and streams uphill, dividing neatly at corners as though guided by invisible curbs. For several minutes the water flows toward the town center. Then the intake grilles open.

Nothing obvious enters them. Still, pressure gauges throughout the settlement climb.

Several workshops continue their production. Behind armored windows, mechanical arms stamp thin metal discs, drill each with seven holes, and drop them into overflowing bins. A foundry melts salvaged fittings and casts them again as identical fittings, which are installed in neighboring buildings while the originals are brought back to be melted. In a chamber beneath the northern wall, looms weave long bands from glass fiber and human hair. The fabric is waterproof, perfectly black, and immediately fed through a slot in the floor.

There are no visible workers. There may never have been any.

The settlement’s erosion has reached an equilibrium with its maintenance. Rust swells; machines shave it away. Masonry dissolves; mineral deposits replace it. The current hollows a step; pale organisms sheath the hollow; a steel plate is bolted over both. Nothing remains pristine, but little is permitted to disappear. The town is neither preserved nor ruined. It persists in a third condition, continuously becoming a weathered version of itself.

Its streets descend toward the center, though survey marks insist the pumping hall stands on the highest ground. Objects dropped in the western lane drift downward and eventually arrive from above. A person attempting to circle the town along the wall will pass the same red valve five times but the same bronze door only twice. Distances change during the pressure cycles. Enclosed corridors are longer when crossed alone. Open water is longer when crossed in company.

At the basin’s lowest visible point lies the square.

It is paved in worn stone and crowded with infrastructure: air bells, capped wells, signal poles, inspection hatches, and a ring of benches facing inward. In the center is a circular opening protected by a waist-high railing. The opening has no cover. Its shaft descends beyond the reach of every lamp, and water pours into it without turbulence, carrying dust, rust flakes, dead organisms, and occasionally one of the town’s manufactured discs.

Nothing fills it.

During the quietest interval of the pump cycle, the flow reverses for one second. A breath of colder water comes up from the shaft. All seven white filter-trees close. Every blind fish in the basin turns its head toward the square. The maintenance crawlers stop where they stand.

Then, from somewhere beneath the town, comes the only distinct sound the place ever makes:

a single small knock,

as if from the other side of a door.
