The city lies in a broad, shallow basin beneath the surrounding ground. It is not deeply buried: from the plains above, one can reach it by descending a long ramp cut through clay and soft gray stone. At the bottom, the sky remains visible as a wide, pale opening, crossed here and there by old bridges and lifting frames. The enclosing banks shelter the basin from wind, so the air is cool and still. Smoke rises almost vertically before spreading into a thin ceiling of haze.

Nearly every part of the floor is occupied.

Houses, workshops, storage courts, kilns, cisterns, animal pens, and market shelters press against one another in uneven blocks. Construction follows the basin rather than a plan. Streets narrow around protruding bedrock, descend into unfinished hollows, then widen where workers have struck poor stone and abandoned a cut. Walls are made from whatever the excavation provides: slabs of pale mudstone, packed clay, chalky rubble, timber braces, woven reeds plastered with silt. Nothing is uniform. Even large public buildings have the look of something assembled by many hands from slightly different ideas.

The city is still being made out of its own ground.

All along its outer edge, teams work the raw banks. They use stone hammers, antler picks, wooden wedges, fire, and water. Small fires are built against a face of rock and kept burning for hours; afterward, cold water is thrown over the heated surface until it fractures. The loosened material is pried away and passed inward in baskets. Useful slabs become walls. Finer spoil becomes mortar, plaster, floors, ramps, and bricks. Broken stone fills foundations. Almost nothing leaves the basin. As the hollow expands, the settlement expands into it, so excavation and construction are the same continuous act.

Because the geology is newly exposed, the surfaces have not yet acquired the shapes of age. There are no worn ravines, rounded pillars, or deep natural channels. Cuts are sharp. Banks show the straight scars of tools and the fresh colors of opened earth: cool gray, bluish white, dull red, and bands of yellow clay. Rain has begun to mark them, but only faintly. Thin runs of sediment draw first lines down the walls, tentative as sketches. At the foot of each cut, fans of fresh grit spread over the streets until someone shovels them aside.

Water is present, though never abundant enough to be taken for granted. Rain falls irregularly through the open sky, sometimes as a quiet mist, sometimes in a short, cold shower. Roofs lean toward jars and stone-lined cisterns. Cloths are stretched between buildings to catch condensation at night. Water beads on the sheltered walls each morning and is wiped into bowls with pads of wool.

Beneath the eastern quarter, several weak springs seep from a bed of dark clay. Their flow gathers in shallow channels lined with flat stone and runs toward the central reservoir. These channels are narrow, often no wider than a hand. People step across them, lay boards over them, wash tools in them, and argue over obstructions. Their quiet trickle is one of the few continuous sounds in the city.

After rain, the basin changes quickly. Dry lanes darken. Clay stairs become slick. Cistern mouths overflow into temporary pools where children float chips of bark and workers rinse dust from their arms. For a day or two, moisture gathers everywhere: in wall seams, under storage jars, along the cold faces of newly opened stone. Then the ground drinks it. Pools shrink to black-edged patches, and the city returns to its usual condition of damp shade surrounded by drying surfaces.

Plants survive chiefly where human use permits them. There is little uncultivated growth. Pale moss forms on the shaded sides of cisterns. Liverwort and short grass occupy cracks around the springs. A few ferns grow beneath leaking conduits, their fronds dusty at the tips. Kitchen plots appear on terraces where compost and carried soil have been laid over stone. They hold onions, bitter greens, peas, medicinal herbs, and small, carefully pruned shrubs. Each bed is edged with broken pottery to keep its soil from washing away.

Above the basin rim, sparse grasses bend in the freer wind. Their roots help hold the uppermost soil, and cutting them is regulated. The city learned early that stripping the rim bare sends mud down onto the roofs. Reed beds grow in one wetter corner near the reservoir, but they are harvested so often that they seldom rise above a person’s knee.

Animals are uncommon. There are a few goats kept in cramped pens near the ramps, valued for milk and dung more than meat. Small gray birds descend at dawn to drink from the channels and leave before the streets grow busy. Mice inhabit grain stores, though cats are rare and highly prized. After rainfall, pale insects emerge from crevices in the clay, and frogs sometimes call from the reed bed for a single evening. Their voices seem unusually loud in the sheltered basin. By morning they have hidden again.

Despite its packed condition, the city is not noisy. Stone and earth absorb sound, and most work is done by hand. One hears the dull tapping of picks, the scrape of baskets dragged over floors, mallets striking wooden pegs, sandals on grit, the pulse of a potter’s wheel, and low conversations carried through courtyards. Traders do not shout for long; voices travel oddly beneath overhanging walls and can disturb whole neighborhoods. Bells are small, made from fired clay or hammered copper, and are used sparingly.

The loudest events are fractures. When fire and water split a working face, the crack passes through the basin with startling force. Everyone pauses. Dust loosens from rafters. Birds lift from the reservoir. Then the foreman calls, baskets begin moving, and the city resumes.

Light enters generously from above, though it reaches the lower streets in shifting portions. At midday the main squares and upper terraces are clear and ordinary under the sky. Alleys beneath bridges remain cool and blue-gray. Walls are coated with pale clay to reflect brightness inward, and courtyards are arranged around shafts of open air. Polished metal is too costly for widespread use, so shallow bowls of water sometimes serve as reflectors, placed where they can throw trembling patches of daylight onto workshop benches.

At night, lamps burn animal fat or pressed seed oil. Their flames are modest. Most movement ceases, and the basin becomes a dark field of flat roofs under the open strip of stars. Guards walk the perimeter cuts because fresh stone can fail without warning. They listen for falling grains, the soft pop of timber braces, or water moving where no channel was intended.

The city has rules shaped less by rulers than by its vulnerable ground. No one may deepen a cellar without consulting neighboring households. No kiln may be fired directly against an untested wall. Rainwater belongs first to the roof that catches it, overflow to the street cistern, and seepage to whoever maintains the channel. A crack appearing in a shared wall must be marked immediately with red clay. Blocking light from an established courtyard is treated almost as seriously as theft.

Space is inherited, traded, borrowed, and continually renegotiated. A family may own a room but not the stone behind it, a roof but not the passage built across that roof, a cistern but only half the water entering it. Workshops occupy street corners by day and fold inward at dusk. Market awnings become sleeping shelters. Stairways double as seats, retaining walls, drying racks, and public notices. The city is dense not because its buildings are tall, but because every surface has acquired several uses.

Beyond the inhabited center, the basin remains open enough to show its full extent. One can stand on an upper ramp and see districts distinguished by color and labor: the red-clay potters’ quarter, the white dust of the stone yards, the dark wet line of the spring streets, the low smoke of the kilns, the green-gray patch of the reservoir gardens. Farther out, raw excavation fronts form an irregular ring. Beyond them rises the untouched land, level and quiet.

The settlement grows laterally and downward, but cautiously. It does not yet resemble a ruin, a cavern, or a finished city. Its edges are too fresh. Its infrastructure is provisional, its streets repeatedly altered, its geology only just being learned. Every newly opened layer changes what can be built. A firm bed of stone invites a public hall. A pocket of clay becomes a season of bricks. A seep becomes a neighborhood. A fracture can erase months of work.

Nothing here has had time to weather into inevitability. The place is crowded, old enough to possess customs, yet physically young—an urban landscape at the moment of emergence, building shelter from the wound it makes in the earth.
