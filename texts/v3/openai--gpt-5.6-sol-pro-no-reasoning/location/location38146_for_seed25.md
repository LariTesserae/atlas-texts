The basin lies low enough that weather does not pass over it so much as settle into it.

Cold air pours down from the surrounding shelves after dusk and remains there, pooled and faintly moving. Moisture accompanies it—not enough for a lake, not enough even for continuous mud, but enough to blacken the stone, bead along every edge, and gather in long, shallow seams. Rain comes irregularly, in fine slanting episodes that seem almost soundless beneath the greater noise of the place. By morning it has vanished into cracks, mouths, and bodies.

There are no plants. Nothing rises by stem or frond; no root binds the ground. Across the whole basin, from the near flats to the dim margins several valleys away, the surface is mineral: old basalt worn into plates, beds of pale gravel, iron-dark ribs of rock, and low banks of clay repeatedly softened and hardened. The erosion is ancient and complete-looking. Sharp forms are rare. Every outcrop has been rounded, drilled, polished, undercut, or made porous. Countless generations of water and habitation have revised the terrain until geology and animal passage are difficult to distinguish.

Nearly every cavity is occupied.

The first impression is not movement but texture. The flats appear furred, scaled, stippled, armored. Then the darkness shifts and the textures separate into animals.

Small shelled things lie packed several layers deep in the damp hollows, opening and closing in slow waves. Their shells are colorless in daylight, if daylight can be said to reach the basin, but in the prevailing dark they hold weak mineral gleams: blue at the hinges, chalk-white along old fractures, a bruised violet where one shell presses against another. Between them squeeze soft, many-footed grazers that consume films too thin to be seen—microbial varnishes growing directly on wet stone. Nothing resembling a leaf is involved. The living foundation of the basin is a skin of bacteria and mineral-feeding colonies, replenished whenever condensation runs over exposed rock.

Above this packed lower stratum is another population, and above that another.

Long-bodied animals knot themselves through fissures. Flat, hand-sized scavengers overlap like loose shingles. Blind jumpers cling beneath ledges in such numbers that their combined weight occasionally tears off sheets of weathered stone. There are translucent sacs anchored in temporary pools, contracting whenever raindrops strike. There are pale burrowers that surface only to exchange chambers, pouring from one hole into another in dense, muscular streams. Some creatures appear to have no front or rear; they advance by passing ripples around the circumference of their bodies. Others stand on narrow jointed limbs and remain motionless for hours, listening through the rock.

The basin is deafening.

No single cry dominates. The sound is cumulative and almost material: shells grinding, claws ticking, membranes rattling, bodies dragging across grit, air pulsing through resonant cavities. Deep under the flats, colonies beat the ceilings of their burrows in synchronized intervals. Along the escarpments, millions of throatless animals vibrate plates in their chests, producing a continuous metallic roar. Predators locate prey by interrupting it. A sudden patch of silence moves through the crowd like a shadow, and wherever it passes, smaller animals fold, flatten, seal themselves shut, or plunge into cracks.

The silence is followed by impact.

Predators here are low and broad, shaped by the scarcity of open space. They do not chase far. Some wedge themselves under dense congregations and heave upward, exposing the soft undersides of hundreds at once. Some emit a low vibration that loosens animals from the walls. One species—rare only in relation to the multitudes around it—moves beneath the gravel, its passage visible as a traveling mound. When it rises, the ground opens in a circular collapse. For several seconds there is only tumbling stone and a frantic white mass of limbs; then the hole closes under animals rushing in from all sides to feed on the injured.

Death does not clear space for long.

Nothing here is wasted into emptiness. Carcasses are entered before they cool. Shells become chambers, then grinding surfaces, then mineral grit. Soft tissue disappears under translucent feeders. Fluids descend through porous stone and sustain buried mats of simpler life. Even the clean-picked armor is reduced by creatures that rasp calcium from it, leaving paper-thin husks that crack in the next rain.

Water organizes this abundance, though water is never abundant itself.

Each pocket has its own temporary ecology. A finger-deep pool beneath an overhang may contain thousands of suspended animals, each no larger than a grain of sand, turning the water opaque. When the pool shrinks, they bind themselves into a rubbery crust. Larger creatures come to scrape away pieces, carrying dormant bodies in their mouths to other basins. Condensation channels are defended like migration routes. At night, beads gather on the cool vertical faces; entire populations climb to meet them. The cliffs seem to flow upward, black with bodies, while hunters wait below for those dislodged by the press.

The rain changes everything without making the place gentle.

When it begins, the metallic chorus falters. Dust darkens. Dry seams fill one after another, briefly connecting isolated pools into a branching system across the flats. Animals emerge from beneath stones and from sealed pits far below the surface. The apparent ground rises as buried bodies force their way upward. For an hour or two, the basin becomes a shallow, crowded delta: rivulets clogged with swimming forms, hollows foaming with eggs, clay banks trembling as masses excavate new galleries.

Some creatures breed only during these intervals. They release threadlike young into running water, and the currents carry them no farther than the next depression. Others hunt exclusively in rain, reading the distortion of drops on thin membranes stretched across their backs. Great filter-feeders, ordinarily curled into stony balls, unfold along the drainage lines and strain the flood through forests of inward-pointing teeth. When the water retreats, they close again, trapping a reservoir within themselves.

There is barely any true daylight. Dense cloud fills the sky, and mineral haze rises from the basin floor. The enclosing highlands are visible only as darker masses against darkness. Most illumination is made by the inhabitants, though not brightly and not continuously. Pinpricks flare when bodies collide. Burrowing swarms produce faint green tides beneath thin clay. Predators carry no lights of their own, but some cultivate luminous parasites around their mouths, mimicking the crowded gleam of a safe wet hollow.

Distance is understood chiefly by sound. The basin extends for many leagues, divided into shelves, channels, gravel plains, and fields of perforated rock, yet none of it feels empty. Far-off colonies send vibrations through the ground. Escarpments answer one another. Rain arriving on the western rim can be heard long before it reaches the flats—not as rainfall, but as successive changes in the animals’ calls.

The terrain itself is stable in the way an old hive is stable. Channels wander, roofs collapse, clay banks slump, but the larger arrangement persists. Burrows follow weaknesses opened by erosion; erosion follows burrows widened by generations. The animals have become a geological force without constructing anything. There are no nests assembled from gathered material, no dams, no walls, no tools. Bodies occupy, scrape, dissolve, compress, and abandon. The basin’s architecture is accidental, produced by repetition.

Its strangest law is that open ground does not remain open.

Clear a patch—by flood, collapse, predation, or falling stone—and the neighboring life detects the vacancy almost immediately. Feelers enter first. Then flat scavengers, then shell-bearers, then the pale things from below. Within minutes, the exposed mineral is obscured. Within hours, the sound regains its former density. The place closes over absence as flesh closes over a wound.

Only during the coldest hour before the dim morning is there any reduction. Condensation thickens, movement slows, and the innumerable bodies settle into tiers. Their noise drops from a roar to a pervasive granular hiss. Across the wet basalt, millions drink in silence or something near it. The weak lights fade. Pools turn black and mirrorless under crowds of floating forms.

Then a tremor passes through the basin.

It may be weather, a distant collapse, or the waking of something buried too deeply to be seen. The packed surface responds in waves. Shells lift. Limbs unfold. Cavities begin to drum. From valley to valley the chorus rebuilds, layer upon layer, until the stone itself seems to be crying out—not in alarm, but simply because here, in the dark and cool damp, existence has nowhere quiet to stand.
