The city occupies the upper face of a plateau so high that the land below is not regarded as ground. It is called the Undersky: a pale, depthless suspension of dust, fumes, and mineral haze through which lower peaks sometimes appear, isolated and black, like stones sinking in milk. No one descends there. Freight routes, maps, and civic imagination all travel laterally—from mesa to mesa, summit to summit—across a world whose habitable surface consists of widely separated heights.

The plateau itself is vast enough to have districts, weather, and geological provinces. Its surface slopes almost imperceptibly over hundreds of kilometers. At the edges it ends in cliffs banded with ocher, charcoal, dull violet, and seams of metallic green. These walls have stood exposed for ages. They are not sharp. Wind has hollowed them into galleries, blind arches, scalloped alcoves, and immense fluted columns. From a distance, the edge resembles the weathered facade of a city larger and older than the one built above it.

There is no water. Not hidden in cisterns, not locked in ice, not remembered in shells. The atmosphere carries almost no vapor, and the few clouds are mineral aerosols—thin veils of electrostatically suspended powder that cast broad copper shadows. Fluids used by the city are hydrocarbons, molten salts, compressed gases, and a clear silicone oil brought through insulated pipes. None behave quite like water. A spill of coolant does not soak into the ground; it beads into trembling lenses, gathers dust, and slowly hardens into translucent amber. Workers cut old spills from factory floors and stack them outside like panes of imperfect glass.

The warmth is dry and pervasive. In daylight the exposed stone reaches the temperature of skin, then warmer, but rarely becomes murderous. At night, heat stored deep in the plateau rises through fissures and keeps the city from freezing. The air is thin. Breath comes in deliberate measures, and every building has a low mechanical pulse as compressors thicken its interior atmosphere. Streets are roofed in places, not against rain, but to hold pressure near the ground and keep the dust from lifting.

Nothing grows.

There are no grasses in cracks, no moss on shaded walls, no roots troubling foundations. Open ground remains open for centuries. Where human construction ends, the mineral world begins without transition: compacted gray regolith, shards of volcanic glass, and fields of stone nodules polished by airborne grit. Color accumulates only through oxidation, chemical exhaust, and the secretions of animals.

Those animals are everywhere, though at first the plateau seems empty.

Most are small and widely dispersed. Six-legged sailbacks lie flat against warm rock, their translucent respiratory membranes folded like fans. When a shadow passes, they raise those membranes to taste changes in atmospheric charge. Clusters of ceramic-shelled mites gather around power conduits and feed on leakage currents, leaving fine branching tracks in the insulating dust. Pale ribbon creatures drift through ventilation trenches, propelled not by wings but by rows of electrostatic cilia. They make almost no sound.

Larger fauna inhabit the cliffs. Herds of long-limbed vaulting animals cross chasms by inflating buoyant sacs inside their rib cages. They wait for hours at a ledge, motionless, then step into the void one by one. Their descent is so slow that a herd may remain visible all afternoon, black specks migrating across the bright haze. In the deepest cliff hollows live broad, armored things that press their bodies against exposed strata and dissolve useful salts directly from stone. Their feeding scars form nested circles hundreds of meters wide.

None of these creatures drink. Their chemistry runs on atmospheric hydrogen, sulfur compounds, and thin films of industrial organics. Some biologists insist they are not animals at all but colonies of smaller entities temporarily agreeing on a shape. A sailback dissected in one season may have no organ corresponding to anything found in the same species the next. Taxonomy is therefore municipal rather than scientific: cliff nuisances, conduit feeders, harmless drifters, pressure thieves, load-bearing fauna.

The last category is protected by law.

Several of the city’s oldest bridges rest on the dormant backs of enormous organisms mistaken for rock when construction began. They have not moved within recorded history, but instruments detect a gradual redistribution of mass beneath their shells. Buildings atop them must be flexible. Streets crossing them are laid in interlocking plates, and surveyors repaint the center lines every few years as the curvature changes. One such district has risen eleven centimeters in a century. Residents consider this good fortune.

The city is not dense. It spreads in widely separated complexes linked by rail, buried cables, pressure tunnels, and long straight roads. Between districts lie kilometers of bare stone. Towers stand alone with sufficient empty ground around them to fall in any direction, though few are expected to. Factories occupy shallow excavations cut into the plateau, their roofs level with the surface so the wind can pass over them. Residential structures cluster in the lee of old quarry walls: low, thick buildings of sintered dust, dark alloys, and amber insulation.

From above, the city looks less like a continuous settlement than a constellation.

Its industry is large but unhurried. Extraction machines move on tracks laid along mineral seams. They are tall, narrow devices with counterweighted limbs, resembling scaffolds taught to walk. A machine may spend a month crossing a basin. Refinery yards contain ranks of sealed retorts heated by the plateau’s interior warmth. Chemical works distill atmospheric gases; foundries melt metals in mirrored furnaces; fabrication halls grow structural ceramics layer by layer. Nothing pours in torrents. Production occurs through pressure, sublimation, deposition, patient heating, and long cooling.

Every process is adapted to scarcity—not scarcity of material, for the plateau is rich in metals and salts, but scarcity of movement. Lubricants are precious. Bearings are enclosed like organs. Dust infiltrates everything and can polish a gear tooth away over decades. Machines are designed to stop gracefully. Their default state is rest.

The city’s tallest constructions are the sky mills. These slender latticed towers climb several kilometers above the plateau, where they sift charged particles from the upper atmosphere. Under normal conditions they appear perfectly still. Only the slow rotation of their collector vanes shows that they are working. At dusk, potential differences discharge along the frameworks in dim blue threads. The light is not bright enough to illuminate the ground, but it reveals the towers’ full impossible height, stitch by stitch.

Sound does not carry easily in the thin air. The city is mostly quiet even at its busiest. Machinery is heard through the feet before it reaches the ears: a faint repeating tremor in a walkway, the granular shiver of a wall panel, a deep pulse transmitted through bedrock from a distant compressor. Close to a factory, one hears ticking metal, gas moving through valves, and the dry whisper of dust against housings. There are no gulls, insects, leaves, streams, or rain to fill the intervals.

Citizens use vibration bells instead of sirens. In emergencies, buried drivers send a coded pattern through the ground. Furniture hums. Cups creep across tables. Cliff fauna detach themselves from the rock. Everyone stops to count the pulses.

Wind comes rarely, and when it does it is broad and slow. It may take half a day for a front to cross the plateau. Ahead of it, the smallest animals disappear into holes and machine seams. Loose dust rises only a handspan, flowing like smoke along the ground. Because there is no moisture to knit particles together, old tracks remain crisp in sheltered places but vanish quickly in exposed ones. A thousand-year-old service road may emerge from beneath a migrating dust sheet, its painted symbols faded but readable.

Erosion here has reached a kind of maturity. The plateau is not being dramatically destroyed. Fragile forms have already broken; unstable slopes have already fallen. What remains is massive, rounded, and internally braced by resistant strata. Wind works at it grain by grain. Thermal cycles open cracks no wider than hair. Cliff faces retreat at rates measured across dynasties. Engineers and geologists use the same calendars.

There are ruins, but not many abandoned ones. Space is plentiful; materials are not worth recovering if a structure can simply be sealed and bypassed. Old plants remain where they ceased operation, connected to newer districts by dead rail lines. Their interiors hold stale pressurized air and machines paused mid-task. Over centuries, conduit feeders enter through deteriorated gaskets. Their colonies illuminate control rooms with faint red metabolic glow. Sometimes an obsolete factory begins drawing power again—not enough to operate, only enough to warm itself. Whether this is malfunction, animal activity, or some residual industrial reflex is debated.

Beyond the last inhabited complexes stand marker pylons, then survey tracks, then nothing constructed for days of travel. The terrain becomes a sequence of shallow basins and raised mineral reefs. Some reefs ring when warmed. Others bend compass fields or cast shadows in directions slightly wrong for the sun. In one region, every loose stone balances on its narrowest point. Attempts to topple them succeed, but by the following morning they are upright again. There is no visible movement and no detectable force.

The strangest province is the Still Migration: tens of thousands of dark, animal-shaped forms distributed across a plain of white salt—not water salt, but a brittle compound of ammonia, metal ions, and carbon. The forms stand several kilometers apart, all facing west. Some are unquestionably stone. Others register internal warmth. A few have changed position between surveys, though never while observed. City law classifies the entire plain as fauna and prohibits mining there.

Once every nineteen years, the western sky darkens as the planet’s ring plane aligns with the sun. For several weeks, daylight becomes striped. Vast parallel shadows travel across the plateau, passing over cliffs, factories, habitation vaults, and the motionless forms of the Still Migration. Temperatures fall only slightly, but animals emerge in extraordinary numbers. Sailbacks cover rooftops. Ribbon creatures fill the trenches in pale currents. The buoyant herds rise from the cliffs and hang above the city, their inflated bodies catching alternating bands of gold and darkness.

Industry slows during this season. Trains stop between districts. Exterior lighting is dimmed. People climb onto roofs and stand among the silent animals.

Then, in the deepest part of the alignment, the whole plateau gives a single low contraction.

It is too deep to hear. The towers flex. Dust leaps from the ground. Far out on the white plain, every dark form advances one pace westward.

Afterward the city resumes. Furnaces reopen. Collector vanes turn. Surveyors measure bridges and repaint streets. The cliff animals continue their nearly motionless feeding, and the ancient plateau releases its heat into the dry night.

Nothing happens quickly here, but nothing is entirely still.
