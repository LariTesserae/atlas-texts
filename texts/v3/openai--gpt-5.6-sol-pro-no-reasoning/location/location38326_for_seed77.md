The plain is made, though no maker remains in it.

It reaches farther than weather can travel in a day: a low, hot expanse assembled from fired clay, lime mortar, dark brick, and immense sheets of pale ceramic. There is no underlying soil. Where cracks open, they reveal further courses beneath—tiles over packed shards, packed shards over vaults, vaults over older floors descending beyond any known depth. The whole continent may be one continuous construction, built upward layer by layer until its purpose was buried within its own completion.

From a distance it appears almost natural. Heat softens the straight lines. Mineral dust gathers in corners and spreads across plazas like sand. Kilometers of shallow roofing sag into broad basins; collapsed domes resemble low hills; walls laid on such enormous curves seem, to anyone standing upon them, as level as horizons. Yet every rise has joints. Every escarpment repeats a bond of brick or stone. Even the loose gravel consists of tesserae, broken roof scales, and rounded pellets of mortar.

The land is young in an unusual sense. It is not being carved down so much as settling into form.

At midday the heat expands its materials. The plain answers with faint ticks and widely separated knocks as tiles shift against one another. Hairline fractures advance through glazed surfaces. Mortar swells where damp and powders where dry. Long floors buckle into ridges only a hand’s breadth high, embryonic mountain chains extending for hundreds of kilometers. Elsewhere, immense slabs sink almost imperceptibly into the empty chambers beneath them, creating depressions too gradual to notice except by the way rainwater gathers year after year.

There are no rivers with permanent sources. Moisture comes intermittently from the air.

Warm winds cross the continent, carrying little visible cloud, but nights are cool enough for condensation to pearl on glazed roofs and polished walls. Before dawn, every seam darkens. Drops gather along the undersides of cornices, then fall one by one into channels built so broad and shallow that they seem like valleys. For an hour or two, threads of water move through the construction. They converge in sunken courts, cistern mouths, collapsed halls, and shallow ceramic pans. By noon most have vanished.

Rain is rarer and more decisive. A storm may form over the hot plain from moisture accumulated in distant basins. Its shadow travels for hours across empty pavement. Then rain descends in a narrow gray column, drumming softly at first, then turning whole districts reflective. Channels wake. Steps become cascades. Water runs along engraved borders and through apertures cut with no obvious practical purpose, following the old intention of the place even though that intention is no longer understood.

Because erosion has barely begun, the rain does not yet make mature valleys. It stains. It loosens grains. It traces routes that may, in distant ages, become ravines. Around drainage holes, the first little fans of sediment spread across lower tiles: cream dust, red brick flour, greenish flakes of glaze. These deposits are only a few centimeters deep, yet they are the closest thing the world has to floodplains.

Vegetation occupies such places sparingly.

Black films of heat-tolerant algae line shaded cisterns. Pale lichens map the cooler faces of walls, growing so slowly that their colonies preserve the outlines of centuries of damp seasons. In joints where condensation persists, wiry plants rise at great intervals—single stalks with narrow, silver leaves, or mats of low succulent tissue pressed flat against the masonry. Their roots do not seek soil. They enter mortar, widening pores as they drink dissolved lime.

Some basins support rings of reedlike growth, though the “reeds” are no taller than a person’s hand. Their dry stems whisper when wind passes over them. Elsewhere, solitary columnar plants stand many kilometers apart, green-gray and ribbed, storing several years of moisture in waxy flesh. They cast severe, compact shadows. When a storm comes, they flower quickly: small white blooms opening at night, fertilized not by animals—there are none—but by wind and gravity. Pollen sifts along grooves in the pavement. Seeds wait in cracks for decades.

The absence of animal life is complete. Nothing flies through the rain. Nothing drinks from the temporary pools. There are no tracks in the mineral dust except those made by runoff, falling fragments, and the slow dragging of plant stems in the wind. Even carrion and shells are unknown. The landscape has no inherited image of tooth, eye, wing, or nest.

Its life consists of surfaces, spores, roots, and weather.

The built forms are too extensive to read as ruins in the ordinary sense. A ruin implies an interruption in a natural place; here construction is the only ground. Broad avenues intersect at angles so shallow that they take days to recognize. Terraces descend toward basins the size of inland seas, though most contain only heat haze and a few shallow pools. Arcades run beneath the plain, their entrances exposed wherever a roof has folded. Inside, the air is cooler. Condensation coats the walls, and fungal veils cling to ceilings in dim gray sheets. These chambers are not machines. They contain no moving parts, wiring, tools, or engines—only masonry, cavities, ramps, drains, and pillars.

Everything was made, but nothing is technological in the living sense. The construction behaves like geology because geology has begun to claim it as a process rather than a substance. Its laws are ordinary ones: weight, heat, capillary action, salt, expansion, growth. A cup-shaped court fills when it rains because it is lower than its rim. A tower falls because its mortar weakens. Water travels downhill whether the slope was designed or accidental. The wonder lies not in violated physics, but in the scale at which familiar physics works.

Weather varies across the expanse. Over black-tiled provinces, rising heat breeds columns of turbulent air. Over pale districts, glare flattens depth and makes distant walls hover. Evaporation from the largest basins sometimes produces local cloud banks that never escape their enclosing terraces. A person could walk from dry blue sky into a region of low mist, then emerge days later beneath another clear horizon without ever leaving the same constructed surface.

The quiet is correspondingly vast.

Close at hand there may be the hiss of evaporation, a drip in a shaded vault, the brittle brushing of sparse stems, or the small crack of cooling glaze after sunset. Far away, a settling roof can collapse with a long muffled thunder. The sound rolls under floors and through buried galleries, arriving as a tremor after its source has fallen silent. Storms speak more fully: rain on ceramic becomes a continent-wide murmur, changing pitch from brick to tile to limewashed stone.

Then the storm passes. Water remains in joints and hollows, reflecting the ordinary daylight. Steam lifts from dark surfaces. Salt blooms white around the shrinking pools. The plants swell almost visibly; spores open; roots press farther into softened mortar. New cracks form where hot dry slabs meet newly cooled ones. A cornice loses a fragment. A basin settles by the width of a grain.

Across the enormous made plain, these modest events occur everywhere at once. The place is active without appearing hurried. It is becoming a landscape from the raw material of architecture—not yet deeply eroded, not yet softened into conventional earth, but already beyond any single design.

In time, its floors will become strata. Its corridors will become caves. Its drainage lines will deepen into rivers, and its settling vaults will produce ranges and troughs. For now it remains near the beginning: hot, low, sparsely green, intermittently wet, and almost silent—a constructed world learning, patiently and at continental scale, how to be land.
