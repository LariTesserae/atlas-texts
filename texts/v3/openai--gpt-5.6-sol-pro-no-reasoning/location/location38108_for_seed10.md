The plateau has been old for so long that it has forgotten height.

It stands above the surrounding world, but not as mountains stand. There are no summits, no young cliffs, no clean escarpments. Wind, ice, and black water have worked upon it until its immense elevation has become a nearly level country of shallow basins and low-backed ridges. From a distance it resembles a dark green shelf laid beneath the clouds. On its surface, horizons run straight for leagues, interrupted only by blunt stone remnants: tors worn down to kneeling shapes, ribs of bedrock polished like old teeth, solitary pillars too eroded to cast a decisive shadow.

Nothing here has been built. Yet from the valleys below, the plateau appears industrial.

Vapor rises from it in columns. Regular concussions travel through the ground. At night, dim amber pulses pass beneath the vegetation in branching sequences, as if furnaces were being opened under the moor. There are whistles, rotary dronings, the metallic shriek of enormous brakes, and intervals of rapid hammering that can continue for hours. No engines can be found. There are no roads, chimneys, cables, foundations, slag heaps, or discarded tools. The industry belongs to the ecology.

Almost every surface is vegetable.

The ground is not soil in the ordinary sense but a deep, interlocked accumulation of living tissues. Peat exists only in thin seams, because little here is permitted to become fully dead. Fallen matter is entered by pale rootlets before decay can claim it, rehydrated, and drawn back into the continuous body of the plateau. What looks like turf is the upper skin of this body: millions of cold-adapted plants compressed into a resilient mat, its surface stippled with tiny leaves, breathing pores, translucent bladders, and dark hairs that collect mist.

The mat may be only ankle-deep over stone, or it may descend hundreds of meters into old erosion basins. It bridges ravines. It drapes over cliffs in slow green cataracts. Across broad regions it floats on water held near the surface, rising and settling with pressure changes. A traveler might cross a plain for half a day before realizing that the plain has been gently inhaling.

Vegetation is total, but the landscape does not feel crowded. Its life has organized itself into expanses. There are miles of low black moss, then a vacant tarn, then one enormous plant standing alone beneath the clouds. Individual presences are separated by great intervals, as though each requires a district of silence around it.

The largest are the bellows-groves.

From afar, each looks like a sparse copse of silver trunks. Up close, the “trunks” are pleated columns of woody fiber, hollow and warm within, rooted into chambers beneath the mat. Wind bends their crowns. The bending compresses one set of chambers and opens another, drawing mist, rain, spores, and small animals through valves in the bark. On calm days the grove contracts by itself. Its columns pump in sequence, making a deep piston rhythm audible across the plateau:

**thoom—hiss, thoom—hiss, thoom—hiss.**

Water is its working fluid. It enters through leaf funnels and weeping bark, descends through narrow vessels, and is forced under pressure into the buried root network. There it drives knuckled wheels of living wood—not true wheels, for they remain rooted, but rotating rings of cartilage-like tissue lined with hooked cilia. These grind stone into mineral paste. They shred old roots. They circulate heat and nutrients through territories larger than forests.

The sound suggests manufacture because manufacture is, in fact, taking place. The plateau grows standardized parts.

Pods emerge in ranks along the underground conduits: valves, floats, lenses, insulated cords, toothed disks, flexible tubes, pressure bulbs. They detach when mature and are carried by water into other organs, where they assemble into temporary animals or new sections of landscape. Nothing is made by hands. Nothing remains a machine for long. A pumping station becomes a flowering thicket; a conduit develops hunger and crawls away; a bank of resonant tubes fills with eggs.

Cold water is everywhere. It sheets across stone too gently to be called a stream, gathers beneath moss membranes, hangs in the air as silver condensation, and wells upward from cracks with the patience of something returning. Rain seldom falls hard. Instead, clouds touch the plateau and surrender their moisture directly. Droplets form on every hair and leaf-edge. Rocks weep without visibly being wet. Shallow pools appear overnight and sometimes remain for centuries.

The tarns are extraordinarily old. Erosion has widened them until many are only a hand’s breadth deep, dark mirrors spread across miles of level ground. Their margins are indistinct: water becomes jelly, jelly becomes bladderwort, bladderwort becomes trembling turf. Under dim daylight, the pools reflect not the present clouds but cloud formations from several hours earlier. During storms the delay grows longer. Lightning may be seen in the water before it occurs overhead.

The sky is low and bruised, usually without a visible sun. Light filters through in blue-gray sheets. Shadows are weak, but the vegetation carries its own subdued illumination: green oxidation along torn stems, amber circulation under translucent bark, red pinpricks where flowers sense movement. In the long cold dusk, these lights give the plateau the look of an industrial coast seen from far offshore.

There is no true frost line. Temperature behaves locally. Ice grows wherever the living mat withdraws attention.

A pool may freeze in a perfect circle around an animal while remaining liquid elsewhere. Rime forms on the leeward sides of plants regardless of wind. Long blades of ice push from saturated ground like transparent grass, then soften and are drunk back into the roots. In some districts, cold falls upward: a crawling layer of freezing air rises from hollows after dusk, coating the undersides of leaves and the bellies of flying creatures.

The animals are abundant, though the openness can hide them.

The moss plains are crossed by herds of spindle-legged grazers, each no larger than a hare but appearing taller because their legs are thin and jointed many times. They step without breaking the water skin. Their mouths are circular rasps that shave luminous films from leaves. When alarmed, a whole herd folds flat at once and becomes indistinguishable from wind-combed grass.

Overhead move slow flocks of bladder-winged animals. Their wings are transparent sacs inflated with warm fermentation gases. They do not flap so much as alter internal pressure, rising and sinking between layers of air. Rain gathers inside them. When burdened, they open valves and release narrow streams that can fall for minutes, so that localized rain follows the flocks across otherwise dry stretches.

The deep root chambers support denser populations: blind burrowers, filter-feeding worms, colonies of warm-bodied mites, and pale amphibious things that swim through plant tissue as easily as through water. Some have no fixed anatomy. Their organs arrive separately through the root conduits and connect only when needed. A feeding animal may consist of a mouth grazing at the surface while its stomach hangs in a cavern fifty meters away. If threatened, the connection closes and both parts continue independently.

Predators often resemble weather. A clear distortion moving low across the turf may be a flock sharing one refractive skin. Sudden hail can be the shed teeth of high-flying feeders. The long, rising siren heard before a squall is sometimes wind in the bellows-groves, and sometimes a migratory organism announcing that the direction called north is about to move.

Directions are not stable here.

The plateau has been eroded beyond geography and into habit. Water has followed certain courses for millions of years, and those courses persist even when the ground changes beneath them. Streams occasionally leave their beds yet continue flowing along the remembered route, suspended at knee height over dry moss. Animals drink from them by raising their heads. Old waterfalls remain in places where cliffs have worn away: upright curtains of water descending through open air, vanishing at the elevation where the vanished valley floor once lay.

Likewise, some hills retain the pressure of their former mass. Entering such a place feels like walking under deep earth although the land is flat and exposed. Sound becomes muffled. Shoulders bow. Roots grow sideways around absent strata. Fossils of trees that never existed protrude from these zones after heavy rain.

The ancient erosion is not peaceful. It has reached a final form that cannot remain final.

Each storm forces the plateau to renegotiate itself. Root mats unzip over hundreds of meters. Tarns tilt and pour uphill. Bellows-groves overpressurize, their trunks sounding like horns as they vent clouds of heated spores. Whole sections of turf rear from the ground, showing pale webs of roots and water-filled cavities underneath, then settle somewhere else. New ravines open but contain old, rounded stones, already worn by ages of rivers that have not yet begun.

Thunder often comes from below. It rolls through the vegetable substrate in long mechanical sequences, setting every pool trembling. Then pressure vents erupt across the flats: geysers of black water, pollen, gas, tiny animals, and smooth pebbles. The eruptions can form temporary assembly lines. Vines catch the airborne material, sort it by weight, and pass it from tendril to tendril with frantic precision. For several hours the landscape clatters and shrieks with production. By morning, nothing manufactured remains except a migration of unfamiliar creatures walking toward the western edge.

The loudest districts are the milling fields.

There the turf forms widely spaced circular mouths, each perhaps a hundred meters across. Inside them, rings of rooted plates revolve in opposite directions, driven by water pressure from distant groves. They grind bedrock that is already almost perfectly flat. The purpose is unclear. Powdered stone blows out in gray plumes, falls on the surrounding plants, and is immediately bound into new tissue. The fields roar continuously, a low sound felt more in the ribs than heard. Yet between the mouths lie immense empty stretches where only meltwater ticks from leaf to leaf.

Nothing on the plateau appears to direct this activity. There is no central organism, no queen, no buried intelligence waiting to be uncovered. Coordination travels as pressure, chemistry, vibration, and remembered shape. A change in one marsh may cause a grove forty leagues away to flower. The death of a single old grazer can halt a river for an afternoon. Lightning striking a tarn may cause every flying animal in the region to turn translucent.

Cause and effect do not always retain their order.

Tracks sometimes appear before the animals make them. A stem may bend, then hours later be touched. Echoes precede certain calls, prompting the caller to answer itself. During severe atmospheric disturbances, dead vegetation rises from the peat and briefly resumes the forms it held thousands of years ago—not restored to life, exactly, but repeated. Ancient forests pass across the open plateau as dim botanical weather: tree-ferns without trunks, vast blossoms suspended over the tarns, forests reduced to shadows and the sound of dripping.

Then the present plants dismantle these apparitions for nutrients.

Despite the turbulence, the place feels terminally ancient. Not ruined, because ruin implies a prior intention. It has simply endured past all sharpness. Every stone is rounded. Every basin is broad. Every process has been repeated until it has worn grooves in reality itself. The plateau has reached the far side of wilderness, where ecology has become infrastructure and infrastructure has become climate.

There are no paths. Yet passage is possible if one observes the rules.

Never step onto vegetation that is breathing out.

Do not follow a stream that casts a shadow.

When the underground pistons stop, lie flat; something enormous is listening through the roots.

If amber light flows west beneath your feet, move north before the direction changes.

And if, in the dimness, you hear the unmistakable approach of a train—wheels hammering joints in a track, brakes screaming, a horn bellowing through fog—do not search for rails. There are none.

Wait beside the shallowest water.

Soon the moss will divide in a long straight line. The rain will rise from the ground. Herds will fold themselves into silence, and the bellows-groves will vent white columns into the cold.

Then the plateau itself will pass.
