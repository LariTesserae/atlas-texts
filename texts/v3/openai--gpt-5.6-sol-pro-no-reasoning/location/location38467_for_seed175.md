The hollow lies high on the warm side of the mountain, a small basin cupped between three rounded shoulders of stone. It is scarcely larger than a grove, yet it feels complete: bounded by low escarpments, drained by no visible stream, open to a pale breadth of sky. From the ridge above, it might be mistaken for a patch of ordinary green. Only on entering does its arrangement become apparent.

Water is everywhere, though almost nowhere deep. It rests in shallow stone pans, threads beneath mats of moss, beads on fern tips, and emerges silently from the walls of the hollow. The rock seems to perspire. Long after rain has passed, clear droplets gather along mineral seams and descend at intervals too slow to follow. The ground gives slightly underfoot, not as mud but as a saturated weave of roots, black soil, and porous stone. Each depression holds a lens of water reflecting the sky.

There are few large plants. The basin’s lushness is low and dispersed: islands of broad-leaved ground growth separated by wet gravel, solitary ferns standing in cracks, dark cushions of moss around the margins of pools. Here and there rises a slender tree no taller than a person’s reach, with smooth, pale bark and a sparse crown. Their trunks lean in unrelated directions, leaving broad intervals of open ground between them. Nothing forms a thicket. Growth has the vividness of abundance without its congestion.

The warmth is persistent and close. Moisture condenses on shaded stone but does not chill it. Bare rock feels almost skin-warm, especially the broad slabs exposed to noon. The air carries the smell of wet basalt, bruised leaves, and something faintly metallic rising from the pools. Mist sometimes collects ankle-high at dawn, confined to the hollow as if too heavy to climb its rim. By midmorning it has withdrawn into cracks and shaded hollows.

The basin is very old in its present shape. Its walls have lost every fresh edge. They are scooped, fluted, and gently undercut, worn not by violent torrents but by the ceaseless passage of films of water. Pebbles lie sorted in crescents around the pools. Mineral deposits have grown over them in translucent skins, binding some into the floor while leaving others loose. Nothing here looks newly broken. Even fallen stone seems to have rested long enough to be accepted into the larger form.

Along the northern wall are a number of shallow recesses resembling niches made by hands. No hands made them. Water hollowed the softer bands of rock and left harder ribs between, producing alcoves at nearly regular intervals. Within each recess lies a flat stone, oval and dark, polished by condensation. Their repetition suggests purpose so strongly that the mind keeps inventing one: seats, altars, sleeping places, stations in a forgotten rite. Yet there are no tool marks, no paths approaching them, and no residue of occupation. The apparent workmanship belongs entirely to geology.

Still, the hollow has something akin to technology.

Certain plants use loose stones.

A creeping vine grows around the edges of the shallow pools. Its thick tendrils curl beneath pebbles and, over years, draw them inward as the vine contracts through dry intervals. The pebbles accumulate in low arcs that retain water around the roots. Elsewhere, pale reedlike stems shed hard, blade-shaped leaves. These fallen blades lodge in narrow runnels, diverting seepage toward patches of moss. It is neither quick nor conscious, but the effects resemble primitive earthwork: dams no higher than a finger, channels the width of cord, reservoirs scarcely deep enough to cover a nail. The basin tends itself with the slowness of growth.

There is no animal life. No insects skate on the pools. No worms disturb the soil, no birds cross overhead while visible from within, no amphibian calls from the wet margins. Flowers open without pollinators and close again untouched. Seed capsules split by heat and moisture alone. Some release dust so fine that it drifts on condensation currents; others grow new stalks from fragments carried a few handspans by overflow. Decay proceeds without scavengers. Fallen leaves soften, blacken, and dissolve gradually into the wet ground under films of fungus and colorless microbial slime.

This absence is not immediately alarming. At first it registers only as quiet. Then, little by little, the missing layers of sound become distinct. There is no wingbeat behind the leaves, no scrape beneath stones, no sudden rustle caused by anything with intention. The loudest noises are droplets striking water, the minute settling of gravel, and the occasional release of trapped air from the saturated soil. Each sound stands alone, surrounded by enough silence to seem deliberate.

The pools have a peculiarity. They reflect the sky accurately, but not always the same part of it. One may hold the sun when the sun is behind the observer. Another may show a bank of cloud on a clear afternoon. The images move so slowly that the discrepancy is difficult to prove; watch one pool long enough and doubt overtakes certainty. The water itself is shallow and transparent. Beneath its misplaced reflection, every grain of sand can be seen.

Near the center lies the largest pool, perhaps six paces across, enclosed by a rim of cream-colored mineral stone. It has no surface ripple. Droplets descending into it vanish without disturbance, as though passing through a membrane. Its level rises during dry weather and falls after rain. When full, it spills uphill in a narrow silver thread that climbs a worn groove for several yards before entering the ground beneath a fern.

Nothing happens quickly here. Water crosses the basin by routes measured in seasons. A stone acquires a green skin, then a white crust, then is exposed again over the course of decades. Trees bend toward patches of shade rather than light. Pools migrate by the width of a palm in a lifetime. The hollow does not seem frozen or dead; it is active at a pace too patient for animal attention.

At evening the pale trunks darken and the low vegetation retains the last light. Condensation thickens on every surface until the basin gleams without becoming brighter. Droplets hang beneath leaves, growing slowly, but many do not fall. They remain suspended through the night, large and perfectly clear, each containing an inverted image of the hollow.

By dawn some have become small stones. They lie beneath the plants: smooth, oval, and dark, identical to those resting in the alcoves along the northern wall. Over many years roots and seepage will move them toward the pools. Over many more, mineral water will polish them. Eventually one may be carried to a recess and settle on its flat floor.

Thus the hollow keeps its sparse arrangement. Nothing builds, yet forms accumulate. Nothing tends it, yet water is guided. Nothing watches, calls, hunts, or flees. The place remains warm, wet, and almost silent beneath the open mountain sky, completing its obscure work one drop at a time.
