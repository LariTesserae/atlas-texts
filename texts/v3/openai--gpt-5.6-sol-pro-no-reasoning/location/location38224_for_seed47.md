The high country begins where the atmosphere becomes too thin to carry weather in the usual way.

It is not a range of mountains so much as one mountain spread across a continent: an immense mineral table lifted nearly out of the sky. Its summits are broad, old, and strangely level. Between them open vertical distances—fault chasms, collapsed chambers, and long descents into blue-black air—but the upper surfaces have been worn almost smooth. Whatever once carved them had ages to finish. Ridges have lost their sharpness. Craters have softened into shallow bowls. Exposed strata end in polished lips as if the whole plateau were a single artifact handled until every edge became dull.

There is no running water, no buried ice, not even much atmospheric vapor. The stone contains little hydrogen. Dust does not clump, and footprints, where the ground accepts them, remain as clean disruptions in the powder until the next overturning of the land.

The plateau is pale under an ordinary sun. Its colors are bone gray, faded lilac, iron blue, and the weak green of oxidized metals. Noon has none of the theatrical glare of a hot desert. Light falls clearly and without mercy, showing distance after distance: terraces wider than nations, isolated black outcrops, and escarpments whose bases are hidden by curvature rather than haze. Shadows are sharp nearby and gradually become indigo. At night, the ground releases its warmth at once. The stars do not flicker.

The emptiness is so complete that solitary objects acquire the force of events.

A column stands forty kilometers away.

A patch of dark growth occupies the lee of one boulder.

Three low structures interrupt the horizon.

Nothing else.

And yet the place is never still.

Its turbulence belongs less to the air than to the underlying geometry. Beneath the ancient surface lies a system of mechanisms, or perhaps a manufactured phase of matter, distributed through the crust. It has operated longer than the present arrangement of continents. No moving parts are visible. It works by persuading mass to behave differently from place to place.

At irregular intervals the plateau changes its gravitational loyalties.

A field of loose stones rises soundlessly and hangs several meters above the ground. Each pebble turns on an axis of its own, slowly at first, then with frantic precision. Dust ascends in vertical sheets. A cliff sheds an avalanche sideways, sending slabs sailing across a chasm to strike a wall that, for those minutes, is down. Farther off, whole mesas drift by degrees: not floating freely, but leaning into temporary gradients, grinding against their roots and opening incandescent seams beneath them.

There is almost no sound. The thin atmosphere cannot carry the scale of the disturbance. Catastrophes occur as pantomime.

Close to the ground, one may hear the dry ticking of grains colliding in suspension. Rock under stress gives a faint, intimate chirp, like cooling glass. Structures emit tones too low to hear directly; they are felt in the teeth and in the fluid of the inner ear. The largest events arrive through the feet as slow pressure, long after they have become visible. An escarpment may fold on the horizon in perfect silence, and only hours later a single soft pulse passes through the soles.

These upheavals do not make the landscape young. They are part of its great age. Every unstable configuration has occurred innumerable times. Every surface capable of breaking has broken. Every block has been lifted, turned, abraded, and set down again until it approaches some final resistant shape. The plateau is turbulent in the way a flame is turbulent: continuously changing, yet preserving an ancient overall form.

The weather is made by stone.

When a gravity front crosses a basin, billions of mineral flakes launch into the air and form a high, slow storm. They do not whirl randomly. Different compositions sort themselves into separate elevations. Iron dust makes low red veils; mica gathers overhead in shining shoals; electrically charged salts draw luminous threads between them. From far away these storms resemble enormous translucent architecture—walls and vaults assembling over the plain, advancing, collapsing, and being rebuilt by the same few tons of matter.

They may persist for months without traveling more than a hundred kilometers. Inside them, visibility shifts between perfect clarity and complete silver opacity. Static prickles over exposed surfaces, but lightning is rare. Instead, charge drains through narrow, straight filaments that join cloud to ground without flash or thunder. At their contact points, stone is altered into smooth black patches with the texture of sealed wax.

The scattered constructions seem designed for these conditions, though not necessarily by the same intelligence that created the buried system.

Most are small relative to the landscape: dark polygonal shelters half sunk into bedrock, needle towers, rings of pale ceramic, and doorless chambers no larger than shrines. Their materials have no visible joints. Some are warm in daylight and colder than their surroundings at night; others maintain precisely the reverse. Dust refuses to land on them. Loose grains approaching their surfaces decelerate and settle in narrow, immaculate halos.

The shelters seldom contain furniture. A typical interior has a sloping floor, a recessed platform, and a vertical slit through which one can see a portion of the horizon. The slit is not always aligned with the exterior wall. Looking through it may reveal a place hundreds of kilometers distant: another shelter, another empty basin, a storm lifting itself from the plain. These views change when the gravitational fields do, suggesting that the buildings do not transmit images so much as borrow straight lines from elsewhere.

Many are damaged. Some have been opened like seed husks by shifts in local mass. Others remain outwardly perfect but lead into rooms that have become immeasurably deep. A tossed pebble falls out of sight without striking anything. Instruments disagree on whether these interiors extend downward, outward, or into no conventional direction at all.

Across certain flats run faint parallel markings. From ground level they look like shallow scratches left by dragging cables. Seen from above, they compose immense branching diagrams that bend around vanished features and continue beneath newer layers of stone. Their intersections are marked by low pylons. Most pylons are inert, but a few brighten at sunset—not glowing, exactly, but reflecting light from an angle where the sun is not.

Sparse life survives in the intervals.

In sheltered cracks grows a black, papery organism that is neither quite plant nor quite colony. It has no need of liquid water. Its metabolism moves ions through flexible crystal membranes, feeding on thermal difference and mineral charge. During calm periods it lies flat against the rock, indistinguishable from soot. When a gravity front approaches, it unfolds thousands of translucent vanes. These stand at conflicting angles, each responding to a slightly different down. For an hour the barren ledges appear covered in dark, trembling flowers.

Pale filaments occur beneath overhangs, growing a few millimeters in a century. They dissolve traces of reactive stone and leave delicate tunnels behind. Large colonies make the rock ring softly when the ground flexes. Their dead layers become a brittle greenish lacquer, sometimes meters thick and older than entire mountain chains.

The rare animals are wanderers between such colonies. They are low, many-jointed things with broad gripping feet and bodies enclosed in dull shells. Most are no larger than a hand. They move only during stable intervals, following routes impressed into them by pressures rather than scent. When the fields begin to shift, they flatten themselves into crevices and lock every joint. A few fail. Their empty shells can be found high on vertical faces or embedded in the undersides of overhangs, records of obsolete directions.

There are rumors of larger creatures in the deepest basins: slow shapes seen crossing the pale ground after sunset, each accompanied by a region where stones rise a few centimeters. If they exist, they may not resist the plateau’s gravity changes. They may carry small gravitational conditions with them, moving inside private definitions of level.

Travel here is a discipline of waiting. Distance alone is not the chief difficulty. A route that is flat in the morning may become an ascent by afternoon without changing shape. Compasses point toward active machinery. Inertial instruments accumulate contradictions. One navigates by old surface polish, by the posture of the black vanes, and by watching the dust layers overhead. Experienced travelers drive thin rods into the ground at each camp. If the rods lean in different directions, no one proceeds.

There is one reliable rule: when shadows begin to detach from their objects, lie down.

This occurs before the strongest transitions. The shadow of a stone shifts several centimeters away from its base, not because the light has moved, but because space between sun and surface has acquired a slight curvature. Soon afterward, elevations exchange burdens. Valleys become easier to fall out of than into. Dust storms flatten into hovering ceilings. The ceramic rings awaken, and every intact ring across thousands of kilometers produces the same nearly inaudible note.

Then the plateau enters its most violent state.

Mesas lift along ancient seams. Chasms close, squeezing out columns of powdered rock. Distant towers vanish and reappear against different horizons. For several minutes, the entire landscape seems to consist of separate masses negotiating where they belong. There is no roar, only the faint rush of dust and the dry patter of pebbles settling onto surfaces that were walls moments before.

At the center of these episodes, the oldest parts of the plateau do not move at all.

These are the final stones: low, rounded islands of dark mineral scattered through the high country, polished beyond texture. Storms divide around them. Gravity fronts lose coherence at their boundaries. No tool has marked them, and no structure stands directly upon them. The sparse organisms avoid them. Under normal light they look unremarkable, almost dull, but their shadows remain attached even when all other shadows wander.

Around each is a zone of profound calm.

Dust drops from suspension. Instruments agree. One can hear, briefly, the small sounds of one’s own body. In a land where everything is ancient and everything changes, these stones have apparently finished changing. They may be natural remnants, anchors of the buried machinery, or pieces of matter brought to a state the rest of the world is still trying to reach.

After an upheaval, the plateau settles with extraordinary delicacy. Suspended stones lower themselves. Mineral clouds separate and fade. The black vanes fold. New scarps stand for a while with clean faces, but even these reveal old polish inside, as though the land has not exposed anything genuinely new—only another surface already aged.

The sun continues over the emptied heights. Light passes across scattered shelters and immeasurable flats. Far away, a solitary column appears to lean, though it may be the horizon adjusting beneath it.

Then there is quiet again: not peace, exactly, but the silence of a world whose disasters are too large and too familiar to require noise.
