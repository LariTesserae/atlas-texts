The land is high, hot, and almost entirely alive.

From a distance it resembles a green plateau lifted above the haze: an immense, uneven tableland whose margins fall away in rust-red cliffs. Yet there is little soil upon it, and less exposed stone. What appears to be ground is a compacted depth of roots, fallen fronds, woody plates, and the interlocked crowns of plants growing upon older plants. In places this living substrate is hundreds of meters thick. Ravines do not cut through earth so much as through accumulated generations of vegetable matter, exposing strata of pale fiber, black humus, amber sapwood, and root systems petrified by their own resins.

The plateau is broad enough to make its own weather. Noon heat raises columns of scented air from its surface, drawing thin clouds out of the surrounding lowlands. These gather over the interior heights and burst irregularly. The rain is seldom prolonged. It falls in hard, isolated curtains, silver at a distance, drumming upon leaves for ten minutes and then vanishing. Most water never reaches the lower layers. It is caught in cups, sheaths, hollow stems, bark folds, and the enormous tilted leaves of reservoir plants. Thus the country is damp without being wet. Water exists in scattered rooms.

Between storms, everything sweats.

Condensation pearls beneath the canopy at dawn, though dawn is brief and warm. Drops gather along waxy leaf-ribs until their weight triggers a sequence of flexing plates. A whole grove may tip at once, pouring yesterday’s moisture into basins farther downslope. The transfer can travel for kilometers: leaf to leaf, cup to cup, a slow irrigation designed by no single organism. Beneath it, the deeper vegetal layers remain hot, fibrous, and nearly dry. Fire sometimes moves there unseen.

The apparent contradiction of the plateau is that vegetation fills nearly all available matter, yet the landscape feels spacious. The plants themselves create the emptiness.

The largest are column groves, widely separated trunks rising from the root plain like towers. Each trunk may stand half a kilometer from the next. Their bark is plated and pale, reflecting heat; their crowns spread high above in broad, sparse fans. Between them lie open reaches of low, tight growth: copper mosses, hard-leaved mats, and expanses of jointed stalks no taller than a hand. Nothing is bare, but little is crowded. The plant cover is continuous in the way skin is continuous—thin in one place, folded and thickened in another, perforated by pores and cavities.

Some hollows are roofed by single leaves.

These leaves grow from buried crowns and emerge as green arches, their edges planted in the substrate hundreds of paces apart. Their undersides remain cool and dim. People, animals, and entire colonies shelter there through the hottest part of the day. Rain striking the upper surface becomes a low thunder within, and water runs down translucent veins as if through channels in a ceiling.

Elsewhere rise the walking forests: sparse assemblies of long-legged trees whose rooted limbs die behind them and descend ahead. Their movement is scarcely visible from hour to hour, but trails abandoned for a season may be swallowed or carried sideways. A landmark can migrate. Shade has a direction and a seasonal destination.

The heat is constant but not uniform. In exposed reaches it has a clean, hammering force. Resin softens. Seed cases snap. The air above dark foliage trembles. Under the reservoir leaves, in root fissures, and around the rare pools, the temperature drops sharply enough to raise mist. Nights remain warm, though the elevated air cools faster than the living ground. After sunset, vapor streams upward from cracks, turning the plateau into a field of low white banners.

The pools are precious and temporary. Many occupy depressions on the backs of gigantic, recumbent plants. Their water is tannin-dark, edged with bladder flowers and breathing roots. A pool may persist for decades, developing fishlike swimmers, grazing shelled animals, and floating pollinator colonies; then its supporting leaf slowly tilts, emptying an entire little lake in an afternoon. The released water charges downslope through dry root channels. For a few hours there are rivers. Creatures follow them in roaring migrations, mating and laying eggs in every saturated fold before the channels close or drink themselves dry.

Permanent streams are rare. One or two great rivers cross the plateau, but even these are not entirely water. They are braided systems of flowing roots, seasonal runoff, migrating reed beds, and mobile rafts of vegetation. At their margins, the distinction between river and forest fails. Banks shift because they grow. Islands climb over one another. Shoals flower.

The sound is immense.

There is no true silence, not even at the zenith of the hot day. Insects produce the foundation: billions of wing-cases, throat sacs, hollow abdomens, and vibrating stems generating a continuous pressure that can be felt through the teeth. Different species occupy different pitches, and their choruses move across the country in audible fronts. A distant swarm may sound like rainfall long before it arrives. Near at hand, its combined note becomes too loud to hear individual wings.

Above this drone come the cracking of heat-stressed pods, the slap of flexing leaves, the wooden groan of migrating trees, and the explosive reports of trunks releasing stored vapor. Herd animals call across enormous distances through chambers in their chests. Their voices are below ordinary hearing, but they stir water in leaf-cups and shake dust from bark. Predators answer with sharp, directional shrieks that ricochet from the column groves.

Thunder is only one more participant.

Life is everywhere, and almost none of it is small in consequence.

Vast grazing organisms cross the low mats in scattered herds. They are high-legged and narrow-bodied, built to shed heat, with mouths arranged in rotating rings that crop several layers of growth without exposing the substrate beneath. Gardens grow on their backs: parasitic shrubs, cooling fans, and bright flowering lures whose pollinators defend the host as if it were terrain. Older animals carry miniature ecologies in the hollows of their armor. When one lies down, birds and climbing scavengers treat it as a hill.

The air belongs to sails, kites, membranes, and swarms. Some animals fly by muscle; others merely rise in the noon thermals and steer. Predatory ribbons coil among them. Bladder creatures drift beneath clouds, trailing feeding threads that can strip the crown of a tree. Migratory flocks can darken a region for hours, although the ordinary daylight beneath them remains bright rather than gloomy: their bodies are translucent, and the sun passes through in amber and red.

On the ground, every root cavity contains motion. Six-legged browsers click along bark ridges. Pale, soft-bodied colonies quarry the dead vegetal layers and reinforce their tunnels with lacquered pulp. Hunters mimic seed pods until touched. Tiny animals live within flowers large enough to have internal weather. Some never leave one blossom during their entire lives; to them, its opening is morning, its closing is winter, and pollination is an apocalypse of golden dust.

Faunal abundance governs the plants as much as the plants govern the animals. Nothing fruits unless bitten. Many seeds will not wake until carried through a heated stomach. Some trees require herds to abrade their bark; without the animals, they suffocate beneath their own growth. Grazers open corridors, predators keep grazers moving, and the moving herds distribute the pockets of water caught in their bodies and back-gardens. The ecosystem’s balance is not peaceful. It is a maintained uproar.

The ground itself feeds.

The interlocked vegetation responds slowly to pressure, warmth, blood, and shade. Rest too long in one place and thin exploratory roots gather beneath you. At first they are merely cool threads. Later they exude an anesthetic sweetness. Dead animals disappear quickly, not through decay alone but through enclosure: leaves overlap them, roots enter them, and new shoots rise bearing minerals in patterns that retain a faint geometry of bone.

There are rules known by anything that survives here.

Never drink from a perfectly clear cup; it belongs to something that filters water.

Never shelter beneath a leaf with its veins turned outward; it is preparing to close.

When the insects cease together, climb.

When the low flowers face downhill, rain is coming. When they face uphill, the river is.

And if a walking forest begins to groan in daylight, leave its path before sunset, because the trees move fastest at night.

Human habitation, if it is human, is scattered and provisional. No city can remain stable on a foundation that grows, migrates, heals, and occasionally digests. Settlements cling to rare exposures of stone along the plateau rim or occupy dead crown-shells high in the column trees. They are small: a few platforms of woven rib, hide screens lacquered against spores, ladders braided from living vines. Some are inhabited only during a harvest or migration. Others have been maintained for centuries while slowly circling their host trunks as new bark accumulates beneath them.

Metal is uncommon and often less useful than horn, shell, glassy resin, or hardened wood. The heat spoils bindings, moisture finds blades, and the constant biological richness corrodes anything not tended. Tools are grown as often as made. A cutting edge may be a jaw removed from a predator and set into a wooden grip. Water vessels are sealed fruits. Boats are trained leaves stitched at the margins while young. Hunters use bows laminated from springwood and insect silk; their arrow poisons are not meant to kill quickly, but to alter a target’s scent so the forest’s lesser predators will follow it.

Structures seldom impose straight lines. A platform adjusts to the annual swelling of its tree. A bridge is a coaxed root that must be pruned, fed, and persuaded not to wander. Roofs flower if neglected. Walls produce shoots. Ruins are difficult to distinguish from gardens.

Only at the outer cliffs do older marks remain. There, weather has exposed black stone beneath the living mantle. Steps descend partway down impossible faces and end in air. Shallow chambers have been cut into the rock, their entrances narrowed by centuries of root growth. On certain ledges stand isolated pillars made from fused ceramic, too large and too heat-scarred for present tools. Their surfaces carry rows of hand-sized hollows. Birds nest in them. In heavy weather, the hollows whistle different notes.

The plateau edge reveals its age. Layers of former ecosystems protrude from the cliffs: compressed forests, mats of ancient roots, shell bands, charcoal horizons from old internal fires. Erosion has settled into a slow exchange. Storms peel material from the margins; vegetation advances over the breaks and knits them closed. Waterfalls appear after rain and vanish before reaching the lowlands, shredded into vapor by the hot updraft. Whole sections occasionally slump away, but even collapse is colonized before the dust settles. Seeds unfold in midair. Flying animals follow the falling land downward.

Toward the interior, elevation rises so gradually it is noticed only in the thinning air and changing calls. The highest region is not mountainous. It is a broad swell known by its pale growth and violent thermals. Here the living ground arches over immense empty chambers. Warm air moves through them like breath through lungs. At intervals the surface rises and falls by a few handspans. Everything rooted there leans with it.

Some say the entire plateau is one organism.

This is not quite true, though truth becomes difficult at such scales. Roots graft to roots. Fungal threads carry chemical warnings for hundreds of kilometers. Herbivores transport hormones in their blood. Rivers shift to feed flowering regions. Forests migrate toward distant injuries. No central will can be found, but responses propagate across the land with troubling coordination.

During the long hot season, moisture dwindles into hidden stores. Leaves turn their reflective sides upward, whitening the country. Animals concentrate around the remaining cups, and predation becomes feverish. The noise reaches its yearly peak. Then the plateau begins to pulse at night. Deep vapor vents open. Clouds gather from within the land as much as above it.

Rain follows—not a monsoon, but scattered deluges wandering across the vastness. From a high crown one can watch five storms at once, each isolated by sunlit distances. Lightning walks between cloud and tree. Brief rivers shine. Millions of dormant creatures emerge, sing, mate, and vanish within days. Flowers open in chains visible to the horizon.

Afterward the water retreats into stems, shells, stomachs, hollows, and dark internal reservoirs. The open country returns: green, immense, sparsely towered, crowded beyond counting.

It looks still only from very far away.
