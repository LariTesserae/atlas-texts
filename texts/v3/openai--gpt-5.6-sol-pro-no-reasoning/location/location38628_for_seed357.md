The high country is built entirely of plants.

There is no bedrock beneath it, no soil in the ordinary sense, no buried skeleton of mineral holding up the ridges. From the lowlands to the far blue shoulders of the plateau, everything visible has been grown into place: piers of bundled root, retaining walls woven from living fiber, terraces stretched taut between immense trunks. Even the peaks are vegetal structures—hollow green cones whose ribs rise from deep, interlocked foundations and meet around chimneys open to the sky.

No one built them. Nothing here possesses hands.

The architecture is an activity of the landscape itself.

At first the country appears densely forested, almost overwhelmingly so. Vegetation covers every surface. But within that total green fabric there is an unexpected spaciousness. Great distances lie between the largest growths. A single tower-tree may stand half a kilometer from the next, its canopy held high and narrow, leaving broad aprons of shining leaf-mat exposed around it. Bridges span empty air with no traffic upon them. Colonnades march for miles across the plateau, but their columns are widely separated, and between them the hot sky opens in vast, lucid chambers.

The ground gives slightly under weight. It consists of overlapping plates of leaf and bark, each plate several paces across, buoyed by bladders of warm sap. Their margins fit together like tiles. Fine roots stitch the seams shut and unpick them again as the terrain shifts. Where a plate has been recently laid, it is tender green, nearly translucent, with pale veins visible inside it. Older districts darken through bronze and purple toward a hard, lacquered black. Nothing becomes stone. It merely enters slower forms of life.

Water is everywhere near the surface. Rain falls often, though rarely as a cool relief. It comes down warm and heavy, striking the leaf pavements with the sound of thrown seeds. For hours afterward, vapor rises from the terraces. Condensation beads on aerial roots and gathers under broad eaves, dripping in steady curtains into the channels below.

The channels, too, are grown.

Their banks are raised lips of waxy tissue; their beds are corrugated bark that swells or narrows to govern the flow. Some carry clear rainwater. Others run amber with dissolved pollen, green with spores, or faintly white with latex. At intersections, petaled sluices open and close according to pressure. Water plunges through vertical throats into cisterns formed by giant cup-leaves, then spills from their rims in sudden, brilliant sheets. These spills are not failures of the system. They are how the upper country redistributes its weight.

All this takes place at altitude, beneath a sun that remains fiercely hot even when cloud surrounds the peaks. The air is thin enough to sharpen distant forms, yet wet enough that every breath seems to contain the plateau in solution. Heat gathers under the elevated canopies. It trembles above black bark and steams from pools. Where clouds drag across the terraces, they snag on spines and trellises, shredding into luminous veils. For a few minutes the world may contract to dripping green walls; then the vapor tears away, and the view opens again across scores of miles.

The plateau is young. Its edges have not been softened.

New ridges stand in serrated rows, their blades made of fresh red bracts reinforced with tightly braided roots. Ramparts have sharp corners. Towers end in wet points. Vast buttresses, still yellow and flexible, thrust from the flanks of older structures and harden visibly in the sun. There are almost no rounded ruins. When part of the landscape grows obsolete, it is not allowed to weather. Threads of fungusless, plant-born digestion spread over it, loosen its fibers, and draw it back into the living substrate.

Thus the country is both ancient in process and perpetually recent in form.

Its turbulence is most obvious at the horizon. Whole groves lean and right themselves, not in response to wind but through coordinated changes in tension. A distant green ridge may buckle upward over the course of an afternoon, unfolding chambers within itself. Elsewhere a terrace splits along a predetermined seam. Its two halves peel apart, exposing a vertical well of scarlet tissue filled with descending water. Suspension vines race across the opening, thicken, and draw the halves into a new arrangement.

Sometimes the changes are more violent.

Pressure accumulates in buried vascular trunks until the leaf-mat bulges. A deep booming travels through the plateau. Then the surface ruptures, and a fountain of hot water, seed husks, and pale root-cord erupts hundreds of feet into the air. The cords fall across the land in tangled arcs. Where they touch, they adhere. Within hours they tighten into scaffolds; within days, walls begin to climb them.

These events give the place its noise. There are no cries, wingbeats, footfalls, or insect hum. No animals live here, not even creatures too small to see. Pollination is performed by rain, thermal currents, and the landscape’s own hydraulic convulsions. Seeds are flung from pressurized pods or carried through internal canals. Flowers open facing one another across deliberate gaps and release pollen when the wind aligns.

Yet the silence of fauna does not make the country quiet.

Leaves the size of plazas clap together in storms. Sap pulses in trunk-conduits with a low, repetitive concussion. Hollow towers moan as heated air rises through them. Vines snap taut like enormous strings. Water drums on canopies, hisses through pores, roars from overflow mouths, and knocks deep within the cisterns. When a new support pier drives roots into the lower mats, the plateau produces a grinding sound uncannily like masonry being dragged across masonry—though there is no masonry here, and never has been.

The structures suggest intention while refusing every familiar purpose.

There are avenues, but they do not lead toward settlements. There are chambers, but nothing inhabits them. High vaults open onto sheer drops. Stairs of thickened leaf ascend a tower and end at a sun-facing flower. Door-shaped membranes dilate at dawn and close at noon, regulating airflow rather than passage. Long galleries with ribbed ceilings collect mist and guide it toward reservoirs. What appears from afar to be a city is, on approach, a single distributed organism—or perhaps many organisms trained by mutual pressure into the habits of a city.

No clear boundary exists between individuals.

A tower may draw water through roots belonging to a terrace, receive sugar from a bridge, and send chemical instructions into a ridge kilometers away. Cut one strand and it seals itself; cut many, and neighboring structures reorganize. A colonnade may abandon its apparent alignment overnight, uprooting alternate pillars and growing new ones farther west. The empty spaces are as carefully managed as the living mass. They are reservoirs of sun, corridors of wind, throwing ranges for seed, and room for the terrain’s next configuration.

This is why the land can be completely vegetated and still feel sparse. Its abundance is not clutter. It is large, deliberate spacing: isolated monuments joined below sight, open courts held between immense green walls, solitary spires rising from wet plains. The eye travels far before meeting detail, then discovers that every distant simplicity contains countless veins, pores, hooks, valves, buds, and seams.

In the broadest basin lies a lake whose floor is a single living leaf.

The leaf is dark blue and slightly concave, with a raised central vein like a causeway. Rainwater covers it to a shallow depth. Heat from the tissue below keeps the lake warm enough to steam each morning. At irregular intervals, the leaf flexes. A wave travels from one end of the basin to the other, lifting the water in a smooth wall that breaks against the grown embankments. The impact can be heard across the plateau.

Along the lake’s margin stand widely spaced structures resembling unfinished palaces. Their roofs are fans of overlapping fronds; their walls are white, fibrous lattices through which mist passes. Each is rooted to the basin’s rim and continually remodels itself. Balconies become gutters. Gutters thicken into branches. Rooms fill with pith and vanish. New halls open where the sunlight has shifted. During rain, their walls flush green. During heat, they bleach almost silver and curl inward to preserve water.

Beyond the basin, the country rises again in successive engineered shelves. Water ascends with it, drawn through capillary trunks into reservoirs at impossible heights. The uppermost shelves are exposed to frequent storms. Lightning strikes the wet spires, blackening them but rarely killing them. The charge passes down conductive sapways and triggers mass flowering across distant terraces. After such a storm, the plateau glows for a night with pale blossoms—not luminescent, but so reflective that ordinary moonlight gathers on them like frost.

By morning, most have closed. Their petals detach and slide into the canals. The water becomes white with them. Sluices pulse open. Entire districts drink.

The governing rule of this place is not growth alone, but replacement. Nothing is permitted to remain merely because it already exists. Every span, tower, pavement, and reservoir must continue justifying itself through circulation: of water, tension, heat, sugar, spores, or light. A structure that ceases to participate is softened from within. Its walls sag, its fibers separate, and adjacent growths draw away its substance. In the hot wet air, demolition can be as swift as construction.

A second rule is that the landscape never changes everywhere at once. While one valley heaves with rootwork and collapsing galleries, another holds perfectly still beneath the rain. While the western escarpment unfurls a new series of bridges, the lake basin may remain unchanged for years. This unevenness preserves the open scale of the place. Turbulence travels through it in fronts, leaving pauses behind—great calm intervals of wet pavement, lone towers, and water reflecting the ordinary daylight.

From a high terrace, one can watch such a front approach.

First the canals begin running backward. Then the slender trellises turn their leaves edge-on. A stain of deeper green advances across the distant mats as buried chambers fill. Towers bend away from one another, widening the sky between them. The sound gathers: knocks in the substrate, the creak of loaded fibers, the escalating thunder of displaced water.

At last the land ahead lifts.

Not like an earthquake, for nothing fractures into inert debris. It rises in articulated sections, unfolding ribs, walls, and vast glossy membranes that shed cataracts from their surfaces. Old avenues tilt into the air and become aqueducts. Cisterns invert into domes. A ridge opens lengthwise and reveals rows of tightly packed green blades, each poised to become a pillar. For several hours the plateau is a storm of its own body—building, draining, tightening, releasing.

Then it settles.

Rainwater streams from the new heights. Torn fibers seal. The heat returns to a steady shimmer. Across the altered district, structures stand far apart in fresh arrangements, sharp-edged and dripping, as if an empty city had just risen without laborers, tools, quarried material, or plan.

Soon pale roots begin stitching the new ground closed.

The lake flexes once in the distance.

And all through the high, hot country, the water starts again to move.
