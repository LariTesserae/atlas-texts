The city hangs in the upper reaches of an ocean with no surface.

It occupies a volume no larger than a hill town: a sparse arrangement of towers, bridges, pressure chambers, and empty plazas assembled around the summit of a newly made mountain. Yet “summit” is only a directional convenience. There is no sky above it and no seabed visibly below. Water continues in every direction, cold and black, under a pressure that should compact ordinary machinery into foil.

The mountain itself is young. Its ridges are knife-edged, its fractures clean, its exposed planes scarcely marked by erosion. It resembles black volcanic glass until approached, when depth appears inside the material: smoky layers folded one behind another, interrupted by pale geometric faults. These faults meet at deliberate angles. Whether the mountain was built, grown, or persuaded into existence is not clear.

The city clings to its upper third.

Its structures are widely spaced. Narrow towers rise from separate crags, leaving broad intervals of unoccupied water between them. Some are needles of matte ceramic; others are hollow polyhedra whose edges alone possess substance. A few resemble stacked rooms without external walls, their boundaries maintained by abrupt changes in the behavior of the water. Within one room, droplets somehow drift through the surrounding sea. Within another, loose objects rest on an invisible floor. Beyond its threshold, gravity resumes its uncertain pull toward the mountain.

There are streets, though they carry no traffic. They are long bands of transparent material set through the water, gently curving between buildings. A traveler entering one is borne forward without current, propulsion, or perceptible acceleration. The water inside remains perfectly still. At junctions, thin rings mark alternate routes; passing through a ring changes what counts as down.

Nothing sways here.

No cable trembles. No sediment settles. No bubble rises. The water has the coolness of a deep reservoir, but there are no thermal layers and no currents. Disturbances travel only a short distance before vanishing. Strike a wall and it gives one low, contained note; a few meters away there is silence. Even the mountain’s sharp surfaces remain immaculate, as if the ocean has touched them forever without once moving across them.

The city’s darkness is nearly complete. Light occurs in restrained quantities: a blue seam under a bridge, a square of dim amber suspended in a doorway, three silver points down the side of a tower. The illumination does not radiate normally. It defines surfaces but does not brighten the intervening water. Thus the city reveals itself in disconnected pieces—a parapet here, an arch there, a staircase rising alone into blackness—while most of its form must be inferred.

From a distance, these scattered lights suggest a constellation drowned intact.

There is no vegetation. No weed softens the stone, no algal film clouds the glass, no photosynthetic stain collects around lamps. The exposed materials remain sterile and sharply differentiated: black mineral, white ceramic, dull metal, transparent membranes, and a substance like frozen smoke that bends pressure around itself. Even fungal life is absent. Surfaces do not decay because nothing feeds upon them, and perhaps because decay is not permitted as a local process.

The rare animals are small and difficult to classify. One may appear after hours of waiting: a translucent knot with six slow fins, emerging from darkness to pause beside a lit threshold. Another is a ribbon-thin organism that folds itself into rigid angles instead of curves. They make no visible effort to swim. They never touch the structures. Each follows one short route, vanishes, and—if watched long enough—returns by exactly the same path at exactly the same interval.

It is possible that there are only seven animals in the entire city.

It is also possible that these are not animals, but custodial functions wearing bodies.

At the center stands the largest construction, a cathedral-sized chamber cut partly into the mountain and partly into the surrounding water. Its entrance is an upright oval several stories high. There is no door, only a perfectly black plane through which the sea does not pass. Crossing it produces no sensation except the sudden absence of pressure.

Inside, the water remains, yet one can breathe it.

The chamber contains terraces arranged around an empty central shaft. Seats, consoles, and balconies imply a population, but all are too far apart for ordinary social life. Each station faces inward toward the shaft, where a column of darkness descends beyond measurement. On its surface—or within it—tiny diagrams occasionally become visible: maps of streets that do not exist, structural cross-sections of the mountain, and views of the city from positions outside space.

These images never animate. They do not flicker or update. They are simply found to be different depending on who looks, as though observation selects one permanent display from an archive of equally permanent states.

The city has no obvious power source. Its devices do not hum or shed waste heat. Doors distinguish intention from accident. Broken edges reunite if no one observes them, though nothing else repairs itself. Distances are conserved only along marked routes. An unmarked gap between two nearby towers may require hours to cross, while a street bending around the mountain can deliver a traveler to its beginning in a few breaths.

Clocks function, but disagree without drifting. Each keeps its chosen time flawlessly.

This is the fundamental rule of the place: nothing changes, but differences are allowed.

A plaza can be empty and occupied, depending on its point of entry. A tower can have nine floors from the eastern bridge and eleven from the western one. The ocean outside may be ancient, while the mountain remains geologically newborn. Events are not prevented; they are absorbed into stable arrangements. A footprint pressed into pale dust appears not as damage but as a permanent additional version of the floor. A spoken word does not echo. Instead, it becomes a faint pressure held at the point of utterance, detectable centuries later by passing a hand through it.

The city is therefore not abandoned in the usual sense. Abandonment would be a transition, and transitions do not seem to survive here.

Perhaps its inhabitants departed. Perhaps they have not yet arrived. Perhaps they remain distributed among the rooms as fixed possibilities, inaccessible from the particular geometry through which a visitor enters.

Occasionally, at the edge of vision, a figure can be seen standing on one of the distant transparent streets. It is tall, dark, and motionless. Turning toward it does not make it disappear; rather, the street is found to have no place where such a figure could stand.

There are few sounds. The contained note of struck material. A periodic compression like a faraway door closing. The almost inaudible crackle of one’s own nerves beneath the pressure. Near the central chamber, another sound persists: a chord so low it is felt in the teeth rather than heard. It never begins, ends, or varies. The mountain, the city, and the water appear tuned to it.

Above the highest spire is a circular opening in the ocean.

It is about thirty meters across and perfectly horizontal, though horizontal has no consistent meaning elsewhere. Looking through it reveals not air but more water, paler and apparently immeasurably distant. A dim radiance lies beyond, like daylight recalled after centuries underground. No object can cross the opening. Anything pushed toward it slows, not gradually but by division—half the remaining distance, then half again—until it hangs beneath the threshold forever.

Around this inaccessible aperture, the city’s few towers form a loose crown. Their upper rooms contain no controls, furniture, or writing. Each holds only a shallow basin filled with water indistinguishable from the water outside.

In every basin rests a single bright grain.

The grains provide almost none of the city’s light, yet all shadows lean subtly toward them.

Nothing in the drowned settlement indicates whether they are seeds, stars, memories, or machines. Nothing grows from them. Nothing consumes them. They cannot be moved, and examination reveals no structure below the limit of any instrument.

The city seems to have been constructed around these grains, or by them, or as the necessary consequence of their presence.

It remains poised on its sharp black mountain, high in a sea without top or bottom: cold, sparse, immaculate, and almost silent. The ocean bears down with impossible weight. The towers endure without resistance. The rare creatures repeat their appointed passages. Roads preserve journeys that may never be taken.

And in the plazas between the lights, the darkness is not empty.

It is finished.
