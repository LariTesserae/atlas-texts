The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a long walk, though “occupies” is misleading. Nothing here stands wholly on the ground. The town is grafted into it.

The basin lies low among hills so ancient that they no longer resemble hills. They are broad mineral swells, worn almost to the level of the surrounding plain, their harder bones exposed in smooth shelves. Water has passed over them for an age beyond counting. It has rounded every edge, loosened every seam, and filled each hollow with black soil. Even the ruins—if any part of the town can properly be called ruin—have been abraded until their surfaces feel grown rather than cut.

Rain has just ended. It ends often and never decisively.

Warm water threads down the rock in transparent skins. It gathers beneath root mats and rises through the streets in clear, trembling beads, as though the basin were sweating. The air is thick enough to leave droplets on eyelashes. In shaded places, vapor condenses continuously and falls in a private drizzle from leaf to leaf. There are no rivers within the town, only seepage, overflow, and innumerable exchanges: gutters giving water to trunks, trunks giving it to cistern-blooms, cisterns breathing it back as mist.

From above, the settlement would look sparse: a scattering of tall green structures around a central wet clearing, separated by generous strips of marsh and open water. On the ground, it feels total. Vegetation is not merely abundant; it is the material through which all other things are expressed.

The streets are plaits of living root, pale where feet and rain have polished them. They flex under weight and slowly tighten themselves where fibers have frayed. Houses are hollowed from the swollen bases of columnar plants, each trunk coaxed over generations into chambers, stairs, ducts, sleeping niches, and damp vestibules. Their outer bark is dark green-brown and armored in hexagonal plates. Their inner walls are amber, warm to the touch, and faintly translucent. By daylight, silhouettes pass through rooms as softened shadows.

Between the larger dwellings stand smaller civic organisms. Water towers resemble sparse, long-legged flowers, their bowls opening to the sky after rain. Bridges are vines trained across gaps and induced to braid themselves into load-bearing cables. At night, lamps do not ignite. They ripen: clusters of clear fruit brighten gradually under the eaves, filled with captive radiance that has no measurable heat.

There is metal here, but little of it is visible. A dull silver vein sometimes emerges where bark has split, branching into capillaries before vanishing again beneath the tissue. Stone apertures appear in the marsh, low and circular, with surfaces too smooth for carving. They may be doors, vents, wells, or all three depending on the hour. The oldest infrastructure is buried below the roots, below the saturated clay, perhaps below the basin itself. It does not remain in one geometry long enough to map.

The people of the town say their machines are not under the earth but under *events*.

When a threshold is crossed, a machine is there. When a seed germinates, when two shadows overlap, when rainfall exceeds a particular rhythm, invisible mechanisms briefly become local. Distance folds to accommodate them. Matter accepts instructions. A broken bridge may heal before the branch that damaged it finishes falling; a room may acquire an extra alcove because someone inside urgently needs solitude. No one considers this miraculous. It is municipal service.

The old systems are extraordinarily advanced and only partly obedient.

Their interventions account for the town’s turbulence. The place is always being corrected toward several incompatible ideal states. One buried intelligence prefers the basin dry and geometrically ordered. Another appears devoted to biodiversity. A third continually rebuilds some vanished ceremonial city using available roots, mud, fungal threads, snail shells, memories, and rain.

Thus a plaza may wake beneath the marsh at dawn: six terraces of wet white stone lifting silently through duckweed, already veined with moss. By noon, trees have migrated into its joints. By afternoon, a colonnade has budded leaves, the stairs have softened into root, and bright amphibians occupy the drainage channels. During the night the entire structure may subside, leaving only concentric ripples and a ring of flowering reeds.

Construction crews work constantly, though they carry almost no tools. They prune probability. They tie colored cords around selected stems, sing measured intervals into cavities, release calibrated swarms of insects, and place bowls of rainwater where future walls should turn. In response, the town rearranges itself. Not always as requested.

This work is loud.

The basin amplifies sound despite its softness. Water drums on broad leaves. Frogs call from hidden cisterns with the depth and regularity of bells. Cicadas pulse in sheets, their chorus abruptly synchronizing, breaking apart, and reforming elsewhere. Rope-bridges creak like ships. Hollow houses groan as their trunks redistribute weight. Beneath everything runs the low, irregular thunder of the subsurface systems: a pressure felt in the ribs before it reaches the ear.

When a new structure is about to emerge, the ground begins to hum. Birds lift from the reeds all at once. Seedpods rattle. The wet paving swells and splits, and a framework rises—not assembled piece by piece, but arriving as a completed relation among things. Roots bend around empty volumes. Rain hangs briefly in the air to outline windows. A staircase extrudes from a trunk in one long spiral. Then gravity resumes its customary behavior and water strikes every surface at once.

The fauna have adapted not only to change but to anticipation.

Long-legged marsh birds nest on structures that do not yet exist. They pace through shallow pools, stabbing at translucent shrimp and soft-bodied insects. Their feathers are gray in ordinary light but flash green when the buried machinery activates. Small mammals with paddle-shaped feet live in wall cavities and move house whenever the rooms do. Lizards graze films of engineered algae from doorframes. Beetles carry luminous mites beneath their wing cases, exposing them when they need to signal across fog.

There are larger animals, though the town affords them only narrow corridors. Broad-backed herbivores wade through the reed belt beyond the outer houses, browsing architectural foliage without distinction between building and plant. Their passage can redirect a street. Nobody drives them away; several essential conduits germinate only after passing through their gut.

The most numerous inhabitants are almost too small to see. Clouds of pollinators occupy distinct civic roles, marked by scent rather than species. Some maintain door membranes. Some carry messages encoded in pollen. Some enter the pale flowers growing inside council chambers and alter their color according to the town’s water reserves. Others belong to no known system and periodically build huge, purposeless combs over windows, causing arguments and delight in equal measure.

There are rules to living here.

Nothing may be declared permanent. No doorway may be locked during heavy rain, because floodwater and displaced neighbors must be allowed to pass through. A new room belongs first to whoever dreams of it, second to whoever finds it, and only later to the household from whose wall it grew. Fruit bearing blue veins must not be eaten while thunder is audible underground. Any staircase with thirteen visible steps must be climbed backward; the number indicates that its destination has not finished attaching to local space.

Most importantly, no plant may be classified solely as infrastructure, crop, ornament, wilderness, or citizen. The categories have caused disasters before.

The town’s borders are clear but mobile. Beyond the outermost houses lies a belt of warm marsh crossed by old causeways. Beyond that, the nearly flattened country extends under grasses and isolated trees. Yet the settlement never spreads far. Every attempt at expansion is slowly drawn back toward the basin, as if the town is bound to a deep point rather than a surface location. Abandoned outlying structures uproot themselves over decades and inch homeward on mats of contracting fibers.

Age is everywhere, but not as decay. It appears as refinement through erosion. The oldest stones have lost their inscriptions and then lost the scars where the inscriptions were. Channels worn into them no longer show where water began or ended; they are simply the shapes water prefers. Ancient foundations sit below newer rootwork like smooth teeth. Some have been washed so long that they retain no tool marks, only a satin polish and shallow depressions where generations have stood.

The town archives its history in these depressions. Rain fills them. People read the patterns of vibration when the underground thunder comes. What is remembered is not names or dates, but former arrangements: a vanished bridge, a century when the central pool was red with fern spores, the season the houses all turned their entrances east, the brief appearance of a second moon reflected in water though never seen in the sky.

Ordinary daylight falls on all this. The strangeness does not hide behind darkness or spectacle. At midday, clouds open and the basin becomes almost domestic in its brightness. Wet leaves throw clear shadows. Steam lifts from root streets. Children chase amphibians around a newly emerged fountain. Workers argue over a vine that has interpreted a transit plan as a flowering instruction. Laundry hangs between trunks and becomes seeded with tiny epiphytes before it dries.

Then the air changes. Condensation thickens on the uphill sides of every surface. The birds fall silent for one breath.

Far below, something revises its understanding of the town.

A ripple travels through the marsh against the wind. Houses tighten their roots. Bridges lift slightly, like animals scenting weather. All across the basin, flowers open out of season and turn toward an empty patch of water.

The humming begins.

People gather on the living paths, calling to one another over frogs, rain, wings, cracking bark, and the mounting subterranean roar. No one knows whether the basin is about to receive a tower, a forest, a railway, a forgotten district, or a form for which the town has no word.

The water rises into a smooth dome.

Green shoots appear inside it.

And before the new thing has even broken the surface, birds are already bringing twigs.
