The plain has no ground beneath it.

What appears to be ground is the uppermost surface of a constructed mass extending downward for hundreds of kilometers: laminations of ceramic, carbon foam, obsolete circuitry, sealed chambers, load-bearing lattices, and materials for which the old engineering names have been lost. There is no soil, no bedrock, no buried river. Drill anywhere and the same fact repeats at finer scales. Every grain is manufactured. Every horizon is an artifact.

From a distance, the surface looks like a warm white desert. It is nearly level, gently descending over continental distances toward depressions too shallow to see. Ancient erosion has softened everything. Towers have been reduced to low, rounded stumps; walls have become parallel swells; immense machinery lies under pale abrasion crusts like bones beneath skin. The oldest exposed plates are polished almost featureless. Their seams wander toward the horizon in curves so broad they can be mistaken for natural geography.

But nothing here is natural, unless age itself counts as nature.

The light is relentless. A broad luminous canopy occupies most of the sky, though it is not a sky in the planetary sense. It is an orbital sheet—or perhaps the underside of another level of the construction—so high that its curvature is perceptible only at dusk. Millions of radiant cells keep the land under a white-gold glare. Some cells have dimmed to amber, and a few are black, making small geometric constellations overhead. The canopy does not rise or set. Instead, illumination migrates through it in slow belts, producing long warm days and brief silver intervals that serve as night.

At brightest phase the pale surface becomes difficult to look at. Heat trembles above the plates. Fine dust levitates in electrostatic veils, not enough to obscure the view but enough to give distant structures the softness of mirages. Temperatures are warm rather than lethal, regulated by systems so old and distributed that no single failure can alter the climate. Heat sinks wake beneath the plain in sequence. Their operation is felt as a faint warmth moving underfoot, then as a coolness spreading outward in enormous circles.

There is almost no water. What little hydrogen survives is locked into polymers, crystalline hydrates, sealed reservoirs, and living tissue. No rain falls. No dew forms. The local weather is made of dust, charge, heat, and gas.

During the long bright period, the surface releases a thin metallic breath: argon, nitrogen, carbon compounds, traces of refrigerant and machine-made volatiles. As the light shifts, these gases travel toward cooling zones. Vast, slow winds develop across the plain, yet they make surprisingly little sound. The atmosphere is dense enough to carry noise well, but there are few loose things to strike together. Wind slips over rounded surfaces with a whisper like cloth drawn across stone.

The quiet gives scale to everything.

A traveler may hear their own footfalls transmitted through the plates for seconds after stopping. Far below, structural compensators answer one another with rare, soft knocks. Once every few hours, there is a deep tone at the edge of hearing as the buried mass alters its internal stresses. The tone does not seem loud, but it can be felt in teeth, joints, and the casings of instruments. Then silence returns, vast and bright.

The plain is densely occupied, though at first it appears empty. Its inhabitants are mostly not mobile creatures. Every square meter contains innumerable presences: sensor films, repair colonies, memory substrates, thermal valves, dormant tools, sealed processors, and microscopic machines moving through capillaries in the material. The apparent desert is crowded all the way down.

The surface crust is made of interlocking hexagonal scales, each several handspans wide. They expand and contract independently, maintaining the plain’s level despite the movements below. Along their edges grows a dark red fuzz. This is one of the few organisms visible without magnification: a dry colonial life-form that feeds on electrical gradients and airborne carbon. It requires no liquid phase. Its cells contain conductive salts suspended in wax, and information passes through the colony as changes in charge rather than chemistry in solution.

In sheltered seams the red growth thickens into wiry tufts. These stand no higher than an ankle. Under intense light they open black, foil-like vanes and cast extraordinarily sharp shadows. During cooling intervals they fold flat, becoming nearly indistinguishable from rust. Their roots do not penetrate in search of moisture; they connect to old maintenance contacts. A whole field may therefore be less a meadow than a patient act of trespass upon the power grid.

Elsewhere, pale mineral fans spread over the plates. They resemble lichen, but are partly fabricated and partly alive. Their branching forms separate atmospheric molecules, retaining carbon and rare metals, while exhaling inert gas. Some are remnants of the original environmental system. Others have diverged over geological ages into things their makers did not intend. There is no clear boundary between ecology and malfunction.

The largest landmarks are the Processional Ridges.

They cross the plain in lines hundreds of kilometers long, never more than thirty meters high. At close range they resolve into the worn tops of buried buildings packed almost edge to edge. Domes, conduits, transit halls, archives, furnaces, and habitation cells have been compressed beneath later additions until only their upper contours remain. Erosion has joined them into continuous low hills. Their exposed material is dark and glassy, riddled with shallow cavities where components were removed by repair swarms long ago.

These ridges create the nearest thing to habitat. Their shaded sides support clumps of charge-feeding growth. Warm gas escapes through hairline vents. In some cavities, membranes span the openings and pulse almost imperceptibly, filtering the exhalations from below. Tiny jointed animals graze there.

Animal life is rare enough that every encounter feels consequential.

Most creatures are no larger than a finger. They have six or eight legs, reflective shells, and slow metabolisms. They spend years in torpor, waking when a particular vent opens or an electrical field changes. Some scrape the red colonies from seams. Others pierce cable insulation and drink plasticizers. A translucent glider occasionally lifts into the thermal currents, its body a narrow knot between broad conductive wings. It does not fly by muscle alone; it adjusts its charge and is carried along the field gradients above the plain.

Larger animals are almost legendary. There are reports of long, low forms crossing the distant glare, stopping at maintenance wells before vanishing into the heat haze. Their tracks, when found, are precise rows of melted circles. Whether these are fauna, autonomous machines, or mobile portions of the landscape is unresolved. Here, classification has become a historical preference rather than a reliable truth.

Nothing is young.

Even the active systems have been operating for spans that make their original purposes irrelevant. Repair mechanisms maintain the environment, but not according to a master plan. They follow local rules, negotiating among themselves through buried networks. One district preserves level surfaces. Another favors heat dissipation. Another reconstructs whatever pattern existed when its memory last stabilized, even if that pattern was a ruin. Where their jurisdictions overlap, strange compromises emerge: ramps leading into sealed walls, doors repeated at diminishing scales, fields of identical vents that open onto solid material.

The landscape is active in a slow, continuous way. Plates turn fractions of a degree to follow the moving light. Dust is gathered into narrow silver drifts, then dismantled atom by atom. Buried cavities migrate as structural material is borrowed from one region and deposited in another. A ridge may lower by a meter over a century while a shallow basin rises elsewhere. To a human observer the plain seems fixed; to the plain, it is flowing.

At intervals, the surface performs renewal.

First the red seam-growth folds shut. Then the overhead light sharpens, becoming colorless. A line appears on the horizon: not a storm cloud, but a vertical discontinuity in the air. It advances without thunder. Ahead of it, dust lifts and hangs motionless. Behind it, every plate has been cleaned, inspected, and subtly altered.

The renewal front moves at walking speed. Microscopic machines saturate the air within it, bright as powdered glass. They remove damaged material, reseal joints, cull invasive colonies, and replace sensor layers. Their passage produces almost no sound beyond a faint dry hiss. Small animals flee along the seams. Plants disconnect themselves from the grid and become inert knots. Anything unfamiliar is examined.

The safest response is stillness.

The front does not attack, but it repairs according to definitions older than the current world. A traveler moving at the wrong moment may be identified as debris, structural damage, unregistered equipment, or—in rare favorable cases—a temporary maintenance agent. After the front passes, footprints are gone. Cracks are closed. Campsites become smooth white plate. Sometimes objects left outside are returned as flawless copies made of the wrong substance.

Beyond the Processional Ridges lies the Mirror Reach, a basin several million square kilometers in area and only a few meters below the surrounding plain. Its surface is not reflective in the usual sense. It records incident light and releases it later. During bright phases it appears matte black, swallowing the canopy. During the silver interval it shines with stored noon, turning the underside of passing dust into gold.

The Reach creates its own climate. Heat released from its surface drives broad convection cells. Static storms migrate around its perimeter, visible as curtains of blue sparks no taller than grass. There is no thunder. Charge flows silently through the constructed ground, awakening distant grids of buried lights. From above, if anything still watches from above, entire countries must flare in response.

At the center of the basin stands a low circular structure called the Mouth, though it has never been seen to open. It is two hundred kilometers across and rises scarcely a meter from the surrounding surface. Concentric bands of unfamiliar material surround it, each etched with patterns too fine to resolve. The bands rotate at different rates. Some take minutes to complete a revolution; others may not have moved since recorded observation began.

The sparse life avoids the innermost rings. Dust avoids them too. Even heat shimmer becomes orderly there, dividing into straight vertical columns.

It is said that the Mouth is not a door into the world below, but an instrument for changing what “below” means. The claim cannot be proved. Instruments brought near it disagree on distance, mass, and the direction of gravity by small but persistent amounts. The disagreements remain manageable, almost polite. This is not a landscape of violent impossibilities. Its strangeness is infrastructural: embedded, regulated, made dependable through immense age.

Near the far horizon, vertical features finally interrupt the flatness. They look like towers, but as one approaches, perspective refuses to bring them nearer. These are the Vanes, thin structures extending from the surface to the luminous canopy. They partition atmospheric circulation over continental distances. Their faces are covered with apertures, balconies, heat exchangers, and dark cavities. Most are inaccessible; their lower entrances have been buried by millennia of resurfacing.

Warm winds circle them continuously. The winds carry flakes of old coatings and the dry scent of hot stone, ozone, resin, and faintly sweet machine oils. At certain light phases the Vanes align with the canopy and cast shadows thousands of kilometers long. For a few minutes the plain enters a narrow, moving twilight. Red colonies unfold. Small grazers emerge. Gliders rise in sudden numbers, filling the shadow with silent reflective wings.

Then the brightness returns, and the landscape appears empty again.

There are rules here, though they are inferred rather than posted.

Do not cut deeply into the surface; lower layers may interpret exposure as catastrophic damage.

Do not follow a seam that remains perfectly straight beyond the horizon; such seams are usually active boundaries, and they may close.

Do not shelter beneath a newly opened vent, regardless of how cool the air feels.

When buried tones sound in groups of three, leave all elevated structures.

If the ground begins reflecting a sky different from the one overhead, remain on the darker plates.

Never assume a ruin is inactive because it is old. Age here is evidence of successful operation.

Above all, do not mistake silence for absence.

The plain is listening through trillions of small devices. It measures pressure, heat, chemical traces, mass distribution, shadow, electrical intent. It does not appear conscious in any simple way, yet it responds with the coherence of something that has had ages to consider every recurring event. A path becomes smoother after repeated travel. A favored resting place gradually acquires shade. Waste disappears. Heat is carried away. If travelers remain for years, the landscape may begin building around them—not houses exactly, but accommodations derived from their habits, anatomies, and equipment.

No one knows whether this is hospitality, assimilation, or routine maintenance.

Seen at continental scale, the whole region is less a desert than the exposed skin of a device that has outlived its operators and made an ecology from the continuation of its duties. It is worn nearly flat, full to bursting, bright, warm, and ceaselessly self-correcting. Nothing grows tall because the renewal fronts shear it down. Nothing flows because there is no free water. Nothing calls because the few animals communicate through vibration and charge.

Still, the place is not dead.

Under every step, innumerable mechanisms exchange material. Along every seam, dark life waits for shadow. Heat crosses the plain in invisible tides. Ancient structures settle by microns. The canopy slowly rearranges its stars.

And in the deepest quiet, when the silver interval passes over the low ridges, the entire fabricated continent can sometimes be heard making one nearly inaudible sound: a long exhalation, as if an immeasurably old machine were resting without ever stopping.
