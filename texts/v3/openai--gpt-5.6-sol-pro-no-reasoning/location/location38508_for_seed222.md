The plateau lies above most weather.

From the lower country it appears as a pale shelf suspended in heat, its rim scalloped by erosion into bastions, alcoves, and long sloping aprons of rubble. Up here, however, it is not flat. It is a broad, weathered region of shallow basins and exposed ridges, large enough that distant features fade into the white brightness before reaching the horizon. Old waterways have divided the stone into smooth-backed islands. Valleys open, narrow, and open again. Nothing looks freshly broken. Every wall has been softened, every fallen slab seated into the dust as if the land completed its collapse long ago and has since learned to live in that position.

The stone is warm ocher beneath a thin mineral pallor. In places its surface has been glazed dark by centuries of condensation, making black terraces that shine like cooled wax. Elsewhere the ground is granular and rose-colored, packed hard except where burrowing animals have raised small fans of powder. Pale seams cross the plateau in curves too regular to be geological, though they vanish under scree, emerge on another ridge, and continue without concern for intervening distance.

Water is scarce but seldom entirely absent. It does not form broad lakes or lasting streams. Moisture gathers in protected places: under overhangs, at the bottom of shaded fissures, inside deep circular wells in the rock. Dawn leaves a film of condensation on the black terraces. The film migrates downslope in silver threads no wider than roots, feeding chains of tiny pools before the sun takes it. Every few weeks, warm rain passes over in isolated columns. One basin may flood while the adjacent ridge remains bright and dry. The resulting streams run briefly through ancient channels, carrying seed husks and insects, then sink through porous stone before noon.

There is a river, though only intermittently in the ordinary sense. Its bed crosses the plateau from northeast to southwest: a wide, sinuous corridor of polished gravel with stands of copper grass along its lowest line. Most days it is empty. Yet below the gravel, water can be heard by placing an ear to certain stones. During rain, the river surfaces in discontinuous segments. Water appears in one reach, vanishes, and reappears kilometers away, sometimes flowing in the opposite direction. The animals accept this without confusion.

Vegetation keeps to widely spaced arrangements. Thorn trees grow singly on high ground, their crowns flattened by hot winds and their trunks encased in a chalky bark. Mats of blue-gray succulents occupy cracks that receive morning shade. Along the buried river, reedlike plants stand in small colonies separated by hundreds of meters of bare gravel. Their stems are metallic green and hollow. When moisture rises beneath them, they lean together, forming temporary arches over the driest part of the channel.

Most plants spend much of the year withdrawn. Bulbs lie deep in the sediment; lichens close their pores; brittle shrubs shed all but a few silver leaves. After rainfall the plateau changes rapidly but not extravagantly. Fine red filaments emerge between stones. Flowers open at ground level, small and intensely colored, and are pollinated within hours. By the second day, winged seeds are traveling on thermal currents. By the fourth, the apparent bloom is over, though its work continues underground.

The brightness is severe. Sunlight comes from a small white sun and from the sky itself, which has a faint pearlescent luminosity even where no cloud is visible. Shadows are sharp at noon but subtly doubled: a dark central shape bordered by a weaker gray echo displaced toward the east. Objects left in one place long enough develop corresponding double weathering. The western sides bleach; the eastern edges accumulate thin, translucent mineral fins. On old stone these fins overlap like scales.

Despite the sparse growth, animals are abundant. Most are small or distant, so the first impression is still one of emptiness. Dust-colored grazers move between basins in loose files, each animal several body lengths from the next. They have six narrow legs and carry their mass low, their backs covered in flexible plates that lift slightly to release heat. From afar their passage resembles loose stones flowing across the ground.

Flocks of black, membrane-winged creatures circle above the cliffs without calling. They feed on insects drawn upward by thermals and descend at dusk to drink condensation from rock faces. Their shadows pass frequently over the plateau, but the creatures themselves are often lost in the glare.

The pools have their own concentrated ecologies. Transparent swimmers lie dormant in dried mineral crust, revive within minutes of wetting, and complete entire generations before the water disappears. Long-legged hunters arrive from cracks in the stone. Pale amphibious things, no larger than a thumb, climb out at night and adhere to the underside of overhangs, where they absorb moisture directly through their skin. Larger predators know the sequence. They wait beside basins that have not yet filled.

The largest native animals are rarely seen whole. They travel within the cliff systems, passing through cavities opened by erosion. At times a ridge emits a low vibration and loose grains begin to hop. Then a segmented head may appear far down a ravine, followed much later by another portion of the same body at a different opening. Whether these are immense burrowers or colonies behaving as single animals is unresolved. They leave smooth, oval apertures and piles of stone sorted precisely by size.

The plateau is quiet enough that movement is usually first perceived visually. Wind seldom roars; it presses steadily through the high passes, making only a dry whisper in the copper grass. Wings produce soft snaps. Gravel ticks as it cools. During the hottest hours even these diminish. The broad silence is punctuated at irregular intervals by a single clear tone issuing from somewhere beneath the ground.

The tone belongs, perhaps, to the structures.

They are scattered across the region with no visible road between them. Some resemble narrow towers sunken halfway into the rock; others are low rings, roofless chambers, or dark planes standing upright without support. They are neither ruined nor maintained in any familiar way. Their surfaces do not collect dust. Erosion bends around them. Rain striking one of the planes disappears without splashing, then falls upward in beads from another structure many kilometers away.

One tower stands near the buried river. It is only twelve meters high, made of a substance that looks alternately like bone, ceramic, and a vertical slice of bright sky. Its doorway opens when a warm-blooded animal approaches, although nothing on the plateau enters willingly. Inside, the air is cool and smells faintly of wet stone. The chamber is larger than the exterior permits. Its walls show a moving relief of the plateau—not as it is, but as an accumulation of all its recent states: flooded channels laid over dry ones, migrating herds crossing their own paths, storms flowering and collapsing in translucent layers.

The structures appear to manage no obvious settlement. There are no cities, cultivated fields, landing grounds, or power conduits. Yet the plateau bears signs of continuous and incomprehensibly exact intervention. Condensation forms preferentially where animals will pass. Pools persist just long enough for their brief ecologies to reproduce. Landslides stop at stable angles with improbable consistency. A species nearing exhaustion finds a new shaded fissure opened in its range. The system does not prevent death, drought, predation, or local extinction. It seems instead to maintain certain proportions—emptiness to abundance, thirst to water, exposure to refuge.

The pale seams in the ground are part of this arrangement. At midday they are inert, almost indistinguishable from quartz. Near evening, they begin transmitting subtle changes. A seam beneath a ridge warms; insects rise from crevices; winged hunters alter course. Far away, reeds bow over the riverbed though there is no wind. Moisture then condenses inside a shaded basin, and grazing animals start toward it before any scent could have reached them.

No single event is dramatic. The plateau operates through nudges.

Its weather is likewise active but restrained. Heat builds during the day, lifting invisible columns from the stone. Clouds sometimes form directly above the highest ridges, small and dense, dragging violet shadows behind them. Rain falls from only one edge. Lightning, when it occurs, travels horizontally through the cloud and then enters the ground without thunder. Hours afterward, black terraces exhale cool mist. The mist creeps close to the surface, pooling in hollows and carrying spores, tiny animals, and mineral dust from one isolated habitat to another.

At night the warmth lingers in the rock. The sky darkens only to deep blue, and the luminous horizon outlines every ridge. Plants uncurl. Burrows open. The plateau’s apparent emptiness becomes busy with cautious, economical life: plate-backed herds kneeling to scrape lichen, translucent swimmers stirring in hidden wells, silent fliers clinging to vertical stone, predators following old drainage lines.

The structures become less visible after sunset, not more. Their edges lose contrast until they seem to have been cut out of the world. Sometimes one can see through such an absence to a landscape that does not match the ground behind it: the same plateau under a broader river, perhaps, or beneath unfamiliar stars; the same thorn trees grown into forests; the same eroded ridges before they were worn low. These vistas do not remain stable if approached.

Travelers learn several rules.

Water found in natural stone is generally safe. Water resting on a seamless surface should not be touched, because it may still be falling elsewhere.

Never cross a pale seam while its two sides cast different shadows.

If the underground tone sounds twice, leave the riverbed. If it sounds three times, enter it.

Do not follow the plate-backed herds to water; watch instead where the circling fliers refuse to cast shadows.

And if rain begins to rise from the ground, remain still. The ascending drops are carrying a record of everything they touch. Movement confuses the record, and the plateau corrects confusion with great precision.

By morning, the strange workings recede into glare. The towers become pale marks. The seams become stone. Animal tracks cross the open basins and fade beneath slow-moving dust. Once again the place seems merely high, warm, ancient, and sparsely alive.

But there is moisture beneath it, motion within it, and attention distributed everywhere. The plateau is not a machine disguised as a landscape, nor a wilderness modified by machines. It is something older in its equilibrium and newer in its means: a watershed that remembers its possible rivers, an ecosystem whose absences are tended, a bright and weathered country quietly engineering the conditions of its own continuation.
