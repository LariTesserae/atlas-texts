The highlands rise beneath a darkness that is not quite night.

There is no sun here, only a dim atmospheric glow spread behind miles of warm cloud: charcoal shading into bruised violet, sometimes paling along the horizon where distant electrical storms move beyond sight. The land receives perhaps one part in twenty of ordinary daylight. Nothing casts a clean shadow. Depth is read instead by temperature, by echo, and by the slow appearance of ridgelines as the eyes adjust.

It is a vast country of weathered plateaus and long descending shelves. Seen from above—if there were anywhere above from which to see it—it would resemble an ancient piece of hammered metal: raised bosses, shallow basins, overlapping fractures, and broad channels worn smooth by rivers that do not presently exist. The stone is dark, iron-rich, and warm to the touch. Its exposed surfaces have been abraded for so long that cliffs rarely end in rubble. They lean into the valleys as rounded walls, fluted and black, settled into shapes that seem less broken than completed.

The elevation is considerable, though the warmth disguises it. Air moves thinly across the upper shelves. Breathing creatures have deep chests, slow pulses, or systems of internal bellows. Far below, unseen depressions gather denser air and become hot inland atmospheres of their own. The largest plateau is broad enough to make weather: cloud is born along one edge, dragged across several hundred miles of upland, and dissolved before reaching the other.

Water comes intermittently and from several directions.

Rain seldom falls as a general storm. Moisture condenses against the stone during the darkest hours, laying a skin of droplets over whole escarpments. The droplets join in grooves and descend through old drainage lines, making threads of water that may run for an hour before vanishing into gravel. In deep channels, pockets persist beneath overhangs: warm black pools with mineral films, crowded shorelines, and depths disproportionate to their visible size. Some connect through fissures to reservoirs far inside the plateau. Their levels pulse without warning. A pool may sink by half a body-length in silence, then rise days later, steaming faintly.

When true rain comes, it comes locally and hard. A single basin may receive a deluge while the neighboring ridge remains dry. The ancient channels fill almost at once. Water races across polished rock in low, gleaming sheets, carrying little sediment because most loose material was washed away ages ago. The floods make a deep vibration rather than a splash, and the vibration travels through the plateaus for great distances. Animals feel it through their feet and begin migrating toward water long before the first flow arrives.

Vegetation is sparse but not fragile. It occupies favorable lines with fierce precision.

Along condensation grooves grow black-green mats that open like velvet when wet and contract to thin scales during dry intervals. Pale spindle shrubs stand many body-lengths apart, each rooted in a crack and surrounded by an empty circle where its underground fibers have taken nearly all available moisture. Their leaves are narrow, metallic on one side and almost perfectly black on the other. They turn slowly over the course of a day, gathering dim light and then shedding condensed water toward the roots.

On sheltered walls hang loose colonies of ribbon growth. These are neither quite vines nor fungi. In dry air they become stiff, tangled wires; after rain they soften and lower translucent fronds into the runoff. Whole cliff faces may briefly acquire a faint amber sheen as these colonies feed, then darken again by the following cycle.

There are no forests. From any height, most of the landscape appears bare. Life reveals itself only when approached: a green seam in a fracture, a crust beneath a ledge, a field of low breathing cups spread over a salt flat with twenty paces between each organism. Emptiness is not absence here. It is territory.

Animals are much more abundant than the vegetation suggests.

The first impression is of a nearly lifeless upland because most creatures remain still and dark while exposed. Then one becomes aware of eyes reflecting from cracks, of minute movements over the stone, of the steady displacement of bodies through the gloom. Thousands of small grazers travel the moisture lines. They have low, broad forms and many padded feet, and they scrape the renewing films from rock without damaging the deeper layers. Their shells are matte and porous; at night, condensation gathers on them and runs toward the mouth.

Larger herds cross the plateaus in widely spaced files. Each animal walks far behind the next, so that a herd of several hundred may extend from horizon to horizon and still seem sparse. They are tall at the shoulder, narrow-bodied, and almost noiseless. Heat radiates from pleated organs along their flanks. These folds open when the air cools, admitting swarms of symbiotic flyers that clean them and feed on salts from their skin.

Predators follow at a distance measured in days. They do not often chase. Instead, they know the drainage country intimately and take positions near pools before the prey arrive. Some flatten themselves into warm ledges, changing the texture of their hides until even grazing animals step across them. Others hunt by producing a subsonic tremor resembling distant floodwater. Thirsty creatures turn toward it. The deception works only in dry periods; during rain, the land itself becomes too resonant and confused.

The air also carries fauna. Broad, nearly transparent creatures drift on updrafts along the escarpments, their bodies supported by warm internal gas and slow muscular fins. They descend during condensation, combing droplets from the cloud. From the ground they resemble moving gaps in the sky. Occasionally one settles over a pool and folds itself down, layer after layer, until a shape the width of a valley occupies no more space than a crouching animal.

Despite such abundance, the country is mostly quiet. Sound is expensive in thin air, and many species communicate through stone. Feet tap coded patterns into bedrock. Burrowing animals grind their armored throats against cavity walls. Herds coordinate by synchronized pauses, sending faint pressure changes through the ground. To an unadapted listener there is only wind, an occasional scrape, and the remote murmur of runoff. With an ear against the plateau, the apparent silence becomes densely inhabited: pulses, drumming, resonant clicks, and the slow rolling signatures of animals too distant to see.

There are no roads, towers, walls, ruins, or tools. Nothing has been assembled. Yet the landscape is industrial.

The industry belongs to the geology and the organisms that entered into partnership with it.

Deep beneath the high plateaus, mineral heat drives enormous natural engines. Water descending through fractures flashes into vapor and forces its way through chambers lined with crystalline valves. Columns of rock lift by fractions, seal, then drop. Metallic ores precipitate in branching conduits. Electrical charge accumulates across dry strata until buried seams discharge in long, measured arcs. The whole plateau functions as a pressure machine whose cycles may last minutes in one region and centuries in another.

Life has learned to use these systems. Root colonies extend conductive filaments into ore seams, diverting small currents toward the surface. In some valleys, millions of separate organisms form grids without central design. When moisture reaches them, the grids awaken. Dark plains flicker with low blue corona. Mineral compounds are separated, concentrated, and carried outward in the bodies of insects. Vent creatures refine sulfur and iron inside layered stomachs, leaving behind regular cakes of nearly pure material—not artifacts, though they can look manufactured.

There are places where the process reaches immense scale. In the central rift, a line of geothermal throats extends for several hundred miles. Each throat inhales cool air and releases it warm, enriched with vapor and metallic dust. Their cycles gradually synchronize. One begins to pulse, then another, until the rift breathes like a bank of engines. The ground hums. Clouds form overhead in straight, dark bands. Condensation falls back as warm rain, feeding a narrow ecosystem assembled around the machine-like rhythm.

Nothing built these engines. No one maintains them. They are as old and equilibrated as rivers: industrial not because they were designed, but because repetition, pressure, heat, and selection have made them efficient.

The oldest surfaces bear evidence of this balance. Erosion proceeds, but without haste. Dust blown from one shelf is caught by living crusts on another. Floods deepen channels by imperceptible degrees. Cliffs exfoliate in broad curved plates, exposing fresh black stone that may remain untouched for thousands of years before the first film colonizes it. Even collapse seems to belong to a mature cycle. When an overhang falls, burrowers arrive to open the new fractures; moisture follows; grazers follow moisture; predators follow grazers.

At rare intervals, the buried systems enter a more vigorous phase. A whole province warms. Pools begin to rise. Conductive plants brighten at their tips, not with flame but with accumulated charge. The flying animals leave the cliffs and gather over the uplands. Herds stop their migrations and face into the ground’s vibration.

Then the plateau releases its stored electricity.

There is no thunder at first. Lines of blue-white fire travel horizontally through the stone, visible in cracks for hundreds of miles. A moment later, cloud and land exchange charge. The horizon flashes, revealing the immense arrangement all at once: successive tablelands, fog-filled chasms, wandering herds, distant curtains of rain, and airborne bodies suspended like translucent sails.

Darkness closes again.

For several days afterward, warm showers move across the heights. Pools overflow into their ancient spillways. Spindle shrubs unfurl seed membranes and let them skate over wet rock. Small animals emerge in numbers impossible to reconcile with the previous emptiness. The quiet plains ripple with feeding, mating, predation, and migration.

Then moisture withdraws into fissures. The engines slow. The animals disperse over distances vast enough to hide their abundance. Plants contract into seams and knots. The uplands return to their first appearance: dark, warm, nearly bare, without any sign that they are working.

But beneath the surface, the valves continue to open and close. Water descends. Minerals gather. Roots conduct their faint current. Far-separated feet speak through the stone.

The whole high country waits between pulses, not dormant, but listening to itself.
