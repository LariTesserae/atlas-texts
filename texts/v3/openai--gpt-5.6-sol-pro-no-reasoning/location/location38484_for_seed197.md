The city occupies a shallow basin no wider than a grove. From the surrounding flats it looks densely green, almost solid—a low mound of leaves under warm daylight—but inside it is astonishingly hollow. Its avenues are broad chambers of air. Its buildings stand far apart. Nearly everything that appears to be construction is alive.

The ground is a pavement of roots.

They have grown together over centuries into smooth, interlocking plates: pale where traffic polishes them, black and soft in the seams, lifted here and there into shallow stairs. No tool laid these streets. The roots arranged themselves according to pressures now too old to identify, tightening around courtyards and dividing the basin into wards. Some streets end in sealed knots. Others split when approached, the paving roots slowly uncurling to reveal a passage that did not exist the previous day.

The buildings are hollow plants, or communities of plants agreeing temporarily to resemble buildings. Tower-trunks swell from the pavement, open inside, their walls braided from ribs of living wood. Broad leaves overlap as roofs. Windows are pores that dilate with heat. Doors are muscular curtains of tendrils, admitting bodies of the correct warmth and scent. Several structures share one buried organism; an injury to a house on the eastern side may cause sap to bead from walls three streets away.

Nothing rises very high. The oldest towers barely clear the canopy around the basin. Yet their interiors do not obey this modest exterior. A narrow trunk may contain a vaulted chamber too broad to fit inside it, empty except for pale aerial roots descending from an invisible ceiling. Looking back through the entrance does not always show the street one left. Sometimes it shows rain falling in another district. Sometimes it shows the same doorway from behind.

The vegetation is not decoration, nor even wilderness reclaiming a settlement. It is the settlement’s material and its civic order. Hedges partition neighborhoods. Vines carry tension between towers like suspension cables. Thick, translucent creepers conduct water along elevated channels, their pulsing visible through green skin. Cup-shaped leaves gather the intermittent rain and pass it downward from lip to lip. Reservoirs lie inside swollen bulbs under plazas, where warm, tannin-dark water rocks with the slow contractions of the city’s roots.

Rain comes seldom but decisively. Before it arrives, condensation pearls on every shaded surface. The streets become slick. Leaves turn their undersides upward, whitening the entire city in a coordinated gesture. Then warm rain falls in heavy, vertical cords for minutes at a time. Gutters unfold from branches. Bark valves open. Dry cisterns begin swallowing so loudly that the paving trembles.

Afterward, water remains only in pockets: in root hollows, flower throats, the cupped backs of enormous ground leaves. The basin beyond the city is mostly dry, and the city hoards moisture with animal intensity. By late afternoon, nearly every exposed puddle has been drunk downward. Mist breathes from vents along the streets, briefly making the low quarters humid and indistinct. At night the mist condenses on hanging moss and falls again indoors as a private, localized rain.

The place is deafening.

Sound is not an occasional event here but a physical medium. Cicadas packed into the roofs produce a continuous metallic pressure. Frogs boom from the hidden reservoirs. Flocks of narrow-winged birds erupt from wall cavities with a tearing roar. Seeds detonate when ripe, striking neighboring trunks like thrown stones. Broad leaves clap together in the basin’s warm crosswinds. Somewhere beneath the pavement, roots grind against one another with the prolonged groan of ships.

The loudest sound comes at midday, when the city flowers.

Thousands of trumpet blossoms open almost simultaneously. Their unfurling releases compressed air through ribbed throats, producing sustained notes: bass murmurs from plaza flowers, piercing whistles from vines, and vast wavering chords from the canopy. Pollinating animals navigate by these tones. The air fills with beetles, moths, bats that should not be awake, and soft-bodied gliders whose translucent skins vibrate sympathetically. For perhaps an hour the city becomes one continuous organ, too loud for speech, thought, or stable distances.

During this flowering, space misbehaves most severely. A sound may arrive before the blossom opens. Echoes take visible form as tremors traveling across leaves. Two creatures calling from opposite ends of a street may find themselves suddenly side by side, while animals separated by a handspan can remain unreachable for days. Distance follows resonance rather than geometry. Places sounding the same are near each other, regardless of where they stand.

This is the city’s fundamental rule: resemblance creates adjacency.

Two courtyards that bloom in the same pattern may merge along one wall. A staircase shaped like a spiral may lead into any other spiral in the basin—a snail shell, a curled fern, the interior of a sleeping animal’s ear. Pools reflecting the same patch of sky exchange their water and occasionally their inhabitants. Predators exploit this. Small jade-colored hunters flatten themselves into leaflike forms, becoming adjacent to every similar leaf at once; they disappear from one branch and erupt from another with prey already in their mouths.

The prey have their own evasions. Herds of many-legged grazers move through the open avenues, cropping soft root growth while constantly altering their outlines. Their skins ripple through stripes, spots, false eyes, and bark patterns so rapidly that the world cannot decide what resembles them. When threatened, a herd becomes visually incoherent and scatters across several incompatible locations. Later its members condense around salt licks, sometimes with duplicate limbs or memories of streets they never crossed.

Animal life occupies nearly every surface, though the city still feels empty. This is partly because most creatures are tiny and partly because the spaces between living structures are disproportionately large. Termites maintain galleries in deadwood scars. Nectar mice nest inside flowers, yellow with pollen. Ribbon snakes hang between buildings like loose cables. Flat amphibians cling to damp walls and drink condensation through their bellies. Pale fish swim in the city’s vascular canals, visible for a moment through the translucent water-vines before vanishing into trunks.

Larger animals appear singly. A long-necked browser sometimes enters the central square and remains there for hours, feeding from upper balconies. Its footfalls cause doors to close throughout the district. Great shelled beasts sleep beneath vacant arcades, mistaken for root mounds until the rain wakes them. Above the canopy, broad-winged scavengers circle in the hot light, each shadow accompanied by a second shadow moving in the wrong direction.

There are signs of habitation, but not of tools.

Nests line the tower interiors in orderly tiers. Fruit husks are stacked beside entrances. Certain plazas are swept clean by colonies of fan-tailed animals. Paths are maintained by repeated passage, and sleeping chambers are padded with shed fibers selected for softness. Yet no stone has been cut, no metal smelted, no branch deliberately severed. The inhabitants guide growth through touch, grazing, scent, song, and patience. A wall is commissioned by sleeping beside a sapling for several generations. A bridge is made by persuading two canopies to dream of one another.

This gives the settlement an urban order without an architect. Traffic follows flowering schedules. Districts are defined by pollen. Waste is carried to fungal pits where it becomes heat and soil. Public chambers open when enough bodies gather outside them; private chambers seal around the scent of whoever sleeps within. The oldest structures enforce customs of their own. A certain avenue refuses animals carrying fresh blood. A plaza folds shut at dusk unless occupied. One tower admits only creatures that have been rained on.

Erosion has shaped everything into settled forms. Rain grooves run deep along the great trunks, their edges rounded and moss-dark. Generations of claws have polished the root stairs. Dead towers slump gradually into bridges, gardens, and nurseries rather than collapsing. Nothing here looks pristine. Every surface bears long negotiation: tooth marks healed over, abandoned nests swallowed by bark, old flood lines high on interior walls, parasitic vines incorporated into load-bearing arches.

Even the impossibilities feel weathered.

A doorway opening onto itself has worn a circular track in the floor from centuries of animals entering and returning. An inverted courtyard, where rain rises from the ground into clouds of roots, has mineral rings showing the height of past upward floods. In the western ward, gravity leans toward a vast old fig. Loose seeds roll uphill to collect around its trunk. Generations of creatures have adapted; their limbs are asymmetrical, stronger on the side facing away from the tree.

Nothing is stable, but everything is established.

At evening, the heat loosens. The great flowering chord descends into scattered calls, wingbeats, croaks, and the endless shrill machinery of insects. Roof leaves rotate to shed the day’s stored warmth. Predators emerge from wall seams. Root avenues flex as underground reservoirs contract, and entire buildings shift a few inches closer to moisture.

In the central plaza stands the emptiest structure: a ring of trunks surrounding open air. No roof covers it. No floor crosses its interior. Seen from outside, it is merely a quiet courtyard. Stepping through the trunks reveals a vertical expanse of sunlit atmosphere, apparently miles deep, full of drifting seeds and distant airborne forests. The roar there is immense—wind, wings, thunder, oceans that cannot exist in the basin. Warm droplets rise through the abyss and vanish overhead.

The city’s animals gather at its edge but rarely enter. They drink condensation from the threshold and call into the depth. Their voices return altered: older, multiplied, sometimes answering questions not yet made.

Then the trunks narrow the opening. The impossible distance closes like an eye. The little basin becomes itself again—warm, damp in hidden places, green beyond measure, crowded with life and almost entirely empty, its living streets moving softly beneath a noise loud enough to rearrange the world.
