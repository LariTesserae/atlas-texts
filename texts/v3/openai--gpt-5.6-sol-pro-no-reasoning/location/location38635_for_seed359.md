The place is called a garden, though nothing in it has ever grown from soil.

It occupies a low, warm enclosure perhaps the size of a small hamlet: a shallow bowl under a roof whose edges cannot be found. The enclosing wall appears close enough to touch from anywhere, yet walking toward it merely adds more garden between. Seen from above—there are ladders, though no true high places—it would resemble a crowded courtyard built into a depression. Its paths, groves, pens, shrines, and workshops fit together with hardly a finger’s breadth wasted.

There is no earth beneath them. The ground consists of fitted tiles of sun-baked ceramic, horn, compressed ash, woven cane, and a pale substance like bone that takes no mark from a knife. Wherever a tile has cracked, another crafted layer shows below it: mosaics under bricks, baskets under mosaics, knotted mats beneath the baskets. No one has reached a bottom. The deepest excavation, a narrow shaft beside the central kiln, descended through twenty-seven successive floors before emerging from the ceiling of an abandoned dovecote three streets away.

Everything here has been made.

The garden’s trees are assembled around jointed wooden spines. Their bark is rope dipped in resin and dusted with fragrant powders. Leaves are thin paddles of hammered green clay, cloth, shell, or translucent wax, tied on with gut. They are numerous enough to form a canopy, and each turns by a fraction over the course of a day, showing first a dull side and then a bright one. In the warmer galleries the foliage crowds down to shoulder height. Ferns made of folded leather unroll from wall niches. Orange paper moss creeps across lintels—not by growing, exactly, but by acquiring new pleats when no one watches.

Some plants are clearly maintained by gardeners. Others seem to have escaped the category of object.

The spindle-vines are the most troublesome. They consist of cords threaded through thousands of beads, each bead carved with a tiny mouth. They crawl imperceptibly from trellis to trellis, tightening themselves around posts and sleeping animals. Their fruit are hollow gourds stitched from hide. Inside each one is a handful of warm black sand and, occasionally, a tooth bearing the name of a person not yet born.

No water runs anywhere. There are no wells, clouds, dew, juices, or blood as those substances would be understood elsewhere. The inhabitants do not thirst. Moisture is treated as a legendary hazard: a solvent capable of making distinctions bleed together. Old warning signs depict drops with legs, chasing houses.

Life uses warmth, oil, dust, and tension instead.

The trees draw nourishment through their roots—braided wicks sunk into narrow furnaces below the floor. Animals lick grease from mineral blocks or browse on wax leaves. The garden’s people consume seedlike pellets of resin, spice, powdered ceramic, and animal fat, softening them against hot stones before chewing. Cuts release a flocking red powder that packs itself into the wound. When sickness comes, it is usually described as slackness: cords loosen in the body, joints cease to agree on their orientation, and the patient begins to occupy adjacent positions.

The heat is steady but not oppressive. It rises through seams in the tiled ground and gathers beneath the false canopy. Surfaces are pleasantly warm to the palm. The air smells of fired clay, singed wool, cedar resin, warm feathers, old incense, and the faint metallic odor of stones struck together. Shade is plentiful, but it is patterned shade, pierced by orderly patches of ordinary amber daylight.

No sun is visible. Light enters from whichever direction the garden currently considers east.

East is not stable.

Each morning, artisans go out with cords, plumb bobs, and marked sticks to determine where the light has begun. They turn doorways to face it when possible. Some houses have been rebuilt so many times that they resemble nests of thresholds: frame behind frame behind frame, all opening onto a room barely large enough for one sleeper. The tools are simple—stone adzes, copper hooks, bone needles, ceramic hammers, abrasive cords—but the environment responds so readily to making that little else is required. Tie two posts together with the correct knot and, by morning, they may have become load-bearing in a structure that did not exist the day before. Carve a stair into a block and something will eventually arrive beneath it to make the ascent meaningful.

Construction is less an act of imposing form than of persuading the world to remember one.

The settlement’s buildings crowd one another in low terraces. There are no towers. Height is distrusted because distances become less reliable above the canopy. Houses are built of brick, basketry, lacquered ribs, slabs of salt, stacked masks, and densely packed chimes whose clappers have been removed. Their roofs overlap like scales. Passageways taper, branch, and rejoin; some are too narrow for a body but are nevertheless swept each morning. Tiny courtyards occur inside walls. Doors open into cupboards, kilns, sleeping rooms, animal burrows, or the back of the same door.

Despite this density, the garden is mostly quiet.

Soft-footed fauna occupy every level. Six-legged hares with felted paws browse among the leather ferns. Long ceramic beetles click only when turned upside down. Flocks of thumbnail-sized birds roost in sleeves and under roof tiles, their wings made of something like stiff lace. They do not sing. At dawn they open their beaks, and the shadows of nearby objects tremble as though hearing music.

Broad, patient animals called lintel beasts lie above gateways. They are quadrupeds built heavily at the shoulders, with faces resembling unfinished carvings. A lintel beast may remain still for years while generations of small moths build nests in its nostrils. When it finally moves to another doorway, people interpret the relocation as civic advice. Streets have been abandoned because of it.

There are also abundant nameless creatures that appear only as partial anatomies: a procession of feet passing beneath a curtain; antlers moving through dense foliage without a head below them; a warm purring space into which leaves and insects are drawn, emerging polished. The inhabitants make room for these things without insisting on completion. Completeness is considered a temporary arrangement.

The garden is young. Its corners are sharp, its brickwork barely weathered. Tool marks remain vivid on everything, even structures said to be ancient. Chips fallen from statues still fit their wounds exactly. There is little dust except where dust has been deliberately manufactured and laid down. Edges have not rounded. Steps have not hollowed beneath feet.

Yet memories of the place are old.

This contradiction is accepted as one of its rules: the garden was completed recently, but it has been inhabited for longer than completion. Elders recall standing in finished rooms before those rooms were built. Certain animals bear scars from accidents scheduled to happen in future years. At the communal kiln, pots are sometimes drawn out already mended. A craftsperson may spend months making the shards that will one day justify an old repair.

Change occurs slowly, almost politely. A lane migrates by one tile-width each season. A grove exchanges its leaves over decades, clay for wax, wax for shell, shell for folded copper. Houses lean toward one another by degrees until their upper rooms touch and a new chamber forms between them. Nobody owns these accidental rooms. They are kept empty for whatever caused them.

The nearest thing to weather is the loosening.

Once in a long while, knots throughout the garden relax at the same time. Roofs sigh. Trellises bow. Animals flatten themselves against the warm floor. People sit with their backs to walls and hold their names in their mouths. During the loosening, distance slips its fastenings. A neighbor’s courtyard may appear between two fingers. One can look through the eye of a needle and see every doorway in the settlement open at once. Footsteps arrive without having crossed the paths between.

Nothing violent usually occurs. Reality does not shatter here; it frays.

When the tension returns, the garden is subtly rearranged. A workshop may gain a grandmother. A tree may become the traditional site of a ceremony no one remembers learning. An entire species of small blue livestock may be found asleep in familiar pens, with feed prepared for them and their images painted on ancestral bowls. The inhabitants record these revisions on tablets of unfired clay, but the tablets revise themselves too. For more dependable archives they use scars, knots, tooth patterns, and the routes taken by beetles.

At the garden’s center stands the House of First Materials. It is neither large nor imposing, only a circular room with benches around the walls. On display are said to be the substances from which everything was originally made: a coil of cord, a brick, a bone awl, a cup of ash, a palm-sized plate of light, and a sealed jar labeled WATER.

The jar is empty.

Or rather, it contains an emptiness that behaves as a liquid. When tilted, the absence slides along the glass. Looking through it erases whatever lies beyond: first color, then shape, then the fact of having been made. The jar is bound with wicker and kept in a cradle of warm stones. A keeper watches it without touching it. Every few years the emptiness rises slightly higher against the glass.

No one knows what will happen if it becomes full.

Outside, under the manufactured leaves, quiet labor continues. Gardeners replace cracked foliage. Children teach beetles to carry straightedges. Potters shape vessels for organs not yet invented. The lintel beasts sleep above the doors. In the narrow lanes, the normal light shifts a little, and every east-facing threshold turns imperceptibly to follow it.

Far below the fitted floors—or perhaps just behind the nearest wall—something enormous and handmade is breathing very slowly.

Each breath takes a generation.

The whole garden warms when it exhales.
