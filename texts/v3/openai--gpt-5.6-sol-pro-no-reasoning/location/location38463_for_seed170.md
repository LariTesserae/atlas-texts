The plateau begins above the weather.

It is an immense shelf of pale mineral, lifted high enough that the horizon curves downward into copper haze. No rivers cut it, no springs darken its slopes. The old channels winding across the surface were never made by water. They are compression scars: shallow, branching trenches where the ground once softened under pressure and flowed, over centuries, like cooling metal.

The heat is steady and depthless. It does not shimmer so much as press. Stones exposed to the sun have the dull red warmth of kiln brick; shaded fissures offer no real coolness, only a lesser degree of burning. The air is thin, dry, and freighted with an alkaline taste. Each breath leaves the mouth feeling polished.

Almost nothing rises from the plain. There are no trees, grasses, lichens, or stains of organic green. Low ridges of black glass interrupt the chalk-colored flats. Farther off stand blunt mesas, eroded into forms resembling immense anvils. Their walls are layered with ash, iron salts, and compacted scales shed by generations of creatures. Because there is no rain, the land wears down by other means: thermal expansion, abrasive winds, the weight of migration, and the patient gnawing of mouths.

At first the plateau appears empty.

Then the distance moves.

The animals are not scattered over the landscape; they are the landscape’s mobile phase. Herds millions strong cover the far flats in bands so broad that they resemble discolorations in the stone. There are tall, jointed walkers with ceramic hides and narrow legs, each animal separated from the next by dozens of body lengths. Between them travel low caravans of plated things, many-limbed and slow, their backs furred not with hair but with conductive filaments. Above both drift immense translucent bladders, buoyed by gases cooked inside their bodies. They cast wandering amber shadows over the plain.

None of them drinks.

Their chemistry is based on hot salts, metallic vapors, and electrochemical gradients. They graze on differences: a warm rock beside a cooler one, an oxidized seam against an unoxidized seam, a charge accumulating between ground and sky. The tallest animals straddle fault lines and feed through their feet. Small needle-bodied swarms settle on newly cracked stone, extracting brief currents released as crystal lattices break. The floaters descend at dusk to inhale mineral dust and exhale it fused into hollow beads.

Life here has no softness. Eggs resemble ball bearings. Blood, where it exists, runs as molten eutectic barely above its freezing point. Wounds crust over with glass.

Vegetation never arose, or arose under definitions no visitor would recognize. In a few sheltered basins, colonies of hexagonal plates spread across the ground. They grow by persuading nearby crystals to adopt their geometry. They neither photosynthesize nor consume. They copy. Over geological time, their patterns advance several centimeters, halt at incompatible strata, and wait for earthquakes.

The noise of the plateau is tremendous.

It is not the noise of haste. Almost everything moves slowly, but it moves at colossal scale. Millions of articulated legs strike the ground in loose synchrony, producing a continuous industrial thunder. Shells rub against shells with the shriek of rail wheels. Gas animals pulse through their vents in bass notes too low to hear cleanly; the sound is felt in the jaw and sternum. Dust-feeders sift gravel through internal mills. At intervals, an entire herd turns a few degrees, and the adjustment travels across the plain as a roar lasting half an hour.

There are engines everywhere, though nothing here was built.

This is the first fracture in the place’s logic.

Some creatures contain pistons. Others carry flywheels, rotary compressors, induction coils, reciprocating hammers, and chambers that perform something very like smelting. Their mechanisms bear wear marks older than any conceivable industry. Teeth are replaced by casting new teeth in abdominal molds. Bearings reproduce by budding. Lubricants are secreted by glandular foundries and distributed through branching copper vessels.

No maker preceded them. No abandoned factory lies beneath the dust. Excavated fossils show that the machines evolved gradually: first simple ratchets in shells, then pumps, escapements, geared limbs, combustion sacs. Here industrialization is an anatomy, not a civilization.

Across one valley moves a migratory refinery.

It is a colonial beast several kilometers long, made of thousands of specialized bodies locked together around a shared furnace tract. Its forward castes scrape vanadium-rich crust from the plateau. Behind them, crushers reduce it to powder. Heat organs separate useful metals, which are passed backward through a chain of mouths. Exhaust rises from dorsal chimneys in a dark, perfectly level plume. At the colony’s rear, finished offspring emerge: small, bright creatures stamped into repeatable forms, identical except for slight variations in gear ratio.

The refinery advances less than a meter each day. Its passage can be heard from beyond the horizon.

The land has reached an equilibrium with these populations. Mesas are undercut at exactly the rate that shell-builders deposit fresh mineral around their bases. Glass dunes are ground down by migration and rebuilt from the vitrified droppings of the floaters. Atmospheric charge is harvested by one species and restored by another. Nothing happens quickly, yet nothing is still. The plateau is an enormous mechanism running at the tempo of geology.

Reality becomes unreliable near the central escarpment.

From a distance, the escarpment seems to be a line of dark cliffs. Approached from the south, it remains distant no matter how long one travels. Approached from the east, it arrives almost immediately and proves to be only knee-high. Animals cross it and change scale without transition: a needle swarm ascends the ledge and becomes a procession of towering tripods; a mountain-sized grazer steps down and continues as something small enough to disappear into a crack.

Sound crosses the boundary differently from bodies. The grinding of a creature that has not yet arrived may pass overhead for days. Footfalls sometimes precede feet by generations. Shed shells can be heard continuing their migrations after the animals that wore them are extinct.

The plateau’s noon light is otherwise ordinary—hard, white-gold sunlight from a familiar-looking star. Shadows fall where they should. Colors remain sober: bone, rust, graphite, sulfur yellow, the blue-black of cooled slag. This normal illumination makes the failures of space more disturbing. Nothing conceals them. One can look directly at a herd occupying two mutually exclusive valleys, or at a mesa whose near side is visibly farther away than its far side.

Tracks do not always indicate where creatures have been. Some are the marks of future migrations, pressed backward into the saltstone. The animals sense these and follow them, walking in the footprints they will make centuries later. If an obstacle is placed across such a route—though there are no hands here to place one—the obstacle weathers as if trampled until it becomes passable.

The world favors established patterns. Repetition acquires force.

A path used long enough becomes shorter. A call repeated by enough throats creates the organ required to hear it. Species that imitate engines become engines in fact. Erosion does not merely remove material; it edits contradictions, smoothing unstable shapes toward forms that have existed before. Novelty is possible, but the plateau resists it with the patience of stone.

At evening, the heat does not lift. The sun sinks through airborne iron dust, reddening the high plain. The floaters descend, their bladders dimly luminous with stored charge. Conductive herds gather along ridges and raise filamented backs toward the cooling sky. Across hundreds of kilometers, living dynamos begin to turn.

Blue arcs pass from animal to animal.

The plateau flashes in vast, slow circuits. Mesas answer with buried resonance. The migratory refinery opens its vents, and sparks climb its smoke in branching columns. For several minutes the whole empty-seeming region reveals itself as one distributed machine: herds as moving contacts, fault lines as wires, glass fields as capacitors, the escarpment as an impossible switch connecting distances that do not touch.

Then the charge drains away. Darkness settles over an expanse still hot enough to glow faintly at its cracks.

The thunder continues.

It may be migration, weather, industry, or simply the sound made by a world laboring very slowly to remain real.
