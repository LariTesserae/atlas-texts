High in the blind interior of the mountain-city, behind a maintenance hatch that no longer appears on any plan, there is a room called the Rookery.

It is smaller than the name suggests. A person can cross it in twelve careful steps, though there is nowhere to take those steps without disturbing something. The chamber is packed from wall to wall and floor to ceiling: pipes sleeved in pipes, ribbed condensers, ceramic insulators, iron vanes, cable looms, pressure vessels, narrow catwalks, nesting shelves, and cages whose doors have rusted permanently open. No stone is visible. If the mountain exists around the room, it gives no evidence of itself. Every surface here was cast, riveted, soldered, glazed, or bolted into place.

The floor is not really a floor. It is a shallow black reservoir crossed by a grating. Water stands just beneath the metal and trembles under the continuous force of sound. Droplets gather on the undersides of pipes, swell to an exact size, and fall at exact intervals. Along the eastern wall, a row of copper faces weeps from their mouths. Their tears run into narrow gutters, through wire filters, and back into the reservoir. Frost whitens the gutters but never closes them. The water is so cold that submerged bolts shine beneath a clear skin of ice; nevertheless, the ice never thickens.

The Rookery is wet without ever becoming wetter.

It is also cold without weather. Breath clouds at mouth height and remains there, suspended in pale strata between the machinery. Every ladder rung burns the hand through a glove. The damp has polished the iron rather than corroded it. Rust exists only in the color chosen for certain enamel plates. Nothing decays. Nothing accumulates. No stain widens.

The room was built for manufacture, though no one remembers what it manufactures.

Its machines are old in design and immaculate in operation. Flywheels revolve behind guards. Pistons enter and withdraw from cylinders. Belts hurry over pulleys. Relays chatter inside sealed cabinets, and heavy contactors close with concussions that can be felt in the jaw. There are gauges everywhere, nested closely enough that their brass rims touch. All their needles point to the same unmarked division between numbers. Lamps burn behind smoked glass, giving the chamber a weak amber dusk, insufficient to illuminate the upper recesses.

The machinery produces no visible product. It consumes no fuel and emits no waste. Inspection panels reveal smaller machines performing parallel motions, and those contain finer mechanisms still. The deeper one looks, the more crowded fabrication becomes: miniature rollers turning out strips that re-enter themselves, tiny stamps descending on blank metal, combs sorting identical grains from one tray into another. The room appears less like a factory than a factory’s thought of itself.

Its geology is manufactured too.

Above the reservoir, banks of extruders press a continuous ridge of dark ceramic from slots in the wall. The ceramic emerges glossy and soft, hardens almost at once, and is caught by a second mechanism that warms and kneads it back into the extruders. Thus a cliff is forever beginning and never completed. Along one pipe, powdered glass is compressed into embryonic crystals—clear, sharp little prisms—which are dissolved by condensation before they grow beyond the size of teeth. Elsewhere, a ram pushes up a wrinkle in a lead floor plate, making the first suggestion of a hill; beneath it, another ram presses the wrinkle flat.

There are no ravines, only the intentions of ravines. No mature fractures, only hairline seams carefully closed as soon as they appear. Water travels through channels too new to have worn smooth, although it has traveled through them for centuries. The whole chamber possesses the rawness of a place assembled this morning and the fatigue of something that has run since before mornings were counted.

Almost nothing grows. In three sheltered screw heads there is a black-green film, thin as breath on glass. A pale tuft stands in the condensation beneath one lamp, neither moss nor mold, with four translucent blades. It never adds a fifth. The tuft does not bend in the machinery’s wind. Its droplets do not fall. This is the sum of the vegetation.

The animals are another matter.

They occupy every interval not claimed by metal or water.

Birds crowd the cable trays in ranks: soot-colored swifts, white cave finches, compact birds with transparent eyelids and metallic claws. Hundreds cling upside down from hot-water pipes. Thousands more are concealed behind the gauges, inside unused ducts, under the grating, and among the revolving guards where no living body should fit. Their feathers are wet but never bedraggled. They do not feed, mate, sleep, grow, sicken, or die.

They cry continuously.

The sound is overwhelming, yet it is not chaotic. Each animal contributes one fixed call. Together the calls form a dense mechanical chord: shrieks like skidding steel, low booming coos, electrical ticks, whistles at the edge of hearing, and rapid bursts resembling ball bearings poured down a chute. The machines answer in rhythm. The pistons pound beneath the birds’ voices; the relays clap; water rings in the copper channels. No sound begins or ends. Entering the room is like stepping into a single noise already held at full volume.

Among the birds are other creatures. Colorless salamanders lace the wet pipes, each wrapped around a valve stem. Silver mites tile the condenser fins. Narrow eels inhabit the reservoir in such numbers that the water seems woven from muscle, though no eel ever changes position. Moths cover the darkest wall three bodies deep, wings open, scales sparkling with frost. In the upper lattice hang pale mammals the size of thumbs, gripping wires with six feet. Their mouths are open. Their cries cannot be distinguished from the motors.

Nothing moves from where it is, and yet everything performs movement.

The flywheels turn but retain the same scars at the same points in space. The birds’ throats pulse, though their beaks preserve one exact degree of opening. Droplets fall, but a droplet is always hanging from each spout and always striking each pool. The eels seem to writhe, but their braid never alters. A visitor’s eye records action; measurement records none.

This is the chief rule of the Rookery: events are permitted, change is not.

Heat may pass through a pipe, but neither end becomes warmer. Water may circulate, but no volume is displaced. Wings may beat, but air cannot be moved from one place to another. Noise may hammer the chamber indefinitely, but nothing loosens. Every force completes its circuit inside the instant that produced it.

A second rule is less obvious: there can be no empty place.

If a bolt is removed, beetles are found packed into the threaded hole. If a panel is opened, birds occupy the cabinet before the door has cleared its latch—though none are seen entering. If water is lifted in a cup, small translucent fish fill the space beneath the cup’s rim. Even the fog is dense with tiny floating shells. Under magnification, the apparent gaps between shells contain articulated metal gnats; the gaps between the gnats contain droplets; the droplets contain black, revolving specks that might be machines or eggs.

Yet the room does not feel vast. Its impossibilities remain intimate. They press against the face. A shoulder touches cold pipework; feathers brush the wrist; unseen claws tap on the back of a helmet. There is no distant horizon, only layer behind layer within arm’s reach. The highest parts of the chamber are perhaps six meters overhead, but the crowded dimness makes them inaccessible, as remote as alpine peaks seen through storm cloud.

The chamber is, in its way, at high elevation. A barometric plate by the hatch states that it stands 8,104 meters above sea level. The plate is correct regardless of where the mountain-city is parked, buried, or transported. Air entering the Rookery immediately thins to that altitude. Water approaches boiling at low temperature but never boils. Visitors become breathless after several minutes, though the oxygen gauge remains fixed at a safe reading. There is a sensation of summit exposure without sky: pressure behind the eyes, a looseness in the knees, the conviction that one might fall a tremendous distance while standing between two walls.

Near the back is a tiny observation niche reached by squeezing under a coolant manifold. It contains a stool, a frosted window, and a speaking tube. The stool is riveted to the grating. The window looks not outside but into a duplicate Rookery, dimmer and apparently much smaller. Within that chamber another window can be seen, and beyond it another. Each is filled with the same machinery, the same suspended breath, the same overwhelming fauna.

In every successive room, a figure sits on the riveted stool with its back turned.

The speaking tube transmits the room’s deafening chorus into itself. Putting an ear to it makes the sound no louder. Speaking into it produces no detectable addition. Sometimes visitors claim that beneath the engines and the birds they can hear a slow sentence spoken in their own voice. The sentence is always the same:

**It is nearly finished.**

This cannot be true. Finished things require a before and an after, and neither is available here.

When the maintenance hatch is closed, the Rookery remains exactly as it was: wet, embryonic, crowded, manufactured, and unbearably loud. The pale tuft keeps its four blades. The copper faces continue to weep. Crystals begin without growing. Cliffs emerge without forming. An impossible multitude of animals sustains its single chord.

Nothing waits.

Nothing proceeds.

The whole hidden landscape persists at the instant of coming into being.
