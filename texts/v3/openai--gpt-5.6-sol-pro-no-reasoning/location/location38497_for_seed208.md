The land is called the Wash, though nothing here remembers what was washed away.

It occupies a broad, shallow depression between hills so old that they no longer possess slopes in the ordinary sense. They are long swells of dark stone, scarcely higher than the plain, worn smooth by an age of weather without soil. From a distance they resemble immense bodies beneath a gray covering. Up close, their surfaces are pocked with cups, channels, and overlapping basins. Water fills every hollow. It slips continuously from seams in the rock, beads on exposed faces, and gathers in black pools whose bottoms cannot be distinguished from reflections of the sky.

There are no plants. No reeds fringe the water, no moss softens the stones, no wind-bent grass gives scale to the open ground. Nothing roots here. The Wash is made entirely of rock, water, silt, bone, hide, shell, and the scattered workmanship of its inhabitants.

The light is rarely more than an iron-colored diffusion. Clouds sit low enough to erase the distant ridges. Moisture condenses directly out of the air, whitening the rocks with innumerable droplets. Even when rain is not falling, everything drips. The cold is not severe, but it is intimate: the chill of wet stone, of soaked clothing, of breath that never quite warms the lungs.

Across this dimness comes the overwhelming sound.

At the center of the depression, water descends through thousands of narrow perforations in a tilted shelf of stone. The drop is not high—only the height of a house in most places—but the shelf extends for many miles, and the combined discharge produces an unbroken concussion. The inhabitants call it the Long Fall. Its roar rolls over the plain like weather made permanent. It is felt through the soles and teeth. Pools tremble in their basins. Loose pebbles creep slowly across flat ledges. Speech is possible only mouth-to-ear, and even then most meaning is conveyed by touch, posture, or the broad gestures of both arms.

Behind the Long Fall lies a shallow lake, replenished by rain and by springs rising through fractures in its floor. Before it stretches an intricate country of spillways: ribbons of fast water, shelves glazed with mineral deposits, drowned troughs, and broad flats of blue-black mud. The channels are never stable. Pebbles lodge in one constriction, pressure builds, a lip collapses, and by morning a stream may be running where generations had known dry stone. Yet the larger form of the Wash is almost changeless. It has been eroded nearly to completion. There are no cliffs left except those continually remade by collapse, no sharp peaks, few loose boulders. Water no longer carves grand valleys here. It worries at the last differences in height.

The animals are everywhere.

They crowd the water because water is nearly the only source of food. The pools harbor dense veils of microscopic life: not green growth, but colorless films that feed on dissolved minerals and the faint warmth leaking from below. In places these films coat the submerged stone like grease or cloudy glass. Nearly every visible creature grazes on them, filters them, or hunts something that does.

Low-bodied shellbacks move through the shallows in herds, scraping rock with crescent jaws. Their shells are soft at the rim and scarred down the center, slate-gray against slate-gray; they are most easily detected by the wake of water around them. Pale sieve-mouths hang beneath overhangs, opening and closing translucent fans in the current. Finger-length darting animals shelter in drilled holes and emerge in sudden multitudes when pressure changes announce rain.

On the mudflats gather thousands of stilted walkers. They have four long legs, each ending in a round pad that spreads on contact with the ground. Their bodies are small, furred, and nearly spherical, protecting heat beneath layers of oily down. They probe the silt with flexible beaks and call to one another in deep vibrations inaudible beneath the falls but visible as ripples around their feet. When alarmed, an entire congregation rises—not into flight, for they have no wings, but upright, balancing on the rear pair of legs to scan the plain. A field of them standing so can resemble a grove of bare black stems, the nearest thing the Wash has to vegetation.

Predators cross the open rock singly. The most common are long, low hunters whose six limbs fold into grooves along the body when they swim. On land they advance by rapid, unpleasantly fluid bounds. They hunt mainly by sensing vibration through the stone, and the omnipresent roar confuses them; to compensate, they strike the ground with a hardened plate beneath the jaw and listen for the returning tremor. Before an attack, one may be seen standing motionless, tapping. Herd animals freeze when they notice it, though freezing does little good unless every nearby creature freezes too.

Farther from the channels are the bone fields. Calcium is scarce in the food-web, and carcasses rarely remain intact for long. Small raspers strip skeletons to lace within days. What survives is usually what has become wedged into cracks or mortared into human walls.

The people of the Wash live in scattered houses built where smooth rock rises just above flood level. Each dwelling is a low oval of fitted stone, hide, and bone, its roof weighted by slabs against the wind. There are no timbers. Doorways face away from the Long Fall and are covered by overlapping curtains of cured shellback skin. The seams are packed with greasy silt that slowly hardens into a waterproof gray cement.

A house is rarely close enough to another for an unaided voice to carry, even if the cataract permitted it. Between them stand marker cairns: narrow stacks with perforated stones at the top. Wind passing through the holes creates notes of different pitch. No single note can be heard reliably over the water, but the vibration can be felt by placing a palm against the cairn. Each route has its own sequence. Travelers move from stone to stone with one hand extended, reading the country through their fingertips.

The people possess chipped-stone blades, shell scrapers, bone awls, braided sinew, weighted nets, and a little hammered copper gathered as dull nodules from the outflow channels. Copper is too rare and soft for most tools. It is made into thin resonant plates worn against the chest. Messages are tapped through these plates during hunts and floods. Children learn the pressure-language before they learn spoken words.

Fire is difficult. Fuel consists of rendered fat, dried dung, shed chitin, and strips of hide too damaged for other uses. Flames are kept in covered stone lamps, small and blue. Cooking is done in pits by heating rocks and dropping them into skin-lined basins. Smoke creeps along ceilings, adding another dark layer to the interiors. In the dimness, polished shell disks and wet eyes catch the lamp glow.

Most structures are less comprehensible than the houses.

Across the plain lie partial rings of standing stones, all heavily rounded, some nearly worn back into the surface from which they were raised. Long rows of shallow sockets lead toward channels that no longer exist. At low water, rectangular foundations appear beneath the lake, but they are too large to have been dwellings and too evenly spaced to be natural. Some say these are fish traps made by the first people, when the water ran elsewhere. Others say the Wash itself was built: the Long Fall a broken machine, the perforated stone shelf its final working piece.

There is evidence for neither account, only workmanship buried beneath erosion so complete that intention and geology have become indistinguishable.

One artifact still functions.

Near the eastern margin stands a hollow pillar of fitted basalt rings. It is no taller than three people, black and blunt, leaning slightly westward. Rain enters an opening at the top, travels through hidden passages, and emerges hours later in a sequence of drops. Each drop strikes one of seven stone tongues inside the pillar. In calm weather the pattern repeats every few days. Before a major flood it changes.

No one knows why.

Families maintain the pillar by clearing shell growth from its outlet and replacing the leather cups positioned beneath it, but they do not attempt to open it. The rings have fused. Its interior vibration can be felt from a considerable distance through the saturated bedrock. During the flood season, people sleep with one hand on the floor.

Floods arrive without spectacle at first. The already dim light dulls further. Pools rise until their separate rims disappear. Grazers leave the channels and mass upon the rock swells; predators follow, abandoning concealment. The air becomes so wet that breath seems to add nothing to it. Then the lake overtops some unseen threshold.

Water advances across the plain not as a wave but as an expanding skin. It connects basins, fills old grooves, surrounds cairns, and begins pressing at the stone houses. The Long Fall deepens in voice. Its usual roar acquires pulses slow enough to count, each one shaking droplets from ceilings miles away.

For several days the Wash becomes a single shallow, violent sheet.

The stilted walkers lock legs in circles around their young. Shellbacks anchor themselves beneath ledges. Burrowing creatures seal their holes with mucus and survive in trapped air. Human families retreat to the old hill-swells, carrying nets, lamps, infants, and bundles of dried food in inflated hides. From those low elevations they watch familiar routes vanish. The few firelights are minute orange points in the rain.

When the water withdraws, it leaves the world rearranged in detail and unchanged in essence. A house may be gone. A new channel may divide two families. Thousands of small animals lie stranded in basins and draw every hunter from the surrounding flats. The people build temporary stone fences around the richest pools and disputes begin—conducted in emphatic silence, palms striking chests and rock, while scavengers gather at the edges.

Afterward comes a period of abundance. Nets fill. Fat lamps burn longer. New shelters rise from the same rounded slabs that formed the old ones. Bones are cleaned, drilled, and given a second life as tools. Water resumes its narrower paths.

And always the landscape continues its final, patient wearing-down.

Nothing grows upward here. Everything spreads, settles, rounds, dissolves, or is carried toward the Long Fall. The animals multiply furiously in the wet seasons and are thinned just as quickly. Structures endure only by being rebuilt. Even memory is adapted to the roar: histories are not recited but danced on flat stones, each footfall and heel-drag transmitted through the ground to the hands of those kneeling around the performer.

To an outsider the Wash might seem barren, almost featureless: a drowned gray plain beneath a dim sky. To those who live upon it, it is crowded with distinctions. Every basin has a temperature and taste. Every slab answers a footstep differently. Every current bears its own vibration. The apparent emptiness is a map too subtle for sight.

At night, if the cloud thins, the wet stone returns a little of the sky. The pools become dark apertures scattered across the flats. Animals move between them in such numbers that the reflections continually break and mend. The Long Fall cannot be seen beyond the mist, but it fills the whole basin with its presence—ancient, deafening, and almost motionless in the scale of the land, grinding the last roughness from the world.
