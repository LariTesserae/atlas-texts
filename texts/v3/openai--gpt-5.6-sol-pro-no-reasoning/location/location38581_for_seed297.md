The plain begins without a shoreline.

It is low, warm, and almost perfectly level, extending beneath a pale yellow sky until distance loses the power to make things smaller. Nothing here is wet. There are no channels, cloudbanks, stains of former rain, or rounded stones. The ground is a fresh mineral crust, dark as unglazed ceramic and broken into acute plates whose edges remain needle-sharp. No wind has worried them. No sediment has gathered in their seams. They look newly cooled, though they are warm to the touch and have apparently lain unchanged for ages.

From every seam grows an extravagant abundance.

At first it resembles vegetation: deep fans, crowded thickets, ribbed trunks and translucent crowns. Low fields of coppery blades spread between waist-high spirals. Black fronds interlace overhead in places, their undersides dusted with green-gold light. Broad leaves overlap by the thousands, filling the plain with the architecture of a jungle but not its moisture. Nothing is succulent. Nothing contains sap.

These organisms conduct their lives through solids.

Their roots enter the crust as hair-thin crystal faults and draw migrating electrons, lattice defects, and minute differences of pressure from the mineral. Their tissues are braided from carbon, silicon, and long metallic molecules that remain flexible in the heat. Leaves do not transpire; they exchange charge with the sky. Their matte upper surfaces collect light, while the fine structures beneath perform reactions for which there are no familiar biological names. Cut one—and cuts do sometimes exist, though nothing is seen making them—and the exposed surface is dry, intricately faceted, and immediately complete. It neither bleeds nor heals. It simply possesses an edge it has always possessed.

The plain is lush in the way a circuit can be lush.

There are dense groves of filament trees, every strand separated from its neighbor by the same microscopic interval. There are meadows of hexagonal scales that tilt toward the sun without moving. There are pale, many-lobed growths like enormous lichens, except that they hover a finger’s width above the ground. Their shadows connect them to the crust more convincingly than roots would. Farther out stand groves of narrow pillars with crowns like folded umbrellas. Their branches penetrate one another without collision, occupying the same volume under slightly different rules.

No structures interrupt this country. There are no paths, walls, towers, excavations, or discarded objects. Yet the entire region gives the impression of engineering—engineering so complete that it has ceased to produce artifacts. The distinction between organism, device, and physical law has been removed.

One senses this most clearly at the margins of sight.

A nearby trunk is rough and dark, with six main limbs. The same trunk seen from a hundred paces may have eleven limbs and a skin like polished bone. Seen in peripheral vision, it can be both forms, alternating without transition. Walking toward it does not resolve the contradiction. Instead, the plain quietly adjusts the distance so that the trunk remains equally far away while other things pass normally beneath the feet. If touched, it is always the six-limbed version. If counted, it is always the eleven.

The place contains many such agreements.

Every blade casts a plausible shadow, but the shadows collectively cover more ground than exists. Rows of fronds repeat at intervals that can be measured but not added together. An open glade may be crossed in forty steps from east to west and twelve steps from west to east. Beneath certain crowns, the sky is visible below the horizon, a calm strip of brightness under the black ground. Occasionally two distant groves occupy precisely the same direction while remaining separately visible, one behind the other and each in front.

These are not disturbances. They do not spread, intensify, or heal. They are stable properties, as ordinary here as weight.

Time, too, appears to have settled into an exact equilibrium. The air is warm but carries no convection. The leaves receive light but do not warm further. No crown opens, no stem lengthens, no frond falls. There are no young plants or old ones. What appears to be germination—a field of fine shoots breaking through a shard of crust—remains forever at the instant of emergence. What appears to be decay—a white trunk dividing into delicate flakes—never completes its collapse. Even loose dust hangs close to some surfaces in motionless veils, each grain suspended at the point where a nonexistent breeze left it.

The illumination is surprisingly ordinary. A sun hangs at a reasonable angle and has the familiar brightness of late morning. Colors remain sober and readable: olive, rust, charcoal, amber, chalk. There is no theatrical glow except where living surfaces generate it for their own obscure purposes. Shadows are neither excessively sharp nor soft. This normal light makes the violations of space harder to dismiss. Everything can be seen clearly. Clarity offers no protection.

Sound is scarce.

Footfalls on the mineral plates make small, dry reports, although sometimes the sound precedes the step. The dense foliage does not rustle. Branches that appear to scrape one another produce silence. If the ear is held near the crust, a remote pulse can be heard—not a rhythm, because no interval passes between its beats, but a single impact encountered repeatedly at different depths.

Now and then a grove emits a faint chord. It has no attack or fade. It is simply present in one narrow place, waist-high and a few meters across. Walk through it and the tone occupies the body, making teeth and bones seem briefly hollow; step beyond it and silence returns. These chords may be signals, metabolism, or structural supports. Some hold invisible slopes in the level ground. Others appear to keep incompatible versions of a plant from becoming mutually exclusive.

Animals are rare enough that the first may be mistaken for another flaw in perception.

A dark, jointed creature occasionally stands between the blades. It is no larger than a hand, with a low shell and three legs arranged along a line rather than around a body. It never visibly arrives. While watched, it remains absolutely still. After the gaze shifts away and returns, it may be closer, though no leaf has been disturbed. Its mouthparts fit exactly into the sharp edges of the ground plates, from which it seems to feed—not by removing matter, but by consuming the distinction between one shard and the next. Behind it, separate plates remain separate, yet their boundary can no longer be found.

Higher among the crowns are minute reflective motes. They could be airborne seeds, insects, or points where sunlight has become trapped. They hold fixed positions despite the absence of branches. Each mote contains, on close inspection, a view of the plain from a great altitude. In several, a circular absence can be seen at the region’s center.

That absence cannot be located from the ground.

The vegetation becomes denser toward it from every direction, but there is no gradient of density. Every grove is already the densest grove. Every clearing lies both outside and within the ring. Travelers attempting to reach the center encounter increasingly advanced expressions of the same ecology: leaves that compute the light before it falls; roots that pass through unbroken stone; pale membranes that maintain pockets where cause follows effect in reverse. Yet there is no sense of progress. The first step inward and the thousandth step inward occur in equivalent terrain.

Nothing guards the place. Nothing needs to.

The world’s rules here are not failing from damage. They have been superseded with such precision that failure itself has become impossible. Contradictions are held in permanent suspension. Energy circulates without gradients. Growth flourishes without change. The organisms are active without events, crowded without competition, and sustained without water or consumption. Whatever intelligence established this condition left behind no machinery because machinery would be redundant. Its work is embedded in what distance means, in how surfaces meet, in the permission granted to one object to have multiple shapes.

Perhaps the plain is a garden. Perhaps it is a computation whose symbols learned to photosynthesize. Perhaps the young, sharp crust is not the beginning of a world but the newest exposed layer of something that has existed outside time, continually arriving while never changing.

Across the open country, every black frond remains motionless beneath the mild sun. The mineral leaves hold their impossible charge. The rare three-legged creatures wait among edges that cannot erode. Nothing approaches. Nothing recedes.

And everywhere, with immense quietness, the landscape continues to be exactly what it has not yet become.
