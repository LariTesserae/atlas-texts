The city lies beneath a warm, shallow sea.

It is a city only in the sense that reefs are cities: no hand planned it, no tool cut its chambers, and nothing here distinguishes architecture from appetite. Across a drowned plain, pale towers rise from beds of dark silt. Their walls are secreted limestone, built grain by grain by generations of soft-bodied animals. Ridges divide districts. Arches brace one mound against another. Narrow passages lead into crowded courtyards, and broad, low causeways wind between colonies like streets. The oldest structures have slumped and fused until they resemble weathered masonry; newer growth clings to them in scalloped galleries.

Nearly everything is underwater, including the air.

Far above, though rarely visible, the sea meets a permanent ceiling of rain. Fresh water falls so heavily that the upper layers churn and blur. There is no clear surface, only a turbulent boundary where one kind of water enters another. Below it, the salt sea remains warm and dense. Storm pressure travels downward in slow pulses, bowing forests of feelers and drawing long breaths through the city’s passages.

The bottom is almost level. It spreads for many leagues, descending so gradually that depth is measured less by slope than by darkness. Channels cross the plain, cut by old currents and kept open by tidal exchange. Their banks are rounded and settled. Shell grit has accumulated in bars and low islands, though none breaks the surface. Between the bars lie basins of black mud, warm enough to release faint streams of bubbles. Nothing looks newly broken. Even ruins have been inhabited so long that their fractures are lined with mouths.

There are few plants. Thin red films occupy the highest exposed ridges, feeding on what little light penetrates the rain and turbid water. In occasional clearings, sparse ribbons of weed lie flat along the current, no taller than a finger. They are dark olive by day, if day can be said to reach this depth, and indistinguishable from stone at night. Most life here does not depend directly on sunlight. The city lives on falling matter, on dissolved minerals, on one creature consuming another, and on warm seepage from beneath the seabed.

Darkness is its principal material.

Seen from within, the place consists of nearness: walls passing close on either side, columns crowded with folded shells, hanging curtains of translucent bodies. Beyond them, whole neighborhoods disappear. Open water exists overhead and in the wider avenues, but it is black and apparently solid. Distance reveals itself only when something moves through it.

A procession of small fish may suddenly take shape—hundreds of narrow bodies flashing once as they turn—then vanish into the same darkness. Broad rays cross above like loose pieces of ceiling. Pale eels knot themselves in wall cavities. Crabs inhabit every horizontal surface, sorting the silt with delicate, incessant movements. Shrimp crowd beneath ledges in layers, their eyes reflecting weak points of blue. Soft worms extend crowns into the current and withdraw them all at once when a predator passes, causing an entire façade to close as though the city has blinked.

The structures are dense with such reactions. A tower may appear inert until touched; then pores contract, polyps fold inward, brittle stars pull their arms into cracks, and a skin of minute animals changes color from soot-gray to bone-white. No building is empty. No stone is merely stone. The dead skeleton of one colony becomes the foundation of another, and hollows left by decay become nurseries, traps, lungs, or dens.

There are plazas: broad depressions swept clean by circular currents. At their centers stand chimney-like mounds with warm water slipping from their mouths. Mineral crusts branch around these vents in dull yellows and bruised reds. Blind grazers assemble there in thick carpets. They feed on bacterial skins, scraping so softly that the combined sound resembles sand moving under a closed door.

There are also walls. Some are kilometers long, grown where opposing currents deposit food. They rise only a few body-lengths above the plain but are continuous enough to redirect the sea. Water enters through gates formed by overlapping ribs and accelerates into the interior districts. Animals station themselves in these passages: feather-mouthed filters, hinged shells, patient fish. The gates are not defended, yet crossing them is dangerous. Every narrowing concentrates hunger.

The city’s oldest quarter lies in a shallow trench. Its towers have eroded into low domes, drilled through by borers and polished by sediment. Roofs have collapsed into chambers beneath chambers. Warm water circulates there sluggishly, heavy with sulfur and decay. Larger creatures prefer it: thick-bodied fish that rest with their fins against the mud, lobsters armored in chalky plates, and long, nearly colorless hunters whose jaws open wider than their heads. They move infrequently. When they do, smaller life drains from the streets ahead of them.

Sound does not travel here as it does in air. The city is mostly quiet, but never still.

There is the distant granular hiss of rain transmitted through the upper sea. The tick of shrimp claws. The faint crackle of feeding colonies. Mud sighing under weight. Shells knocking once, then settling. From far channels come low compressions rather than audible notes: the tail-beat of something large, a submerged collapse, thunder blurred by miles of water. These vibrations pass through walls and flesh together. Creatures feel an event before they know its direction.

Light is rarer than sound. It appears biologically and with purpose.

Tiny animals rising from the silt leave green sparks behind them. Predators pulse cold blue along their flanks, briefly outlining arches and crowded balconies. Some chambers contain colonies that glow continuously but dimly, making their interiors resemble moonlit rooms. The light attracts drifting larvae; the larvae attract mouths. In the deepest alleys, a flash may be a warning, a lure, a mating signal, or simply the last disturbance of something being eaten.

Warmth keeps everything active. Growth does not stop for a hard season. Instead, the city follows subtler cycles: the freshening of the upper sea during heavier rains; tides that reverse the avenues; blooms of invisible food; weeks when pale eggs coat every sheltered wall; nights when juvenile animals emerge in such numbers that the water becomes granular with bodies. Predation intensifies, then settles. Shells accumulate. Towers gain another thin layer.

Erosion is equally alive. Boring sponges weaken foundations. Urchins rasp the surfaces smooth. Acids released by buried microbes hollow stone from within. Currents carry away every loosened grain and deposit it elsewhere, where tube-builders bind it into a new parapet. Destruction and construction are not opposites here. They are consecutive phases of digestion.

Nothing in this place is manufactured, yet the city has infrastructure. Its sewers are channels maintained by burrowing animals. Its reservoirs are sponge-filled caverns that hold and filter water. Its roads are current paths, widened by migration. Its towers compete for flow and exposure. Its districts differ according to salinity, heat, depth, and the chemical character of the mud. Even traffic has patterns: grazers moving outward at the dimming of the upper water, hunters entering from the plain, clouds of larvae ascending through the streets.

There is no ruler and no central plan. The city’s law is occupation. Any stable surface will be covered. Any cavity will acquire a tenant. Any flow will gather feeders, and every gathering of feeders will draw hunters. Height earns food but risks storms. Shelter offers safety but little oxygen. Brightness attracts. Warmth accelerates. Death leaves structure.

At the outskirts, the constructions thin into isolated pinnacles. The dark plain opens between them, strewn with shells and the impressions of passing bodies. Here the scale of the submerged region becomes apparent. Other reef-cities stand in the distance, known only by faint chemical traces and migrating schools. Channels connect them across the low seabed. During certain tides, spawn from one district drifts into another, and the cities exchange their inhabitants without ever touching.

Above all of it, rain continues to fall into the sea.

Below, the warm black water circulates through a metropolis that has never seen fire, metal, sky, or an empty room. It is ancient without remembering. It repairs itself by feeding, expands by dying, and remains quiet because nearly every act of violence happens with a mouth already open.
