The city exists inside a flooded expansion joint.

From outside, if outside can be said to exist at this depth, the joint is only a dark seam in the foundation of an older and much larger structure. It runs between two slabs of black ceramic bedrock, six meters long and scarcely wider than a hand. The slabs descend farther than instruments can resolve. They may be the footings of a drowned continent, the shell of a machine, or two natural plates that happened to cool with impossible regularity. Pressure lies over them like a second geology.

Inside the seam, distance behaves differently.

The opening widens after entry—not abruptly, but by increments too slight for the eye to catch. A diver moving a few centimeters through the crack would find the walls retreating until the passage became an avenue, then a district, then a narrow metropolis folded into a volume that should contain no more than a bucket of water. The city is low and compressed. There are no towers. Everything spreads sideways in shelves, trenches, stacked galleries, underpasses, and rooms fitted beneath other rooms. Roofs lie close to streets. Streets pass through roofs. Every usable surface has been claimed.

Water fills it all.

It is cool, mineral-heavy, and clear enough for ordinary light to travel through it, though there is no ordinary source. Illumination issues evenly from the water itself: a pale daytime brightness, neither blue nor green, without visible lamps or sun. Objects cast mild shadows in several directions. At intervals the light strengthens as if clouds have moved away overhead, though overhead is a ceiling of recently fractured stone.

This brightness makes the place feel less abyssal than it should. You can see the fine aggregate in the walls, the silver edges of transit rails, the orange warning marks painted around vents. You can see suspended droplets within the water—spheres of denser liquid that do not mix, drifting like glass beads. When they cross a shadow they flash briefly with images of other streets.

The architecture is compact to the point of suffocation. Buildings are wedged into fissures and bolted face-to-face across alleys. Their outer skins are made from pressure-grown ceramic, translucent metals, dark basalt foam, and a chalky composite that repairs itself in branching white scars. Some structures seem poured; others were folded from single continuous sheets and then persuaded to occupy more interior space than their dimensions allow. Doorways open directly against neighboring walls, yet remain passable. Windows look into rooms displaced several blocks away. Pipes are so numerous that they form false ceilings and secondary streets.

Nothing is empty. Where there is not a dwelling there is a conduit; where there is not a conduit there is a reservoir, anchor, transformer, brace, public inscription, maintenance swarm, or bundle of dormant cable. The density has become its own structural material. Remove one kiosk-sized pressure chamber and the district around it may settle inward, rearranging hundreds of rooms.

The city appears newly made. Corners are sharp. Excavated faces show bright, unweathered fractures. Rubble still hangs where it was cut, held motionless in cages of tuned pressure. Certain avenues end at naked stone on which boring fields work continuously, peeling away the rock molecule by molecule. Behind them the city advances: first a hollow, then a membrane, then ducts, rails, rooms, and civic machinery. Construction travels faster than sediment can accumulate. There are almost no rounded stones.

The governing technology is not built around resisting the sea. It assigns the sea jobs.

Pressure carries freight. Differences in salinity compute municipal forecasts. Currents turn no turbines, yet emerge from power stations with their histories altered. A pipe may receive cold water at both ends and discharge heat halfway along its length. Vast calculations are performed in microscopic rearrangements of dissolved ions; the entire flood is memory, processor, roadway, and atmosphere. To enter the city’s water is to enter its operating system.

There are no animals here—not even bacteria, larvae, mites, or the pale scavengers expected in submerged places. Filters confirm the absence with unnerving consistency. Nothing swims unless it was manufactured. Nothing breathes. Nothing watches with an eye.

Sparse vegetation survives in sanctioned cracks: black ribbons of engineered algae, fans of silver moss, and stiff translucent stalks rooted in municipal silt traps. They grow slowly and in straight lines. Their purpose is partly chemical, partly ceremonial. In several plazas, solitary plants are enclosed by railings, each leaf given more open space than a resident’s room. No creature grazes them. Dead tissue is collected immediately by soft magnetic combs and returned to solution.

The lack of fauna leaves the city without small incidental sounds. There is no clicking shell, no fin-rustle, no distant call. Instead, it is loud with infrastructure.

Pumps beat through the walls in overlapping rhythms. Pressure gates shut with concussions that can be felt in the teeth. Construction fronts produce long ascending shrieks as stone changes state. Trains move inside sealed streams, announced by a deep chord before they appear as a distortion racing along the avenue. Thousands of pipes sing continuously at different pitches. Because sound travels efficiently through the packed water and denser walls, every district hears some portion of every other. The metropolis has no silence, only quieter mixtures.

Its loudest event occurs several times a day, though “day” is merely the bright phase of the water. The two sides of the expansion joint move apart by less than the width of an atom. The city senses this and expands to follow.

Sirens issue a single low note. Buildings unlock from one another. Streets become flexible. The water thickens until loose objects can rest on it, and the entire settlement unfolds into the new space. New rooms appear in hairline gaps. Bridges lengthen. Reservoirs bloom between pipes. Then the stone plates ease together again, but the city does not completely contract. It hides the excess volume in folded chambers, growing incrementally with every tectonic breath.

This is its principal weather.

Other weather is internal. Warm fronts are released to prevent brittle failure, passing through neighborhoods as visible veils. Sudden vertical currents lift grit, tools, and unattended furniture toward the ceiling. Brine storms erupt when memory banks are flushed: walls of concentrated salt sweep through designated canals, carrying obsolete records as glimmering crystals. Sometimes the illumination falters and the water turns gray. During these intervals, inhabitants—if there are inhabitants—are expected to stop moving.

Evidence of occupancy is everywhere, but bodies are not.

Transit schedules update. Doors open and close. Workbenches hold incomplete objects. Messages accumulate on public surfaces. Cups cling magnetically beside communal heaters, filled with liquids heavier than the surrounding water. Apartments are warmed, decorated, and meticulously maintained. Yet there are no animals, and therefore no human beings in any conventional biological sense.

The citizens may be patterns traveling through the municipal water.

Certain currents pause at intersections as if deciding where to go. Lamps brighten around them. Doors admit volumes of water and close afterward. In crowded districts, streams braid around one another with unmistakable courtesy. Some occupy pressure suits shaped vaguely like people, but the suits contain only circulating brine, mineral dust, and faint knots of light. Others appear to inhabit buildings, vehicles, or stretches of pipe. Citizenship here is measured not by flesh but by continuity: a legally recognized persistence of temperature, charge, dissolved metals, and memory.

This produces peculiar urban rules. It is forbidden to boil public water without consent. Distillation is treated as bodily harm. Mixing currents can be marriage, conversation, commerce, or assault, depending on the valves opened beforehand. Privacy is achieved by freezing a thin shell around oneself. Death is not drowning—it is diffusion beyond recovery.

At the center of the city is the Empty Reservoir, a room perhaps three meters across, making it by far the largest open place in the seam. No water enters it. The surrounding ocean presses on its transparent walls with enough force to collapse ordinary matter, yet the chamber remains dry and brightly lit. Within stands a single young plant in a tray of black soil: a low, broad-leaved thing, green rather than silver or black.

Its leaves tremble constantly.

No mechanism touches them. There is no air current inside the chamber. The soil is fresh. Occasionally one leaf develops a small crescent-shaped bite along its edge, though the city contains no animal life and its instruments record no intrusion.

Each time this happens, the infrastructure roars louder. Trains halt. Construction pauses. Every mobile current gathers along the reservoir walls, packing the surrounding galleries until not another liter can enter. The city watches the missing piece of leaf.

Then the plates breathe apart.

New space opens in the seam, raw-edged and brilliantly lit, and the flooded metropolis rushes in to occupy it.
