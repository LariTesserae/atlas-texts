Beneath the surface lies a room no wider than a cottage and deeper than it should be.

It is not a cave. There is no stone in sight.

Roots enclose it, but even that is misleading. Roots normally travel through something—soil, fissures, damp rubble. Here they are the ground, the walls, and the distant-seeming ceiling. They have grown around an absence, making a hollow within a nearly continuous body of vegetation. Fine root-hairs knit into russet felt underfoot. Pale cords descend in widely spaced pillars, while high overhead immense taproots pass through one another in slow, muscular braids. The chamber is hidden inside a living mass so complete that “underground” no longer means beneath plants. It means inside one.

For all the abundance of life, the hollow is nearly empty.

That emptiness is its most immediate quality. A few strands cross the open air, glistening with condensation. A single curled leaf hangs without visible attachment. Knuckles of fresh green tissue protrude here and there from the walls, bright as peeled fruit. Between them spreads a surprising volume of clear, warm air. The eye expects undergrowth and clutter, but finds distance instead: an intimate void, contained yet difficult to measure. The far side might be twelve paces away. It sometimes appears to be fifty.

Light has no source.

It is ordinary-looking light—soft, warm, sufficient to show color and cast mild shadows—but there is no opening above, no luminous fungus, no flame. The roots themselves are not visibly glowing. Illumination simply occupies the chamber as air does. It strengthens and fades in cycles resembling daylight, though those cycles may last twenty minutes or several days. Shadows usually fall downward. Occasionally they lean toward whatever is looking at them.

Moisture arrives intermittently. Warm droplets condense along root tips, swell, and fall in a brief interior rain. Most vanish into the floor, leaving dark circular stains that close like pupils. Some gather in shallow pockets between root ridges, forming pools no larger than bowls. These pools are astonishingly deep. A finger lowered into one meets no bottom; a dropped seed may be heard striking water again far below, then again farther off, each impact louder than the last.

There is never enough water to make the place lush in the familiar sense. Its vegetation does not depend on streams or saturated earth. It is self-supporting, feeding on stored heat, minerals carried through far-reaching roots, and perhaps on the dimensions it has displaced. The chamber is damp rather than wet: a place of sweat, breath, and sudden showers, followed by long warm intervals when every surface grows taut and matte.

The tissue is young. Cuts in the root walls show no old rings, no mineral staining, no accumulated decay. Everything has the raw geometry of recent growth. Root edges split into sharp fins. New shoots break from the floor in translucent spikes, then bend over and fuse into it before leaves can open. Pale seams appear across the walls with the crispness of fresh scars, widen for an hour, and seal by evening. The whole hollow seems newly made, although nothing suggests what occupied the space before it.

It is also loud.

The plant’s internal transport is audible everywhere. Sap hammers through major vessels in heavy, regular surges. Fibers stretch with the groan of green timber. Root caps force their way through unseen substrate beyond the walls, producing prolonged grinding roars. Small pockets of gas collapse with percussive cracks. During a growth pulse, the chamber bellows from all sides at once: tearing, knocking, rushing, the thunder of a forest compressed into one organism.

Yet there are quieter sounds nested inside the noise. A dry clicking follows visitors from behind. Pools whisper syllables just below comprehension. Sometimes a falling drop hangs motionless in the air while its impact is heard repeatedly beneath it. At other times the chamber becomes silent without the movement ceasing. Roots continue to snap and writhe, but their sound is delayed, arriving all at once several minutes later in a deafening avalanche.

Animals are rare because the place offers no stable food web. Tiny white mites graze the fleeting films that form after rain. Colorless gnats emerge from closed root tissue, fly in perfect squares, and return through the unbroken surface. Once in a long while, a narrow-bodied creature passes between the hanging cords. It has the posture of a lizard but folds rather than walks, reducing itself joint by joint until it becomes a flat green mark on the wall. The mark then photosynthesizes.

No predator is consistently present. The chamber itself may fill that role.

Seeds enter from nowhere. During the dim phase they appear suspended in the open air, widely scattered, each rotating around an axis different from the chamber’s. Some sprout before landing. Their roots extend upward, their leaves downward, and for a short time each seedling establishes a private gravity. Droplets curve toward them. Loose hairs stand on end. A nearby traveler may feel one foot becoming heavier than the other. Most seedlings are eventually absorbed into the walls. A few continue to grow into directions the eye can see but the hand cannot follow.

The rules of distance are weakest near the ceiling. A root cord viewed from one side may pass behind itself; walked around, it becomes two cords with a bright gap between them. Touching both at once causes the fingers to feel each other, as if the root were absent. Looking upward too long gives the impression that the chamber is not enclosed by roots but suspended from a much smaller chamber above, which is suspended from another, and so on—an inverted canopy of nested hollows dwindling into brilliance.

The floor is more dependable, though not inert. It breathes shallowly. Root-hair felt rises under weight and firms into temporary paths, then loosens after passage. Footprints do not remain where they were made. They migrate to the walls over several hours, climbing in dark sequences until they reach the ceiling and fade. If two beings cross the hollow together, their tracks may exchange owners.

Nothing has been built here. There are no walls beyond growth, no tools left behind, no worked surfaces. Nevertheless, the place invites primitive craft. Flexible root-skins peel away in strips suitable for cord. Hardened thorn buds can serve as awls or blades. Cup-shaped husks catch condensation. A practiced hand might braid a foothold, cut a drinking tube, or bind a charm against the shifting pull of gravity. Such objects cannot be kept for long. Separated plant material remains loyal to the greater organism. Cordage sends rootlets into the hands that carry it. Bowls slowly invert and pour their water upward. Thorn blades soften when brought near living tissue, refusing to cut what they came from.

Fire fares poorly. A spark can persist, but flame opens green instead of orange and gives off cool shadows. Smoke sinks into the floor. Charcoal, if somehow made, germinates.

The hollow changes quickly, though not always forward in time. A new ridge may thrust across the floor in minutes, gleaming and tender. An hour later it is absent—not withered or reabsorbed, but not yet grown. Rain sometimes falls before the droplets condense. Broken fibers recoil a moment before they are stepped on. These reversals are local and brief, passing through the chamber like gusts. One can recognize them by the smell: crushed stems, hot dust, and the sharp sweetness of fruit that has not formed.

At the height of activity, the chamber undergoes what might be called flowering.

The walls pull apart by a handspan, revealing neither earth nor rock but a bright, depthless green. Sap pressure rises. Every root cord snaps straight. The empty center fills with a roar like continuous applause, and huge petal-shaped absences open in the air. They are not objects; they are missing portions of the room, outlined by drifting pollen. Through them can be glimpsed impossible adjacent places: a sunlit field beneath miles of soil, a dry river flowing vertically, another hollow where roots observe with animal eyes.

Then the petals close. The roots slacken. Warm rain falls for a few minutes, loud on the living floor.

Afterward the chamber is smaller or larger by some modest amount. One hanging strand has changed position. One pool is gone. A new creature may be crouched against the wall, pale and bewildered, carrying no evidence of where it came from.

The light resumes its sourceless afternoon.

The plant continues growing around the emptiness, fiercely and endlessly, as though the hollow were not a flaw in its body but its rarest organ—an underground clearing cultivated for the production of distance, noise, and impossible arrivals.
