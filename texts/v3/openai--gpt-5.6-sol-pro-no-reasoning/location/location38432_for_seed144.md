At the top of the hot world stands a country of fins.

They crowd a high plateau nearly five kilometers above the sea: thousands of vertical plates of rust-colored stone, some thin as walls, some broad enough to carry whole slopes on their backs. From a distance they resemble a city seen edge-on—packed towers, tilted roofs, crowded avenues—but there are almost no buildings here. The apparent architecture is geology. Beds once laid flat have been folded upward and worn into parallel blades, close-set over an area larger than a valley. Between them run narrow corridors of shade, talus-filled courts, blind gullies, and sudden openings from which the horizon can be seen for a hundred kilometers.

The air is both scorching and thin.

At noon the stone is too hot to touch. Sunlight falls with little softness in it, clean and ordinary in color but fierce from altitude: blue sky, white glare, dark shadow. Exposed metal burns the hand. Bare rock releases the smell of hot iron and dust. Yet sweat vanishes almost at once, and every steep climb makes the heart pound. The plateau gives the contradictory impression of being inside a kiln built on the roof of the atmosphere.

The country is packed, though not lush. Almost every available space is occupied—not by vegetation, but by surfaces, fractures, mineral crusts, nests, machines, spoor, ledges, and stone. There is scarcely a patch of level ground. One fin presses behind another. Boulder fields choke the troughs between them. Scree lies sorted by size beneath the cliffs, old enough that each fragment has settled into a stable angle. The place is weathered without looking ruined. Its forms have reached a long accommodation with gravity: sloping skirts beneath upright walls, channels following ancient weaknesses, polished saddles where wind and animals have crossed for generations.

Water comes rarely from above, but regularly enough to govern everything.

Most days are dry. Moisture arrives first as condensation. Before sunrise, warm air rising from the basin below meets the cold upper stone, and beads form on the shaded faces of the fins. The droplets gather along mineral ribs and descend into cracks. Pale lichens swell. Insects emerge to drink from seams no wider than a fingernail. Small ceramic gutters, fixed to certain cliffs by corroded brackets, collect the same dew and lead it through black hoses toward cisterns buried under stone.

Rain is more dramatic. Storms build in the late afternoon over distant lowlands and climb toward the plateau in dark columns. They may surround the country without touching it. When one does break overhead, the packed ravines become an instrument of water. Every fin sheds a sheet; every crack becomes a spout. Dry channels fill within minutes, brown and muscular, carrying pebbles, feathers, dung, and scraps of wire through the gorges. Pools appear in rock basins and persist for days or weeks beneath overhangs. A few deep clefts hold water throughout the year, hidden from the sun and cool enough to mist when opened to the hot air.

These pockets are the true centers of the plateau. Everything knows where they are.

Vegetation stands apart rather than spreading. Grey thorn shrubs grow from fault lines, their roots descending far beyond the visible plant. Tufts of wiregrass occupy the lee of boulders. There are low, fleshy rosettes whose leaves remain tightly closed through the day and open after dusk to receive dew. Along the deepest water clefts grow narrow ribbons of green: sedge, dark moss, and a knee-high reed with copper-colored stems. No meadow joins these patches. Between them lies bare mineral country—red sandstone, black volcanic rubble, yellow crusts of sulfurous salts, and chalky deposits left by evaporated pools.

From a distance the sparse plants seem inconsequential. Up close, each is an inhabited structure. Beetles shelter beneath the rosettes. Tiny pale frogs retreat into damp root hollows, sealing themselves behind membranes of dried mud between storms. Grass clumps hold nests woven from hair and discarded cable insulation. Flowers, when they open, may receive dozens of visitors in an hour and then remain untouched for months.

Animal life is abundant, but much of it avoids the open glare.

At noon, the plateau appears almost empty. Heat shimmers between the fins. A black kite circles so high that it looks fixed to the sky. Lizards press themselves into the thin strips of shadow, shifting sideways as the sun moves. Colonies of cliff swifts occupy holes in the stone walls; they leave at dawn in such numbers that their passage resembles smoke, though the sound is only a dry flickering of wings.

The larger animals travel in the morning, evening, or storm light. Long-legged rock antelope move single-file along ledges barely wider than their hooves. Their coats are the color of oxidized stone, and their horns curve backward close to the skull so they can pass through tight slots. Troops of small black-faced primates raid the cistern pipes, testing valves with practiced fingers. They know which inspection caps can be twisted loose. Around permanent pools, tracks overlap until the mud becomes unreadable: cat, antelope, rodent, bird, snake, maintenance boot.

The plateau’s principal predator is a lean, high-chested cat that hunts by using the fins as screens. It rarely runs far. It waits at intersections where several corridors meet and listens for hooves on stone. The antelope, in turn, rely on echoes. They stamp before entering a narrow passage; the returning sound reveals whether the way ahead is open, blocked, or occupied by something soft. This produces one of the country’s few recurring noises: a solitary knock, a pause, then another knock farther away.

Otherwise, the quiet is immense.

There is wind, but the stone divides it. Above the fins it can be violent, streaming dust eastward in long banners; inside the corridors it dwindles to intermittent breaths. An insect buzz can travel around corners. Pebbles dislodged by an unseen animal click downward for a surprisingly long time. On hot afternoons the cliffs themselves make sounds—short cracks and hollow ticks as their sunlit faces expand while their interiors remain cold.

Human industry exists here in fragments connected across distance.

The visible structures are small: a pump shed sunk into a saddle, a corrugated shelter bolted beneath an overhang, a lattice mast on the highest ridge, a row of heat-bleached marker posts. Yet buried pipes and armored cables thread the plateau. They belong to a dispersed extraction system built to harvest pressure, not ore. Far below, hot gases move through porous layers trapped under the uplifted beds. Boreheads in a dozen remote clefts draw the gases upward. Compressors dry and separate them, then send them through narrow pipelines down the escarpment to works in the basin.

There is no single factory to dominate the view. The factory is the landscape-wide network itself.

Its machines have adapted to the packed terrain. Compressor housings are wedged vertically between fins. Service rails climb slopes too steep for roads, carrying one yellow maintenance cradle at a time. Pumps wake only when underground pressure reaches a threshold, so their activity comes in isolated episodes. A turbine begins somewhere beyond three ridges: a low electric hum, the soft hammering of a valve, then silence. Warning lamps blink even at midday, modest red points against enormous stone.

The system collects water as carefully as gas. Condensate from the pipes drains into sealed tanks. Cooling vanes are shaded and turned toward the night sky. Dew gutters span cliff faces that no person could reach without ropes. Some of these devices are old enough to have become ecological fixtures. Swifts nest behind the vanes. Wasps build paper combs inside disused control boxes. Mineral seepage has covered steel anchor plates with blue-green crusts. Animals drink from the slow drip beneath pressure regulators, and predators wait nearby.

Nothing is abandoned exactly, though much of it looks so. Maintenance crews come only in pairs or trios, traveling the aerial rails before dawn. They inspect, replace, lubricate, and leave. Their shelters contain narrow bunks, ceramic water jars, spare filters, and thick manuals printed on heatproof film. No settlement survives on the plateau itself. The thin air, violent runoff, and absence of broad ground forbid it. The workers’ town lies far below, visible at night as a dim geometry on the basin floor.

Several older constructions predate the industrial network. Stone steps ascend to ledges with no present purpose. Shallow cups have been cut into the tops of certain fins, positioned to catch the first rainfall. Here and there, walls seal narrow clefts; behind them lies damp sediment, animal bone, and blackened grain. These structures are too scattered to form ruins in the usual sense. They seem more like corrections made to the geology by people who understood its hidden circulation.

One such wall bears a rule in three scripts:

**TAKE WATER AFTER IT HAS OVERFLOWED ONCE.**

The industrial crews still observe this. So do the herders who occasionally bring animals up from the lower escarpment. The reason is partly practical: the first water of a storm washes dust, droppings, salts, and dead insects from every surface. But the rule has acquired wider force. No tank is opened at the first rain. No pool is approached while it is still rising. Even the plateau’s animals tend to hesitate, waiting above the channels until the initial brown flood has passed and the basins begin to clear.

There are other apparent rules, though no one has written them down. Shade belongs first to whatever is already in it. Loose stone is never thrown into a cleft, because a cleft may breathe gas. A humming pipe should not be stepped across. If the antelope stamp twice and receive no echo, the corridor is avoided.

The strangest feature of the plateau occurs during the hottest part of the dry season. Air trapped in the stone corridors separates into layers of sharply different temperature. Sounds crossing these layers bend. A machine operating nearby may appear to murmur from kilometers away, while a falling rock on a distant ridge seems to strike just behind the listener. Bird calls descend from empty ground. Footsteps arrive before the walker is visible.

The local animals navigate this distortion better than people. Swifts alter their flight by tiny increments. Antelope hold their heads at uneven angles, listening with one ear high and one low. Workers carry paired bells tuned to different pitches; if the higher bell seems farther away than the lower one, they know the air has begun to fold sound and suspend nonessential travel.

Toward evening the whole country changes without becoming cooler at once. The stone retains the day’s heat and releases it slowly. Shadows rise out of the gullies and join across the fins. Plants unfold. Beetles climb to exposed ridges to collect condensation that has not yet formed. Bats issue from drill holes, feeding above the water pockets and around the warm machinery. The cat begins to move.

Far below, the basin darkens first. Up here the last light remains on the highest blades, turning them from red to orange, from orange to a brief clear gold. For a few minutes every mineral seam is visible. The industrial masts, stone cups, thorn branches, nesting holes, pipes, and old steps all cast long shadows in the same direction, and the landscape ceases to distinguish between what grew, what eroded, and what was installed.

Then the sun drops.

Pumps start in the cooling air. Their separate vibrations pass through the rock without merging into a roar. A valve opens with a distant exhalation. Somewhere in a concealed pool, frogs begin calling—only a handful, but the fins repeat them until the plateau seems full of water.

It is not. It is full of stone, heat, animals, machinery, and the expectation of water: every surface crowded, every hollow claimed, the whole high country waiting for its next brief flood.
