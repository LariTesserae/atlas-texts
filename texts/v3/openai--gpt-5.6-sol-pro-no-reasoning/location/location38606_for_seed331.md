The basin is small enough to cross before noon, though no one does. Its boundaries are low, rounded ridges worn so thoroughly by age that they scarcely deserve the name: long backs of pale stone rising from a warm plain, without ledges or peaks, polished by water, roots, and uncounted seasons into a final, restful shape. The place resembles an amphitheater whose walls have slumped almost flat.

From the rim, the basin appears mostly empty. Bright grass lies in loose patches over apricot-colored mud. Pools reflect a white sky. A few broad trees stand far apart, each attended by its own island of shade, and between them are occasional structures too slight to read at a distance. Nothing crowds anything else. There is ample space for light to strike the ground.

Only after descending does the fauna become apparent.

It is everywhere.

The mud is crosshatched with minute tracks. Transparent mites raft across puddles on skins of trapped air. In the thin water below the grass, schools of needle-shaped fish arrange themselves around each footstep without ever quite touching it. Beetles with mirrored wing cases emerge from holes in the stone, warm themselves for several seconds, and vanish again. Narrow-bodied lizards lie along fallen branches in such exact alignment that the branches seem to have grown eyes. Every patch of shade holds pale moths; every shaft of sun is sifted by gnats.

Yet the basin is almost quiet.

Wings open without beating. Small jaws work silently. Frogs inflate their throats but release only faint pulses beneath the threshold of ordinary hearing, felt as a softness in the teeth. Even the larger animals tread with an inexplicable care. The loudest natural sound is usually water: drops leaving the underside of leaves, a thread spilling down stone, the occasional soft collapse of saturated earth.

Water is near the surface everywhere. Press a heel into the ground and the print slowly fills. The oldest rocks weep continuously, though there are no springs visible in their upper faces. Moisture condenses along shallow grooves and travels against gravity for short distances before gathering into clear beads. In the mornings, every trunk shines as if varnished. By midday the heat has lifted much of that water into a low, luminous haze, but the basin never dries. Rain comes frequently and briefly, descending in warm, nearly vertical curtains while sunlight remains bright beyond it.

The stone beneath the basin is immensely old. It contains no dramatic strata, no sharp fractures, no record legible at human scale. Whatever mountains once stood here have been reduced past ruin, their substance ground, dissolved, eaten by roots, and laid down again as clay. Even the pebbles are flattened discs with edges soft as fruit. Erosion no longer seems destructive here. It is the condition into which everything has settled—the long completion of hard things.

The trees share that appearance of advanced age without being large. Their trunks are low and broad, divided early into horizontal limbs. Leaves occur in widely spaced fans, thick and green, each with a silver underside that flashes when rain approaches. Moss grows only in cool seams, but liverworts cover the wet stones in intricate, dark rosettes. Reeds gather around the deeper pools, never densely; they stand an arm’s length apart, their roots connected by translucent cords under the mud.

Those cords are not entirely biological.

Here and there, emerging from the basin floor, are objects like small roofless shrines: three or four slabs of dull material arranged around a hollow. Some are no taller than a knee. The largest could shelter two people sitting close together. Their surfaces have no seams, and age has neither pitted nor discolored them. Lichen avoids them. Water touching their sides divides into evenly spaced droplets and climbs.

At first they seem abandoned. Then, in certain angles of sunlight, faint structures appear between them.

Not beams or walls, but distortions—planes where insects alter course, where rain slows and hangs for a moment before continuing downward. A dragonfly passing through one may emerge from another enclosure several hundred paces away. Sometimes it returns with pollen from a plant not found anywhere in the basin. Tiny animals understand these routes perfectly. They queue at empty air, pass through one by one, and disappear.

The basin’s overwhelming abundance is organized through this invisible architecture.

Each pool is vastly larger inside than its visible banks allow. Fish descend through a handspan of brown water and do not return for hours; when they rise, cold darkness streams briefly from their scales. Burrows intersect places that should not meet. A colony of thumb-sized mammals occupies the hollow beneath a single flat stone, although thousands emerge from it at dusk. The open spacing of the landscape is therefore misleading. What can be seen is only the set of entrances. The true habitat is folded into surfaces, distributed among pockets of distance and climate that current geometry would not hold.

This may explain the quiet. Sound does not travel normally through the basin. It is absorbed, redirected, or stored. A snapped twig can be heard days later from inside a pool. Thunder occasionally issues from a dry pebble after a storm has passed. Calls are delivered privately: one animal opens its mouth, and only the intended animal turns its head. Predators hunt without alarm spreading. Prey flee according to signals that leave the air otherwise undisturbed.

The largest creatures are long, lightly built grazers with six jointed legs and skin the gray-green of wet bark. They move alone between the trees, cropping only one leaf from each plant. Flocks of tiny white birds travel over them, entering vents along their flanks and emerging clean, as though the animals contain rooms. In the deeper marsh lives something broad and submerged. It is rarely seen, but rafts of insects rise and drift when it passes beneath, and the reeds bow outward in orderly rings. Nothing panics. The ecology is violent in detail—larvae devour larvae, fish take the gnats, lizards take the fish—but composed at a distance, every chase local and almost ceremonially brief.

At the center of the basin stands the only structure recognizably made for upright inhabitants. It is a narrow pavilion with a floor sunk ankle-deep in clear water. Four dark columns support no visible roof, though rain never falls between them. A bench curves around an empty circular space. On its surface are shallow depressions polished by use: some hand-sized, others suited to paws, talons, coils, or bodies with no familiar symmetry.

The pavilion still functions. At noon, when the light is fiercest, points of brightness assemble above its center and make a slowly rotating map—not of stars, but of living things. Each animal in the basin appears as a minute spark. The sparks descend through the floor in branching layers, revealing populations hidden in folded pools, distant burrows, interiors of leaves, and chambers tucked into fractions of space. The visible basin is only the bright topmost membrane of an ecology extending impossibly far inward.

No maker remains to interpret the display. Mud nests cling to the columns. A vine has threaded one side, producing a few blue flowers that close whenever approached. On the bench lies a tool or toy: a smooth ring that casts the shadow of a many-branched tree, though there is no such tree nearby.

Toward evening the heat loosens. Condensation begins first on the shrine-slabs, then on stone, hide, shell, and leaf. The basin shines. Animals emerge in numbers the eye cannot count, but they spread rather than mass: one presence in every interval, a body at each pool, a wing in each space between trees. The ground subtly rearranges its hollows as burrows open. Invisible passages switch destinations. For several minutes, reflections show different skies.

Still, almost nothing is heard.

There is the tapping of water. A soft stirring in the reeds. Once in a while, from very far away or very deep inside the nearest stone, a single clear note arrives. At that note, every creature pauses—not rigidly, not in fear, but as if listening to an old instruction.

Then the bright insects resume their measured flight. Fish stipple the pools. Warm rain begins under an unclouded sky, and the ancient basin receives it without changing shape.
