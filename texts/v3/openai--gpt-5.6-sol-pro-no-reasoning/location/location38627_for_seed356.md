There is no ground in the ordinary sense.

The continent is a single plant.

It lies low across the warm basin of the world, broader than weather systems, its upper surface rising and falling by only a few dozen meters across distances that would take seasons to cross. What appears from above to be a dark plain is an unbroken anatomy: overlapping plates of leaf, trunks spread horizontally until they have become terrain, roots woven into watertight floors, and vast, soft organs whose purpose is not immediately apparent. The entire landscape is alive, but it does not visibly grow.

Nothing here moves.

The oldest visible surfaces are not old. They retain the hard-edged freshness of things newly unfolded: ribs sharp as ridgelines, seams unweathered, leaf margins precise enough to resemble black glass. Yet no new tissue emerges, and none decays. The place occupies a permanent instant just after its own formation. Moisture beads on every surface without swelling, falling, or evaporating. Splits in the vegetation shine with recent sap that never dries. The landscape seems to have completed itself all at once and then entered equilibrium.

Darkness is its normal climate. There may be a sun elsewhere, but little of it reaches the surface. A canopy hundreds of meters thick arches over the continental plant, though “canopy” is too simple a word. There are roofs above roofs: leaves like inland seas, suspended veils of root hair, black fans fitted together edge to edge. They intercept the light, consume it, and pass downward only a dim vegetal afterglow. In the deepest places, vision gives way entirely. Forms can be sensed by differences in dampness and warmth: a trunk-wall radiates mild heat; a reservoir leaf breathes cold against the skin; pale sap channels show as long, nearly invisible lines.

The air is warm without being hot. It clings. Moisture condenses in it continuously, not as rain but as a universal fine wetness. Every exposed edge bears an exact row of droplets. Each droplet is clear, convex, and motionless. On the undersides of leaves, water hangs in great inverted lakes held by surface tension, some spanning kilometers. They bow downward between structural veins but never break. Their presence can be felt as a cool pressure from above.

Where the living floor dips, there are marshes. They contain little open water. Instead, the water is threaded through green-black mats, held in bladders, capillaries, translucent stems, and porous tissue. Walking there—if walking were possible without damage—would mean passing over a buoyant weave that gives slightly and then holds. Beneath it is neither soil nor mud, but older layers of the same organism compressed into dense, wet fiber. No rot has touched them. There are no bacteria, worms, larvae, or grazing mouths. Nothing converts the dead into earth because nothing is properly dead.

This absence of animals is more profound than emptiness. There are no wingbeats in the high dark, no insects working at flowers, no distant breathing. Leaves have no bite marks. Bark carries no tracks. Pollen remains sealed inside elaborate organs that will never need visitors. Fruits, where they occur, are massive closed vessels without sweetness, seed casings built for a dispersal that does not happen. The plant possesses all the architecture of ecology but is itself the entire ecology.

Its industrial character becomes apparent slowly.

At first, the straight channels might be mistaken for veins. Then one notices their consistency. They run beneath the surface in parallel bundles, branching at fixed intervals, carrying milky fluids at distinct temperatures. Pressure chambers repeat across the plain with a regularity too broad to perceive from any single vantage. Stomata open into shafts descending kilometers through stacked tissue. Deep below, root systems separate dissolved minerals, sort them by ionic charge, and feed them into specialized growth foundries. Membranes act as valves. Woody pistons rest inside cellulose cylinders. Long belts of articulated seed scales pass through dark cavities, each scale holding an identical mineral nodule.

There are no buildings and no machines placed upon the landscape. The plant is factory, grid, pipeline, refinery, warehouse, and product.

Its engines do not turn. Their state is stranger than rest. In every vascular turbine, pressure is perfectly balanced. In every sap column, molecules are poised at the threshold of flow, yet the gradients that would move them have been abolished. Electrical potentials extend across root nets wider than nations, but remain constant. Vast batteries of acidic fruit hold their charge. Chloroplast fields under the upper leaves contain stored daylight from some previous age. Nothing leaks. Nothing discharges.

The result is an industry without labor and a mechanism without noise.

Now and then—though “now and then” may only be the observer’s own body intruding on the silence—there seems to be a faint ticking. It is not rhythmic. It may be condensation tightening on a leaf tip, or the small strain of kilometers of wet tissue bearing its own weight. Press an ear to a trunk and there is no rush of sap. Instead there is a barely perceptible electrical hum, too low to be heard so much as felt in the teeth. Farther down, under the marsh-layers, something gives off a continuous note at the edge of sensation. It never rises or fades.

No wind crosses the plain. The air has been divided by vegetation into countless chambers, each close, warm, and saturated. Some are the size of rooms, others the size of seas. Their climates are internal properties of the plant. One chamber holds a permanent fog whose droplets remain evenly suspended. Another contains air so clear and still that distant leaf columns appear unnaturally near. In the broadest hollows, low banks of vapor take the shapes of weather fronts but do not advance. Lightning exists in a few of them as silent filaments trapped between conductive vines: pale blue branching frozen across the dark.

The terrain, though low, is not featureless. Great leaf faults rise from the plain, their edges raw and geometric, revealing layers of purple pith and silver vascular cable. Root deltas spread across depressions in branching fans. Young woody escarpments stand sharp above black pools. There are calderas that are in fact open flowers, each tens of kilometers across, their petals thick enough to carry forests of smaller organs. At their centers, stamens rise like narrow mountain ranges, dusted with inert pollen whose grains are each the size of a fist.

Beyond them extend the production fields.

Here the organism grows repeated forms in numbers beyond counting: identical pods arrayed to the horizon, hexagonal bark cells, spiral condensers of vine, ranked towers of hollow reed. Their order is not imposed from outside. It is the order of phyllotaxis enlarged to continental scale. Every fifth tower aligns. Every thirteenth channel intersects a reservoir. Looking along those alignments gives the unsettling impression that the darkness itself has been machined.

Yet no tool has ever entered this place.

The vegetation is dense, but not tangled. Each body—or each apparent body, since all may be one—fits closely beside the next. Leaves slot into gaps. Roots braid without crushing one another. Bulbs nest within hollows shaped precisely for them. There is almost no unused volume. Even the air is occupied by hairs, membranes, suspended tendrils, and thin photosensitive films. Passageways exist, but they resemble conduits left open for a fluid that has not arrived. Some narrow gradually until they become capillaries. Others broaden into avenues hundreds of meters wide, floored with dark bark and roofed by a continuous arch of leaf.

The largest avenue runs toward the basin’s center.

For thousands of kilometers it remains perfectly level. Water shines along both edges in motionless gutters. On either side, trunk-pillars emerge from the floor and vanish upward into darkness. Their surfaces are warm and faintly ridged, each ridge carrying sealed pores like rows of rivets. There is no destination visible ahead. Perspective collapses before the avenue ends.

At the center of the continent stands—or grows—the Still Heart.

It is not elevated. It occupies a shallow depression, almost a wetland, and can be overlooked until one is close. There, all the vascular lines of the world-plant gather beneath a translucent membrane. Through it can be seen an immense folded organ, glossy and dark red, divided into chambers large enough to contain weather. Around it lie rings of pale root valves, millions of them, nested with mathematical precision.

The Heart is neither beating nor dead. Each chamber is full. Every valve is ready. The pressure on one side exactly equals the pressure on the other. It has held this position for an interval that cannot be measured because the landscape provides no change by which duration might be known.

Above it hangs a single drop of water.

The drop is immense, perhaps a kilometer from surface to surface, condensed around a lattice of root hairs. Within it, inverted and dim, the Heart is reflected. The water does not fall. No ripple crosses it. Its lower curve almost touches the central membrane, leaving a gap no wider than a hand.

Everything in the landscape appears arranged around that gap.

The charged fruits, the silent chemical furnaces, the sealed flowers, the motionless weather, the level waterways, the electrical roots: all are connected, all ready, all perfectly opposed. The plant has not ceased its work. It has brought every process to the exact verge of beginning and maintains the whole continent there.

In this world, equilibrium is not inactivity. It is production suspended at infinite scale.

Perhaps the drop is meant to fall. Perhaps the Heart is meant to contract. Perhaps the low dark plain is only the first organ of something that would, upon that contact, unfold beyond the horizon and alter the atmosphere of the planet.

But “meant” implies a future.

Here there is only warm darkness, wet leaf, stored power, and the almost inaudible tone of a living mechanism that never changes.
