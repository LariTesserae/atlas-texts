Beneath a continent of black salt lies the Stillwarm.

It is not a cavern in the ordinary sense. There are no chambers, no passages, no reliable distinction between open country and enclosed space. The buried land consists of countless mineral blades standing close together in darkness: fins, columns, flakes, and walls of dull crystal, packed so densely that travel becomes a matter of threading through the narrow intervals between them. Some rise only to the knee. Others continue upward beyond the reach of sound. Their roots vanish into a floor of compressed ash; their tips enter the unseen ceiling or stop just short of it, sharp and newly formed.

The entire region is warm. Not feverishly so, but with the steady warmth of stone that has held a day’s heat for centuries. There is no apparent source. The floor is warm, the pillars are warm, the air is warm, and even newly exposed surfaces have the same temperature. Nothing cools toward the heights or heats toward the depths. A traveler cannot use warmth to judge direction because there is no thermal direction here.

Nor is there water. The dark air is aggressively dry, tasting of alkali, iron, and a faint sweetness like scorched bone. Breath leaves no mist. Skin cracks unless rubbed with wax or animal grease. Bodies do not rot easily; they tighten, harden, and become light. In sheltered niches, the dead may remain kneeling for generations, leathered hands still gripping their tools.

The mineral forest is young but motionless. Its blades have the raw geometry of things created yesterday: knife edges without chips, fracture faces still brilliant beneath the dust, seams that fit together so precisely that a sliver of hammered copper cannot enter them. Yet nothing is seen growing. No crystals lengthen. No cracks advance. Dust hangs in some hollows but never seems to settle further. Tracks remain exactly as impressed until another foot crosses them.

This is the chief rule of the Stillwarm: nothing changes by itself.

A stone placed on a ledge remains there. A column loosened by blows will not fall when the final supporting chip is removed; it waits until it is pushed. Powder thrown into the air travels only as far as the hand’s force carries it and then becomes fixed in a dim, suspended cloud. Echoes, when they occur, are similarly stubborn. A hard strike can leave a thin metallic note caught between two blades, audible for years from one precise spot and nowhere else.

The buried land is therefore full of old actions.

At a narrow pass, one hears three footsteps from someone long vanished. Beside a low red ridge, there is the rasp of a saw drawn once across stone. Farther in, a child’s brief laugh is pinned beneath an overhang, so faint that it might be mistaken for insects. Hunters mark routes by making sounds rather than carving signs. A clap lodged at shoulder height means shelter. Two clicks near the floor mean danger. A sustained scrape warns that the passage ahead does not return.

Away from these marks, the vastness is almost silent.

Light is scarce and local. There is no sun, no luminous fungus, no flame that sustains itself. Fire burns only while directly tended; unattended coals neither spread nor die, but sit in a fixed red condition, giving almost no heat beyond what the country already possesses. The inhabitants carry shallow lamps made from fired black clay. In them, rendered fat is drawn through cords braided from animal sinew. The flames are small, blue at the base and amber only at their tips.

Such lamps reveal very little at once. A mineral fin appears, pale as an old tooth; behind it, ten more surfaces answer with weak reflections. Shadows layer upon shadows until the country seems not empty and dark but overcrowded by darkness itself. Distances are difficult to judge. A wall glimpsed across a gap may be an arm’s length away or the face of a formation large enough to contain a city.

There are no plants. Nothing roots, sprouts, flowers, or turns toward light. The local ecology begins instead with a grey film that coats certain salts. It is not moist and appears no different under magnification from ordinary dust. Given darkness, warmth, and the pressure of untouched stone, however, it slowly produces brittle threads of sulphur and carbon. It does so without visible change. The people call this film *the first hunger*.

Tiny animals graze it: many-legged slate mites, glass-backed crawlers, and soft white things that fold themselves through pores narrower than their bodies. Their mouths are crystal scrapers. Their blood is oily and black, thick enough not to evaporate. They do not breed in the usual sense. When one is cut in two, each half remains inert until pressed against unworked mineral; then, sometimes, both halves resume living.

Larger creatures feed upon them in astonishing numbers.

The gaps between the blades are crowded with shell-bodied grazers, blind ribbon animals, warm-skinned burrowers, and long jointed predators whose limbs feel ahead of them like walking canes. Most move rarely. Movement must be chosen here; no current, slope, hunger pang, or changing temperature urges anything onward. An animal can wait in one posture for decades. When it acts, it does so suddenly and completely.

A herd may occupy what first appears to be a field of rounded stones. At the touch of a lamp’s warmth—not because the warmth is greater, but because it has been deliberately carried there—the whole herd unfolds. Hundreds of low creatures rise on six polished legs. Their shells knock together once or twice, then they stream through the mineral fins without another sound. When they stop, they stop all at once.

Predators imitate architecture. The largest are called clasp-worms, though they resemble no worm: two vertical plates joined by a muscular hinge, their outer surfaces crusted in the same salts as the columns. They wait open, indistinguishable from narrow clefts. Small animals pass between their halves until some obscure count is reached. Then the plates close.

Nothing triggers them accidentally. A fall of dust cannot startle them because dust does not fall. A sound cannot lure them unless someone first makes it. This makes the fauna at once abundant and eerily absent. Every direction contains life, near-touching and layered in cracks, under ledges, inside translucent stone—but most of it is paused.

Human settlements, if the inhabitants are human, are scattered far apart.

They build where several broad mineral blades lean close enough to roof a pocket of open ground. Walls are stacked from flat shards and bound with a black cement made by grinding shell, salt, and grease. Since structures do not slump unless acted upon, the masonry can be impossibly delicate. Homes have ceilings of overlapping flakes no thicker than fingernails. Bridges consist of long splinters balanced end to end over dark chasms. A finger could destroy them. Centuries do not.

Metal is rare. Copper occurs in thin green veins and is worried out with antler picks, stone mauls, and wedges of fired shell. It is too precious for weapons. Copper needles, lamp hooks, surgical blades, and tuning forks pass from parent to child until the metal wears translucent. Most cutting is done with chipped volcanic glass brought from a distant buried region known only through trade.

The settlements have no fields. They maintain grazing walls coated with first hunger and keep shell herds in fenced galleries. They trap slate mites in porous jars. Meat is sliced thin and dried, though everything is already dry. The purpose is ceremonial as much as practical: food should bear the sign of preparation. To eat an animal exactly as found is considered dangerously close to becoming part of the Stillwarm’s unchosen stillness.

Their doors are curtains of linked shell disks. These do not sway. One must part them by hand, and afterward place every disk back into its intended arrangement. A doorway left disturbed is an accusation: someone has passed through and refused to complete the act.

There are paths, but no roads. Frequent travel polishes the ash floor into narrow black ribbons winding among the fins. Because prints endure, crowded routes become thick records of feet, claws, dragged burdens, and walking staffs. Track-readers do not merely follow a person. They identify which impressions are recent by studying intention: the depth of a hurried step, the angle at which a traveler avoided an old print, the placement of dust by a hand.

Beyond the inhabited pockets, the buried country expands to a scale no expedition has measured. There are reports of column plains wide enough to generate their own pressure tides, though the tides do not move unless disturbed. There are vertical territories where the floor drops away and thousands of crystal shelves hang in darkness, each carrying its own motionless menagerie. There are hollows filled with suspended black grains, every particle fixed in place, forming dunes one can walk through only by pushing the sand aside with the body.

Deepest of all is said to be the Unmade Distance: an open space so vast that lamp signals cannot cross it. Explorers standing at its rim feel warm air on their faces, but it is not wind. It is simply air occupying them as they occupy it, pressing evenly from all directions. Sounds cast into that gulf do not echo. They travel outward and remain somewhere beyond sight.

Occasionally, surveyors find structures there.

A single stair cut into a freestanding slab, ascending thirty steps and ending in darkness.

A circle of stone seats facing inward toward nothing.

A door of hammered native copper set into solid mineral, with no chamber behind it.

A row of low cairns, each containing the dried body of an animal unknown elsewhere in the Stillwarm. The bodies have too many joints and smooth depressions where mouths might have been. Their poses are identical, not approximately but exactly, down to each curled digit.

These artifacts show only hand workmanship. Their marks are rough, their measurements irregular, their tools no more advanced than those used in the settlements. Yet no known people claim them, and some are found in places that cannot be reached without breaking the surrounding stone. Since nothing here closes or heals by itself, this suggests either that the artifacts were built before the mineral forest existed or that their makers entered by a direction the inhabitants cannot perceive.

The people do not regard the Stillwarm as dead. Death, to them, is an event, and events require action. The buried world is something more difficult: a place at equilibrium, crowded with waiting causes.

They say the upper surface is different. They say dust travels there without being thrown, that temperature changes of its own accord, that lights cross an unreachable ceiling, and that water—an almost mythical substance—moves merely because it is placed upon a slope. Such stories are told as marvels or warnings. A world where things happen unattended seems obscene.

Below, every change has an author.

Every fallen shard was struck.

Every extinguished lamp was pinched dark.

Every footprint belongs to a foot.

And wherever the mineral blades stand flawless, the animals hold their breath, and the old suspended sounds fail to stir, the Stillwarm remains exactly what it has always just become: immense, crowded, newly cut, and waiting for a hand.
