The highland begins above the clouds, though there are no clouds here and perhaps never have been. It is a broad mineral plateau suspended in warm, clear air, its horizon interrupted by low ridges that seem only partly decided. Their slopes are blunt, scarcely weathered, and mantled with pale fragments still sharp from their making. Nothing has yet had time—or the means—to round them. There are no gullies, no riverbeds, no alluvial fans. The terrain does not display the familiar handwriting of water. It looks assembled rather than eroded: slabs set down beside slabs, shallow basins pressed into the crust, escarpments lifted in single clean motions and then abandoned.

The light is ordinary enough to make the rest more disquieting. A steady sun gives the place honest colors and modest shadows. There is no perpetual dusk, no lurid sky. The heavens are a washed, breathable-looking blue, and the warmth is mild and even, as if the plateau were held at the temperature of a vast conservatory. Yet the air is astonishingly dry. Exposed salts do not cake or soften. Powder remains powder. Dead minerals have none of the darkening that moisture gives them.

Across the plateau grows a dispersed abundance of vegetation.

From a distance it resembles lushness: belts of green-black fronds, low bronze thickets, and wide patches of something like moss. Up close, the impression becomes stranger. These are not wet tissues. The fronds are thin vanes of carbon fiber and silicate, flexible only near their roots. The moss is a mat of branching mineral filaments, each tip capped with a tiny photosensitive crystal. Thick succulent forms rise here and there, but their swollen bodies contain no stored water. They are packed with wax, liquid hydrocarbons, and slow-reacting salts. When cut—though nothing here cuts them—they would not bleed sap. Their interiors would show concentric chambers filled with amber oil and soft metallic foam.

They grow sparsely in any one place, with hundreds of meters of bare ground between colonies, yet their domains are immense. A single grove may extend along a contour for thirty kilometers: not a forest, but a very long thought of a forest, each individual separated from the next by empty sunlit stone. Their buried systems are greater than what appears above. Beneath the plateau, hair-fine conductive roots span the distances, following seams of iron, sulfur, and carbon. What looks like many plants may be one organism distributed over a valley-sized circuit.

No animal life exists. There are no insects beneath the fronds, no burrow mouths, no distant birds. Nothing grazes, pollinates, hunts, decomposes, or cries out. Flowers, where they occur, are not invitations. They are dark radial collectors that unfold to face the sun and remain facing it forever. Reproduction is industrial rather than intimate. Deep under certain colonies, root-nodules refine raw minerals grain by grain, accumulating them around minute templates. Over centuries, a finished seed is assembled: a hard capsule containing catalysts, conductive threads, and enough hydrocarbon gel to begin another network. The ground slowly extrudes it. Static charge may lift it; thermal expansion may shift it a handspan. Most seeds remain beside their makers, unopened and immaculate.

This is where the plateau’s industry lies. There are no factories because the ecology itself performs the work of factories. No towers, roads, tools, or ruins disturb the surface. Production occurs in distributed organs hidden under stone. Solar vanes harvest light. Root furnaces exploit tiny chemical differences between mineral layers. Metallic nodules are purified, sorted, and redeposited. Carbon is drawn from the thin atmosphere and polymerized into stems. Silicates become translucent armor. Sulfur serves as both reagent and memory.

The processes are mechanized without machines, scaled without buildings, electrified without wires anyone has laid. At noon, a traveler might feel—not hear—a faint vibration through the soles: millions of microscopic valves changing state together. Then it stops. It may not happen again for years.

There is almost no weather. The temperature shifts so little that stones do not crack from frost or bake into dust. The air circulates in broad, negligible movements. Without rain, runoff, animals, or vigorous wind, the landscape retains every raw contour. It is geologically young in appearance but not necessarily in age. A ridge could have risen yesterday or ten million years ago; either would look much the same. The only erosion is a thin whitening of exposed surfaces where sunlight has broken chemical bonds. Shadows preserve the original dark material beneath overhangs, making each rock look freshly unwrapped.

Toward the plateau’s center lie several shallow depressions. They are not dry lakes, for they have never held water. Their floors are fields of hexagonal plates, matte gray and warm under the sun. The plates fit without gaps, although no hand arranged them. At their margins, immature ridges emerge as low wrinkles. The crust is apparently still under construction—but so slowly and evenly that change cannot be witnessed. Stone crystallizes upward molecule by molecule from a deep, viscous substrate. New ground is manufactured from below.

Here the vegetation grows tallest. Sparse columns stand twenty or thirty meters high, each one a stack of fluted chambers bearing a crown of black-green vanes. They are widely spaced, giving the basin the scale of an empty city without resembling one. In bright light their crowns rotate by fractions of a degree, but the motion is too slow to see. They cast long, narrow shadows across the hexagonal floor. Some columns carry pale bands indicating cycles of production, though the bands cannot be reliably counted as years. Time here is measured in completed compounds, not seasons.

The silence is nearly complete. There is no splash, rustle of animal passage, or wingbeat. The stiff foliage moves little even when the air does. Occasionally a silicate vane contracts with a glassy tick. Far away, a seed capsule may split with a muted click and expose its mirrored interior. In the deep hollows, electrical discharge passes through root networks as a subaudible pulse, making loose grains jump once and settle exactly where they were.

Nothing rots. Matter is disassembled only deliberately, by contact enzymes at the root boundaries. Fallen vanes—rare, because there are no storms and little fatigue—can lie intact for ages until the network extends a reclamation filament to them. The filament adheres, spreads a translucent skin, and returns the vane’s useful elements to circulation. There is no soil in the usual sense. Beneath the plants is a manufactured substrate of sorted dust, catalytic flecks, and exhausted crystal, laid down in layers thinner than paper.

Despite the warmth and greenery, the place does not feel fertile. Fertility suggests excess, appetite, multiplication. This plateau has none. Its living systems maintain a fixed inventory. Each new growth is balanced elsewhere by an equal reclamation. Colonies do not compete because their underground borders are chemically locked. They do not invade bare ground beyond assigned margins. The great spaces between them remain empty—not from failure, but from rule.

That rule seems to govern everything here: nothing may accelerate.

Geology rises without upheaval. Life grows without proliferation. Industry produces without expansion. The atmosphere moves without weather. Even decay has been replaced by exact accounting. The landscape is unfinished, yet it is not progressing toward completion. It occupies the state of becoming as a permanent equilibrium.

From the highest raw ridge, the whole region can be seen: broad plateaus, clean escarpments, gray basins, and attenuated green belts extending into the lucid distance. It is open enough to promise travel, empty enough to make travel meaningless. Every horizon reveals more of the same restrained abundance—living mineralworks spaced across new stone under a normal sun.

Nothing watches from among the fronds. Nothing ever has.

Still, at certain angles, the colonies align. Their distant vanes catch the light one after another until a straight line of reflections crosses the plateau, leaps a basin, ascends a ridge, and continues beyond sight. It resembles a signal, though there is no receiver. It recurs at the same solar angle each day, perfectly and without urgency: a bright sequence traveling through a world that otherwise declines to change.
