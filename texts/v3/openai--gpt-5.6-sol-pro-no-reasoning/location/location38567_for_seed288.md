Beneath a limestone plateau lies a city that no one built.

The cavern is broad enough to have weather, though not rain. Its ceiling hangs low at the margins and rises toward the center into a darkness beyond sight. Across the floor, pale ridges mark the remains of ancient sediment beds, worn nearly level by ages of dust, pressure, and migration. There is no standing water, no dripping stone, no hidden river. The rock has been dry for so long that every surface seems cured: chalk, salt, compacted clay, and dull gray limestone, all rubbed smooth by innumerable bodies.

The “streets” are natural passages between blocks of stone. They run in long, branching lines, broad in some districts and narrow in others, meeting at chambers like squares. Rows of pillars divide the larger spaces. Terraces climb shallow scarps. Shelves, alcoves, arches, and collapsed galleries repeat with such regularity that the place resembles an old underground town—yet no tool has touched it. Beds of unequal hardness weathered apart, fractures opened into lanes, and soft layers slumped away beneath harder roofs. Time supplied the planning.

Life occupied the plan afterward.

Low forests grow in scattered courts. Their plants are pale, dense, and leathery, fed not by sunlight or water but by mineral dust and the warm breath of animals. Thick fans rise from cracks like broad leaves, cream-colored at their bases and darkening to olive where colonies of bacteria live across them. Rope-like stems spread slowly along walls. In the deeper plazas stand groves of column plants: ribbed trunks no taller than a person, crowned with overlapping plates that collect shed skin, dung, spores, and powdered limestone.

Nothing here is wet. The plants conserve a heavy internal solvent, oily and slow to evaporate. Cut surfaces seal almost at once beneath wax. Their roots do not drink; they grip salt seams and trade nutrients with mats of microbes living in the stone. Growth is measured in years per finger-width. A grove may appear lush because every surface is layered with fans, crusts, and folded fronds, but between groves lie long bare avenues where only dust moves.

The cavern is cool and nearly constant in temperature. There are no seasons in the ordinary sense. The great cycle is the breathing of the plateau above. Over months, pressure changes push air through remote fissures. For half a year the city exhales; for the other half it draws inward. These currents are weak enough that a loose flake takes minutes to cross a chamber, but over centuries they sort the dust into dunes, clear paths around pillars, and bury entire districts to the level of their roofs.

The air is dimly luminous. No single object gives much light. Pale growths carry bacterial films that release a muted green-gray glow, while mineral faces retain a faint radiance after being exposed to those films. Shapes are visible at a modest distance, but color quickly disappears. Farther streets dissolve into ash-colored haze. High overhead, the ceiling is absent except where moving shadows reveal it.

The animals make the place deafening.

Millions inhabit the galleries. Small shell-backed grazers rasp bacterial skins from the rock in ceaseless unison. Their mouthparts produce a dry, metallic susurration, like wind-driven sand scraping a vast window. Flocks of blind, warm-bodied gliders hang from ceiling seams. At intervals they launch together and fill the cavern with wingbeats. Their wings are stiff and papery, and a passing flock sounds like a prolonged tearing of cloth.

Larger herd animals move along the avenues. They are low, six-legged, and padded against the sharp salt, with blunt heads made for pushing through the column groves. They communicate by stamping. The stone carries these signals for miles, multiplying them through buried strata until a distant herd can sound close enough to touch. Courtship produces still greater noise: males brace their ribbed flanks against pillars and vibrate, turning whole neighborhoods into resonant chambers. The note is too low to hear cleanly, but it shakes dust from ledges and makes the chest ache.

Predators announce themselves by silence. When one enters a district, the rasping ceases in a spreading circle. Winged animals clamp themselves flat against the ceiling. Herds stop stamping. For several breaths, the city becomes unnaturally still. Then comes a rush of feet, cracking fronds, and the immense returning roar of everything that was waiting.

There are quieter inhabitants in the buried quarters. Pale burrowers travel through old dust drifts, leaving raised lines like streets beneath streets. Tiny animals live inside hollow plant trunks and emerge only when the pressure turns. Egg colonies occupy warm fault faces, each egg sealed in lime and wax. Because free liquid is unknown, reproduction is built around stored fluids: parents provision young heavily, plants release large dry capsules, and even decay occurs within skins that remain closed until their contents have been reclaimed.

Nothing is wasted quickly. A carcass may lie in an avenue for decades, drying without rot. Grazers shave it layer by layer. Fungal fans—not true fungi, but slow sheets of colonial life—spread beneath it and digest it from below. Bones persist much longer, polished by passing feet until they become part of the pavement. In the oldest plazas the floor is not stone at all, but a compact mosaic of shell, tooth, stem plate, and mineralized hide.

The city has districts shaped by ecology rather than intention. Nursery chambers cluster around faint geothermal seams. Grazing courts form where ceiling dust is richest. Predators keep dens in blind arcades with only one entrance. Vast open squares remain nearly barren because the vibrations there are overwhelming; their thin stone floors amplify every footfall from caverns below. Animals cross them at speed, and plants grow only along their edges.

At the center lies the widest chamber, a shallow basin several miles across. Erosion has worked it almost perfectly flat. Sparse pillars rise from it like ruined towers, each surrounded by a dark skirt of growth. The basin is crossed by migration tracks worn into the rock—not deep grooves, but satin-smooth bands visible in the dim light. Herds have followed them for longer than the surrounding species have existed. Routes persist while bodies change.

This is the oldest stability of the underworld: not lifelessness, but life arranged around the fact that almost nothing new arrives. Stone becomes dust; dust becomes tissue; tissue becomes shell; shell returns to stone. The city neither grows nor falls in any human span. Arches fail once in ten thousand years. Groves shift across a plaza in centuries. A species may vanish and leave its pathways to another before a single pillar noticeably thins.

Yet the place is never peaceful. It trembles with accumulated life. Scraping, stamping, wingbeats, mating drones, alarm clatter, and the granular hiss of falling dust overlap until sound seems to be one of the cavern’s materials. The walls hold it, pass it onward, and return it altered. Even the bare districts murmur with events from far away.

In that dim, dry, cool expanse, emptiness and abundance coexist. The avenues are wide. The groves stand apart. The animals are everywhere, but rarely still enough to be clearly seen. What appears at first to be an abandoned subterranean city is, on longer acquaintance, a city at full occupancy—its architecture geological, its traffic migratory, its laws older than rain.
