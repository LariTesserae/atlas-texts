The lowlands extend farther than the eye can organize: a warm, level country of pale mineral ground, crowded from horizon to horizon. There are no rivers, ponds, marshes, or wells. Rain is unknown here. The sky carries only high, dry clouds, and the sunlight falls cleanly through them, softened at noon by airborne dust. Yet the plain is not barren. It is packed with life.

What first appears to be a city is a forest.

Countless earthen towers rise from the flat ground, each one fluted, buttressed, and pierced by narrow openings. Some are no higher than a person; others stand like hills, their broad foundations fused with neighboring structures. Together they form walls, courtyards, covered passages, branching streets, and immense contiguous districts. Nothing was planned, and no hand built them. They are the collective work of burrowing insects, root colonies, nesting birds, and the slow accretion of windblown mineral dust.

The country has almost no bare space. Where towers do not stand, low plants press shoulder to shoulder: waxy fans, thorn cushions, gray grasses, and mats of small round leaves. Their green is restrained beneath protective coatings, but when the light changes the plain reveals many colors—olive, silver, yellow-green, and deep blue-green in the shade of the mounds. There is no lush canopy. The lushness is horizontal, compressed into every crack and shaded seam.

Water is absent as a free substance. Life here depends instead on moisture held inside flesh, sealed roots, eggs, and wax-lined chambers. Plants draw minute traces from hydrated minerals deep below the surface and release almost none of it. Their leaves open their pores only briefly before dawn. Many animals never drink. They obtain what they need from seeds, bulbs, prey, and the dense, pale tissues of underground fungi.

The largest plants grow within the earthen architecture. Their roots strengthen walls; their stems emerge from parapets and crests. Some resemble compact trees, though they rarely rise above the towers. Their trunks are swollen and smooth, dividing into hundreds of short branches crowded with hard leaves. During the warm season, they flower all at once. For several days entire districts are dusted cream, rust-red, or violet. The flowers offer no nectar. Beetles and small mammals visit them for pollen, carrying clouds of it through the passages.

The ground itself is young. It has not yet acquired long gullies, weathered cliffs, or settled drainage. Fresh ridges of clay push slowly upward as salts crystallize below. Thin crusts buckle under the expansion of roots. New towers rise beside old ones faster than the dry wind can wear them away. Their edges remain sharp; fallen sections lie where they collapsed, still angular, already occupied. This is a landscape being assembled rather than eroded.

Its builders are everywhere but seldom conspicuous. Pale termites move in enclosed galleries. Ants carry seeds through roofed lanes beneath the soil skin. Burrowing rodents widen abandoned chambers and line them with husks. Lizards wait at warm apertures, barely distinguishable from fired earth. Small foxes travel the broader corridors at dusk, placing each foot quietly. Flights of finches settle on flowering crowns in such numbers that whole towers seem briefly feathered.

The densest colonies have the order of urban places. Main avenues run between fused mound walls. Ventilation shafts open into shared courts. Deep nurseries remain at stable temperatures while upper chambers follow the day’s warmth. Refuse is carried to designated hollows, where fungi and armored detritivores dismantle it. Predators have their own routes: snakes use the cooling ducts; owls nest above major junctions; thin-bodied hunting mammals slip through cracks too narrow for the animals they pursue to turn around in.

No species understands the whole structure. Each maintains a portion according to instinct, opportunity, or appetite. A broken wall exposes insect galleries; birds enlarge them; roots enter; dust catches around the roots; another mound begins. What resembles infrastructure is ecology repeated over immense distances.

The weather belongs to the plain’s scale. By afternoon, warm air rises from the outer crust while cooler air remains trapped among the towers. This difference produces slow inland winds that move through the constructed districts like breath through a lung. Vast belts of seed fluff lift and migrate. Dust accumulates on the windward faces of towers, extending them grain by grain. On some evenings dry electrical storms pass high overhead, their lightning silent at first and their thunder weakened by distance. No rain follows.

Despite the abundance of animals, the land is quiet. Most movement occurs underground, inside walls, or beneath leaves. Insects communicate by vibration. Mammals use scent and soft contact calls. Birds sing only around dawn, and even then their voices are short and sparse, as though loud sound would be wasteful. During the day one hears the faint abrasion of dry stems, the trickle of dust through a cracked chamber, wings beating once or twice before folding. A nearby collapse makes a muffled sigh rather than a crash.

Heat gathers gradually. The mornings are mild, the afternoons warm, and the nights retain enough stored warmth that little becomes dormant. Activity merely changes depth. At noon, surface animals withdraw and deep colonies expand their tunnels. Toward evening, seed-eaters emerge into the streets, pursued by reptiles and foxes. Before dawn, flowers open and roots perform their brief exchange with the air. There is no truly still hour; there are only layers of motion becoming visible and hidden.

From a height, the lowland resembles a single sprawling settlement without boundary. Districts differ in color and height according to soil: red-clay provinces, chalk-white fields, black mineral basins, and broad regions of ocher towers crossed by green seams. Some complexes extend for hundreds of kilometers, interrupted only by salt flats where vegetation gathers in dense rings around mineral fractures. Even these open places are temporary. Wind deposits dust, insects establish covered paths, and the living city advances.

Nothing here is ancient in form, though the plain may be ancient in age. Structures are continuously raised, fused, abandoned, inhabited, and absorbed. There are no ruins because nothing ceases to be useful long enough to become one. A deserted chamber becomes a burrow; a fallen spire becomes shade; a dead root becomes a corridor. The landscape’s governing rule is occupation: every shelter invites a resident, every resident alters the shelter, and every alteration creates room for another kind of life.

Thus a waterless world becomes crowded rather than empty. Its fertility is conservative, sealed, and patient. It spends almost nothing. It wastes neither shade nor tissue nor hollow space. Across the immense warm plain, the quiet work continues—walls rising, roots tightening, wings opening, hunters passing, and the young ground becoming, grain by grain, a continent made by life.
