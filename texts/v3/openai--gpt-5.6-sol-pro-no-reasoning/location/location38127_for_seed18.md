The place is called the High Conservatory, though there is no lower one.

It occupies the inside of a manufactured summit: a narrow, hollow pinnacle rising far above the cloud deck, built entirely from cast iron, ceramic brick, copper mesh, glass, and old structural stone that is not stone at all but slag poured into blocks. The chamber within is no larger than a cathedral nave. Its enclosing walls lean inward until they nearly meet overhead, then open through a long slit to the sky. From the floor, the opening looks close enough to touch. It is actually hundreds of meters above.

Everything else is emptiness.

The interior volume is so large compared with what occupies it that the few structures seem solitary and ceremonial. A single gantry crosses the chamber. One elevator cage climbs a toothed rail. Three immense pipes descend through the open air without touching one another, each separated by a gulf of warm haze. Suspended platforms hang at different elevations, but most hold nothing except rust, nesting bowls, and the shadows of cables. There is no visible ground beneath them. Looking down through the grating reveals a shaft narrowing into darkness, periodically lit by slow-moving inspection lamps.

The Conservatory is lush, though not in the manner of a forest. Vegetation clings only where the architecture permits condensation to gather. It grows in long vertical gardens on the cooler faces of the pipes, in overflowing seams between bricks, around rivet heads, inside drained cisterns, and along the cableways where braided fibers retain moisture. This leaves great expanses of air entirely bare, while isolated islands of foliage crowd themselves into green excess.

Ferns with copper-colored undersides spread in layered fans. Thick creepers descend hundreds of meters without finding soil. Their roots enter porous ceramic gutters and feed on mineral condensate. Pale bromeliads occupy the hollows of corroded machinery, holding small dark pools in their cups. Figlike trees grow sideways from wall sockets intended for equipment, their roots passing through conduits and fuse cabinets. Moss covers certain iron plates so densely that they resemble upholstered doors. Elsewhere, the metal remains naked and warm.

There is no natural substrate. What resembles earth is a fine compost made over centuries from leaf litter, insect shells, bird droppings, grease, soot, and particles worn from the building itself. It accumulates in trays and corners. When it becomes too deep, an automatic scraper—ancient but still functional—moves along a wall rail and pushes the soil into collection chutes. The scraper passes perhaps twice in a human lifetime.

Moisture comes intermittently.

For days the pinnacle may be merely humid, its walls sweating faintly where warm interior air meets cold upper plating. Droplets swell under beams, travel along engraved drainage lines, and fall into the open shaft. Each one seems distinct as it crosses the emptiness. Then a cloud rises from below and enters through ventilation apertures. The entire chamber disappears into white. Leaves shine. Glass fogs. Water ticks from every edge. Shallow basins fill, frogs emerge from impossible crevices, and flights of tiny silver insects hatch all at once.

Occasional rain falls through the roof slit, but rarely reaches the depths. It strikes the upper gantry and bursts into mist, or is intercepted by vegetation. The Conservatory was engineered to gather every drop. Slanted ribs guide water into ceramic channels; channels feed narrow reservoirs; reservoirs overflow into chains of basins. Water never forms a broad surface here. It exists as cups, films, dripping lines, sealed tanks, and pockets hidden behind walls.

The temperature is warm at most levels, maintained less by sunlight than by the engines buried in the pinnacle. Deeper down, machines turn with patient, nearly geological regularity. No one living understands their complete function. They may stabilize the tower against high winds, pump heat from lower altitudes, compress air for some abandoned industry, or simply continue a manufacturing cycle whose product is no longer collected.

Their sound fills everything.

It is not a busy sound. Nothing moves quickly enough for that. It is a sustained industrial enormity: a bass vibration beyond hearing, a continuous iron roar felt in the teeth and chest. At intervals, turbines far below enter phase and the whole interior becomes one colossal note. Handrails tremble. Pools corrugate. Birds lift in alarm, though they have heard it all their lives. Loose leaves walk across platforms.

Every seven hours, pressure is released through the three descending pipes. The event begins as a low approaching concussion, followed by a blast so powerful that it erases all lesser sound. Vapor jets from joints in brilliant horizontal plumes. Hanging vines stream outward. Flocks tumble and recover. The discharge lasts only minutes, but speech remains difficult afterward because the chamber seems to retain the noise in its metalwork.

The deafening quality of the Conservatory is therefore not evidence of rapid change. The machinery is slow. Flywheels take hours to complete a revolution. Pistons as broad as houses rise by barely perceptible degrees. A counterweight visible across the shaft descends less than a meter each month. The landscape changes through pressure, corrosion, root growth, and fatigue: forces that are immense but patient.

Erosion here is mature and balanced. Water has traced permanent black paths down the brick. Brass steps have been worn into smooth saddles by vanished workers and the claws of generations of animals. Rivets have rounded. Corners have softened beneath mineral deposits. Where acidic roots touch iron, the iron swells into red scales; where the scales fall, moss immediately occupies the pit. Maintenance organisms and maintenance machines together keep decay from becoming collapse.

One wall is periodically resurfaced by a line of small mechanical carts. They move so slowly that orchids grow on them. Each cart grinds corrosion from a strip of plating, lays down a dark protective resin, and advances the width of a fingernail. Behind the carts, the wall is glossy and bare. Ahead, it is an old hanging garden. The complete circuit takes nearly three centuries. Thus the vegetation travels around the chamber in an enormously delayed wave, flourishing, maturing, and being erased.

Animal life is abundant but dispersed through the volume. The emptiness between creatures is often greater than the habitats they occupy.

Swifts nest in bolt holes and spend nearly their entire lives airborne within the shaft. Their wings are short and strong, adapted to turbulent pressure releases. Blind geckos live behind warm switchboards, emerging to lick condensation from porcelain insulators. Blue-black beetles graze rust fungus. Colonies of translucent ants cultivate molds inside cable sheathing. In the temporary pools dwell miniature shrimp whose eggs survive decades of dryness.

Larger creatures are rare and conspicuous. Long-legged macaques move among the upper gardens, using pipes as roads and never descending to the lower dark. Their calls cannot compete with the engines, so they communicate through gestures and by striking tuned sections of railing. Each family possesses a distinct sequence of blows. At night, these metallic signatures pass from platform to platform, almost indistinguishable from the machinery.

There are also sail-backed lizards that leap from the walls and glide across the chamber. They appear to violate scale: a small animal crossing an enormous void, vanishing into haze before landing on some distant patch of green. When the pressure valves open, they turn into the blast and remain suspended, feeding on insects swept from the foliage.

No humans reside here permanently. Evidence of builders remains everywhere—ladders, numbered doors, worn control desks, emergency telephones with no voices behind them—but occupancy has receded into architecture. A maintenance party may arrive every few years in the elevator cage. Its members wear vibration armor and communicate with lamps. They stay for hours, examine gauges, collect water and tissue samples, oil a few accessible bearings, and depart. Their work seems comically small beside the mechanisms they inspect.

The rules of the place are embedded in its construction.

Nothing may fall freely for long. Nets, branches, crossbeams, thermal updrafts, and magnetic fields intercept most descending things. A dropped tool might reappear years later on a lower platform, encased in roots.

Nothing grows on the sunward wall during the hot season. The plating becomes too warm, and the garden retreats into cracks. In the cool season it returns explosively from buried rhizomes.

Doors open according to pressure, not intention. Some remain sealed for decades, then swing inward during a particular alignment of weather and machinery. Behind them are narrow rooms full of dry instruments, tiled reservoirs, banks of silent relays, or another view into the same central emptiness from an unexpected angle.

The highest rule is that the pinnacle must flex. In strong winds, the entire Conservatory leans. The movement is slow enough to doubt at first. Hanging cables deviate from vertical. Water runs backward in gutters. Distant platforms shift against one another. At the height of the bend, the roof slit frames not sky but the dazzling side of a cloud, and sunlight travels down the chamber like a white column.

Then, over many minutes, the structure returns.

The light is ordinarily unremarkable: clear daylight from above, green reflections from the gardens, amber work lamps in the lower shaft. Yet the depth of the chamber makes normal light behave strangely. Sunlit leaves hang against backgrounds of absolute darkness. A bright bird crossing the void seems detached from distance. Shadows from the gantry fall across walls too remote for their source to be obvious. During cloud immersion, illumination comes from everywhere and nowhere, flattening the industrial forms until they resemble drawings on white paper.

At dusk, the vegetation dims first. Then old indicator bulbs become visible across the void: red, green, and honey-colored points arranged in vertical constellations. Some indicate pressure or temperature. Others correspond to systems long removed. A few blink in sequences so extended that no observer has yet seen them repeat.

Night never becomes quiet.

The tower groans under cooling metal. Turbines maintain their continuous roar. Water ticks through enclosed channels. Animals knock messages into rails. Far below, a hammer falls at intervals just irregular enough to resist prediction. Each strike rises through the pinnacle as a physical wave, shaking dew from the leaves.

The High Conservatory feels at once overgrown and vacant, intimate and bottomless. It is a room built at the scale of an abyss, containing only a handful of things, each allowed to become intricate through isolation. Its wilderness is not opposed to industry. The two have aged into one another. Roots follow wiring diagrams. Birds navigate by warning lights. Rust feeds gardens. Engines generate weather. The made world has been operating long enough to acquire seasons, ecologies, and an erosion of its own.

From outside—if there were anywhere outside to stand—the pinnacle would appear narrow, severe, and almost featureless above the clouds.

Inside, it is warm, green, wet in patches, and roaring beyond endurance.
