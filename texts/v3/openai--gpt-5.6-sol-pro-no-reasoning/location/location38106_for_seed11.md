The country stands at the height where mountains usually sharpen into teeth, yet nothing here is sharp.

It is an immense upland of worn stone: a plateau so old that its summits have been rounded, lowered, and hollowed by ages beyond counting. The horizon is made of long backs and shallow domes, rising one behind another beneath a warm, ordinary sun. Their shapes suggest dunes, but each is solid rock—brown, violet, iron-black, and pale as old bone. Between them open valleys of extravagant depth. From the rim, the land appears gentle; one step nearer reveals drops of a mile or more.

The plateau has reached a kind of final erosion. There is little loose soil left. The ancient surface is polished and densely pitted, its harder inclusions standing like knots in weathered timber. Canyons descend in nested curves, following rivers that no longer exist. Cliffs have been pared into immense concave walls. Certain isolated mesas are so thoroughly worn that they resemble low tables covered in moss, though their bases plunge through haze into unseen ravines.

It is warm even at this elevation. The air has the mild, enclosing temperature of breath. Sun-warmed stone holds heat after dusk, and frost is unknown. Yet moisture is everywhere in small amounts and almost nowhere in abundance.

Clouds do not often cover the sky. Instead, vapor rises from the chasms each morning and condenses against the plateau’s cooler undersides, along shaded fissures, and beneath overhangs. Water pearls on every leaf. It threads down the trunks of low trees, darkens the rock, collects in thumb-sized hollows, and vanishes before it can become a stream. Rain comes suddenly and locally: one hill engulfed in silver while another, two miles away, remains bright. In the deepest clefts lie long black pools fed by seepage, still enough to reflect stars at noon. They have no visible outlets.

The vegetation lives by intercepting this uncertain water.

From a distance, the upland seems richly green. Mats of thick, overlapping leaves cover the rounded summits; fernlike crowns spill from cracks; curtains of moss descend vertical walls in bands hundreds of feet long. But the growth is less like a forest than an immense, thin tapestry laid over emptiness. Plants cluster where condensation gathers, leaving broad intervals of naked stone. There are no continuous woods. Each grove is an island separated from the next by open rock, ravines, or air.

The largest plants are wind-bent trees with pale trunks and crowns flattened close to the ground. Their roots travel openly across the stone for extraordinary distances, seeking hidden damp. A grove of ten trees may in fact be one organism, joined through fissures. When one crown receives rain, droplets are drawn through the shared roots and appear hours later on leaves half a valley away.

Elsewhere grow dense cushions of scarlet-stemmed herbs, tall hollow reeds around the seep-pools, and broad waxy leaves shaped like shallow bowls. By morning these bowls are full. At noon they tilt, seemingly of their own accord, pouring water into chambers among their roots. Flowering is brief and violent. Entire slopes turn yellow or blue for a single afternoon, then shed their petals before sunset.

For all this lushness, the country feels nearly vacant. Groves are far apart. Pools may be separated by days of walking. A solitary standing stone can remain the only vertical object across twenty miles of plateau. Distance is exaggerated by the clear air: things visible on the horizon may be unreachable because a canyon lies between.

Animals are uncommon. Small translucent lizards cling beneath the collecting leaves. Pale moths emerge during rain, breed in the wet air, and disappear into crevices as soon as the rock begins to dry. At some pools there are blind, soft-shelled creatures that surface only when thunder passes directly overhead. They seem to feel storms through the stone.

Now and then, a large gliding animal crosses a valley. It has no true wings, only skin spread between four elongated limbs. It rises on the violent updrafts and may remain aloft for days, turning slowly over the chasms. Its passing shadow is often the only sign of animal life in an entire region.

The emptiness is visual, however—not acoustic.

The plateau is deafening.

Warm air rises continuously through the canyon system. The ravines are connected below the visible land in a labyrinth of shafts, caves, and buried river passages. Wind driven through these openings turns the whole country into an instrument. Some fissures whistle at a pitch that pains the teeth. Caverns produce slow booming pulses. Narrow stone throats shriek; broad chasms roar without pause. The deepest valleys emit tones too low to be heard directly, though they shake water from leaves and make the ribs tremble.

The sound is never stable. Pressure shifts inside the plateau, and one valley falls silent as another erupts. A distant cliff may begin drumming in clear, regular blows, as though struck by a colossal mallet. Groves hiss and clap. Hollow reeds scream in hundreds of separate voices. Pebbles dance on ledges. Pools wrinkle under vibrations arriving through the ground.

Conversation in the open is nearly impossible. Those few people who cross the upland communicate with hand signs and lengths of colored cord. They speak only in sheltered pits, with their foreheads almost touching.

Storms transform the noise into something beyond endurance.

Clouds assemble rapidly over the warm chasms, not as broad weather fronts but as vertical towers. Lightning travels upward from the plateau rims. Rain may fall sideways or be caught in an updraft and carried back into the cloud. Water races across the bare rock in sheets only a finger deep, sufficient to tear loose whole mats of vegetation. Temporary waterfalls pour into the canyons and dissolve into mist long before reaching the bottom.

At such times the landscape visibly alters. Soft stone skins slough from the cliffs. Moss curtains peel free and sail across valleys. Pools overflow into channels that have been dry for generations. New vents open with concussive reports; old ones choke on debris. A hill that moaned yesterday may wake screaming after the storm. Another may never sound again.

This turbulence is not limited to weather. The stone itself seems incompletely settled despite its age. Vast slabs flex with changes in heat and pressure. Canyon walls inhale and exhale by fractions of an inch. Along some valleys, vegetation migrates as the condensation lines move, so that a green belt may climb a cliff over decades and leave dead gray stems beneath it.

Rarely, an entire hollow collapses. There is first a drop in sound—a shocking silence spreading outward—then the earth gives way in a slow, almost graceful descent. Trees, ruins, and sheets of rock rotate into the deep. Dust rises, catches moisture, and becomes mud-rain over the neighboring heights. Within days, vines begin colonizing the fresh scar.

Human marks are sparse and difficult to date.

Small shelters crouch beneath the quiet sides of boulders: dry-laid stone domes, low enough to crawl into, lined with woven grass and polished bark. Some are occupied for a season; others have stood empty so long that roots bind their walls. Beside the seep-pools are chipped stone basins and gutters sealed with dark resin. Rope bridges occasionally span narrow cuts, made from braided root fiber and replaced whenever weather permits. Across larger abysses there are no bridges, only rows of holes where some earlier crossing once hung.

No settlement is large enough to be seen as a town. A household may occupy one summit, another household a green ledge half a day away. Their paths are marked not by cairns, which vibrations overturn, but by shallow carvings in the bedrock: spirals for reliable water, crossed lines for treacherous air, open circles for caves in which the noise diminishes.

Metal is rare. Tools are flaked stone, shell, hard wood, bone, and occasionally soft copper hammered from green veins exposed in canyon walls. Cutting edges are prized, but hearing protection is more essential: layered plugs of wax and fiber, leather caps padded with moss, and rigid wooden masks that cup the ears. Long staffs are carried to feel vibrations ahead. A skilled traveler reads the trembling of the staff and can tell whether a distant booming comes from weather, a collapsing shelf, or the opening of a new vent.

People do not build upward here. Height invites wind, lightning, and resonance. Their structures cling, burrow, and yield. Roofs are tied down with living roots. Walls contain deliberate gaps so pressure can pass through them. Nothing is made perfectly rigid. The plateau punishes rigidity.

This appears to be one of its laws: whatever cannot bend will become an instrument, and whatever becomes an instrument will eventually be shaken apart.

Another law concerns silence. Silence is never trusted. When the usual roar ceases, animals enter cracks, leaves fold against their stems, and travelers lie flat with their mouths open. A quiet valley is storing force. Sometimes the sound returns gradually. Sometimes the valley floor rises in one enormous breath.

There are also places the noise cannot enter.

Deep beneath certain overhangs, or at the exact center of circular sinkholes, all vibration abruptly cancels. Step across the invisible boundary and the world becomes mute. The nearby trees still thrash, mouths still move, rain still strikes the stone, yet nothing can be heard or felt. These zones drift by several yards each year. Shelters are sometimes built near them, never within them. Long exposure is said to cause a person to hear other things: footsteps beneath the plateau, water flowing upward, or voices repeating words that have not yet been spoken.

At evening the ordinary sunlight reddens across the rounded heights. Mist accumulates in the ravines until the plateau resembles an archipelago above a white sea. The groves darken to nearly black. Their leaves release the day’s stored droplets, and for a short time every plant ticks and patters in the warm dusk.

Then the canyon winds reverse.

The sound rises from below—not one roar but thousands, layered and shifting across the open country. Hills answer hills. Cavities miles apart find common tones. Loose stone hums underfoot. The scattered shelters, the root-bound trees, the ancient worn summits, and the hidden pools all vibrate together.

For a few minutes each night, the whole plateau seems less like land than the exposed shell of some immense, hollow being: very old, almost empty, clothed in gardens, and nowhere near asleep.
