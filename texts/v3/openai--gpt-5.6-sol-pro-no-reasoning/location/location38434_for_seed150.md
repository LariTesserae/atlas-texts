The settlement occupies a high, narrow saddle between two young peaks. It is no larger than a hamlet, yet it feels urban because every part of it has been claimed, divided, roofed, bridged, and built upward. There are lanes, courtyards, galleries, cisterns, towers, and crowded façades—but none are made of quarried stone or worked timber. The city is entirely alive.

Its foundation is a single mat of alpine vegetation several body-lengths thick. Roots, rhizomes, moss, and compressed leaf-fiber have accumulated across a notch in the mountain, spanning it like a green raft anchored to fresh black rock. The saddle beneath is steep and recently exposed, its edges still angular, with little scree at their feet. Water seeps from fractures in the cliffs and enters the living mat from below. It never forms a broad river. Instead it travels through innumerable veins, appearing in gutters, cups, dripping walls, and shallow black pools before vanishing again among roots.

The cold controls everything. Leaves are small where they face the air, furred or waxy, often blue-black, violet, or the deep green of wet needles. Under the canopy, however, plants grow pale and broad, using the little light that filters through layers of vegetation. Every surface holds another surface: moss on bark, bladderwort in moss, silver lichen on the bladderwort’s dead winter leaves. There is no bare soil. Even decay is occupied before it can become ground.

The buildings began as dense stands of columnar shrubs. Over generations—or simply through the patient responses of growth—their stems have joined, leaned, and thickened into chambers. Their branches interlace overhead to form steep roofs that shed snow and gather condensation. Doorways are arches where trunks have grown apart. Windows are thin patches of translucent leaf-skin, dull green by day and almost black at night. Floors are springy lattices of roots, polished by contact but never cut.

Nothing here shows the mark of a blade. Construction happens by pressure, shade, weight, and adjacency. A wall bends because another plant crowds it. A stair forms where generations of shelf-fungi harden along a sloping trunk. Bridges are braided aerial roots that descended into mist, found the opposite side, and tightened. Gutters are channels between overlapping leaves. Cisterns are enormous pitcher-shaped flowers whose inward-pointing hairs keep water from spilling in the wind.

The city is packed almost beyond passage. Its streets are narrow clefts between living walls, often shoulder-wide, sometimes requiring a traveler to turn sideways. They twist rather than intersect. Each lane has a different texture: one lined with cold, beadlike berries; another curtained in horsetail stems; another carpeted with moss so saturated that every step wells up around the foot. Courtyards are only brief widenings beneath openings in the canopy. From these one can see the enclosing peaks, sharp and close, their upper faces intermittently erased by cloud.

Vertical growth provides the city’s districts. Low chambers lie warmest, buried in the root mat beside slow black water. Above them rise thickets of dwellings, then suspended walks and branch plazas. At the highest level, exposed to sleet, are narrow green spires: tightly furled conifers, columnar heaths, and flowering stalks armored in ice. They resemble towers from a distance, though they sway together in storms.

The highest structures catch moisture from passing cloud. Their needle fans comb droplets from the air, and the water descends along waxed stems into branching channels. After rain, the whole settlement weeps. Drops fall from roof to roof, strike taut leaves, gather in flower-basins, and run in bright threads down root walls. The sound is subdued but everywhere: tapping, ticking, the occasional soft pour of an overflowing cup. Beneath it is the slow creak of stems under accumulated water and the faint fibrous tearing of new growth forcing room for itself.

There is almost no wind at street level. The city’s density divides it into weak currents that carry the smells of wet bark, resin, fungal sweetness, and cold mineral water. On exposed bridges the wind returns abruptly, thin and severe. It draws vapor from the foliage and sends it sideways in streamers. Frost grows there on leaf margins, outlining the living architecture in white.

Light enters reluctantly. Clouds press low over the saddle, and the many roofs filter noon into a green dusk. Here and there, droplets focus what little brightness exists. A lane may be illuminated by hundreds of tiny cold points, each suspended from a moss filament. In the deepest chambers, pale plants manufacture their own dimness: ivory leaves collect reflected light; translucent roots carry a weak green glow from upper surfaces; small fungal fans shine the color of old snow. This is not enough to banish darkness, only enough to give it structure.

Animals are uncommon. The cold, the altitude, and the absolute possession of space by plants leave little room for them. A few white moths sleep beneath leaves, their wings furred and nearly flightless. Minute black beetles move through the water channels, feeding on pollen and rot. Occasionally a long-legged mountain bird descends into a courtyard, stands motionless among the cistern flowers, and drinks. Its arrival stills the small movements of the place. Then it lifts away over the roof-spires, and for days there may be no visible animal at all.

Yet the settlement is active. Buds open and close with pressure changes. Roots tighten bridges after rain. Pollen travels along the gutters in yellow rafts. When a chamber becomes too shaded, its inner leaves pale, dissolve, and are replaced by fungi; when a roof tears under snow, upward-reaching shoots close the gap. Paths migrate by inches each year. A familiar doorway may narrow during summer and reopen elsewhere after winter. A stair can soften into a slope. A courtyard can become a room.

The city has no builders apart from these processes, and perhaps no inhabitants in the usual sense. Its urban order emerges from competition held in delicate balance. One species makes columns, another binds them. One sheds water, another stores it. Creepers enforce boundaries by releasing bitter oils where neighboring growth becomes too dense. Moss keeps the floors damp; deep roots drain them before they drown. The whole place behaves less like wilderness reclaiming ruins than like a city whose laws are botanical.

Those laws are strict. Open space is temporary. Every gap invites growth. Nothing remains dead on the surface for long. Water must descend, even when it seems to flow sideways through capillary stems. Weight is answered by thickening. Damage is answered not by restoration but by redirection. A broken bridge becomes two balconies; a fallen tower roots where it lands and turns into a covered street.

Beyond the last wall, the living mat ends abruptly. Roots grip the lip of the saddle, hanging over raw stone in dark ropes. Below is a steep basin filled with cloud. Meltwater can be heard in it, though rarely seen. From outside, the settlement appears as a compact, irregular crown laid between two barren summits—lush almost to excess, dark with moisture, edged in frost.

From within, there is hardly any sense of an outside at all. There are only close walls, dripping roofs, bends in the lanes, and layers above layers. The mountains appear in fragments. The sky is a gray window glimpsed through moving leaves. The place is small enough to cross in an hour, but because its passages continually close, branch, and reform, it cannot be learned completely. It is always constructing itself without tools, quietly, in the cold.
