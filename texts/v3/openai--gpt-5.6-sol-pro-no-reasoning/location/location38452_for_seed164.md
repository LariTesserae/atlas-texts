The plateau begins above the weather and continues beyond the useful curve of sight.

It is not flat. It is an immense new country of black ridges, shallow basins, knife-edged scarps, and terraces lifted almost to the roof of the atmosphere. The stone has the raw appearance of something recently made: dark glass where it fractured, blue-gray metal beneath, pale mineral seams still too clean to have gathered soil. Peaks rise only modestly above the general level, but their sides are abrupt and unsoftened. Nothing here has been rounded by age. Even rubble retains bright corners.

Distance makes the land seem empty. A person could look for a hundred kilometers and count only three objects: a line of pylons, a vapor tower, a settlement standing around the foot of some machinery. Yet each of those objects is colossal. The pylons are kilometers apart. The tower is broad enough to develop cloud along one side while the other remains clear. The settlement, approached over several days, resolves into whole districts assembled beneath the ducts and braces of a single industrial plant.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming a sea.

It lies just under the thin black ground, sealed into countless shallow chambers in the rock. It beads on steel. It sweats from cliff faces and hangs from cables in perfectly spaced droplets. Narrow marshes occupy the basins, their water dark, cold, and scarcely deeper than a hand. The mountain walls weep from long horizontal seams, making silver curtains that descend ten or twenty meters and then vanish into porous stone. There are no broad rivers. Drainage seems to be vertical: rain enters the plateau, circulates through its interior, and emerges higher up elsewhere.

The air itself is near saturation. Every surface has a damp sheen, but frost remains limited to pale margins around pools and rivet heads. It is cold enough that breath lingers, not cold enough to lock the country in ice. Condensation continually forms and continually fails to fall. From a distance the atmosphere appears layered: clear gray air below, a belt of white vapor at the height of the largest chimneys, then an upper clarity of hard blue.

Vegetation thrives wherever the rock allows it purchase, though from afar it resembles mineral discoloration more than life. There are no trees, and almost nothing rises higher than the knee. Dense mats of bottle-green filaments coat the marsh edges. Black moss fills cracks so exactly that it might have been poured there. On sheltered walls grow translucent fronds with branching patterns like frost on glass. They hold beads of water at their tips, each bead trembling in the constant mechanical vibration.

The plants have no flowers. There are no seeds drifting, no insects attending them, no burrows beneath them. Their reproduction is obscure and apparently unnecessary. Every observed patch has always occupied precisely the same boundary. A moss bed may look lavish, soft, and fertile, yet its edge has not advanced across the adjacent stone by the width of a grain.

There are no animals.

No birds cross the open sky. Nothing swims in the marshes. There are no gnats in the vapor, no worms in the saturated soil, no scavengers in the streets. The absence is so complete that animal life begins to seem like an eccentric idea from somewhere else—a restless arrangement of matter that this world never required.

The cities are correspondingly difficult to understand.

They occur at intervals across the plateau, joined by elevated rail lines and thick conduits laid in long, uncompromising chords. Each city is organized around a production complex: refineries, turbine halls, pumping stations, foundries, pressure vaults, and cooling fields. Housing blocks stand beyond them in orderly wedges. Streets are narrow and roofed against condensation. Walkways climb the sides of machinery and continue through maintenance doors into enormous interiors.

Everything is functional. Nothing is inhabited.

Lights burn in windows. Street lamps shine against wet pavement. Elevators ascend and descend behind glass. Trains move between cities at fixed intervals, drawing empty strings of freight wagons. Assembly lines operate. Cranes rotate through measured arcs, lift ingots from one platform, and place them on another. Warning beacons flash above silent intersections where no one has ever waited to cross.

The machinery is not decaying. Paint remains intact beneath the water film. Bearings are greased. Tracks are bright where wheels pass. Broken pieces are not found. Nor is there any indication that the systems repair themselves. They simply do not deteriorate.

The cities manufacture components for other cities. One stamps vast steel vanes. Another produces braided cable. A third refines the pale conductive mineral mined from vertical shafts. Freight moves outward, is transferred, processed, assembled, and eventually returned in altered form. No final product has been discovered. Industry is the circulation of matter through a closed grammar.

Its sound governs the plateau.

The emptiness does not make the place quiet; it gives noise room to become geographic. Turbines produce a bass note felt through boot soles hundreds of kilometers away. Stamp mills strike at exact intervals, their blows passing from ridge to ridge like slow artillery. Pressure valves release columns of white steam with a roar broad enough to flatten the nearby moss. Rails sing before the trains arrive and long after they pass. In deep shafts, pumps hammer at groundwater with a rhythm indistinguishable from a gigantic heart.

The separate machines are synchronized. Their cycles overlap without drifting. At certain hours—if “hours” is the correct word—the foundries strike, the vents open, the trains cross their bridges, and the entire highland becomes one sustained chord. Water standing in the marshes patterns itself into fine concentric ridges. Condensation falls from every cable at once.

Then the droplets stop halfway down.

This is the first of the plateau’s rules that visitors notice and the last they learn to accept: change occurs locally, but never accumulates.

Rain falls, yet the marshes do not rise. Furnaces consume fuel, yet the stockpiles remain the same height. Ore leaves the mines, but the excavations do not deepen. Trains travel vast distances without becoming older or nearer to retirement. A droplet may descend from a cable, strike a roof, run into a gutter, and pass belowground; later it is present again at the cable tip, not replaced by an identical droplet but somehow continuous with itself.

Nothing repeats visibly. There is no lurch, no reset, no reversal of motion. Time proceeds in every immediate sense. Steam expands. Wheels turn. Clouds travel. Yet the total condition of the country does not alter.

Even the weather has reached industry-like equilibrium. A bank of cloud perpetually forms over the western scarp from moisture lifted through warm machinery. It advances, rains across the central basins, thins above the rail corridor, and dissolves at the eastern towers. Seen again later, it has not circled back. It has continuously been the same event without a beginning or expenditure.

Because the plateau is so vast, it carries several climates at once. Snow grains blow across its northern expanses. Cold rain darkens the central rock. Far south, pale fog fills trenches deeper than cities. These zones keep their positions but not their shapes. Their borders flex, branch, and retract, always summing to the same distribution.

The highest terrain is stranger still.

There the atmosphere becomes too thin for conventional weather, but water continues to gather on upward-facing stone. It forms suspended sheets a few centimeters above the ground, transparent and perfectly level, spanning valleys without support. Roads pass beneath these aerial lakes. Transmission towers rise through them. When the turbines enter their strongest cycle, the sheets ripple, and their shadows travel across the plateau like the shadows of enormous unseen wings.

No source powers the machines in an ordinary way. Fuel is burned in some cities, falling water turns turbines in others, and broad electrical fields flash silently between remote pylon crowns. Each source can be traced to another process until the circuit covers a continent. The system’s efficiency should be impossible. Heat should escape into the cold upper air; friction should accumulate; reservoirs should empty. Instead, losses appear elsewhere as causes. Waste heat condenses water. Falling water exposes ore. Refined ore repairs no machine, but enters the factories and sustains their load. The plateau is not perpetual motion so much as a landscape in which cause and consequence have been bent into a closed ring.

At the continental center stands the greatest city.

Its outskirts begin as scattered substations among low moss plains. Then come canals of black water, freight yards, reservoirs, ranked cooling towers, and districts of windowless factories. The density increases for nearly two hundred kilometers, though the city never feels crowded. Buildings are separated by ceremonial amounts of vacant ground. Boulevards are broad enough for weather to move down them. Public squares hold only rain and electrical hum.

At the center is an open shaft whose diameter is larger than many mountain ranges. Its walls descend through alternating bands of raw stone and constructed galleries. Bridges cross it at different depths. Factories cling to its interior. Clouds form below the rim, illuminated by amber work lights, and thunder without lightning rises from unseen levels.

The shaft is not being excavated. It is not complete. Those statements coexist.

Cranes continually remove material from the walls, and conveyors carry it upward. Fresh-cut rock gleams wherever one looks. But surveys performed decades apart give the same depth, the same volume, the same sharp profile. The plateau’s youth is permanent. Its cliffs have just broken. Its lava has just cooled. Its cities have just been commissioned. Nothing has had time to weather, though immeasurable operational time passes through every gear.

From the rim, one can hear the entire industrial continent gathered into the hollow: engines, mills, trains, vents, water, transformers, the subsonic labor of buried pumps. The noise is immense but not chaotic. It has spacing, recurrence, harmonic structure. After long listening, it seems less like machinery than a single held utterance.

No inscription explains who built the cities. No remains suggest builders ever stood here. Control rooms contain levers shaped for hands, stairs sized for bodies, benches positioned before windows. But there are no portraits, no records, no personal objects, and no signs of departure. Perhaps the apparent human scale is coincidence. Perhaps the absent inhabitants have not yet arrived. Perhaps the plateau manufactures the possibility of inhabitants in the same way it manufactures cable and turbines: endlessly, without reaching the finished thing.

At ordinary light levels, under an ordinary gray-blue sky, it can almost seem comprehensible. Wet rock. Cold marshes. Green growth. Steel infrastructure. Cloud against high ground.

Then a train crosses a bridge twenty kilometers away, sparks pouring from its wheels. The sparks fall into the abyss and remain visible far below. A pressure horn sounds from a city beyond the horizon. Every pool on the plateau shivers in answer.

For one instant, all the suspended water descends.

Rain touches every roof, every marsh, every bare and newborn ridge. The sound becomes so great it erases distance.

And afterward nothing is wetter than before.
