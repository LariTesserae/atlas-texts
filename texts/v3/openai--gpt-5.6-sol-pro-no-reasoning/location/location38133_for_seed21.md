The place occupies a notch high in a volcanic range, no larger than a village and enclosed on three sides by young black cliffs. The rock has not yet weathered into dignity. Its fractures are keen, its shelves angular, its mineral colors almost embarrassingly vivid: iron red, sulfur yellow, vitreous blue-black. Yet water is everywhere. It seeps from seams too narrow to see, pearls beneath overhangs, and runs in clear threads across hot stone. After noon rain, every cliff weeps.

The floor of the notch slopes toward a shallow marsh whose boundaries change by the hour. It is not fed by any visible stream. Water rises through the ground, cool in some places and nearly boiling in others, making the mud flex and breathe. Between pools stand islands of pale pumice, scarcely softened by moss. Reeds grow only in separated clumps, leaving broad intervals of reflective water and dark gravel. Their blades are thick, waxy, and ribbed with silver tissue that sheds the heat. Low red ferns occupy the mistiest cracks. Higher up, wiry shrubs cling to ledges with translucent roots spread visibly over the stone like fine glass hands.

The altitude should make the air cold, but it is intensely hot. Heat does not rise uniformly here. It gathers in invisible, slow-moving chambers. A traveler can step from mild rain into air hot enough to sting the throat, then another pace into a cool pocket smelling of wet clay. Clouds graze the rim of the notch and descend in ragged streamers, but sunlight often reaches the basin intact. In ordinary daylight the strangeness becomes harder to dismiss: every improbable thing is plainly illuminated, without shadow tricks or theatrical color.

At the center of the marsh is a circular area of bare water about twenty paces across. It appears shallow; stones can be seen beneath it. Those stones, however, are not on the bottom. They hang at different depths, unsupported, each trailing a narrow upward plume of bubbles. During rain, drops stop just above the circle and remain there, accumulating in a trembling horizontal sheet. When enough water has gathered, the sheet folds soundlessly inward and vanishes. A few minutes later, springs throughout the notch surge.

There are three structures.

One is a roofless shelter on a dry shelf above the marsh, built from local slabs fitted without mortar. It might be mistaken for an abandoned field hut except that its interior is always one degree cooler than the coolest air outside. Condensation forms diagrams on its walls: branching lines, nested loops, occasional blocks of minute symbols. They persist for several minutes and then run down into a narrow drain. The drain has no outlet anyone has found.

The second structure is a row of seven dark posts driven into the marsh edge. They are slender and slightly taller than a person. No two stand at the same angle. Their surfaces look like charred wood from a distance, but close inspection reveals a material with neither grain nor stable hardness. A fingernail dents it; a knife does not. Each post casts the expected shadow, plus a fainter shadow pointed toward the central pool regardless of the sun. When clouds cover the basin, only the inward shadows remain.

The third is barely a structure at all: a white arch set against the cliff, framing bare rock. Its proportions suggest a doorway for something narrow and very tall. Vines avoid it. Water running down the cliff divides neatly around its upper curve and rejoins below. Passing beneath the arch does nothing obvious, but afterward one's footsteps arrive a fraction late. The delay increases toward the central pool and disappears at the rim of the notch.

Whatever made these things did not clear or dominate the landscape. Its work is scattered, precise, and seemingly incomplete. Small artifacts sometimes emerge from the mud after a spring surge: hollow beads whose interiors are larger than their exteriors; flexible squares that grow rigid when named; a kind of transparent wire that can be tied only in darkness. Most decay quickly in open air, collapsing into salt, water, or a brief odor of lightning.

Life here is lush only where moisture and temperature happen to agree. There is no continuous green cover. Instead, growth collects in rich, isolated congregations: a fern bank crowded beneath one spring, an orchid thicket on a single floating stone, mats of emerald algae around cold vents. Between them lie expanses of naked rock and water. This gives the notch the appearance of a garden in its earliest planning, with generous empty spaces awaiting decisions.

Animals are uncommon. Small black insects skate the warm pools at dawn, each making a minute hexagonal pattern in the surface film. A long-legged marsh bird visits irregularly. It has gray plumage, a bare orange throat, and no audible call. It feeds by standing perfectly still beside a spring until something beneath the mud imitates its feet; then it strikes. Sometimes it pulls up a translucent, jointed creature. Sometimes it withdraws its beak empty and immediately flies away.

Lizards live among the cliff cracks, though they are seen chiefly as brief movements crossing exposed stone. Their bodies are thin, their skin the color of rain-dark basalt. They bask not in sunlight but in the faint inward shadows of the posts. If disturbed, they vanish into fractures that seem too narrow to admit them. Shed skins are found occasionally, whole and warm, with no opening through which the animal could have left.

The basin is mostly quiet. Water drips, reeds click in the breeze, and insects trace soft scratches on the pools. Thunder sometimes rolls beyond the mountain, muted by cloud. The loudest regular sound is a deep, restrained note produced when rainwater spills from the central circle into the ground. It is felt first in the soles of the feet. The seven posts answer one by one with tones too low to be heard clearly, and for a short while droplets on leaves quiver without falling.

Weather moves quickly across the notch. Morning begins with bright heat and low vapor. By midday, clouds gather against the cliffs and rain falls hard for ten or fifteen minutes, steaming on the upper shelves. The marsh expands. Plants unfold extra surfaces to drink. Springs reverse, swallowing water and scraps of vegetation. Toward evening the clouds lift, leaving the rock polished and the air dense with condensation. At night, the pools remain faintly warm while the bare stones lose heat with unnatural speed.

The landscape is young in more than geological appearance. New cracks open cleanly after storms. Fresh mineral ridges sometimes rise from the marsh, sharp as broken ceramic and still warm inside. Yet nothing accumulates into ordinary age. Fallen leaves are gone by morning. Silt disappears from channels. Moss never spreads beyond certain invisible borders. The notch seems continually recent, as though erosion and decay are being corrected—not reversed everywhere, only edited enough to keep the place near the beginning of itself.

There are local rules, learned through accident.

Do not drink from water that reflects the clouds when the sky is clear.

Do not touch two of the posts at once; the space between them becomes briefly much farther apart.

Never sleep inside the stone shelter during rain. Those who do wake rested, but their dreams continue in the condensation for several days, visible to anyone who enters.

If the arch casts a shadow, leave the basin before sunset. The arch normally does not cast one.

And when all sound stops—not quiet, but the complete absence of dripping, insects, wind, and one's own breath—stand on bare rock. The marsh is about to change depth.

It does so without rising. Reeds, stones, posts, and reflections remain where they are, yet the water acquires a sense of great downward distance. For several minutes the shallow pools seem to open into a warm, clear abyss filled with slow lights. The suspended stones in the central circle descend, or appear to descend, until they resemble a constellation beneath the feet. Something immense may be moving among them, though no full shape has ever been seen. Then a marsh bird may call from very far below, despite the species having no call, and the ordinary bottom returns: black mud, pale gravel, tangled roots.

By dawn the place is again small enough to cross in a few minutes. Rain shines on new stone. The shelter sweats its unreadable diagrams. Sparse shrubs hold clear beads along their leaves, and the high cliffs contain the heat like walls around a courtyard. Everything looks fresh, exposed, and almost understandable.

Only the delayed footsteps suggest that some part of the traveler is still crossing.
