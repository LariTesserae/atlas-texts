The place occupies a shallow bowl high on the mountain, no wider than a small grove. Its boundary is unmistakable. On every side, the alpine slope rises in raw black fins, sharp-edged and scarcely weathered, while within the bowl the ground lies nearly level beneath moss, sedge, and clear standing water. Nothing marks the transition except a ring of pale stones and the sudden disappearance of wind.

There are no paths, walls, tools, ruins, or discarded things. No hand has arranged the stones. Yet the whole basin has the character of a machine already running.

Water is everywhere without seeming to travel. It pearls on the undersides of leaves, hangs in the branching moss, fills narrow seams in the new rock, and stands ankle-deep in dark pools. The air continually condenses into a fine mist, though no droplet is ever seen to fall. Along the eastern face, five ledges weep in perfectly parallel silver threads. They do not deepen their channels. The stone beneath them remains fresh, angular, and glassy, as if broken open an instant ago.

The basin is cold enough to numb fingers but not cold enough for ice. Ferns grow in dense green fans where the water meets the rock. Low shrubs carry thick leaves with bluish undersides; their branches spread broadly, leaving generous spaces between one plant and the next. This makes the vegetation feel both lush and sparse: every living patch is saturated with growth, but the patches are islands separated by open water, bare gravel, and beds of black moss. Tiny white flowers stand in the clearings. Each blossom faces exactly north.

In the center rises the basin’s only prominent feature: a block of raw basalt about the size of a cottage. It has no carvings, apertures, joints, or worked surfaces. Even so, it operates.

A slow piston-stroke sounds from inside it.

The rhythm is enormous, industrial, and absolutely regular:

**THRUM.**

A pressure can be felt through the soles.

**THRUM.**

The pools dimple in identical concentric rings.

**THRUM.**

Mist is drawn toward the stone, then released.

Around this pulse are finer mechanical sounds with no visible source: belts running at speed, relay chatter, a deep electrical hum, the dry rotation of bearings. Sometimes a metallic shriek crosses the bowl, sharp enough to send instinctive alarm through the body. The animals do not react. There is no machinery to see, and no fuel, smoke, heat, cable, slag, or exhaust. Industrial process exists here stripped of industry’s physical apparatus, as if the laws governing production have arrived before matter has decided what is being produced.

The fauna is abundant, but distributed with the same strange restraint as the plants. Blue-black beetles occupy one stone each. Pale frogs sit at measured intervals along the pools, their throats swelling together with every buried piston-stroke. Long-legged birds stand in the sedges, spaced so evenly that they resemble marks on an instrument dial. Moths cling to the wet basalt in hundreds, but never overlap. Translucent worms write looping galleries through the moss. A small russet mammal moves between fern islands carrying a seed in its mouth; it is always halfway between two islands.

None of these creatures appears to age, hunt, sleep, mate, hatch, feed, or die. Their abundance is not a population but an inventory.

Closer observation makes the stillness more disturbing. The bird lifting its foot does not put it down. The frog’s throat remains forever at the fullest point of its call. The moths’ wings tremble but never complete a beat. The russet animal advances through the grass, yet its distance from the central stone never changes. One may watch the water rings widen for hours; they cross the same three inches repeatedly without reaching the shore.

Everything is active. Nothing changes.

This is the basin’s governing rule. Motion may occur, but consequence may not.

Rain is present here in the form of wetness, sound, and fresh impact marks, though no rain falls. Erosion has begun but cannot proceed. Roots enter cracks without widening them. Water descends without arriving below. Creatures expend effort without consuming energy. The hidden engines labor without wear and manufacture no visible product. Even sound is trapped in a steady state: loud enough to fill the bowl, incapable of fading beyond it.

At the pale boundary stones, reality behaves differently depending on which direction they are crossed. Looking inward, the basin appears small, perhaps a hundred paces wide. Looking outward from its center, the ring seems several days away across an immense marsh. Walking toward the edge brings neither nearer nor farther. Turning around restores the original distances at once. The mountain peaks visible over the rim are sharp and young, their fractures bright as freshly split coal, but the same peaks repeat at unequal sizes in the surfaces of the pools. In some reflections they stand upside down beneath a green sky. In others, they have not yet risen.

The central basalt has a reflection in every body of water except the pool nearest it. In that pool appears an object absent from the landscape: a colossal assembly line descending through darkness.

On the line lie mountains.

They pass beneath invisible presses one by one, each arriving as a wet, angular mass crowded with moss, frogs, mist, and suspended birds. Mechanical arms that cannot be seen except by the movement of condensation fold the slopes inward. A die stamps a circular basin into each summit. Parallel nozzles apply waterfalls. Somewhere beyond the edge of the reflection, finished ranges are stacked in thunderous rows.

The image never progresses. The same mountain enters. The same press descends. The same mountain waits beyond it. Yet the clamor suggests limitless production.

There is no sign that the basin was built. It may instead be a naturally occurring factory: a place where geological formation has become repetitive rather than historical. The surrounding mountain is not ancient terrain being worn down but a fresh unit held at the instant of completion. Its cliffs retain their cutting edges because time here is not a river. It is a flywheel.

The ecology has adapted—not through evolution, which would require change, but through perfect participation. Each creature occupies a fixed function in the cycle. Beetles collect cold. Frogs regulate pressure. Birds hold open narrow vertical spaces through which the invisible belts pass. Moths absorb sparks from a current that has no wires. The russet animal bears its single seed forever between origin and destination, preserving the possibility of a forest without permitting one to arise.

Beneath the moss, the soil vibrates. If it is pressed aside, there are no roots and no bedrock underneath, only another dim basin oriented at right angles to the first. Its pools cling to vertical ground. Its birds stand sideways. Far below—or beside—the same black block pulses, and another observer may perhaps be kneeling there, parting their moss to look into this place.

The vegetation closes again as soon as it is released, not by growing but by having never been disturbed.

Nothing can be taken from the bowl. A plucked flower remains rooted while also seeming to rest in the hand. A stone carried across the boundary is found again in its wet hollow, although its weight persists in the pocket. Water swallowed stays cool on the tongue and remains in the pool. The place permits impressions but rejects subtraction.

Nor can anything be left. Footprints fill before the foot rises. Breath condenses briefly, then returns to the lungs. Blood from a cut beads red but will not fall, and the wound remains both open and healed until the boundary is crossed.

Above, the sky is a low pewter ceiling, dim and without a sun. Its light is sufficient for every detail but casts no shadow. At intervals—which cannot be measured, because no interval truly passes—a muted flash reveals vast pistons moving behind the cloud layer. Their rods rise from several distant peaks, though no rods can be seen between flashes. Thunder follows at once, always the same thunder, with the same tiny catch near its end.

The basin answers:

**THRUM.**

Frogs swell their throats.

Water rings widen and do not widen.

The five falls descend across unmarked stone.

Everything glistens in the cool, industrial dusk: lush, crowded with life, spacious, unfinished, and permanently complete.
