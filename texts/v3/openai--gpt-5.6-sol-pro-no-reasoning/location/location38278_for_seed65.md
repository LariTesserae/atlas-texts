The country stands above nearly all weather.

It is a broad, high basin enclosed by peaks so distant and bright that they seem less like mountains than the broken rim of the sky. The basin floor lies at an elevation where breath comes thin and sleep is shallow. Nothing is sheltered for long. Sunlight pours through the spare atmosphere with a white, almost mineral force, bleaching the stone and laying short blue shadows beneath every projection. Yet the air remains cool. Even at noon, frost survives inside cracks no wider than a finger.

The land has been weathered into repose.

Long ridges of pale rock run across it in parallel, rounded rather than sharp, their surfaces polished by ages of windborne grit. Between them are shallow troughs filled with slate fragments, pumice, and old gravel sorted into patient bands. There are no fresh scars, no unstable slopes, no sense that the mountains are still rising or collapsing. Every cliff has already lost what it was going to lose. Every loose stone seems to have rolled into its final hollow.

From the basin floor, immense columns of eroded stone rise at intervals of several miles. They are broad at the base and worn smooth higher up, with shallow cavities opening through them. None is dramatic in isolation; together they give the region its measure. A traveler may walk toward one from dawn until noon and find it scarcely enlarged.

Water is scarce but never wholly absent. Moisture gathers at night on the colder faces of stone and freezes into cloudy skins. Under the morning sun these skins loosen and run in fine threads, feeding small black pools hidden beneath overhangs. The pools are seldom larger than shields. They have no visible outlets, yet they neither deepen nor dry. Elsewhere, narrow streams appear between boulders, travel twenty or thirty paces, and sink back into the gravel. Their courses are so old that the surrounding stones have been worn into low, perfect gutters.

No rain can be seen falling in the basin. Sometimes a cloud snags far above a peak, and hours later the rock darkens as if remembering rain.

Vegetation clings close to the ground. There are mats of gray lichen, hard cushions of blue-green leaves, and scattered stalks no taller than a hand. Their flowers are small but intensely colored: yellow like exposed sulfur, red like fresh iron, violet nearly black against the glare. They do not sway. Their stems are too short and their tissues too dense for that. In the damp hollows, translucent moss grows under stones rather than on top of them, nourished by condensation and the faint light passing through pale quartz.

There are no trees, no thickets, no continuous turf. One plant may stand hundreds of paces from the next. From a distance the basin appears entirely barren.

Its animals are everywhere.

They gather most visibly around the hidden water: long-legged grazing creatures with ash-colored coats, squat horned beasts whose broad feet spread over scree, and flocks of white ground birds that crouch among the stones until the whole gravel bar seems to lift at once. Small mammals inhabit the cracks in astonishing numbers. Their faces appear briefly in the dark seams—round ears, black eyes, pale whiskers—then vanish. Beetles with mirrored shells move through the lichen. Thin-winged insects circle the seepages in dense shining halos, although the air seems too cold for flight.

Above all are the sky animals.

Vast flocks travel the bowl of the basin in fixed circuits. They resemble birds only at a distance. Their wings are narrow and nearly transparent, and each has a long trailing tail that divides into vibrating filaments. They rarely land. They bank around the stone columns, rise along the sunlit slopes, cross the open center, and return by precisely the same route. Old individuals lead; young ones occupy the inner curves. Their population is immense, but their pattern never varies.

They are the source of the noise.

The basin is deafening from sunrise to sunset, though there is scarcely any visible commotion. Wind drives continuously through the holes and grooves worn into the standing rocks. Each formation holds a steady note: some a low pressure felt through the ribs, some a high metallic shriek, some a broad roar like distant surf. The flying creatures add a dry, thunderous resonance as air passes through their featherless wings. When a flock crosses overhead, conversation becomes impossible. Pebbles tremble in their hollows. The surfaces of the black pools stipple without wind touching them.

The sounds do not rise or fall. There are no gusts, pauses, crescendos, or sudden silences. The same colossal chord occupies the basin at every hour.

At night the light weakens but does not entirely depart. The surrounding summits retain the sun after the basin floor has gone blue, and frost crystals cast a dim, granular radiance. The animals settle into their customary cracks and depressions. The great fliers continue their circuits as silhouettes against the luminous peaks. The wind keeps sounding through the stone.

Nothing here seems seasonal. The flowers neither open nor close. Shed feathers lie beneath habitual flight paths, yet they do not accumulate. Tracks remain in damp silt, but there are never enough to obscure older tracks. Carcasses are rarely found, and no animal appears immortal; nevertheless, the abundance of life neither increases nor thins. Birth, feeding, predation, and death occur without altering the total shape of things.

This is the landscape’s slight impossibility: change exists locally but not collectively. A stone may be overturned, a pool disturbed, an animal taken, but the basin restores its arrangements without visible agency. By the following brightness, the overturned stone has settled with its weathered side upward. The pool is clear. The herd occupies the same distant contour. Nothing rewinds; nothing visibly returns. The place simply refuses to acquire a history beyond the one already written into its ancient surfaces.

There are no structures and no roads. If people have ever crossed the basin, they left no durable sign. A few of the larger animals split stones with smaller stones to reach grubs and salt deposits. Certain ground birds use flakes of volcanic glass to cut the fibrous roots of cushion plants, carrying the flakes briefly in their beaks before abandoning them. Along one seep, generations of horned grazers have arranged flat rocks around the water—not deliberately enough to call it masonry, but with sufficient regularity to suggest an intelligence held just below invention.

Any traveler who enters must live at that same threshold. Fire is difficult in the thin air and there is almost nothing to burn. Shelter can only be found, never built: a lee behind a ridge, a dry cavity in a column, a shallow trench among sun-warmed slabs. Tools must be stone, horn, sinew, hide. Metal, if carried in, feels foreign in the hand and rings painfully in sympathy with the basin’s endless chord.

Distance is deceptive here. The brightness removes haze, making far peaks look clean-edged and close. The absence of trees and structures gives the eye no familiar measure. A white speck may be a bird beside the path or a herd animal five miles away. A dark opening in a cliff may be a handspan crack or a cavern large enough to contain a flock.

Despite the noise, the emptiness is the strongest impression. Creatures abound, but they are dispersed across enormous intervals, each herd and swarm a small knot in the open stone. There is room enough around every living thing for solitude. Even when thousands of wings cross the sky, the sky seems hardly occupied.

The basin does not feel hostile. Hostility would imply response. It is too settled for that—bright, cold, loud, and complete. It offers water in measured pockets, life in profusion, and almost no comfort. Its laws are simple: nothing is wasted, nothing accumulates, and nothing changes the whole.

The mountains continue holding their immense chord.

The flocks continue turning.

The clear drops continue forming under the stones, each one trembling in place before falling into pools that never fill.
