The city occupies the upper walls of a valley that has not yet finished becoming.

There is no true valley floor. Far below the inhabited ledges, the mountains draw apart around a depth filled with dim vapor, scaffolding, and the occasional cold glint of water. Surveyors call it a rift, though it is not widening in any measurable way. Geologists insist it is an embryonic river basin, its drainage still being negotiated. Older residents say the low country simply has not arrived yet.

The surrounding peaks rise higher than weather. Their summits are seldom visible, not because of cloud but because altitude distorts distance here: the upper crags recede when watched, exposing further ranges behind them, paler and apparently more remote. Climbers sometimes return from a single afternoon with frost on their clothes and records of several weeks having passed. Consequently, the city spreads sideways rather than upward, clinging in long, interrupted belts to terraces of young black stone.

The rock looks recently poured. It has few gullies, no softened shoulders, none of the patient signatures by which rain usually claims a mountain. Great planes meet at unfinished angles. Mineral seams end abruptly in blank material and resume hundreds of meters away. In places, cliff faces retain a glassy skin beneath which crystals are still aligning, very slowly, with faint clicks like cooling metal. New shelves emerge over decades. Buildings on them are given provisional addresses until the geology decides whether they belong to the eastern or western district.

Rain is infrequent and fine. It falls in narrow curtains that may occupy only one street, one bridge, or half a factory yard. Most moisture comes from condensation. At night the cold surfaces of the city gather beads of water: roofs, cables, window frames, iron handrails, the broad cooling fins of the power houses. Before dawn, gutters begin to murmur. The droplets are led through silvered channels into cisterns cut behind the inhabited walls.

Water is valuable, but not rare enough to be sacred. It is treated as something temperamental. Reservoir doors are marked with tide tables despite there being no sea. Public fountains operate only during certain fogs. A chain of shallow pools lies on one terrace beneath the freight district, each no wider than a room and astonishingly clear. They have no visible source. Their levels rise when machinery elsewhere is switched off.

The city itself is spacious despite its industry. Districts stand apart on separate shelves, joined by viaducts, cableways, funicular rails, and tunnels whose far ends are not always located at the same altitude as their entrances. Between settlements lie long stretches of naked stone and low vegetation. From a distance, habitation appears scattered: a constellation of furnace windows, sodium lamps, chimney crowns, and signal beacons suspended across the mountain darkness.

Up close, each district is dense with construction. Factories are mortared directly into cliffs. Worker housing stacks beneath reservoirs and over turbine halls. Pipes cross alleys at shoulder height, sweating cold water. Elevators descend through shafts older than the neighborhoods they serve, though the neighborhoods themselves are older than the rock according to municipal records. Bridges have bridges beneath them, built as insurance against revisions in gravity. Many doors open onto stairs that are still being constructed by slow mechanical rigs, one step every few months.

The architecture is dark and mineral: basalt block, riveted steel, green-black copper, smoked glass, ceramic conduits, timber cured in engine heat. Bright paint is reserved for warnings and animal passages. Since daylight is weak, windows are narrow and lamps are kept low, hooded against fog. The entire city seems lit from under its eyelids.

Power comes from pressure differences deep inside the mountain. Boreholes descend into strata where distance has weight. The engines built over them are immense, patient devices—flywheels turning once every several minutes, pistons as tall as houses, belts moving with the solemnity of processions. No one agrees on what they extract. Officially it is geothermal force. Mechanics say the machines consume unused elevation. The oldest diagrams label the product as *descent*.

Their operation gives the city its principal sound: not a constant roar, but overlapping pulses. Metal groans travel through the terraces. Warning horns speak from opposite walls of the rift. Ore trams rattle across bridges, followed seconds later by echoes that come back altered—sometimes softer, sometimes in the voices of animals, sometimes announcing train numbers that do not exist. Ventilation fans beat in slow ranks. Cable wheels shriek. Loose sheet metal chatters in the high wind.

Between these noises are enormous pauses. In such moments, condensation can be heard ticking into the gutters, and wings moving in the dark.

Animal life is everywhere.

The city’s sparse human layout leaves broad territories for other species, and almost every structure supports an ecology. Pale mountain goats walk the aqueduct parapets, their hooves producing no echo. Colonies of black swifts nest inside warm exhaust ducts and emerge in spirals at shift changes. Foxes sleep on transformer housings. Long-bodied amphibians crowd the condensation sumps, feeding on moths with metallic dust on their wings. Moss beetles graze the green films inside drainpipes. High above, large gliding creatures circle between the terraces; depending on angle, they resemble vultures, rays, or holes cut in the sky.

The most numerous animals are small, six-legged grazers known as lint deer. They browse among cable grass and boiler moss, moving in herds through vacant lots. Their bodies are no larger than cats, but their antlers branch into delicate structures several times their size. In damp weather, droplets collect along these antlers and ring softly when the animals walk. The city tolerates them because they detect structural failure. Hours before a bridge shifts or a wall delaminates, every lint deer in the district turns to face it.

Vegetation thrives in pockets rather than blankets. Where condensation gathers, the stone is crowded with ferns, liverwort, cool orchids, and dense blue moss. Trees grow only in sheltered cracks and civic gardens: thin, silver-barked forms with horizontal roots that grip the cliff like fingers. Their leaves are dark enough to appear black. Industrial heat creates isolated jungles around laundries, foundries, and exhaust courts, where vines climb smokestacks and broad fronds tremble in chemical drizzle.

Elsewhere, the terrain remains bare for kilometers. A single mat of grass may occupy a whole shelf, bright as fabric against the raw geology. Lichen advances across new rock at the speed of a neighborhood. Botanists map its margins with stakes, returning after twenty years to find it has crossed a hand’s breadth.

Decay is similarly reluctant. Rain has not yet learned how to carve the mountains. Streams run over the rock without cutting channels, as if surface and water have not agreed which one yields. Loose sediment is scarce. When cliffs fail, they break into clean geometric slabs that remain sharp for generations. Ruins do not soften into the landscape; they stand beside newer construction with every edge intact.

Because erosion is young, the city must manufacture soil. Organic waste, factory ash, shed feathers, bone meal, moss scrapings, and powdered stone are composted in heated galleries. The resulting dark earth is issued by weight to gardens. Unauthorized soil is contraband, since it can harbor spontaneous topography. In one notorious district, an illegal rooftop allotment developed a slope, then a horizon. Three apartment blocks now overlook a small country inaccessible from the street below.

Such failures of reality are not dramatic here. They accumulate slowly, becoming municipal problems.

A staircase may gain an additional step each winter. A warehouse may acquire a second interior that workers enter only on night shifts. Shadows lag several seconds behind pedestrians in the upper wards. Certain bridges are longer on the return journey. An entire tram line runs through a district visible only in reflections on wet steel. Its passengers occasionally disembark carrying valid work permits, old coins, and seeds from plants no botanist has identified.

The city has rules for these things.

Never enter an elevator if its floor indicator shows a direction rather than a number.

If fog forms indoors, open all western windows, regardless of which way west currently lies.

Do not follow an echo that addresses you by occupation.

Animals always retain right of way on suspended bridges.

No excavation may continue after workers uncover daylight.

These rules are posted beside ordinary notices about boiler pressure, freight schedules, and water conservation. They are not treated as superstition. They are provisions in the building code.

Beyond the last terrace, the landscape opens into a high, dim region of unclaimed shelves and unfinished escarpments. Transmission pylons stride away into it, widely spaced, their cables vanishing in low cloud. Herds move between isolated wetlands. Abandoned drilling towers stand on ridges, red lamps blinking at intervals too slow to be useful to aircraft. There are roads, but many end at stone faces where future tunnels have been marked in chalk.

The weather changes gradually. A cold front may take a month to pass through the valley. Fog rises by centimeters. Frost develops in elaborate plates along rooftops and remains all season. The city measures time through these slow transformations: migration of swifts, filling of cisterns, alignment of crystals, drift of stairs, the turn of the great flywheels below.

Even emergencies possess patience. A landslide can be predicted years before it moves. Sirens sound on the first day of each month to remind residents that a particular cliff is falling. The actual descent is expected within three generations. Buildings in its path are dismantled room by room, their materials carried to a new terrace that is itself still condensing from the dark.

At night, the settlement seems both immense and nearly empty. Lamps mark the separate districts. Furnace glow stains the vapor beneath the bridges. Trams creep along the mountain walls like illuminated needles. Above them, the peaks continue into distances that should not fit beneath the stars.

Far below, where the valley floor ought to be, water can sometimes be heard.

It is not the sound of a river already flowing. It is slower and more intermittent: a gathering, a testing of routes, the noise of droplets assembling an intention. Engineers lower instruments into the vapor and retrieve them coated in silt from landscapes that do not yet exist.

The city waits above this unborn river, drawing power from depth and drinking from the skin of the night. Around it, animals nest in machines, gardens root in manufactured earth, and raw mountains hold their sharp forms. Everything appears permanent because almost nothing moves quickly.

Yet the whole place is under construction—not merely the streets and bridges, but altitude, drainage, distance, and the ordinary agreements of matter. The city is not built upon the landscape so much as built alongside it, both of them unfinished, each waiting to see what the other will become.
