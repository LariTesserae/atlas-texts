Inside the uppermost girder of the western tower is a mountain range no larger than a railway carriage.

It was built there deliberately.

A rectangular maintenance hatch opens onto its eastern end, and beyond the hatch the floor drops away into a deep, narrow basin of black ceramic. Across it rise seven angular peaks, each assembled from interlocking plates of nickel steel. Their ridges are unworn, their faces planar, their scree fields composed of numbered bolts and sharp curls of machining waste. Nothing has rounded. Nothing has had time to become old, though the range itself may have existed for centuries.

The air is painfully cold and perfectly dry. Moisture is not merely absent; it is forbidden. Frost cannot form. Breath entering from the service corridor is seized by a grille in the threshold, separated into gases, and pumped away before it can cross the first summit. Warning marks engraved beside the hatch show a droplet beneath a barred circle. Water would crack the ceramic, poison the lubricant, perhaps disturb whatever exact balance the little mountains maintain.

The basin stands at tremendous altitude. Through narrow inspection ports in the outer wall, there is only pale sky and, very far below, the geometric haze of the factory continent. Yet the chamber itself is intimate. A person can cross it in twenty careful steps. Kneeling at the threshold places the peaks at eye level, making them seem remote and immense. Every summit is reachable by hand, though touching them is prohibited. Their knife edges can part a glove without resistance.

Everything here is manufactured: the mountains, the valleys, the dust, the cold, even the apparent distance. There is no native rock beneath the plates. The deepest bore ends in wiring channels, vibration isolators, and hollow structural ribs. What resembles mineral grain is fused industrial abrasive. What looks like obsidian is smoked glass. The pale soil in the valleys is powdered alumina metered from sealed hoppers, each particle graded to within a narrow tolerance. Even gravity is slightly misleading. Electromagnetic beds beneath the basin tug at its ferrous slopes, giving loose fragments a preference for “down” that does not quite match the tower’s true vertical.

The landscape is sparse. Most of it is bare black incline and empty interval. Here and there, along sheltered seams, stand low growths resembling thorny lichens. They are not alive in any ordinary sense. Each is a branching deposit of conductive carbon, cultivated by leakage current across the ceramic. Copper-colored tips gather static charge; hair-fine roots enter sockets under the mountain skin. They grow only until they complete a prescribed circuit. Then they stop forever.

No insect visits them. No animal has ever left a track in the powdered valleys. There are no nests, bones, droppings, or burrows—none of the small disturbances by which a place admits that it is inhabited. The only marks are registration crosses, inspection stamps, and shallow parallel furrows cut by calibration sledges during construction.

Yet the chamber is deafening.

Behind every wall, the tower’s machinery runs. Turbines, reduction gears, compressors, stamping lines, lift motors, and immense ventilators combine into a continuous physical roar. It is too steady to resemble weather. The sound has no approach or recession, no gusts, no pauses, no individual blows. It is a single sustained industrial magnitude. The metal peaks receive it and divide it into standing waves. In one valley the noise becomes a crushing bass pressure felt in the teeth; along a ridge it thins into a needle-bright scream. Between the third and fourth mountains lies a pocket of near-silence scarcely wider than a palm, surrounded on every side by violence.

Nothing visible moves.

The abrasive dust does not stir despite the noise. The carbon thorns do not tremble. Loose steel chips remain balanced on impossible tips. This stillness is not due to calm but to design. Opposing vibration systems cancel every motion at the basin’s surface while preserving the sound that passes through it. The cancellation is exact and continuous. A flake suspended above a magnetic hollow hangs there year after year, neither falling nor rising, quivering less than the width of an atom.

The light is ordinary, almost disappointingly so. White maintenance strips run along the ceiling, bright enough to show true colors and cast firm shadows. There is no theatrical glow, no mysterious darkness. Under this practical illumination, every impossible detail is plainly available: serial numbers printed on cliff faces; bundled cables descending a ravine like frozen roots; tiny ladders leading to access panels the size of fingernails; yellow hazard bands around a crater that is actually the mouth of a pressure sensor.

At the center stands the range’s strangest feature: a narrow pass containing two identical cairns of machined hexagons. Between them is an empty gap. The gap is the purpose of the entire place.

Sensors measure it from six directions. Cooling loops keep its dimensions constant. The peaks around it act as resonators, the ceramic basin as an insulator, the conductive “plants” as charge drains, and the suspended debris as indicators of field stability. The factory, the tower, and perhaps the larger complex beyond exist to maintain this small absence at one exact width.

No plaque explains why.

The world here obeys rigid local rules. No moisture may enter. No object may be removed. Nothing may cross the central gap. The sound must never cease. The temperature must not rise. If one carbon branch were to lengthen, a relay would burn it back; if a grain of pale dust shifted, hidden actuators would restore it; if a ridge softened through fatigue, replacement plates would rise from beneath before the old ones were withdrawn. Even decay has been engineered into a closed loop.

Consequently, the range is always new and never becoming. Its edges remain fresh. Its artificial scree appears newly fallen but never falls. Its sparse black flora seems caught at the first moment of colonization, though no succession will follow. It is a landscape arrested immediately after its own construction.

Standing at the hatch, wrapped in cold gear and acoustic protection, one feels not that the place is abandoned but that abandonment itself has been manufactured and is being maintained at enormous cost.

Outside, the factory roars across the high air.

Inside the girder, under normal white lamps, seven immaculate mountains preserve a measured emptiness between two stones.
