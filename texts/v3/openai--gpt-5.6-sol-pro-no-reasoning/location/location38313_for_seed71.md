The plateau is so high that distance behaves differently upon it.

From the lower world, it appears as a pale bar laid across half the sky: an eroded continent raised above the clouds, its old mountains worn down to shallow swells and long-backed ridges. From its own surface there is no sense of standing on a summit. There are plains, basins, watersheds, ruined escarpments, and cities separated by journeys of months. Weather is born here and dies here. Rivers begin, lose themselves, and begin again without ever descending from the plateau’s edge.

The land is ancient beyond sharpness. Nothing points upward except what has been built.

Bedrock lies close beneath a skin of black soil and silver turf. The stone has been weathered until it resembles bone soaked for centuries: rounded, fine-grained, and faintly porous. Old cliffs have relaxed into stairs several leagues wide. Canyons persist only as soft trenches filled with fog. Across the open country, enormous boulders rest alone at intervals of ten or twenty miles. Each has the shape of something once angular, but rain, frost, and an immeasurable succession of winds have left them smooth enough to hold the sky without distortion.

There is water, though never enough to unify the place.

Rain falls in narrow moving territories. One may watch a gray column crossing the plain for an afternoon, trailing its dark hem over moss and stone while the country on either side remains dry. Water gathers in shallow tarns, in ruts, under slabs, and in long rivers no wider than a road. These rivers flow slowly through beds vastly too broad for them, as though they are the final threads of waters that once filled the valleys from rim to rim. Some vanish beneath gravel. Some climb.

The climbing rivers are accepted without comment by those who live here. On certain slopes, particularly at dawn, water runs upward in branching films, collecting from hollows and ascending toward the ridge. It does not fall back when the sun rises. It simply ceases to have been there. The moss remains wet.

Cold governs the plateau, but it is not the clean, killing cold of glaciers. It is a damp cold that enters woven cloth, wood joints, packed earth, and memory. Frost flowers form inside sealed vessels. Condensation appears on the outer walls of houses when the air is driest. There are weeks when every surface develops a thin pelt of ice except the standing pools, which remain black and liquid. Snow is rare and indecisive. It falls as scattered flakes beneath clear skies, often rising a short way before settling.

Vegetation is abundant close to the ground and sparse above it. Vast mosslands cover the old stone in shades of peat-green, blue-gray, rust, and almost black. Lichens make pale archipelagos across exposed rock. Tough grasses grow in circular colonies with empty centers, and broad-leaved plants crowd the river trenches, their leaves thick as felt and cold to the touch. In sheltered basins stand groves of low, twisted trees. Their trunks run horizontally beneath the soil for hundreds of paces, sending up isolated stems so that what appears to be a scattered woodland may in fact be one plant.

Nothing flowers in the ordinary sense. Some plants open white interior surfaces to daylight, but these have neither pollen nor scent. No insect visits them. There are no birds, no burrowing things, no fish under the river skin. Nothing grazes the grass or nests in the trees. Even microscopic animal life appears to be absent. Fallen leaves are not eaten; they darken, soften, and slowly become soil through the action of root, water, and nameless mineral processes.

The silence left by this absence is immense.

Wind can travel for days without finding anything tall enough to make it speak. Rain touches moss with little sound. Rivers move without chatter over the time-rounded beds. In the cities, one hears footsteps, the knock of wooden mallets, the drag of stone sledges, the rasp of saws, and occasionally a human voice carrying farther than intended. Between settlements, silence is not an interval between noises. It is the principal substance of the air.

The cities stand widely separated, each built around a discontinuity.

They are large without being crowded. Walls of fitted stone enclose terraces, gardens, reservoirs, kilns, meeting courts, and districts of low houses, but broad empty spaces remain among them. A street may be fifty paces wide and serve six doorways. Entire squares are planted only with moss. Roofs are made of timber, turf, or overlapping stone flakes; metal is rare, usually soft copper hammered into hooks, needles, mirror plates, and narrow ritual blades. Most labor is done with stone adzes, bone-shaped ceramic tools, ropes braided from root fiber, and patient leverage.

There are no bones to use. There have never been animals here, according to every account, and the human body seems less an exception than an intrusion nobody can explain.

The inhabitants cultivate river plants, lichens, tubers, and the layered flesh of certain subterranean fungi. They burn peat and oily wood. They wear felted bark and cloth woven from pale grass. For transport they use runners, rollers, rafts, and wind sledges with square fabric sails. Nothing pulls a cart. The wheel is known but not especially useful on the softened, fissured land, and in some places it behaves unreliably, completing more turns than the distance should allow.

Every city is organized around such failures.

At Oru, the central plaza is broader after sunset than before. Houses facing it must have two sets of stairs, one for morning and one for evening, though both occupy the same stonework.

At Vel Astan, rain falls upward from a circular cistern and disappears into a low ceiling of cloud. The city harvests it with inverted roofs. Its people build downward, yet each lower district enjoys a wider horizon.

At Mere, shadows remain for several minutes after the objects casting them have moved. Public messages are written by arranging posts at noon and reading their delayed shadows later.

At the nameless eastern city, no wall can be completed. When masons close an enclosure, some other boundary opens: a doorway vanishes, a courtyard joins the plain, or a distant section of the city becomes interior. Its citizens therefore construct in spirals and leave deliberate gaps, believing architecture must be allowed to breathe.

The oldest structures are not ruins. Ruin implies a passage from completion into decay, and these buildings seem to have bypassed both states. Low embankments cross valleys without beginning or end. Flights of steps descend beneath moss and emerge hundreds of miles away, sometimes in another city, sometimes in an uninhabited basin where the steps continue upward into the air for a short distance. Towers of rounded masonry lean at impossible angles but cast upright shadows. Rooms are occasionally discovered inside boulders whose exteriors are smaller than the chambers they contain.

Tools remain simple partly because complexity attracts instability. A lever is trustworthy. A pulley usually is. Two pulleys used together may lift an object before the rope is pulled. Gears sometimes transfer motion into adjacent days. Written numbers, if arranged too densely, alter the quantities they record. The plateau tolerates craft better than machinery and repetition better than precision.

For this reason, knowledge resides in gestures, songs, knots, and spaced inscriptions. Builders measure with their bodies and adjust continuously. Reservoir keepers learn the temperament of each pool. Roads are marked not by distances but by sequences: black stone, split ridge, grove of seven stems, place where breath smokes blue, river crossed twice. Maps are made on flexible cloth and never fully unfolded, because a complete view of the plateau has been known to make remote regions coincide.

Human settlement continues despite all this, slowly and with great care. Fields are tended. Walls are repaired. Children learn which reflections belong to them. Travelers carry packets of home soil so that they may establish a small area of ordinary direction around a sleeping place. At city gates, newcomers are asked where they have come from three times. If all three answers are identical, they are welcomed. If the answers differ, they must wait outside until morning, or until one origin becomes dominant.

There are no roads in the deepest interior, although lines resembling roads move across it.

These are long pale bands visible from high ground. They advance by a few yards each year, pressing moss flat without killing it. Where two meet, rain begins. Where three meet, a new pool forms, perfectly circular and without visible depth. Settlements avoid these junctions, but temporary markets sometimes gather nearby because objects exchanged there lose small portions of their past. A cracked pot may become uncracked because it was never dropped. A woven cloak may forget its dye. A person may return home remembering a sibling no one else recalls.

Beyond the cities, the plateau’s activity is slow but unceasing. Frost lifts stones and sets them down elsewhere. Groves migrate by sending roots forward and letting the rear trunks sink into peat. Rivers revise their direction. Fog condenses into upright sheets that drift across the plain like walls of dull glass. Soil opens in long seams during the coldest nights, exposing not rock but daylight—ordinary daylight, warm and golden, shining from a depth that cannot be reached.

No animals emerge from these openings. Nothing ever does.

The people regard the absence of beasts as the oldest law of the world. They have words for movement, hunger, fear, pursuit, and flesh, but no native category for a living thing that is neither plant nor human. Decorative carvings show leaves, hands, rivers, tools, clouds, and geometric forms. When travelers dream of winged or four-footed creatures, they do not interpret them as inventions. They call them *the shapes that life refused*.

At the plateau’s margins, the land does not end in a cliff. It becomes progressively thinner.

Grass gives way to lichen, lichen to exposed stone, and stone to a gray surface that looks solid until touched. Beyond it lies weather seen edge-on: stacked storms, clear layers, slow lightning, and cloud systems turning like submerged continents. Objects thrown outward do not fall. They diminish while remaining apparently the same distance away. Voices spoken toward the margin return years later in unfamiliar mouths.

Some believe the plateau is the final worn surface of an entire world, all mountains and seas eroded into one high, cold plain. Others say it is a roof, and that the lights exposed in soil fissures belong to rooms below. A few surveyors maintain that elevation is not height here but age: the plateau is high because it is old, and the lower country glimpsed through clouds is simply younger time accumulating beneath it.

This would explain why erosion no longer seems to lower the land. Wind and water continue their work, wearing every edge rounder, smoothing every surface toward an ultimate simplicity, but the plateau remains high. Material removed from one place appears as terraces in another. Dust settles before it rises. Broken masonry becomes ancient bedrock; bedrock, after enough winters, reveals doorways.

On calm days, when no rain territory is crossing and the fog walls have dissolved, the light is plain and colorless. One can see for astonishing distances: empty moss country, a black thread of river, solitary stones, perhaps a city standing like a handful of teeth on the horizon. Smoke rises from its kilns and bends toward the ground. Farther away, another city may be visible above the first, though no mountain supports it.

Nothing calls across the distance.

The plateau changes continuously, quietly, and without witness enough to measure it. Water climbs. Roots travel. Streets lengthen after dark. Old stairs arrive at new destinations. Beneath all of it, the rounded stone rests in what appears to be its final form.

Yet every morning there is slightly more horizon.
