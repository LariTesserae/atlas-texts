The city occupies a high basin where the mountains have not yet finished becoming mountains.

Their slopes are blunt extrusions of black mineral, shouldering upward through fractured terraces and half-folded strata. Some ridges still retain the smoothness of pressure rather than weather: dark, convex masses without gullies or scree, as though raised only recently from below. Others are wrapped in scaffolds that disappear into the cloud ceiling. The city has fastened instruments to the raw stone—rings, braces, kilometer-long needles—and appears to be assisting the range in its ascent.

It is almost completely dark.

There is no sunrise here in the ordinary sense. A heavy atmospheric layer fills the basin and presses against the upper slopes, condensing continually on every surface. Light arrives only as a diluted bruise behind the clouds, too weak to cast shadows. Below it, illumination is infrastructural: thin blue seams along parapets, intermittent white wells beneath bridges, amber symbols drifting over transit apertures. Many structures carry no visible lamps at all. They are navigated by sound, pressure, electromagnetic sense, or the shared perception of their inhabitants.

Water is everywhere, though little of it is open.

It lies just beneath the plazas, circulates through porous walls, beads on suspended cables, and seeps from the newly exposed rock. Every façade weeps. Condensation crawls down towers in silver films and gathers along ledges until gravity releases it in heavy, scattered drops. The streets have deep central channels, but these are rarely full; the water is intercepted, divided, and drawn sideways through thousands of narrow slots. Beneath the city, cisterns interlock with natural pockets in the mountain. They store meltwater so cold and clear that machinery submerged in it remains visible for kilometers.

Where the basin floor can be seen, it is bare mineral: iron-dark pavement, pale salts, glassy extrusion, wet basalt, and expanses of a blue-black substance neither rock nor manufactured ceramic. There are no mosses in the cracks, no lichens on the walls, no stubborn alpine grass. Nothing roots. Nothing flowers. The air carries no pollen and the soil, where excavation has produced something resembling soil, is sterile grit.

Yet the city is overwhelmed by animals.

They crowd every dry surface and many of the wet ones. Vast colonies sleep under bridges, cling to ventilation towers, fill abandoned conduit shafts, and pack themselves onto roofs in layers. Some resemble familiar cold-country creatures only at a distance: humped bodies with dense silver coats; six-limbed climbers whose broad feet seal to wet stone; blind, white swimmers that rise through flooded stairwells to breathe. Others have polished skins that shed water instantly or shells grown in geometric plates. Flocks move between towers without wingbeats, altering their buoyancy in coordinated pulses. Long-bodied grazers—though there is nothing here to graze—wander slowly through the plazas, their hollow ribs humming in the wind.

Their abundance seems impossible because no visible food web supports it.

The city does.

Heat exchangers exhale dense curtains of nutritive vapor. Mineral reservoirs extrude edible films onto designated walls. Subterranean foundries assemble complex organic slurries from water, atmosphere, and deep carbon. At certain hours, black channels along the streets fill with warm, faintly luminous broth, and the animals descend in numbers sufficient to hide the paving. Feeding is noisy: shells knocking against stone, throats clattering, millions of wet mouthparts rasping manufactured food from the channels.

The inhabitants did not bring animals into the city. It may be more accurate to say that the animals built a reason for the city to remain.

Human-shaped figures are occasionally visible on high walkways, but they are rare and heavily enclosed. Some may be machines designed after bodies long gone. The principal citizens are the colonies themselves, registered not as individuals but as enduring patterns: the West Viaduct Flock, the Nine Warm Reservoirs, the migratory mass that occupies the upper towers for sixty years and then moves east. Doors respond to swarms. Elevators wait for herds. Transit schedules accommodate spawning tides. Whole districts are legally dormant while enormous brooding bodies settle across them.

The architecture is correspondingly difficult to distinguish from habitat.

Towers rise in close ranks from the basin walls, near enough that their upper sections touch. Their exteriors are ridged, hollowed, perforated, and warmed in bands. Bridges branch repeatedly until the air between buildings becomes a dense three-dimensional street system. Beneath the bridges hang roosting vaults; beneath those, pipe forests; beneath the pipes, mesh cradles heavy with sleeping creatures. Every volume is occupied. Even the cold rain has inhabitants—transparent specks that remain suspended within droplets and aggregate when water runs down a pane.

Nothing moves quickly except sound.

The city is loud without being hurried. Its dominant noises are sustained: the organ-deep vibration of mountain braces, the roar of water passing through pressure gates, the continuous granular hiss of claws over stone. Animal calls take minutes to unfold. A single note may begin in the lower basin and be answered, much later, from the high terraces. Resonant cavities in the towers amplify these exchanges until the entire settlement seems to breathe through a stone throat.

Occasionally a pressure signal passes through the bedrock. Windows tremble. Water standing on flat surfaces organizes into parallel ridges. Every animal becomes silent at once.

Then, from somewhere far below, comes a slow concussion.

It is not an earthquake. It is the arrival of another piece of terrain.

The mountains are being printed upward from structures sunk beneath the crust, although no one knows with certainty whether those structures were placed there or grew there. Matter emerges by degrees: first as a pressure anomaly, then as warm darkness, then as seamless black stone. A new ridge may take five centuries to rise twenty meters. The city adjusts around it. Streets are lifted intact. Foundations soften and re-form. Towers incline by fractions of a degree, migrating so gradually that colonies pass through generations before the change is apparent.

Erosion has barely begun. Water touches these surfaces before wind or frost can mark them, but it has not yet had time to cut valleys. It gathers uncertainly in shallow depressions, testing possible rivers. Drainage routes alter as the ground rises. Some streams run uphill because the city’s gravity systems were established according to an older topography; they climb the sides of buildings in trembling sheets and enter reservoirs suspended above the basin.

Cold fixes everything into patience. The wet stone skins over with clear ice, thaws from below, and freezes again. Icicles descend from the aerial streets in dense curtains, but they grow around heated filaments and never fall. Fog crystallizes briefly into slow clouds of needles. The animals carry this frost on fur, shell, and membrane until they resemble moving fragments of the architecture.

There are rules here, though they are not advertised.

No surface may remain unused.

No body is counted alone if it belongs to a colony.

Water must descend eventually, but not necessarily today.

New stone has right of way.

Darkness is not considered an absence. It is a civic medium, kept deep and continuous for creatures whose migrations depend on it. Bright light is treated as a pollutant and is permitted only during surgery, structural emergency, and the rare inspection of the lowest cisterns. Even then, screens descend over nearby roosts, and warning vibrations pass through the walls hours in advance.

From the highest inhabited terrace, the city extends across the basin as a nearly lightless density of wet planes and layered silhouettes. The surrounding mountains are less a boundary than the next district under construction. Clouds move among the upper bridges. Water glints in momentary lines. Here and there, entire façades flex as sleeping colonies turn together.

Far below, another ridge presses upward.

The sound takes a long time to arrive. When it does, millions of animals answer—not in alarm, but recognition—and the unfinished valley resonates with their voices.
