The high country begins above the clouds and does not appear to end.

From a distance it resembles a black plateau, its rim sharp and newly broken, but there is almost no stone in it. The whole elevation is vegetable: an uplifted continent of roots, trunks, husks, and densely braided fibers, growing upward and outward faster than weather can soften its edges. What look like mountains are old crowns buried beneath younger growth. What look like valleys are separations between colossal stems. Their walls descend for miles into darkness without reaching ground.

There may be no ground.

The upper surface is a cold, uneven wilderness of bark. Great plates overlap like newly formed shale, glossy where frost has sealed them and pale where the wind has stripped away their outer skin. Ridges rise in hooked fins. Splinters the size of towers lean over chasms, still yellow at the break. Along the freshest fractures, sap has frozen in translucent blue curtains. Nothing here has had time to become rounded. The terrain grows, splits, and sheds too quickly for age to take hold.

Vegetation is not something upon the landscape. It is the landscape, the atmosphere’s scaffolding, and almost every object that seems separate from it.

Low forests of needle-felt cover some exposed backs, though “low” means only that they rise a few dozen feet. In sheltered folds, jointed stalks stand hundreds of feet apart, each carrying a broad, black leaf that turns slowly to follow a light too faint for human eyes. Filamentous moss bridges gulfs. Hollow vines, inflated with thin gases, climb into the sky and vanish among the clouds; when they break loose, they trail for days across the upper dark like green-black streamers.

Despite this profusion, the place feels empty. Growth occupies nearly all available substance but very little apparent space. Plants make single immense gestures separated by terrifying distances: one leaf spread across a basin, one pale flower opening on a summit, one root cable spanning a horizon. Between them are miles of cold air and shafts of absolute blackness. There is none of the clutter of a terrestrial forest. The scale has thinned abundance into solitude.

Day brings only a change in the quality of darkness. Beneath a soot-colored sky, the plant continent absorbs nearly everything. Frost gives the ridges a dull outline. Sap channels sometimes shine from within, carrying weak green pulses through the bark, and tiny organisms gather along them like settlements beside roads. During the long night, flowers open in distant sequence. Their lamps—violet, amber, livid blue—appear one at a time across hundreds of miles. Seen from a high edge, they look less like blossoms than towns signaling to one another.

Water is present, but never securely. Rain crosses the plateau in narrow moving territories. One ridge may be hammered for an hour while the next remains powder-dry beneath stars. The rain does not often form lakes; it enters the living mass through pores and funnels, descending into hidden cisterns. What stays above gathers in leaf cups, split knots, and the concave backs of giant fungi. By morning these pockets skin over with ice. Beneath the ice, small ecosystems continue in amber water: silver larvae, root-feeding worms, translucent swimmers that carry bubbles under their shells.

Condensation is more dependable. Clouds snag on the high stalks and comb themselves apart. Droplets travel down hairs and grooves into temporary rivulets, which may run for several miles before vanishing through a breathing fissure. A few channels have become true rivers, though they are rivers only intermittently. They awaken during storms, black water rushing between bark banks, carrying seeds, shed shells, and rafts of woven roots. Then the channels drain. Their beds contract and close, leaving only wet seams that pulse faintly as the greater organism drinks.

The cold is severe but not still. Wind moves almost continuously over the uplift. Because the world is hollow and fibrous, it does not merely whistle across the surface; it plays the entire terrain.

Air enters open stems and produces notes too low to hear cleanly, felt instead in the ribs and teeth. Torn leaf edges crack like sails. Seed pods rattle in their millions along far slopes, a dry advancing roar. Root bridges groan under their own growth. Sometimes a fracture runs through the continent with a sequence of concussions, each more distant than the last. Hours later, the broken face begins knitting itself closed.

There are quieter sounds embedded within the great ones: claws ticking on frost, the papery rush of wings, water dripping deep inside a trunk, animals calling across distances that alter their voices. But silence never settles. Even in calm weather the plateau creaks, contracts, pumps, and murmurs. It is less like standing in a forest than standing on the chest of an enormous sleeping thing.

Animal life is abundant, though seldom obvious at this scale.

Most creatures live close to the surfaces that sustain them. Herds of six-legged grazers migrate along warm sap lines, scraping frost lichens from the bark with stone-hard mouthplates. From above they resemble drifting ash. Pale tunneling beasts inhabit the soft layers under fresh growth; their passages swell and close behind them, causing long traveling humps in the terrain. Predators wait at breathing pores, warmed by the exhalation. Some are thin as cords and strike by looping around prey. Others imitate broken twigs until the wind changes.

The sky fauna is larger. Broad gliders launch from the sharp young ridges and may remain aloft for months, feeding on pollen clouds and airborne seeds. Their wings are ribbed leaves stolen from the plants during infancy and kept alive by vessels rooted into the animals’ backs. In storm season, flocks wheel around the vine towers, crying loudly enough to be heard through the wind. When they die, they do not fall at once. The living wings continue to climb toward light, carrying the carcass until cold or starvation makes both descend.

In the chasms are deeper animals, known mostly by sound. Something knocks from wall to wall at irregular intervals. Something else follows the underground rivers when they run. On rare nights, vast translucent bodies rise through the gaps, buoyed by warm fermenting gases. They hang above the plateau like moons made of skin, trailing feeding threads into the canopy. Smaller creatures swarm around them, and for a few hours the darkness becomes crowded with wings.

Scattered through this living immensity are signs of people.

They do not build on the exposed bark if they can avoid it; no foundation remains fixed for long. Their shelters are grown into the lee side of spines or suspended from braided root cables. A settlement may consist of three woven chambers, a smoke-blackened platform, and a ladder descending beyond sight. Another may be no more than a wind screen beside a reliable condensation groove. Distances between them are immense. Fires are small and sheltered in clay-lined seed husks, for wood cut from the terrain often remains alive and resists burning.

Their tools are bone, flaked shell, glassy frozen sap, and fibers cured in bitter plant secretions. Metal is rare, appearing only as dark nodules drawn up from whatever lies beneath the roots. A metal hook may pass through twenty generations. People use such hooks to cross gaps, harvest ice, or anchor themselves during the violent opening of flowers.

Most construction is cooperative with growth. Walls are trained, not raised. Bridges begin as cords thrown across a fissure, then fed and watered until they thicken. Water vessels are immature pods bound into shape. Doors are sleeping leaves persuaded to curl shut at a touch. This is not effortless: the plants have their own direction and timescale, and a dwelling neglected for a season may seal itself, migrate several yards, produce thorns, or open into a flower.

Paths exist only as habits. A route is marked by cuts that heal slowly, by bones tied to stalks, by knots whose orientation indicates the next safe crossing. Maps are sung because the land rearranges its surfaces. The songs record not positions but relationships: follow the downwind side of the humming ridge; wait for the third exhalation; cross when the lantern bloom closes; never sleep above a rising seam.

The most important rule is that emptiness is not absence. A clear span of bark may be the tongue of a trap flower. A quiet chasm may be occupied by an animal whose breath takes an hour to rise. A bare summit may be bare because it has recently unfolded.

Another rule concerns water: no pool is ownerless. Every pocket is either being gathered by a plant, guarded by animals, or remembered by travelers. To drain one thoughtlessly is both dangerous and a kind of theft. In the cold season, communities cut blocks only from ice that has lifted away from its basin. Ice still touching the water is considered alive.

Storms transform the plateau quickly. Cloud walls arrive from below, boiling through the fissures rather than crossing the horizon. Moisture darkens the bark. Dormant mats inflate. Leaves that seemed to be plains rear upward and turn their undersides to the rain, revealing forests, nests, and entire migrating herds clinging there. Rivers awaken with a bellow. Flowers detonate open, flinging warm pollen into the freezing air. Predators emerge from dry seams. The people lash themselves to their shelters and set resonant bowls outside, listening to the pitch of the rain to judge whether the structure beneath them is swelling, splitting, or beginning to move.

Afterward comes a brief, luminous thaw. Heat from growth melts the nearest frost. Mist lies in the hollows. Seedlings erupt in soft red crowds, race through their whole lives, and collapse before the cold returns. Animals feed, mate, hunt, and scatter. Waterfalls stream from leaf tips into bottomless gaps. For several hours, the dark country smells of pepper, resin, wet fur, and opened earth—though no one living has seen earth itself.

Then the frost advances again, silvering the new edges.

Far out on the upper reaches, beyond the inhabited ridges, the vegetable continent is still rising. Fresh towers push through the surface with their bark folded and tender. Root arches lift into the clouds. Great cracks open soundlessly at first, showing a dim interior full of hanging gardens and slow green lightning. The land is young there, unfinished and hazardous, but travelers go nonetheless. They seek new water pockets, new metals, new songs, or simply the possibility of seeing what supports the world.

No expedition has reached the bottom.

Those who have descended far enough report that the air grows warmer but the spaces grow larger. The roots cease to braid and hang separately in darkness, each one thick as a mountain and impossibly distant from the next. Rain rises there in droplets. Animals fall upward. Sounds arrive before the events that cause them.

Whether these accounts are true matters less than the certainty felt on the high bark: this cold, dark wilderness is only the crown of something vaster. Its ridges are buds. Its rivers are beads of condensation. Its inhabited reaches, immense as nations, occupy a single season’s growth.

And beneath every footstep, the world is still becoming.
