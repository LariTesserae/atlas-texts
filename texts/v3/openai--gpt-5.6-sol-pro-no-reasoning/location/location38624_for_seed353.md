The place is called the Black Conservatory, though nothing has conserved it and no one remains to use the name.

It lies across a high plateau under a sky made lightless by airborne carbon. The plateau was once mountainous, perhaps, but age and industry have worked toward the same conclusion: ridges planed down, valleys filled, every gradient softened into long shallow inclines. From a distance the land appears almost level. Up close, it is a continuous accumulation of roofs, terraces, conduits, root vaults, collapsed furnaces, and vegetation so dense that terrain and growth cannot be distinguished.

There is no exposed earth.

Where a path cracks open, it reveals not soil but earlier construction: ceramic decks over iron lattices, carbon brick over woven pipes, those over still older decks compressed into black mineral strata. The whole plateau is built to an unknown depth. Its geology consists of obsolete floors. Ancient retaining walls have become bedrock; buried factory districts serve as aquifers would elsewhere, except there is no water in them—only hot gases, spores, dust, oils, and slow rivers of molten resin.

The vegetation grows from this architecture and has replaced its purposes without erasing their shapes.

Tall boiler trees occupy the main halls. Their trunks are squared where they rose through assembly frames, banded with the ribs of machinery they engulfed. Their bark is dull and heat-proof, like charred cork plated with bronze. Instead of leaves, they carry black vanes: layered, jointed surfaces that spread into the sooty air and absorb what little infrared radiation descends from above. In darkness they are nearly invisible. One recognizes them when they occlude some dim red furnace seam farther away.

Between the trunks, every available volume is filled.

Cable vines run in braided bundles along old gantries. Some have copper-colored skins and transmit faint electrical pulses between root colonies. Fan palms turn slowly inside ventilation shafts, their stiff fronds shaped by generations of growth against spinning blades. Low mats of clinker moss coat walls, tanks, floors, and ceilings alike. They need no light. They feed on heat gradients and on metallic compounds breathed out by the buried works. When disturbed, they close their scales in sequence, and a patch the size of a plaza can become smooth and armored in seconds.

Nothing flowers in the familiar sense. Reproduction occurs through fittings, seams, and pressure. Pods form inside sealed vessels until they force the lids open. Spores travel through ducts. Certain trees manufacture seeds as long ceramic cylinders and feed them one by one onto dead conveyor belts; the belts, intermittently awakened by roots, carry them kilometers away before stopping again.

The Conservatory is not abandoned machinery overtaken by plants. The distinction is older than memory and no longer valid. Its plants are industrial, and its industry is botanical.

Roots operate the plateau.

They tighten around pistons, pull flywheels through partial rotations, open valves, close shutters, and maintain enormous pressure differentials beneath the surface. Some roots are fibrous and pale. Others are articulated, glossy, and hard enough to strike sparks. They enter relay boxes and bridge contacts with moistless conductive sap—a resin rich in dissolved salts but containing almost no water. This resin circulates under pressure, amber at low temperatures and nearly black where it runs hottest. It is blood, lubricant, coolant, adhesive, and food.

Free water cannot persist here. The air is too hot, the surfaces too hungry for it. Any trace of moisture is seized by desiccating tissues and split chemically or locked into waxy storage knots deep inside the trunks. Rain, if it ever falls through the carbon ceiling, never reaches the ground. The upper canopy catches every drop and dismantles it. Far below, the dominant chemistry is carried in oils, molten wax, volatile hydrocarbons, and mineral vapors.

Heat rises from everywhere.

It leaks through floor joints in long red lines. It breathes from vents in slow exhalations. It trembles above the broad plates where groves have trained themselves to tolerate temperatures that soften tin. The hottest districts are not barren. They are the most crowded. There, forests of thin refractory stalks grow packed as furnace brushes, each stalk sheathed in white ash and tipped with a cup that gathers sulfur vapor. Their roots descend into smelting chambers and separate metals as part of their metabolism. Under them, old foundries continue to cast objects no inhabitant needs.

A mold opens. Resin pours in. A gear, a grate, or a hollow brick emerges and travels away on a creeping belt. Vines inspect it with fine tendrils. Some products are incorporated into new growth. Others are stacked in vast orderly terraces until pressure collapses them into the older strata. Production is not directed toward completion. It is a reflex of the ecosystem, like fruiting.

This makes the landscape turbulent without making it loud.

Most of the time there is only a restrained industrial respiration: the sigh of hot gas through leaves, a distant chain settling tooth by tooth, the faint electrical ticking of vine networks. Sound does not travel cleanly through the packed growth. It is absorbed by bark, moss, cables, and hanging curtains of root fiber. A major collapse several streets away may arrive only as a soft inward thump followed by a tremor underfoot.

Then some pressure threshold is crossed.

A buried turbine wakes and the neighborhood rearranges itself.

Shutters open in sequence across kilometers. Heated gas blasts upward through canopy wells, inflating translucent sacs that lift whole mats of vegetation into the air. Gantries tilt. Root bridges tense and pull factory towers closer together. Seed cylinders rattle through overhead chutes in dark torrents. Ash ascends instead of falling. For an hour the Conservatory may become violently active—conveyors running, pistons hammering, forests bending in coordinated waves—then stop all at once. The resulting silence feels less peaceful than suspended.

The motions are chaotic locally but ancient in aggregate. Over unimaginable time, all turbulence has worn toward a stable pattern. Towers are raised only to lean, collapse, and become terraces. Terraces are split by roots and compacted into foundations. Furnaces migrate slowly as their feeding groves exhaust one mineral seam and ignite another. Destruction does not open space. It merely changes which living structure occupies it.

There is never a clearing.

What resembles a road is the back of a broad segmented organism rooted beneath several districts. What resembles a plaza is a single leaf, stone-hard and dust-covered, spread across old rooftops. Shafts descend through layers of vegetation only to reveal hanging forests underneath. Even the air is occupied by pollen plates, soot-fed lichens, floating bladders, and microscopic threads stretched from wall to wall. Movement through the Conservatory means being admitted through temporary alignments of living parts.

Those alignments obey rules.

Warm objects are treated as organs and enclosed. Moving objects are given channels. Conductive objects are connected. Anything that remains still long enough acquires a skin of clinker moss, then cable roots, then load-bearing trunks. Nothing inert is permitted to remain merely itself.

Fire is not an emergency here. It is weather.

Flames crawl without illumination, deep blue and almost invisible, along vapor gutters. Groves deliberately burn their outer bark to shed accumulated metals. Firestorms pass beneath the canopy rather than above it, traveling through trenches and sealed pipeways. They can melt a district internally while leaving its black foliage standing. Days or years later, the shell folds inward and a new hot basin forms, immediately crowded by seedlings germinated by the collapse.

There are no animals. No pollinators move between the rigid flowers, no burrowers disturb the root vaults, no birds occupy the girders. What seems at first like insect motion is usually a scatter of self-propelled seed rivets walking on sprung metal filaments. What sounds like wings is a pressure valve fluttering behind leaves. Nothing watches.

Yet the vegetation responds with unsettling specificity. A footfall may cause distant vanes to turn. A spoken sound, if anyone were present to make one, would travel through pipework and return hours later as changes in conveyor speed. The plateau has no central intelligence that can be found, but it remembers disturbances as altered growth. A passage becomes a conduit. A touch becomes a graft. Repetition becomes architecture.

Light is rare and biologically valuable. Most visible illumination is generated inside the system: dull furnace red; green corona along high-tension vines; brief white sparks buried in foliage; orange resin glowing through thin pod walls. Plants crowd around these sources without necessarily consuming their light. Some appear to cultivate it. Mirror-leaf colonies line deep chambers with tarnished metallic surfaces, bending a single ember into a hundred weak reflections. In such rooms every trunk casts overlapping shadows, and the impression is of an enormous congregation gathered around the last visible thing.

Above, the plateau extends beyond sight beneath its packed canopy. Its horizons are obscured by soot and heat, but elevated structures occasionally rise from the dark: cooling towers split open by trees, ribbed gasometers swollen with seed tissue, chimney ranges linked by suspended root bridges. Their silhouettes suggest a city, then a mountain chain, then the internal structure of some organ too large to understand.

The highest point is not a summit but an exhaust garden.

There, on the worn crown of the plateau, thousands of hollow trees stand around vents reaching into the deepest factory layers. Their trunks are polished by ages of hot wind. Every few minutes, one releases a column of black spores into the black sky. The column cannot be seen directly. It announces itself by blotting out stars that may not actually be stars, only distant electrical wounds in the atmospheric soot.

The spores rise, cool, and descend somewhere else.

Where they land, they adhere to whatever has briefly become exposed. A gear tooth. A fractured tile. The cooling crust of resin. Another plant’s bark. Within moments, filaments search for heat, pressure, current, and structural load. They choose one, begin to specialize, and cover the surface.

Thus the Conservatory repairs its perfect saturation.

It is extraordinarily old, but not still. It has reached a final form composed entirely of change: a landscape where nothing new can enter, nothing can leave, and nothing is allowed to remain empty.
