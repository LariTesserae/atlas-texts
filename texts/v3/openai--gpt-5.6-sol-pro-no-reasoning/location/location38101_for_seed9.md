At the summit of a warm, cloud-wrapped mountain stands a garden that has no soil and no mountain beneath it.

It is a small place, no larger than a walled monastery, though its interior has been folded into terraces so intricately that one can walk for hours without reaching the opposite side. Every surface is constructed. The apparent bedrock is poured black ceramic; the cliffs are hollow shells of riveted alloy faced with porous stone. Even the pebbles have seams. They lie in deliberate spills beneath drain mouths, each one molded around a kernel of iron so that hidden magnets can gather them again after heavy rain.

The garden is high enough for clouds to pass through it, yet it is almost always warm. Heat rises through grilles underfoot, breathing from old machinery in the enclosed levels below. When mist crosses the summit, it meets this warmth and condenses at once. Railings pearl with water. Leaves shine. The imitation cliffs begin to weep from thousands of capillary pores, and shallow channels fill along the paths.

Water is frequent but never deep. It travels.

It slips down glazed steps, enters narrow rills, vanishes beneath perforated plates, and returns a few minutes later from brass mouths shaped like split seedpods. After rain, the entire place sounds faintly busy: patter on broad leaves, ticking gutters, the soft gulp of cisterns. There are pools, but each is only a handspan deep, its floor tiled in dark green glass to suggest an impossible depth. Pumps keep the surfaces trembling. Now and then a circular ripple appears without any drop having fallen, caused by a pressure change somewhere in the system.

The landforms look newly made because they are.

The garden’s hills are raw bulges of sprayed mineral foam over steel ribs. Its ravines have smooth, undecided sides, not yet bitten into gullies. Channels have been scored only shallowly, as though erosion has just been invented and is waiting to learn its craft. At the edges of some paths, pale aggregate remains exposed where the surface skin has not finished hardening. Warm rain loosens grains from it, carrying them a few centimeters downhill before filters catch them. Overnight, maintenance pipes return the sediment to the upper slopes. Thus the garden is always beginning to erode and always being restored to the moment before erosion becomes history.

Nothing here has had enough time to become ancient, although much of the machinery is old.

The vegetation is abundant, layered, and nearly touching. Ferns crowd beneath heliconias; moss pads thicken around ceramic roots; glossy vines loop from trellises to condenser towers. Dwarf palms lean over waist-high marshes filled with jointed reeds. Bromeliads cup rainwater beneath lattices of copper pipe. The foliage makes the enclosure feel smaller than it is. Passing through requires turning sideways, lifting curtains of vine, or stepping over pale pneumatic roots that subtly change position from day to day.

Most plants are real in the limited sense allowed here. Their roots enter mesh cradles rather than earth. Warm nutrient water pulses through these cradles at intervals, making the leaves quiver all at once. Some trunks are living wood sleeved around structural columns; others are composite tubes clad in bark cultivated from sheets of cork cambium. It can be difficult to tell where plant ends and apparatus begins. A fig may carry fruit on one branch and inspection gauges on another. A stand of reeds may bend in the wind, then continue bending after the air has gone still as valves underneath them regulate the wetland’s pressure.

There is no wilderness, but there is profusion.

The place was built to manufacture beginnings: seedlings, rain patterns, immature streambeds, first-stage soils, miniature climates. Its underground chambers contain grinders, fermenters, heat exchangers, seed drums, fungal baths, and banks of pistons that slowly tilt the terraces. Yet little of this industry is heard directly. The dense garden muffles it. Usually there is only a low vibration in the soles of the feet, the occasional click of a relay, or a long exhalation as vapor is released among the trees.

At certain hours, unseen machinery alters the incline of the entire enclosure by less than a degree. Water chooses new routes. Pools drain into neighboring basins. Wet moss becomes dry moss; dry channels begin to shine. Fine gravel shifts with a sound like grain poured into a bowl. The changes are small but cumulative, so that a path remembered from the previous day may now end at a reed bed, while a former pond has become a warm clearing stippled with germinating plants.

The garden does not imitate a finished landscape. It continually rehearses the making of one.

Its walls rise three stories, mostly hidden behind leaves. They are assembled from repeating panels: vents, translucent reservoirs, inspection doors, trellises, and windows admitting ordinary daylight. The sky above is genuine but tightly framed. Clouds drag across it at startling speed. Sunlight, when it enters, is neither theatrical nor dim—just clean mountain daylight, softened by moisture and broken into moving patches by the foliage. At noon the wet metal flashes silver. In late afternoon every rivet casts a tiny shadow.

The boundaries are unmistakable. Follow any channel far enough and it reaches a numbered grate. Push through any apparent thicket and eventually a warm wall answers beneath the leaves. There is no untouched ground beyond the constructed ground. Outside the walls lies a steep roofscape of ducts and condensation towers, then open air.

Animals are scarce. A few pale moths live in the service cavities and emerge after rain. Tiny snails graze algae from the waterways; their shells bear minute colored marks applied by an automatic census arm. Once in a while a long-legged bird descends from the clouds, stands bewildered in one of the false marshes, and departs when the reeds begin their synchronized adjustment. No fish inhabit the pools. Their movements are supplied by flexible silver vanes beneath the glass, which occasionally flash like bodies turning below.

There may also be one resident creature, though visitors disagree about it.

Something small travels through the drainage system. It leaves three-pronged tracks in the mineral silt and sometimes steals ripe fruit into grates too narrow for a hand. Sensors register its weight, but never its shape. The machinery treats it neither as contamination nor as an authorized component. Doors open for it. Pumps pause as it passes. In the quietest part of the afternoon, one can sometimes hear a faint rapid knocking inside the artificial cliffs, moving against the direction of the water.

The strongest rule of the place is that nothing may settle into permanence.

Roots are trimmed before they can split their cradles. Sediment is lifted uphill. Fallen leaves are pulped and returned as nutrient slurry. Mosses are peeled from seams and replanted on blank surfaces. Every process is allowed to begin but not to conclude. Floods swell, but never scour. Slopes slump, but never collapse. Forest develops, but never closes fully. The landscape is held in a warm, wet adolescence.

Yet the garden is active enough to resist this rule.

Roots enter bolt holes. Algae cloud the depth-tiles. Rain chooses routes not drawn on the engineering plans. Vines bridge moving terraces and strain when the sections tilt apart. Small deposits of genuine soil accumulate behind brackets: leaf dust, snail castings, pollen, rust, and windblown grit. In those narrow pockets, unregistered seedlings appear.

This is where the place feels most alive—not in the designed abundance, but in the tiny dark ledges where manufacture has accidentally produced nature.

After a storm, steam rises from the paths. Water runs everywhere, bright and shallow. Pumps throb far below. Leaves unload heavy drops onto other leaves. The walls sweat; the false cliffs weep; the young channels carry their first temporary sediments downhill. Meanwhile, hidden elevators return yesterday’s gravel to the summit, and along one overlooked seam, a root no thicker than thread presses deeper into the structure.

The whole garden seems to be growing and being built at once, with neither process quite able to distinguish itself from the other.
