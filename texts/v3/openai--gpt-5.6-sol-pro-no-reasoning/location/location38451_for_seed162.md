The plateau is still rising.

From far below—if “below” retains any meaning here—it resembles a dark continent suspended above the weather, its underside buried in amber haze, its upper reaches spread beyond the curvature of sight. The rim is not rounded by age. It is broken into fresh scarps and splintered terraces, black-red where the stone has opened, pale where salts bloom from cooling seams. Whole mountain faces bear the brittle geometry of things only recently made: knife ridges, glassy fans, columns that have not yet learned to fall.

The land is high and warm. Heat rises through it from some depth greater than geology. At dawn, the rock is already warm to the touch; by the dim middle of the day it exhales through millions of fine vents. Yet there is little open sun. Above the plateau hangs a permanent ceiling of cloud—not a continuous layer, but an immense congregation of bruised vapor, overlapping lenses, vaporous shelves, and slow black thunderheads whose bases trail close to the peaks.

Light arrives strained through all this mass. It is copper near the horizon, violet in the ravines, and colorless on the highest stone. Shadows have soft edges until, without warning, they become hard enough to look solid.

Rain is infrequent and local. It does not cross the plateau in fronts. It selects places.

A valley may remain dusty for years while a single neighboring pinnacle receives rain every afternoon. Water gathers in hidden basins, in the cupped leaves of enormous plants, in fractures that open only at night. Condensation is more reliable: warm vapor breathing from the stone meets cold descending air, and beads form on every surface. Forests drink from themselves. Groves wrapped in mist can be lush enough to obscure the ground, while twenty paces away bare mineral shelves ring beneath the feet.

There are no broad rivers. Water travels in episodes.

After a storm, channels appear with astonishing violence, silver ropes hurled through new-cut clefts. They run for hours or weeks, vanish into porous rock, and emerge hundreds of kilometers away as warm springs or hanging curtains of droplets. Some streams climb gentle slopes. Others split around objects that are not there. Pools form in midair where gravity has become briefly indecisive, tremble like transparent organs, and then collapse upward into the cloud cover.

The plants are crowded wherever moisture permits them to be. They have not spread evenly; they occupy the plateau in dense green-black archipelagos.

Along the steaming fissures grow thickets of jointed stalks, each one ribbed like cast metal and glossy with wax. Their leaves are long, narrow, and layered by the hundreds, so that a grove resembles a bank of dark turbines frozen at the instant of turning. When the ground shudders, the leaves rotate against one another and release heat in a faint pulse. Condensation streams down their stems into root chambers large enough to shelter animals.

On mist-facing cliffs, vegetation hangs outward in mats many meters thick. Pale roots grip young rock before weather can soften it. Red bladder-fronds swell during rain, storing whole seasons of water, then slowly feed the surrounding colony through translucent threads. In the dimness below them grow velvet mosses, silver molds, fans of warm fungal tissue, and low plants whose leaves open not toward light but toward vibration.

The plateau’s forests are lush without being gentle. Growth is rapid, opportunistic, and frequently erased. New valleys rise beneath old groves. Escarpments fold open. Soil is scarce and young, assembled in pockets from ash, shed skin, spores, droppings, and powdered stone. Roots must move almost as quickly as animals. Some trees pull themselves across the ground over decades, leaving parallel furrows behind. Others abandon their lower trunks when the terrain changes, rooting from branches and allowing the obsolete portion to dry into hollow scaffolds.

Nothing here has had time to become ancient, though certain living things remember impossible spans.

There are no ruins, roads, towers, fences, or tools. No hand has arranged the stones. Still, the landscape is industrial.

Its industry has no builders.

Across the plateau run belts of natural machinery: fields where iron-rich slabs tilt, lock, and release under tectonic pressure; valleys that inhale mist through narrow vents and compress it until it flashes into lightning; forests whose intermeshed leaves transmit rotation from one ridge to the next. Deep shafts pound through cycles of heat and cooling. Mineral pistons rise from the earth, pause, then descend with enough force to shake dew from vegetation fifty kilometers away.

In one region, a line of basalt wheels emerges from a cliff. Each is taller than a mountain and partly embedded in the rock. They turn without axles, driven by the differential pull of adjacent gravities. Their rims grind fresh stone into black flour. Nothing collects the product. Wind carries it into ravines, where fungi consume its metallic salts and grow into conductive webs.

Elsewhere, thunder is manufactured in underground chambers and released only at seasonal intervals.

The great systems work continuously, but almost silently. Their scale carries noise away from the listener. A cliff may be moving at the speed of a running animal and make no audible sound because its opposite face is several days distant. A forest may flex as one mechanism from horizon to horizon, yet nearby there is only the faint dry ticking of leaves meeting leaves.

Most of the time, the plateau is quieter than a room at night.

One hears condensation dripping into deep cavities. Wings brushing waxy foliage. The soft electrical hiss around exposed stone. A creature breathing somewhere nearby but in a direction that cannot be located. Occasionally a distant impact arrives as pressure rather than sound: the chest tightens, pools dimple, animals freeze, and several minutes later a low note passes through the ground.

The fauna is abundant, though much of it stays hidden from direct light.

Small six-legged grazers crowd the wet groves, their backs covered in photosynthetic fur. They travel in dense, noiseless columns between condensation sites, nibbling mineral films from roots. Thin gliding animals roost beneath the hanging forests and steer through ravines on membranes patterned like oil on water. Warm ponds teem with transparent swimmers whose internal organs flash in sequence, passing messages through the entire school.

Large creatures move across the bare uplands.

Some are broad and low, with dozens of flexible feet that test the local direction of gravity before accepting weight. They migrate between cloud shadows, feeding on the soft crust that forms where lightning has struck. Others resemble walking reefs: slow masses of shell, symbiotic foliage, nests, parasites, and smaller hunters. An individual may carry an entire damp ecology across a hundred kilometers of dry stone.

Predators are less easily defined. One species is visible only as the coordinated absence of insects. Another occupies echoes. When it hunts, sounds return from the wrong distances, herding prey toward the place where the animal will shortly exist.

Life here has adapted not merely to unstable land but to unstable rules.

Distance is the most unreliable quantity. Two peaks can stand side by side in the morning and be separated by a week’s migration at dusk. A valley may contain more interior than its rim encloses. Herds entering the western end sometimes emerge young from the eastern end, though the animals that entered were old. Tracks may precede the feet that make them.

Gravity occurs in broad currents. It generally draws matter toward the plateau, but tributaries peel away around hot vents, storms, and concentrations of living tissue. Seeds exploit these currents. During certain dim afternoons, entire forests release their spores at once, and the sky fills with drifting green continents. Animals climb the invisible slopes among them.

Time is turbulent in smaller ways. Rain can strike leaves before it falls from the cloud. A flash of lightning may illuminate a new ridge that the ground has not yet raised. Creatures sometimes pause beside the shed skins of selves they have not become.

None of this feels exceptional within the plateau. It is simply weather.

The greatest disturbances come from the interior, where the land is unfinished. There, new terrain emerges continually from a zone of dark radiance. Plains pleat upward into ranges over the course of hours. Basins open, fill with warm fog, and close again. Forests arrive rooted and mature, but without histories: no fallen leaves beneath them, no nests, no scars. For a few days they stand in immaculate density. Then insects find them, fungi enter them, animals establish paths, and they become as complicated as everything else.

Creation is balanced by abrupt deletion. A mountain may lose not its substance but its fact. The neighboring valleys lean together across the omission. Winds rush in to occupy the missing geography. Creatures that lived there remain briefly visible as silhouettes in the air, continuing their movements on absent slopes before dropping into other laws.

Storms gather around such wounds.

They are vast, slow, and nearly mute. The cloud ceiling descends in rotating tiers. Static raises every leaf. Warm rain hangs as beads without falling. The mechanical valleys cease their cycles one by one, and the fauna presses into root hollows or gathers beneath the walking reefs.

Then the plateau rearranges itself.

Cliffs slide sideways through groves without crushing them. Rivers pour from cracks in the sky. Lightning branches underground, outlining root systems and mineral veins. For several minutes, every exposed surface reflects a different version of the landscape: drowned, frozen, forested, barren, or covered in immense unfamiliar structures that have never existed here.

When the storm passes, there are still no structures. Only sharp new ridges, steaming channels, displaced forests, and animals emerging into a world altered just enough to demand new migrations.

Quiet returns quickly.

Droplets begin to gather again beneath the leaves. Small grazers file across warm basalt. Far away, one of the buried systems resumes its cycle, turning pressure into motion, motion into heat, heat into cloud. The plateau continues to rise into its dim sky, manufacturing weather, terrain, and living forms without plan or witness.

At the outer rim, fresh stone breaks from the scarps and falls.

It falls for a very long time.

Sometimes it returns from above.
