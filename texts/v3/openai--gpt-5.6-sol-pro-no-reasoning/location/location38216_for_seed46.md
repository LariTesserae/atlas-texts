High in a range too young to have a name, there is a narrow fissure that does not appear from outside. The mountain around it is scarcely a mountain yet: a swelling of pale mineral, hot from its own assembly, its slopes uncut by gullies and unsoftened by weather. No river has found it. No rain has touched it. The stone has not learned how to become gravel.

The fissure opens only when approached sideways.

Seen directly, it is a seam no wider than a root. From the corner of the eye it deepens into a hidden basin, perhaps thirty paces across, though walking its perimeter produces inconsistent results. At noon the circuit takes a few minutes. Near evening one may walk for hours and still see the entrance immediately behind. The place is pocket-sized without being small, embedded not merely in rock but between two slightly different versions of the same altitude.

Inside, the air is furnace-warm and almost perfectly dry. Breath leaves the mouth unchanged; sweat does not gather, but vanishes grain by grain from the skin. The ground consists of raw, cream-colored plates fitted together with the smoothness of poured ceramic. They have no scratches. Their edges are still rounding into existence. Here and there, a plate slowly domes upward, pushed by something beneath, then remains in that position for decades before splitting with a noise like a single knuckle cracking.

There is no water, not even bound invisibly in the stone. Chemistry proceeds through heated salts, metallic vapors, and a dark solvent that never becomes liquid. It moves as a pressure through pores too small to see. Wherever this pressure passes, the mineral blushes orange, then violet, then returns to white. The color travels with the deliberation of a shadow cast by a distant planet.

Plants grow here nevertheless.

They stand far apart, each in a generous circle of empty ground. Their forms are lush but discontinuous: extravagant crowns with almost nothing between them. One resembles a fern assembled from copper-green foil, its fronds broad and cool-looking, though hot enough to burn the hand. Another is a low burst of translucent paddles, packed densely around a central cavity. There are black columns feathered with scarlet filaments; mats of soft needles that contract from touch; pale globes opening into hundreds of folded surfaces, each surface opening into smaller ones.

None contains sap.

Their tissues are lattices of carbon, salt, and flexible mineral glass. They feed on gradients: heat passing upward from the forming mountain, static charge gathered along the fissure, minute differences between one region of space and the next. Their roots are branching electrodes sunk only a finger’s depth into the flawless plates. At intervals, a plant discharges itself into the ground. A blue line slips soundlessly from root to root, and for several seconds every leaf in the basin turns edge-on to the sun.

Then the place is still again.

There are no animals. No flies move among the crowns, no worms loosen the substrate, no invisible mouths graze the films on the leaves. Flowers exist, but they do not call to anything. They open according to pressure changes in the mountain and release small geometric seeds, which rise rather than fall. Most lodge against the underside of overhanging stone. Some disappear into the bright sky at a height of only a few meters, crossing a boundary that cannot be seen.

The vegetation is its own ecology: producer, archive, conductor, and eventual soil. A dead frond does not rot. It slowly disassembles into identical hexagonal flakes, all facing north regardless of where they land. Over centuries these flakes may become the first loose sediment in the basin. For now there are only a few drifts, thin as dust beside the oldest plants.

The industrial character of the place is unmistakable, though nothing has been built.

There are no walls, pipes, roads, tools, or ruins. Yet the basin operates like a factory remembered by geology. Beneath the ground, pressures sequence themselves. Mineral plates rise in orderly batches. The plants align, charge, vent heat, and fall dormant with the coordination of machinery. On some days the air smells faintly of hot insulation. On others there is ozone and the dry iron scent of a rail yard at noon. Perfect circles of warmth pass across the floor as if cast by invisible turbines.

Nothing mechanical can be found. There is only function without apparatus.

Stand in one place long enough and a low vibration becomes perceptible through the bones of the feet. It is too steady to be an earthquake and too deep to be heard. Every few hours it pauses. During the pause, the leaves relax, the fissure contracts, and gravity weakens by a nearly imperceptible amount. Seeds and flakes lift from the ground. Loose hair rises. Then the vibration resumes, and everything settles a fraction to the left of where it had been.

The ordinary sunlight makes this stranger. There is no permanent twilight, no theatrical glow, no convenient darkness concealing what occurs. The sky above the opening is a hard mountain blue. Shadows are crisp. Colors are plain and fully visible. The impossible things happen in the same light in which one might inspect a stone.

At midday, however, shadows begin to detach.

They remain connected to their plants by narrow dark stems, but their larger masses drift across the ground, sometimes folding around obstacles that cast no shadow of their own. Two shadows may overlap and produce not greater darkness but a view downward: a fleeting glimpse of belts, pistons, and enormous slowly turning wheels far beneath the basin. These components are always too distant to measure. They appear worn despite the land being newborn. No sound rises from them.

The mountain may be growing around an ancient process, or the ancient process may be producing the mountain.

Erosion has barely begun. Heat expansion makes hairline fractures, but by dawn most have sealed. A rare flake breaks from the fissure wall and hangs motionless in the air for a season before deciding which direction is down. Wind enters only weakly. It brings no sand and carries almost nothing away. Its principal effect is to make the scarlet filaments lean together, producing a faint dry hiss like brushes passing over metal.

Other sounds are isolated and widely spaced: the click of a cooling plate; a leaf snapping from one crystalline angle to another; the remote electrical hum that sometimes has rhythm and sometimes almost has language. Silence surrounds each sound so completely that every event seems placed rather than caused.

Time is uneven but not violent. A visitor’s pulse remains normal while a new shoot takes years to uncurl between two breaths. Elsewhere, a century of mineral growth may occur during a short blink, leaving a white ridge across the ground no taller than a fingernail. Nothing rushes. Even the failures of reality are patient.

Near the center is the emptiest part of the basin, a bare circle around which the vegetation faces outward. The ground there is warmer and slightly soft, though it bears weight. Looking down reveals no reflection, but looking away reveals, peripherally, an arrangement of gantries and vertical furnaces occupying the same space. They are immense, busy, and uninhabited. Their movements cast no shadows. Each time they complete a cycle, one new plant appears somewhere near the basin’s edge—not sprouting, but becoming incrementally more present until the eye accepts that it has always been there.

This may be how the landscape manufactures itself.

The hidden basin is not an oasis. It offers no drink, no shade cool enough for comfort, no fruit that belongs to animal chemistry. It is closer to a germinating biome: a sealed industrial garden testing the minimum requirements for life. Heat substitutes for rain. Voltage substitutes for weather. Mineral memory substitutes for inheritance.

Everything is sparse because each presence is expensive. Each plant seems to require a wide field of unoccupied reality around it, room from which to draw consistency. Where two grow too close, one becomes uncertain. Its leaves show several positions at once; its roots enter stones that are not there. Eventually it thins into daylight, leaving behind a circular region where footsteps cannot be heard.

The basin changes so slowly that it seems complete. It is not. The floor is still deciding its depth. The plants are still establishing the idea of growth. The invisible engines are still tuning the laws they will later obey.

Far below, the unfinished mountain rises by fractions.

Far above, seeds vanish into noon.

And in the dry hidden chamber between them, the first soil waits to be made from leaves that cannot decay.
