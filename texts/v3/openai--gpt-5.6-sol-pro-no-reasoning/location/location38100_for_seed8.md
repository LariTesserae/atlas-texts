The land is almost level, though not truly flat. It rises and falls by increments too slight to notice underfoot: a shallow back of pale stone here, a basin of dark water there, long shelves of mud descending toward a broad, slow river. Seen from a distance, these small differences determine everything. Water gathers in the old depressions. Grass occupies the margins. Bare mineral ground remains on the higher swells, some of which stand no more than the height of a hand above the floodplain yet stay dry for much of the year.

It is warm, and the warmth has weight. Moisture hangs close to the ground after rain, softening the far horizon without hiding it. The air smells of wet clay, mineral water, and the faint fermentation of drowned leaves. Condensation pearls on stone even when the sky is clear. In the mornings, low mist lies in separate pools, revealing the land’s contours more clearly than sunlight does.

This is an exceedingly old surface. It has not been violently cut into ravines or peaks; it has been worn beyond them. Ridges that may once have been mountains persist only as long, blunt ribs of rock emerging from the plain. Their stone is rounded without being smooth. Rain, heat, shallow floods, and minute living films have opened it grain by grain, leaving shallow cups and channels across its surface. Water moves through these grooves in thin, transparent sheets. Some spill from one basin to the next with a continuous whisper; others end in cracks where the ground drinks them.

There is very little loose stone. Most of what could be carried away has already gone. The remaining ground is either firmly bedded rock or exceptionally fine sediment: gray clay, red-brown silt, and black mud so soft that an animal’s print may fill with water before the animal has passed from sight. In the broadest basins, mud is covered by a shallow skin of water that reflects the sky and makes the plain seem perforated by pieces of brightness.

The main river is wide but not forceful. It moves in several channels separated by low bars, reed beds, and exposed plates of ancient rock. From the bank its motion is hard to see, but floating seeds and bubbles reveal a steady lateral drift. During the wet season, these channels merge. The river steps quietly over its banks and spreads across the plain until only the stone backs and isolated grass hummocks remain above water. There is rarely a dramatic current. Flooding here is enlargement rather than upheaval.

Vegetation is sparse, not because the land is dry, but because the soils are thin, waterlogged, or repeatedly submerged. Tough grasses grow in separated clumps, their blades narrow and waxy. Low mats of salt-tolerant herbs occupy cracks in the stone. Sedges form loose rings around deeper pools, leaving open water at their centers. A few broad, low trees stand far apart along the oldest channels. Their crowns are flattened by heat and exposure, and their roots spread visibly over the rock before entering seams filled with silt. Many carry only a modest number of leaves. Beneath them there is no forest shade—only a darker patch of open ground.

The emptiness between plants makes every living thing conspicuous. Grazing animals move in scattered groups across the shallows, long-legged and light-footed, browsing on submerged stems and patches of new grass. Small amphibians are extraordinarily numerous after rain. They remain hidden in fissures through the hotter hours, then call from every pool toward evening. Their voices overlap until the apparently vacant plain becomes loud enough to obscure the river.

Birds provide the sharpest sounds. Waders cry from mudflats; flocks lift with a clatter of wings and settle again a short distance away. Broad-billed birds sift the shallows, while lean hunters stand motionless beside them, each occupying its own interval of water. Insects hum over the pools in dense but localized clouds. At dusk, clicking, trilling, croaking, and wingbeats assemble into a continuous wall of sound. The landscape changes little, but it is rarely quiet.

Larger animals announce themselves more slowly. Dark backs surface in the deeper channels, pause, and sink. Reptiles bask on rock shelves warmed by the afternoon, so still that algae sometimes dries on their scales. Herds cross the plain along routes defined by footing rather than by visible trails. They avoid the deceptively bright mud, remember submerged ledges, and rest on the same elevated stone patches generation after generation. No path is made, because each flood relaxes the surface and removes the marks.

Life here follows water levels by inches. A basin becomes suitable for eggs, then for larvae, then for fish, then for grazing birds, all without any obvious transformation to an observer standing beside it. A falling river isolates pools. Some persist through the warm season, fed from below; others shrink to warm, crowded mirrors and finally to dark stains in the clay. Seeds wait in the exposed sediment. Tiny crustaceans seal themselves into the drying mud. When rain returns, the plain does not repopulate from elsewhere so much as awaken where it lies.

Decay is quick in the heat but incomplete in the flooded ground. Bones blanch on dry stone and vanish into mineral crust. In mud, fallen stems soften into black layers. Tree trunks lodged in old channels become dark, water-heavy shapes, slowly losing their edges. Nothing accumulates high. The land accepts material, spreads it thin, and wears it down.

There are storms, but even they seem to belong to a longer rhythm. Rain approaches as a gray thickness across the open country. The first drops strike bare rock loudly, then merge into a broad hiss. Shallow cups overflow. Channels appear where minutes before there were only faint discolorations. Frogs begin calling before the rain has fully arrived, and birds walk into the downpour to feed. For several hours every surface moves with water. Afterward, the flow diminishes almost at once, but wetness remains—in clay, cracks, roots, mist, and the dimpling pools.

Under ordinary daylight, the colors are restrained: weathered ocher stone, violet-gray mud, dull green sedges, silver water, and occasional white gatherings of birds. Nothing towers. Nothing encloses the view. The horizon is broad but softened by warmth, vapor, and the nearly imperceptible curvature of the plain.

There are no structures, boundaries, or worked surfaces. No object suggests that the place has ever been organized for any purpose. Its order is older and less visible: height measured in finger-widths, channels remembered by roots, nesting places determined by annual flood marks, and deep water recognized by generations of moving bodies.

The governing rule is patience. Rock becomes clay almost too slowly to name. Rivers change course by leaning against one bank for centuries. Pools migrate as sediment settles. Trees appear, broaden, hollow, and fall while the plain seems unchanged around them. Even the loudness of the animals is cyclical rather than urgent—a recurring fullness of sound over a landscape whose physical gestures are slight.

From any low rise, one can see water in many separate forms at once: river channels, rain-filled hollows, shining mud, distant flood sheets, vapor above warm stone. Between them lie long reaches of bare ground and scattered life. The place is wet without lushness, populous without crowding, and ancient without ruin. It has passed beyond sharpness. What remains is a vast, warm, breathing surface, worn close to its final shape and still being shaped.
