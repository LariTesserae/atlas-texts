The high country is entirely made.

From one rim to the other, across a breadth too large to cross in a day, the ground consists of fitted stone: pale slabs, steep stairs, shallow cisterns, retaining walls, causeways, terraces, gutters, and close-set roofs with no visible chambers beneath them. There is no soil. Where a slab has cracked, the opening reveals another course below, then another—masonry descending farther than any sounding cord has measured. Even the distant peaks are constructed. Their sharp crests are stacks of enormous wedges, newly seated and scarcely weathered, rising into a white, luminous sky.

Water is everywhere, but seldom deep. It lies in the joints of the place and gives the whole plateau a dark, polished articulation. Rain has passed recently, or is always just passing elsewhere. Every horizontal surface carries a trembling skin. Every stair releases a thread down its center. Rows of stone spouts project from walls and drip into basins already brimming, so that one basin spills into the next through square notches cut by hand. Under bridges, narrow channels move with the clear, deliberate pace of poured oil.

The water seems close beneath every surface. Set an ear against a paving block and one may hear faint pressure below: a muted fullness, the almost-sound of immense cisterns. In certain plazas, droplets bead directly from the stone, gathering along chisel marks before sliding into engraved grooves. The walls weep without moss, stain, root, or lichen. Their dampness leaves only mineral traces—white fans of salt, blue-gray seams, and thin rust-colored veils where simple iron clamps have been driven between blocks.

Nothing grows here. There are no grasses in the cracks, no film upon the pools, no insects dimpling the water. The absence is not ruinous. It feels original, as though the plateau was completed before life had been invented, or as though its builders understood construction but not fertility. What little softness the eye expects is supplied by water: sagging reflections, rounded drops, mist collecting under lintels. Otherwise, every form is cut, stacked, pegged, braided, or packed.

There is almost no empty space.

Walls stand behind walls, each within arm’s reach of the next. Stairways climb over aqueducts; aqueducts pass above vaulted drains; drains emerge into courts crowded with columns, troughs, marker stones, and low domes of tamped ceramic. Ramps cross one another at improbable angles. Some lead to terraces only a few paces wide, already occupied by altars, rain gauges, stone levers, and racks of fired-clay vessels. Even broad avenues are divided by channels and rows of waist-high partitions until passage becomes a choice of narrow, exact routes.

Yet the landscape remains open in extent. From a sufficiently high stair, one sees mile after mile of this density: a gray-white region corrugated by roofs and reservoirs, interrupted by angular artificial hills. The farthest structures dissolve not into forest or bare rock but into brightness. After rain, each wet plane catches the sky, and the plateau flashes in fragments. It can be difficult to look across it at midday. The whole high basin becomes a broken mirror whose pieces have been arranged into a city no one inhabits.

Its construction is astonishing but not sophisticated. The blocks retain peck marks from stone hammers. Timbers, where needed, have been replaced by bundled ceramic rods or slabs balanced through corbeling. Fastenings are rawhide in design but made from braided mineral fibers: long, stiff cords spun from glassy strands and softened by constant wetting. Doors turn on stone pivots. Floodgates are raised with ropes, counterweights, and teams that are no longer present. Measurements repeat, but imperfectly. A channel narrows by the width of a thumb. A stair gradually changes pitch. The place bears the intimate errors of handwork on a continental scale.

Perhaps there were builders. Nothing now confirms them. There are no bones, nests, footprints, droppings, or discarded meals. Workshops contain hammerstones arranged by weight and blades of volcanic glass set neatly in grooves. In sheltered alcoves, unfinished blocks wait beneath curtains of condensation. Their fresh faces are bright and granular. Some still have red guide lines painted across them, made with mineral pigment that has not yet washed away. This gives the plateau a disturbing sense of recent interruption. It is old only in size. Its edges are sharp. Its dust has barely settled.

Sound does not travel normally among so many partitions. Most noises remain local: a drop striking a jar; a soft runnel in a slot; the brief ceramic click of a gate shifting under water pressure. Beyond that is quiet. Wind passes high overhead but seldom reaches the crowded lower courts. When it does enter, it threads through thousands of apertures and produces a single low tone, too stable to resemble music. One can walk out of that tone by turning a corner.

Louder events occur far away. At long intervals, a hidden reservoir fills enough to tip a stone balance. A gate opens. Water moves through an entire district with a subdued, spreading rush, first audible beneath the paving, then visible as channels brighten one after another. Basins rise. Siphons begin. Overshot wheels without axles rotate a quarter turn and stop. Hours later, another gate closes under its own counterweight. The district resumes dripping.

These cycles are the closest thing the place has to weather.

The sky is cool and glaring, a high veil of condensed light without a visible sun. Mist forms in hollows but seldom obscures them completely. It gathers waist-deep in sunken reservoirs, leaving parapets and towers floating above smooth white surfaces. On shaded stairs the water becomes painfully cold, though it rarely freezes. At the highest ridges, thin plates of ice sometimes form beneath overhangs, perfectly clear and rectangular because they inherit the shape of the molds in which the water rests. By afternoon they loosen and fall, breaking with one bright report that may be heard across several courts.

The plateau’s rules reveal themselves slowly. Water always seeks the worked channels even when an unwalled slope would be shorter. Drops striking an uncarved slab hesitate as round beads; once they touch a chiseled line, they accelerate. Joined stones remain dry at their centers and wet at their edges. Reflections do not quite reproduce the sky: the smallest basins show distant stairways, and some deep cisterns reflect terraces that have not yet been built.

This may be why unfinished construction is found everywhere. The makers might have used water as a plan. A mason could look into a trough and see the next wall, then copy it with hammer and cord. The city would thus have built itself forward from reflections—each completed basin revealing another structure, each structure collecting more water, each new surface offering further instructions. If so, the process has not ended. It has merely slowed past ordinary patience.

In the northern quarter, stones shift between rains. No hand moves them. A wedge settles by the thickness of a grain. A balanced lintel tilts. Water enters the altered gap and, over months, carries mineral powder into it. The powder hardens. A new joint is made. Elsewhere, repeated dripping turns loose piles of grit into smooth, cemented knobs that resemble crude pegs. The landscape continues assembling itself by weight, saturation, and example.

Its youngest region lies along the eastern rim. There the masonry climbs abruptly from luminous cloud in crowded, raw tiers. Blocks appear freshly split, their corners keen enough to cut. New channels end in midair. Staircases rise to no landing. Stacks of tiles wait beside roofless chambers, each tile cupping rain. Nothing is eroded except the faintest dust from the tool marks. The edge seems less abandoned than paused.

From there one can look back over the whole constructed highland: shining gutters, sealed courts, packed towers, damp pyramids, a maze of reservoirs and hand-cut ridges extending to every horizon. No bird crosses it. No weed claims it. No distant smoke answers the brightness.

Only water moves, carrying no leaf, no seed, no living color—passing patiently through the immense work, measuring it drop by drop, and perhaps, in its quiet reflective way, deciding what must be made next.
