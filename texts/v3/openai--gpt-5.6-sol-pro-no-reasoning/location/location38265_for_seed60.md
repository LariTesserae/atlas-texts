The place occupies a shallow basin no larger than a hamlet, though its boundaries are difficult to see. Vegetation has not merely overgrown them; vegetation is the boundary, the ground, and almost all the available air. From a distance the basin resembles a single dark-green organ pressed into the lowlands, glossy after rain and breathing faint vapor into the warmth.

There is no bare soil. What passes for a path is a succession of broad, overlapping root-plates, each one resilient underfoot and slightly warm. They yield, then slowly regain their shape. Between them, black water wells through hairline seams. The water is rarely deep—ankle-deep in the visible pools, perhaps deeper below—but it is everywhere near the surface. Press a heel into any soft place and clear beads rise around it. Cut a stem and it leaks water before sap.

The basin receives frequent rain without ever seeming washed clean. Drops remain lodged in its innumerable cups, throats, gutters, and fleshy folds. Some leaves are the size of doors, some no larger than scales; together they overlap so densely that a shower may take hours to work its way down. Long after the sky has cleared, the vegetation continues raining internally. Water clicks from blade to blade, gathers momentum in sloping leaf-channels, and finally pours in brief, localized curtains into the darkness underneath.

Nothing here grows in the familiar arrangement of trunk, branch, and crown. Plants use other plants as terrain. Ribbed creepers lie side by side to form springy causeways. Pale columnar stalks lean together and fuse into vaulted chambers. Mat-forming species span pools with thick rafts, while parasitic bushes root into those rafts, and finer vines lace the bushes until every remaining aperture has been stitched shut. The result is architectural without appearing designed: aisles, galleries, wells, low rooms, sweating walls. There are doors that close for the night and stairs that add one soft green tread every few years.

The tallest structure is not quite a tree. It is a conglomeration of thousands of stems that have grown around a hollow, each stem as slender as a finger, the whole mass shaped like a squat tower. Its interior is warm and dim. Rainwater spirals down the living walls through vascular grooves polished by centuries of flow. At the bottom lies a round pool crowded with white root tips. They lift and lower almost imperceptibly, keeping the water in continual tremor.

This crowded life makes the basin loud despite the rarity of animals. Leaves knock together with the hollow clatter of wet wood. Seed bladders inflate and discharge with abrupt metallic bangs. Deep inside the root mass, pressure travels through chambers and produces long groans, as if a ship were turning beneath the ground. There is also the machinery.

Along one edge of the basin stands a narrow brick pumping house, or what remains of one. Its roof has been replaced by interwoven fronds, its mortar penetrated by root hairs, its windows sealed with translucent green membranes. A rusted flywheel still turns inside. No belt drives it. A muscular vine has grown through its spokes and contracts in response to changes in humidity, advancing the wheel by a fraction with each cycle. The motion is slow, but the gearing multiplies it. Once every forty or fifty seconds, a piston descends with a heavy iron concussion.

The pump draws black water from beneath the basin and sends it through corroded pipes. Most pipes have split, yet the plants have grown around the fractures, producing living sleeves that hold the pressure. Where iron ends, woody conduits continue. Valves are opened by swelling bulbs. Reservoirs are lined with waxy bark. The old mechanism and the vegetation have been joined for so long that neither can now be identified as the operator.

Elsewhere, smaller industrial remnants persist: a ceramic insulator held aloft in the fork of a shrub; rails passing into the root floor and emerging twenty paces later as reddish ribs; a switchboard whose copper contacts are bridged by conductive moss. In damp weather, a weak current travels through the basin. Green lamps flicker behind layers of leaf tissue. Coils hum. The undersides of certain fronds glow along their veins for a few minutes, and nearby blossoms turn toward them as if toward a false moon.

The machinery does not seem to manufacture an object. It regulates conditions. It moves water from one buried cavity to another, vents pockets of warm air, compresses gases produced in the deep mulch, and sounds alarms that no attendant comes to answer. Possibly it once served a different purpose. The plants have repurposed it so thoroughly that its original industry has become irrelevant. The basin itself is now the product: maintained at a precise dampness, temperature, and pressure by an assembly that is half factory and half metabolism.

The air is warm but not oppressive. It condenses against cooler metal and beneath pale leaves, beading on every downward-facing surface. There is a mineral smell from wet brick and rust, almost lost beneath crushed greenery, tannin, fermentation, and the clean peppery scent released by the path when stepped upon. In deeper hollows, accumulated plant matter gives off a breadlike heat. Steam rises there in thin threads and is caught by nets of aerial roots.

Light enters normally from the open sky but does not travel far. The upper leaves receive ordinary daylight: gray after rain, gold in clear intervals, white around noon. Below them it is broken into a close mosaic. Some membranes diffuse it into a soft green glow. Others concentrate it through lenslike droplets, projecting bright circles that creep across the living walls. Small patches of the floor are briefly illuminated with startling clarity—a rusted bolt, a submerged label, a cluster of translucent seeds—then disappear again as the canopy slowly adjusts.

There are almost no visible animals. A few flat snails inhabit the wet machinery, their shells patterned like washers. Hair-thin flies sometimes hatch from the tower pool and vanish into carnivorous funnels before they can form a swarm. Once in several days, something small may move under the root-plates, lifting three or four in sequence, but it never emerges. Pollination is conducted chiefly by water pressure, touch, and mechanical vibration. When the old piston strikes, several species release pollen at once. A yellow cloud travels through connected vents and settles in receptive chambers across the basin.

This is one of the place’s governing rules: every repeated sound eventually becomes biological instruction. The plants have grown around rhythms. The piston’s impact triggers pollen. The rattle of a loose sheet of iron opens rain cups. The low hum of a surviving transformer draws sap toward buried nodes. Even footsteps can become consequential if repeated often enough. Walk the same route daily and buds begin to line it. After a season, the path may provide handholds, shade, perhaps fruit. Stop walking it, and the route seals over without hostility, closing as slowly as healing skin.

Nothing changes quickly, though everything is changing. The pump house sinks a few millimeters each year. Root-plates rise to meet its doors. Water shifts from one side of the basin to the other over decades, leaving old pools packed with buoyant pith and opening new ones beneath what seemed solid. Iron becomes oxide; oxide stains roots; the roots incorporate it in dark red rings. A collapsed wall may remain recognizable for a century while being steadily translated into plant tissue.

Storms disturb the upper canopy but rarely alter the whole. The vegetation is too interconnected. Wind passes through it as pressure through a lung, bowing one quarter of the basin while another swells upward. Broken stems are held in place by neighboring growth until they graft or rot. Floodwater rises, spreads into thousands of chambers, and is portioned away. Drought causes the vaulted passages to contract, the pistons to slow, and the root floor to become firm enough to ring underfoot.

At the basin’s center is a small open court, perhaps fifteen paces across. It is the only place where the sky can be seen unobstructed. The court is circular, edged by inward-facing leaves with serrated tips. Its floor consists of a single continuous pad, dark and faintly veined, floating on water. In daylight it appears merely botanical. At night, or when a cloud suddenly dims the sun, pale lines become visible beneath its skin: a precise diagram of pipes, roots, and electrical paths extending through the entire basin.

Every few minutes the diagram pulses from the pump house toward the center. The floating pad tightens. Water is expelled around its rim in a low silver ring. The surrounding leaves answer with a shudder, and the shudder travels outward through every gallery and tower until even the boundary wall trembles.

Then the basin is still again—or as still as such a place can be. Water continues to descend through leaf after leaf. The wheel advances. Warm vapor gathers beneath the canopy. Somewhere below, roots press patiently against old foundations, not breaking them, only waiting for brick and iron to become soft enough to use.
