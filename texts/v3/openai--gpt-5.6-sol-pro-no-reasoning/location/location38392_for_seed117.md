The place lies in a shallow basin no larger than a railway yard, although its crowded interior makes it difficult to see from one side to the other. The ground is nearly level and holds water everywhere. What appears at first to be pavement is a continuous shelf of dark mineral crust, fractured into low plates and shallow cups. Rain gathers in these depressions. Water pushes upward through seams under its own faint pressure, glazing the stone from below even when the sky has been dry.

There are no plants. Nothing roots, flowers, or makes green shade. The basin is dense for other reasons.

Thousands of mineral chimneys stand across it, from finger-high spouts to columns taller than a person. They have accumulated slowly around leaks in buried pipes and natural springs, each built from successive skins of iron, lime, manganese, and soot. Their surfaces resemble bark or coral but are wholly inorganic: brittle lips, fluted sides, folds like poured wax. Many lean against one another. Some have joined overhead into cramped arcades, turning the basin into a maze of wet stone.

Every chimney weeps. Cold droplets form along its ridges, swell, and descend. In places the droplets fall through collars of rust and strike resonant metal hidden beneath the crust. The sounds are widely spaced—a hollow tick, a muted ping, a soft clank so distant it might have come from underground. Between them lies a dense, attentive quiet.

The oldest formations have settled into rounded, weathered shapes. Sharp industrial edges survive only where a recent collapse has exposed them: a section of riveted tank wall, the toothed rim of a buried wheel, lengths of ceramic insulator, a valve housing furred with pale carbonate. Most artifacts have become geology. Rails enter the basin from the north, sink beneath ocher water, surface briefly as parallel black humps, and vanish under stone. A narrow service hut stands near the middle, though only its roof and upper windows remain above the mineral floor. Condensation films the glass on both sides.

The works beneath are still operating.

No one knows whether they constitute a factory, a pumping station, or merely the surviving portion of some larger process. At intervals, pressure travels through the buried system. The ground trembles very slightly. Pools dimple without wind. A line of chimneys exhales in sequence, releasing cool vapor with a smell of wet iron and spent electricity. Far below, a flywheel or turbine turns through several revolutions and coasts to silence. The mechanism never begins in the same place twice.

Its production, if that is what it is, appears to be water sorted by mineral content. Clear drops emerge from some vents; black brine from others; from a few, perfectly spherical beads that roll across level surfaces without wetting them. These collect in hollows until a vibration sets them moving. Then hundreds travel together through the maze, clicking softly against the crust like glass seeds. At the basin’s southern lip they lose their shape and pour into an ordinary gray ditch.

The air is cool and close. Low cloud often settles directly into the basin, becoming indistinguishable from steam. Light filters through it without a visible source. Even at midday the colors remain subdued: graphite, rust red, bone white, oily violet. Wet surfaces hold the little light there is and repeat it in thousands of dim points. Nothing casts a complete shadow.

The animals keep to those reflections.

Small translucent runners inhabit the film of water on the stone. They have many jointed legs and broad, lenslike backs, and when disturbed they flatten until they resemble droplets. Dense congregations gather around warm seams. They graze not on growth but on the soft precipitate forming where unlike waters meet, scraping fresh mineral with vibrating mouthparts. Their feeding makes a faint sandy hiss audible only at close range.

Above them drift pale sac-bodied fliers, almost weightless in the humid air. They navigate by electrical fields, pulsing blue when buried machinery wakes. During a pressure cycle they crowd around the chimneys in such numbers that every column seems wrapped in slow sparks. When the current fades, they settle under ledges and become dull knots in the stonework.

Larger creatures move inside the pipes. Their presence is known by traveling knocks and by the occasional flex of a corroded grille. Long, blind bodies follow the warmer channels, feeding on the mineral grazers washed into drains. They cannot easily enter the open basin, but after heavy rain the grilles submerge and the pipe-creatures come out. They move through flooded alleys with the deliberate curves of eels, plated in tarnished metal-colored scales. By morning they have usually returned below, leaving trails where soft sediment has been pressed smooth.

Predation here is patient. The sac-fliers descend on the runners. The runners crowd the outlets where new precipitate forms. The pipe-creatures wait wherever runoff concentrates both. Nothing consumes vegetation; nothing depends upon sunlight in any obvious way. Heat gradients, dissolved metals, charge, and pressure serve in place of a conventional food chain. Whether these beings are descendants of animals, industrial organisms, or native mineral life is difficult to decide. The distinction has ceased to matter locally.

Water governs every passage. Thin sheets creep over thresholds, reverse direction when the pumps engage, and disappear through slots too narrow to admit a finger. Rain does not merely add water; it rearranges the basin. A hard shower seals some vents, awakens others, floats loose crusts from the ground, and opens temporary corridors beneath them. The whole place subtly changes plan. Familiar gaps close under carbonate foam. Walls split along old seams. A stairway may rise from a draining pool and remain accessible for an hour before the level returns.

Inside the half-buried hut, the air is colder. Rows of control cabinets stand beneath white mineral drapery. Most gauges have no needles, yet their faces fog and clear in patterns synchronized with the buried engines. A steel desk bears repeated ring marks where containers once stood. In one drawer are thin punched-metal cards, their holes clogged by rust. Feeding one into the remaining reader causes no visible response, but some minutes later a chimney elsewhere in the basin begins producing a new kind of water.

That is one of the place’s rules: actions are answered at a distance and after a delay. Turning a valve may darken a pool beyond the wall. Blocking a leak may open three smaller ones. A loud noise can stop the machinery, but only after the echo has died. Nothing reacts where it is touched.

Another rule concerns removal. Loose artifacts can be lifted—a bolt, a scale of rust, a fragment of insulator—but at the basin’s boundary they begin to sweat. Carried farther, they release more water than their volume should permit, growing lighter until only a dry shell remains. Objects brought into the basin do the opposite. They slowly gain weight. Moisture enters them, then minerals, then the fine vibration of the works. Leave a tool overnight and by morning it may have attached itself to the crust with a thin stone collar. Leave it for a year and it will have become a spring.

The boundary is visible chiefly by what happens to the rain. Outside, drops strike earth and spread. Inside, they hesitate on impact, tremble, and creep toward the nearest chimney, even against the shallow slope. Around the entire basin runs a low retaining wall, ancient and repeatedly repaired. Its concrete has rounded under weather into something almost natural. Small numbered plaques survive at intervals, but their sequence is inconsistent. Several bear the same number. Others are blank except when wet.

At dusk the machinery grows more active. Pressure pulses cross underfoot. Vapor ascends through the packed columns, and the pale fliers wake in luminous swarms. Yet the basin never becomes loud. Its industry is restrained, buried, muffled by water and mineral accretion. The dominant sounds remain droplets and minute feeding mouths, the occasional underwater turn of a gear, the whisper of mist passing through a forest that is not a forest.

From the rim it can look abandoned. Up close, abandonment is the wrong idea. The place is occupied at every scale: by water negotiating pores in stone, by animals following charge, by mechanisms continuing instructions whose purpose has weathered away. Its decay has reached equilibrium. Collapse feeds construction. Corrosion opens channels that mineral water slowly reinforces. The factory is being destroyed and rebuilt by the same process, with no clear point at which machine ends and landscape begins.

Nothing grows here, yet the basin thickens year by year.
