At the top of an artificial mountain, there is a flooded factory no larger than a cathedral.

Nothing here is natural—not the mountain, not the water, not even the darkness beneath the leaves. The peak is a dense stack of foundries, pressure chambers, reservoirs, and load-bearing vaults, all cast as one continuous structure. Its uppermost room stands several kilometers above the drowned lowlands. From outside it resembles a blunt crown of green-black metal. From within, it is an enclosed sea.

Warm water fills the chamber from tiled floor to vaulted roof.

The ceiling is lost only thirty meters overhead, but refraction makes it seem distant. Rows of lamps burn behind thick glass, emitting a steady cream-colored industrial light. Their brightness is ordinary, almost domestic, and that ordinariness makes the room stranger: a well-lit workshop in which every bench, stair, gantry, and instrument lies submerged. Shadows remain soft. Leaves catch the light from below. Bubbles are rare.

The water is held under pressure and kept at the temperature of blood. It tastes of iron, wet insulation, and dissolved fertilizer. There are no tides, no currents strong enough to feel. At most, a slow thermal drift passes upward along the walls while colder water descends through the central machinery. A loose filament may take an hour to bend from one direction to another.

There is no animal life. No fish move between the pipes. No larvae inhabit the sumps, no shellfish seal themselves to the bolts, no pale scavengers wait in the drains. The system was sterilized when it was commissioned, and its safeguards have never failed. Even the vegetation is not entirely biological.

It is luxuriant nonetheless.

Every available surface is occupied by engineered growth. Copper-veined fronds unfold from ceramic sockets. Velvet-black moss coats the undersides of catwalks. Transparent vines, full of green nutrient gel, knot themselves around valve wheels and descend in heavy curtains. Broad leaves have been trained across the vault like overlapping roof plates, though there is no air from which they need shelter. Their roots enter numbered ports in the walls rather than cracks. Some bear clusters of glassy bulbs that look like fruit, each containing a slowly revolving impeller.

The growth is neither garden nor infestation. It is part of the plant.

Photosynthetic membranes clean the water. Root mats draw metals from solution. Tendrils sense pressure differences and tighten leaking joints. Pale fans spread over heat exchangers, feeding on waste warmth; dark green ropes wrap the main conduits to damp vibration. What seems at first to be exuberant vegetation resolves, on examination, into a distributed maintenance system. Yet what seems at first to be machinery resolves just as readily into botany. Pistons have woody rings. Leaves terminate in electrical plugs. Flower-like apertures open to reveal gauges. Seed pods click into sockets and begin functioning as fuses.

The chamber is packed nearly solid with these hybrid organs. There are only narrow passages through them: vertical wells, maintenance throats, and old aisles now reduced to green tunnels. A person could cross the room only by moving carefully along the remaining handrails, squeezing between warm trunks of braided cable, and passing through curtains of leaves. In places, even this would be impossible. The garden has not grown wild; rather, it has completed its design.

Beneath it stands the original machinery.

The factory was built to manufacture elevation.

Far below, when the world still had coasts, similar installations consumed stone, scrap, and seawater. They separated minerals, cast structural cells, and added layer upon layer to immense inhabited towers. This one raised itself continuously, always building beneath its own weight. Its summit chamber climbed as the lower works extruded new foundations. Then the flood came—not suddenly, but over generations—and the factory continued rising through it. The lowlands disappeared. Cities became reefs of vacant architecture. The artificial mountain alone kept pace with the water.

The summit room was intended to remain above the sea. Instead, near the end, its builders sealed it and filled it deliberately. Water was easier to regulate than air. It carried heat, supported the mass of the garden, suppressed fires, and equalized the strain on the vault. Outside, storms could strip paint from steel. Inside, not a leaf trembled.

The surrounding mountain is still young. Its edges have not softened. Exterior buttresses descend in sharp black planes, unrounded by soil or weather. Fresh seams remain visible where the latest courses were fused together. There are no beaches on its flanks, no sedimented ledges, no mature corrosion. Rain and sea have barely begun their patient work. Where the summit crown protrudes above the cloud layer, warm rain strikes it almost continuously, but the runoff is caught before it can carve channels. Every drop enters a gutter, every gutter a tank, every tank a calibrated descent.

Within the flooded chamber, erosion is treated as an error.

Exposed corners wear sacrificial collars. Rust is harvested by red-brown films and returned to the smelters below. Fine particles settle onto sloping plates that periodically tilt, though so slowly the movement cannot be watched. One may look away and later discover that a floor has become a wall, or that an aisle has narrowed by the width of a leaf.

The mountain still grows, but only a little.

Once every several years, deep pumps awaken. Their action is felt first as a pressure behind the eyes, then as a faint vibration in the teeth. Warm water passes through the root masses. Lamps dim by a fraction. Far beneath, buried furnaces heat, presses close, and a new ring of material is added to the base. The summit rises a few centimeters. The process takes months.

No dramatic signal marks its completion. A line of mercury-colored beads creeps along one gauge. A row of leaves changes from green to blue. The chamber becomes quiet again.

Its silence is never perfect. The lamps give off a thin electrical hum. Metal contracts with small, distant ticks. Occasionally a valve exhales one silver bubble, which climbs through the foliage and enters a ceiling vent without bursting. The loudest sound is produced by the garden’s long leaves brushing against one another when the circulation cycle reverses: a soft, papery murmur transmitted through the water, less like a forest in wind than a library turning one enormous page.

There are places within the chamber that resemble rooms. A ring of consoles stands around the central shaft, their screens still lit. A submerged desk bears a cup fixed to it by mineral sealant, although no loose object is permitted here. Chairs have been grown over by cushion-thick moss. Behind a partition of perforated brass is a narrow grove of upright cylinders, each furred with fine white roots. Labels identify them as *climate memory*, *coastal reference*, *pollinator absence*, and *unbuilt soil*.

The cylinders contain archived conditions the world no longer possesses.

One maintains the chemistry of rain before the flood. Another holds the microbial recipe for meadow earth, though there is no meadow and no exposed earth on which to use it. Several preserve flowering sequences that once depended on insects. Because no animals are allowed in the chamber, the blossoms pollinate through mechanical gestures: stamens rotate, petals close like hands, and internal needles transfer dust from one chamber to another. The process is careful and silent, performed for species whose ecological partners are gone.

At the heart of the room rises a narrow tower of white enamel. It reaches from floor to ceiling and is so densely wrapped in vines that only portions remain visible: numbered hatches, a ladder, the circular lips of inspection ports. This is the altitude engine’s final control column. The greenery around it is exceptionally lush, fed by a warmth that never varies.

Here the leaves do something peculiar.

They orient not toward the lamps but toward the sealed metal core. Their upper surfaces face inward, as if light shines from inside the column. When touched, they display tiny images in their veins: horizon lines, cloud decks, a pale sun over open water. The images are not stored recordings. They are forecasts calculated by the mountain, pictures of the height at which its summit might someday stand above every storm.

The date attached to that forecast recedes rather than approaches.

Each new layer makes the mountain heavier. Each correction requires more energy than the last. The sea continues to rise in minute increments, fed by warm rain and distant melt. The factory and the water are engaged in a race so slow that no living witness could distinguish victory from defeat.

Still, the summit chamber performs its work.

It cultivates the garden. It purifies its enclosed sea. It listens to the pressure of the ocean against the outer walls and instructs the buried levels to add thickness where needed. It retains memories of dry ground without ever producing any. Its lamps observe an unwavering day, dimming slightly at intervals to imitate a night that the plants do not require.

Boundaries govern everything here. Roots stop at painted lines unless a leak calls them across. Vines yield narrow corridors around emergency controls. Water remains within tolerances of heat, salinity, and depth. No animal may enter. Nothing may decay without being measured. Nothing may fall; loose matter is caught and reincorporated. Every surface must serve at least two purposes, and every living shape must also be a tool.

Beyond the wall is weather: warm ocean, vertical rain, and clouds wrapped around the fabricated peak.

Within is the small, impossible sea-garden, bright and crowded and nearly still.

It hangs at the world’s highest occupied point, although no occupants remain. It continues to rise by fractions. Its leaves face inward. Its machines dream of altitude. And all through the warm water, among the silent instruments, artificial flowers open one by one as if expecting something winged to arrive.
