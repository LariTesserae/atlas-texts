The city occupies the upper basin of a mountain that has no summit.

From the low country, if there is still a low country, one would see only a broad darkening in the clouds: a plateau hidden inside its own weather, lifted above the ordinary atmosphere. The ascent roads enter through tunnels worn smooth by heat and mineral runoff. Beyond them lies a shallow caldera many kilometers across, its rim notched with spillways, chimneys, gantries, and the black ribs of unfinished bridges. The ground is high, but the basin feels sunken. Cloud presses close overhead, making a ceiling where the mountain’s missing peak should be.

It is always hot here. Not the clean heat of sunlight—the light is weak, reddish, diffuse—but a stored and exhaled heat. Pavement radiates through the soles. Iron handrails cannot be held for long. Condensation gathers on the shaded sides of buildings, trembles, then runs downward in warm threads. Rain comes frequently and evaporates reluctantly, filling the avenues with low steam. Water stands in joints and depressions, dark as oil until disturbed.

Nothing grows.

There are no weeds in the cracks, no moss on the wet walls, no pale grass at the margins of reservoirs. Even decay has a mineral character. Rust spreads in branching fans, salt builds terraces inside broken pipes, and soot accretes in soft black shelves beneath vents. On certain walls, manganese stains produce shapes almost botanical—fronds, veins, clustered crowns—but they are slow precipitations, records of seepage rather than life. They can remain unchanged for decades, then lengthen several centimeters during a single storm.

The city was built for a population that its volume still anticipates. Blocks of narrow towers stand around enormous courtyards. Elevated rail lines divide into twelve platforms where one would have sufficed. Warehouses extend beyond sight beneath the cloud, each marked with faded numbers and fitted with loading mouths large enough to admit houses. Yet there is almost nothing in them. Their interiors are open chambers of warm darkness, interrupted at long intervals by a crane, a stack of ceramic pallets, or a parked engine furred with condensation.

Emptiness is not ruin here. Much of the city still functions.

Pumps begin without attendants. Switchyards rearrange their rails. Conveyor belts wake and carry single objects across halls built for thousands: one ingot, one sealed vessel, one block of wet translucent stone. The objects vanish through numbered doors. Hours later, perhaps kilometers away, another machine starts. No visible overseer coordinates these events, but they possess an industrial grammar: extraction, weighing, heating, separation, cooling, return. The cycle is too slow to look purposeful from moment to moment. Seen over weeks, it is precise.

The oldest structures have settled deeply into the basin. Their foundations are fused with native rock, and runoff has weathered both construction and mountain into the same rounded forms. Staircases descend until their lower flights become streambeds. Cornices have softened under centuries of hot rain. Brickwork bulges but does not collapse; the mineral mortar swells when wet and closes its own fractures. New deposits seal old doors. Old pipes emerge from cliffs that may once have been walls.

Water is everywhere near the surface, though there is no river in the usual sense. It circulates.

Rainwater enters grates and sinks into warm galleries. It passes through filtration vaults lined with charcoal glass, emerges in rooftop cisterns, spills down chains, crosses plazas in shallow geometric channels, and disappears again. Some streets flood ankle-deep each evening as if obeying a tide. By dawn they are dry except for white mineral outlines around bolts and paving stones. In the lower wards, broad settling pools occupy former squares. Their water is hot enough to haze the air and clear enough to reveal submerged lamp posts thirty meters below.

No one knows whether those drowned levels subsided or whether the water rose.

Along the inner rim stand the weeping rocks: cliffs perforated by thousands of drilled apertures. Warm water beads from them continuously. Each droplet carries dissolved metal, and over time the cliff faces have acquired vertical skins of copper green, sulfur yellow, iron red, and a deep violet deposit that absorbs almost all light. Collection gutters lie beneath, but many have warped or overflowed, producing mineral curtains around them. The cliffs make the city’s most continuous sound—a soft, dispersed ticking, like distant rain in many rooms.

Otherwise, it is quiet.

Machinery is insulated behind thick walls. Rail cars move magnetically and without wheel noise. Steam escapes in long breaths rather than whistles. Across an open district, one might hear a chain strike a mast, then wait minutes for the next sound. Thunder occurs above the cloud ceiling, muffled into immense pressure rather than a crack. Sometimes the entire basin answers with a subterranean hum. Window glass shivers. The standing water forms concentric rings. Then the vibration passes eastward under the city, starting pumps as it goes.

There are inhabitants, though rarely where architecture suggests they should be.

A handful of people live in pressure-cooled rooms built into old administrative towers. They travel before the evening flood, wearing reflective capes and masks that condense drinking water from their breath. They do not occupy apartments so much as routes: a lift, a bridge, a control chamber, a shaded arcade. Their dwellings are marked by small arrangements of ceramic chimes, because doors and rooms are otherwise difficult to distinguish in the dimness.

They avoid bright lamps. Strong light excites something in the wet masonry, causing it to release stored heat abruptly. For this reason illumination remains low and amber, sunk into floors or screened behind grilles. The brightest phenomena are electrical storms within the cloud and the occasional opening of a furnace, when an alley becomes orange noon for three or four seconds.

Animal life is rarer still. Thin, heat-loving things inhabit the waterworks—nearly transparent bodies like folded ribbons, without obvious heads. They gather around electrical leakage and vanish when pumps stop. In dry upper galleries, small shelled creatures cling to ceramic insulators and feed, perhaps, on static charge or condensed salts. They move so slowly that their trails are more often noticed than they are: delicate lines in soot, ending at an empty wall.

There are no birds. The sky is too low for them.

The basin has its own rules. Water flows downhill except during the hour before rain, when droplets creep upward along metal surfaces toward exposed edges. Echoes do not reliably return from the direction in which a sound was made. Heat accumulates in enclosed spaces, but in very large chambers it collects in distinct invisible columns, leaving neighboring air almost cool. Certain streets lengthen in wet weather; their address numbers remain correct, yet the walk between consecutive doors takes longer. Engineers account for this with articulated pipelines and sections of rail designed to telescope.

No one considers these effects supernatural. They are treated as local conditions, like altitude or corrosion.

Beyond the main city, the basin opens into wide industrial barrens. Cooling towers stand kilometers apart, each surrounded by a circular apron of dark concrete. Aqueducts cross the distance on thin piers and disappear into vapor. Here the emptiness becomes clearest. The city is dense in plan but vacant in substance: too many conduits carrying too little, too many stations awaiting arrivals, too many windows overlooking unoccupied ground. It resembles a machine built around the expectation of a material that never came—or came only once.

At the caldera’s center is a depression called the Receiving Field. No buildings stand there. The surface is a single expanse of old, rain-polished glass, gently concave and broken by drains. During ordinary weather it reflects the clouds as a dull metallic plane. During heavy storms, water spreads across it no deeper than a finger joint. The submerged glass then reveals lines beneath: city blocks, pipes, foundations, perhaps another plan of the same city rotated several degrees from the present one.

The machines become most active at these times.

Shutters open in distant factories. Elevators descend. Empty trains converge along separate tracks and stop around the field. Pumps draw water inward until the reflective surface becomes perfectly still despite the rain. For several minutes the entire basin falls silent, even the weeping cliffs. The cloud ceiling lowers. Shapes that might be towers appear in the reflection where no towers stand above.

Then something passes under the glass.

It is never seen clearly. A displacement, a darkness, a region in which the reflected city bends. The trains open their doors. Far below, engines answer one another through the rock.

Afterward the storm loosens. The water drains away. Machinery returns to its sparse routines, carrying solitary loads through enormous halls. Warm rain resumes its ticking from beam to beam. Steam gathers in the abandoned avenues, hiding their distance. The basin settles once more into its old equilibrium: wet, hot, elevated, and nearly empty, a city maintaining itself around an arrival that may be imminent, or may have happened long ago.
