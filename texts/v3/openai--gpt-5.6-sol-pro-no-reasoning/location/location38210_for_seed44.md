The town occupies a shallow basin no more than a few streets across, built where the river once divided into marshy channels. The river has since been confined behind low brick embankments, but its water remains everywhere. It darkens the walls, rises through cellar floors, gathers beneath iron grates, and feeds the narrow canals that run behind the factories. After rain, every surface shines. Gutters continue dripping long after the clouds have passed, and condensation pearls on pipes even during clear weather.

Warmth settles heavily here. The air is not hot enough to empty the streets, but it is always damp enough to soften paper, swell doors in their frames, and keep clothing faintly clammy. Rainwater evaporates from paving stones and hangs under the foliage as a thin, mineral-smelling haze. Brick, rust, silt, sap, and wet wood mingle into the town’s characteristic odor.

Almost nothing stands apart from vegetation. The place was constructed among old water-loving trees, then gradually taken into them. Plane trees and willows lean over roofs, their trunks enlarged around railings, staircases, and sections of pipe. Moss coats walls down to the waterline. Ferns rise from cracked masonry. Reeds fill drainage ditches and abandoned yards so densely that the ground disappears beneath them. Along the canals, mats of duckweed make the water resemble a dull green floor until a falling drop opens a black circle.

There are gardens on roofs, though few were planted deliberately. Sedges have rooted in clogged gutters. Fig trees emerge from chimney pots. Creepers cross alleys from building to building, hanging in cables thick enough to gather leaf mold and support another generation of plants. Some houses can be identified only by their doors and windows, dark rectangular apertures cut into a continuous wall of leaves.

The settlement is compact, perhaps the size of a large industrial quarter, and bounded on all sides. To the west is the embanked river. To the east, a raised railway line curls around the basin on old stone arches. North and south, the wet ground thickens into cultivated marsh: willow beds, reed plots, and rectangular ponds divided by narrow paths. From above, the town would look less like a patch of buildings than a complicated knot in an otherwise unbroken green fabric.

Its streets are narrow and thoroughly used. Brick workshops, workers’ houses, pumping stations, warehouses, and covered markets press close together. Many are old enough that their original purposes have been forgotten. Newer machinery has been inserted wherever space allowed: electric motors bolted to century-old floors, pumps wedged beneath staircases, insulated cables carried through holes once meant for belts and drive shafts. Overhead pipes cross the lanes at several heights, shedding warm drops onto corrugated awnings.

Industry here is adapted to wetness rather than opposed to it. The largest works process reeds, fibers, oils, dyes, timber, and plant resins brought in from the surrounding lowlands. In long brick sheds, stalks are washed, crushed, combed, steamed, pressed, and dried. Pulp moves through vats the color of weak tea. Bundles of cane ride slow conveyors beneath dripping rollers. Wood is bent in humid chambers and clamped into frames. Tanks of tannin stand under open roofs, their surfaces covered by iridescent skins.

The machinery seldom stops, though little of it moves quickly. Flywheels turn at deliberate speeds. Chain drives climb toothed gears one link at a time. Pumps strike deep, steady beats beneath the pavement. Fans labor behind louvered walls, pushing warm vegetable air into the streets. The town’s loudness is cumulative rather than violent: motors humming, belts slapping, water rushing through sluices, rain ticking on metal, railway wagons grinding around the elevated curve. Individual noises blur into a continuous industrial weather.

Conversation happens close to the ear. Bells and whistles divide the working day, but their signals are low and prolonged so that they can travel through foliage and walls. Even at night, one hears the pump houses maintaining the water level—three slow strokes, a pause, then three again. Silence comes only during power failures, and when it does, the remaining sounds of dripping and leaves seem unnaturally intricate.

The ground beneath the town has been settling for generations. Streets dip gradually toward old channels. Doorsteps have been raised with successive courses of brick; some entrances now sit half a meter above the original pavement. Walls bow but rarely collapse, having found stable shapes around their distortions. Foundations are not expected to remain rigid. They rest on timber piles, layers of rubble, root mats, and compressed peat, all yielding together.

Erosion here has no dramatic edge. Water rounds mortar joints, polishes stair treads, and wears shallow grooves beneath drainpipes. Rust has thickened ironwork into textured orange masses, but the corrosion is slow and familiar. Anything exposed long enough becomes furred, stained, softened, or incorporated. The town does not decay so much as acquire additional surfaces.

Repairs follow this logic. A leaking roof is covered with another roof. A sinking walkway is bridged with planks and later with iron. If roots enter a culvert, the culvert is widened around them. Pipes are rerouted rather than trees cut down, partly from habit and partly because the root systems now help bind the saturated soil. Survey marks on walls record old flood heights; beside them, newer marks indicate rates of settlement and safe pump pressure.

Open water appears in courtyards where buildings were removed. Some are formal cisterns with brick rims and ladders descending into green darkness. Others are accidental ponds, fed from below. Floating plants cover both. Their water is warm at the surface and cool near the bottom, carrying the faint vibration of nearby engines. In a few workshops, floors have been built as gratings over channels, so that workers can look down and see runoff passing beneath them among pale roots.

Animals are uncommon. The dense vegetation suggests abundance, but the usual movement is missing. There are insects, especially around standing water, yet even they seem fewer than expected. Small fish sometimes enter the canals when the river rises. A heron may appear on a factory roof at dawn, startling in its isolation. Rats are spoken of more often than seen. The near-absence of animal life leaves pollination, seed dispersal, and decay largely to wind, water, fungi, and human labor.

People pollinate greenhouse crops by hand with soft brushes. Seeds are carried along designated irrigation channels. Composting works occupy a warm, walled yard near the railway, where plant waste is turned by slow mechanical arms. Nothing organic leaves the basin if it can be pulped, fermented, pressed, burned, or returned to the soil.

The sky is visible only in fragments: above the river, between roofs, and in trembling patches through the canopy. Daylight is ordinary but filtered, greenish in some lanes and silver where it reflects from wet brick. Factory interiors are lit by broad windows filmed with moisture and by practical electric lamps beneath enamel shades. At dusk, the town does not transform dramatically. It simply loses detail. Leaves become one dark mass; water keeps the last gray light; lamps multiply in puddles.

Seasonal changes are gradual. In the wetter months, canals rise, pumps work harder, and lower paths vanish. In the warmer months, growth closes over signs and windows, and crews spend weeks cutting access corridors through vines. Nothing occurs all at once. Floods announce themselves through days of seepage. Buildings lean over decades. Trees absorb iron bars slowly enough that generations can date the process by old photographs.

The town’s central square is scarcely a square anymore. Four enormous trees have joined their crowns above it, and their roots have lifted the pavement into broad ridges. Market stalls fit between these rises like boats among shoals. Beneath the trees stands the municipal pump house, a small octagonal building of dark brick. Its doors remain open for ventilation, revealing polished rods moving up and down in the dim interior.

This is the closest thing the place has to a governing monument. Water levels, more than clocks or laws, organize daily life. Painted gauges stand at street corners. Green means ordinary seepage; yellow closes cellars and low workshops; red redirects power to the emergency pumps. Every building is required to keep its drains clear, though “clear” means passable, not bare. Moss and fine roots are accepted. Woody growth is not.

The town has long since reached an arrangement with its conditions. It neither conquers the marsh nor surrenders to it. Engines move the water just fast enough. Roots hold the soil just firmly enough. Brick channels guide what cannot be removed. People cut back the vegetation without trying to eliminate it, while the vegetation shades roofs, cools workshops, filters runoff, supplies the mills, and slowly takes possession of every unattended space.

From the railway embankment, the whole basin appears almost still. Steam rises in low veils from drying sheds. Tree crowns conceal most streets. A freight wagon advances behind leaves and disappears. The factories announce themselves not through smoke but through vibration and sound: the deep pulse of pumps, the low electric drone, the long scrape of machinery working wet fiber.

Below, hidden under canopy and roof, water passes through the town in thousands of routes—river, pipe, gutter, canal, root, vapor, wall. Everything built here depends on controlling that passage, and everything living depends on its control remaining incomplete.
