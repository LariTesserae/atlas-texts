The place is a warm sea suspended near the summit of the world.

It occupies a hollow no larger than a hamlet: a steep-sided basin of newly split black stone, high above any ordinary coastline. There is no shore. The basin is full beyond its rim, yet the water does not pour away. It bulges upward into a trembling dome, several times the depth of the hollow beneath it, held there by a law that applies nowhere beyond the surrounding peaks.

To enter is to climb into drowning.

The outer slopes are dry, sharp, and recently born. Their glassy ribs have not yet softened into soil. Cracks run through them in hot red seams, and water beads along their edges without descending. A few pale lichens stipple the sheltered faces. There are wiry mats of salt grass in three or four crevices, each tuft flattened outward by the pressure of the sea hanging overhead. Nothing forms a continuous cover. Most surfaces remain naked: basalt, pumice, sulfurous crust, translucent mineral splinters.

Above these slopes rises the water.

Its lower boundary begins abruptly at the rim of the basin, a vertical blue-black wall with no container. From outside, it resembles a standing storm cloud polished smooth at its edges. Fishless currents turn visibly inside it. Sheets of silver bubbles stream sideways, vanish into darkness, and return moments later as stones. The surface—if it is a surface—arches high overhead, dimly reflecting a sun that cannot be found in the sky.

Within, the water is warm, almost bodily. It carries alternating layers of heat: broad mild currents, then sudden feverish ribbons issuing from fresh vents in the rock. Visibility is poor. The dimness is brown-green close to the stone and violet farther out, though the enclosed sea is not broad enough to possess a true distance. A swimmer can see the opposite wall one moment; the next, it appears miles away, reduced to a thin black tooth under an immense pelagic dusk.

Depth is equally unreliable. Descending ten strokes may bring one to the bottom, or to open water with cold stars below. Rising may lead to air, to denser water, or back to the same ledge from beneath. Bubbles often fall. Sand hangs in vertical lines. Occasionally an entire current folds like cloth, and everything caught within it—silt, broken shells, warm brine—is turned inside out without moving.

The noise is absolute.

It is not the muffled pressure of an ordinary deep sea. The basin roars through water, rock, bone, and thought. Submerged cataracts fall in circles. Vents detonate beneath the floor with the slow concussion of mountains striking bells. The suspended sea grinds constantly against its own invisible boundary, making a sound like surf multiplied inside a cavern. At irregular intervals, a deeper note enters: too low to hear at first, then suddenly present in the teeth and joints. Loose stones leap from the bottom. Cracks open. Clouds of black sediment blossom and are ripped away before they can settle.

Silence does occur, but only in small moving pockets.

They drift through the water as transparent chambers, some no larger than a head, others large enough to stand within. When one passes over a swimmer, the roar vanishes completely. Even the pulse cannot be heard. From inside such a pocket the surrounding turbulence looks distant and theatrical, a storm behind thick glass. Then the chamber collapses, and the whole accumulated sound returns at once.

The floor is young and unfinished. Thin crusts bridge glowing fissures. Cones of pale mineral rise overnight, branching like antlers, only to snap and dissolve by noon. Ridges buckle upward in minutes. Others soften and slump into trenches whose sides remain knife-sharp. Erosion has scarcely begun because no shape survives long enough to be worn smooth. Formation and destruction happen faster than weathering.

There are a few structures, if structures is the word.

Around the innermost vent stand seven low arches of dark stone. They are too regular to look accidental and too grown to look made. Each consists of two basalt uprights joined by a bridge of porous limestone, all coated in a skin of red mineral. Nothing has cut or fitted them. No tool marks exist. They may be the exposed ribs of some buried organism, or the places where the basin repeatedly attempts the idea of a doorway.

Their positions change. Not while watched, but between blinks, during concussions, or whenever the water goes briefly clear. Sometimes they form a circle. Sometimes they are scattered across ledges at impossible angles. One occasionally appears outside the basin on the dry slope, dripping warm seawater uphill. By the next disturbance it is gone.

Near them lie fragments resembling masonry: flat slabs, shallow steps, a narrow shaft descending into rock. Yet the fragments have no consistent scale. A step that fits a human foot from one direction becomes a cliff from another. The shaft is only an arm’s length wide, but stones dropped into it return days later from the bulging top of the sea.

No culture can be inferred from these remains. They are not ruins so much as architectural symptoms.

Life is scarce. The few organisms present cling to intervals of stability. Colorless worms inhabit cracks around the cooler vents, extending glassy fans into the current. Small, blind crustaceans gather beneath overhangs and flee before earthquakes by several breaths, their shells ticking softly—a sound perceived only when a silence-pocket passes.

Higher in the water, solitary translucent animals drift with no visible means of swimming. They resemble loose knots tied in clear membrane. Each contains a single green filament, perhaps the only true plant in the basin. The filament turns toward darkness rather than light. If two of these creatures meet, they merge without struggle; if three meet, all disappear, leaving behind a brief square of empty water.

There are no shoals. Nothing grazes widely. No predator patrols. Now and then a long pale body is glimpsed crossing the far side of the basin, though the far side should be close enough to touch. Its passage bends the water around it. The roar drops in pitch, and the stone arches incline as if under wind. No head or tail has ever appeared—only the same unmarked middle moving past for an hour.

Vegetation survives mostly above the waterline that does not exist. Threads of red algae trail from dry rock into the suspended sea, violating the direction of buoyancy. A few cup-shaped plants grow upside down beneath ledges. They collect darkness in their centers. At intervals they open, releasing neither spores nor seeds but tiny pulses of cold that drift into the warm water and provoke localized storms.

The basin has weather, though it is entirely submerged. Rain forms inside it. Droplets condense from liquid water and fall through liquid water, each one visibly denser and darker than its surroundings. Where they strike the floor, little craters erupt. Lightning crawls slowly between currents, branching blue through the dimness. Thunder is almost redundant here, but it adds a dry, cracking edge to the continuous roar.

At the top of the water-dome, waves break inward. Their foam sinks in luminous skeins, outlining currents that turn through dimensions the basin cannot visibly contain. Some nights—or periods corresponding to night—the dome flattens and the enclosed sea spreads horizontally across the sky, becoming a ceiling from horizon to horizon. The summit then stands briefly beneath an ocean with no apparent depth. Warm drops fall upward from the rocks. The stars swim below.

By morning, the sea has contracted again into its steep, trembling boundary.

Nothing remains fixed except the basin’s smallness. However immense the interior becomes, its outer circumference can always be walked in less than an hour. A person circling it sees, through the dark wall, trenches, distant ridges, storms, and slow eclipses occurring within a space scarcely wider than a village square. Sometimes another figure can be seen walking along the opposite rim. It keeps the same pace. When approached, it proves to be no reflection: it carries different wounds, pauses at different things, and may be walking through an older or not-yet-formed version of the place.

The chief rule is that the sea does not escape. Everything else may.

Heat escapes as red mist. Sound escapes through stone and rolls down the mountain for hundreds of miles. Creatures are occasionally found fossilized in the outer glass though nothing corresponding to them lives inside. Doorways escape and stand briefly on barren ridges. Time leaks out in clear drops, leaving small patches where lichen grows, flowers, whitens, and becomes dust between two impacts of the roar.

But the water remains.

It heaves above the young summit, warm and dim, continually generating depths it has no room to hold. It builds ridges and pulverizes them. It proposes life, architecture, weather, distance, and chronology, then withdraws each proposal before it can become a world. The sparse plants clutch the margins. The rare animals endure by becoming difficult to define.

Around it, the high bare peaks are quiet only by comparison. They lean away from the basin’s pressure. Their fresh edges point outward as though the mountain did not rise to hold this impossible sea, but was shattered into existence by something inside it trying to be larger.
