The country is a low, hot expanse of handmade wetlands.

Nothing here is natural substrate. Beneath the shallow water lies no clay, bedrock, or buried soil—only fitted slabs of pale fired ceramic, extending farther than anyone has sounded. Each slab is broad as a courtyard and joined to the next by seams packed with tar, fiber, and powdered shell. The slabs are newly sharp. Their edges have not rounded; their glaze still catches the sun in hard white flashes. Yet reeds grow from sockets cut into them, trees stand in enormous pots sunk flush with the pavement, and marsh water rests everywhere in channels chiseled by hand.

The horizon is open but not empty. Low structures are scattered across it with generous distances between: roofless halls, stepped cisterns, isolated walls, causeways leading to square pools, towers only two or three levels high. All are made from baked mud, woven cane, shell-lime, wood pegs, and blocks of a porous green glass that no local craftsperson knows how to produce. There are no mines and no quarries. Materials are collected from the architecture itself. A damaged wall is dismantled to mend a bridge; a bridge is shortened to tile a cistern. The world is not built upon anything. It is built from itself.

No ruin is visibly old.

Corners remain acute. Plaster bears fingerprints. Reed ropes look newly twisted even when families remember them from before the first ancestor. Water stains stop at precise horizontal lines and never darken further. Lichen grows lushly over selected surfaces, but it does not spread. A wall may be mantled in flowering vines while an identical wall beside it remains bare, and neither condition changes.

The heat is brilliant and constant. The sun hangs high enough to bleach color from the central hours, but it does not cross the sky. Shadows point in one direction and keep their length. Beneath awnings, the air is green and wet; beyond them, white paving and standing water multiply the light until distant objects waver without actually moving. Sweat forms quickly on skin but does not cool it. Moisture condenses on the undersides of bridges, on shaded pottery, and on the bellies of sleeping animals. Fat drops gather there, perfectly clear, and fall at long intervals into channels below.

There is no rain, though every surface looks post-rain.

Water issues from weeping walls and perforated columns. It beads along carved faces, runs through shallow runnels, crosses plazas in narrow silver sheets, and collects in rectangular marshes. Its flow is too slow to hear except with an ear against the stone. No source feeds it. No outlet carries it away. If water is taken from a pool, the level is found restored once nobody is looking. If a channel is blocked, the water waits against the obstruction without rising. Unblocked, it resumes precisely where it had been.

The marshes have been planted with almost ceremonial care. Papyrus rises in separate clumps, leaving broad lanes of open water between. Broad-leaved plants float in grids, their roots descending into ceramic cages. Date palms, figs, and flowering shade trees stand far apart in circular beds, each bed filled with black compost manufactured from reeds, dung, kitchen scraps, and the soft remains of insects. Creepers cover trellises but seldom bridge the gaps between them. The vegetation is luxuriant without becoming crowded: each plant has its assigned abundance, each thicket its surrounding emptiness.

Some gardens are constructed entirely from imitations. Their trunks are bundled cane plastered with mud; their leaves are hammered copper-green shell or painted cloth; their flowers are cups of glazed clay holding drops of scented oil. Living vines climb these counterfeit trees readily. Birds nest in them. Fruit bats hang from their rafters. No creature appears to distinguish between artifact and organism, perhaps because the distinction has little authority here.

Animal life is plentiful, though it rarely breaks the quiet. Long-legged marsh birds pace the tiled shallows. Their feet make faint clicks rather than splashes. Lizards sleep on hot parapets with their mouths open. Small antelope graze the planted verges, moving from one allotted patch to another across bare plazas. Pale frogs cling to the sweating walls, their throats swelling without producing calls. Dragonflies hover over the cisterns, fixed so steadily in the bright air that they resemble ornaments suspended on invisible threads.

There are fish in the channels, but their nature depends on where they are observed. Seen from above, they are dark living shapes slipping between reflections. Lift one in a net and it is a jointed object of lacquered wood and wet leather, flexing around wooden pins. Cut it open and there is warm flesh within. Return the pieces to water, turn away, and the fish passes whole beneath the next bridge.

The inhabitants do not regard this as extraordinary. They live in sparse compounds raised a few steps above the marsh pavement. Their houses have thick mud-plaster walls, woven doors, and roofs of palm rib and reed matting. Nothing requires advanced machinery. Pots are formed by hand. Timber is shaped with stone adzes and soft bronze chisels used until their edges curl. Fire is made in open kilns. Loads move on sledges because wheels jam in the seams between slabs. Barges are poled through channels so shallow that their hulls scrape the manufactured bottom.

The builders work constantly, but the landscape does not become more complete.

A crew may spend a season assembling a causeway toward a distant pavilion. Each morning their work stands intact, clean-edged and sunlit. Yet the pavilion remains the same number of days away. Measurements disagree without fluctuating: a cord laid along the causeway says it is eight hundred paces long; walking it requires thirteen hundred; viewed from a tower, it crosses scarcely more than a field. Each result is reliable. Repeating the test gives the same three answers.

For this reason, architecture is governed less by geometry than by custom. A doorway must be crossed carrying something, or it returns the traveler to the room they left. A stair with an even number of steps leads upward; an odd number reaches the same level from the other side. Roofless chambers accumulate coolness like water, and coolness can be ladled out at noon in black bowls. An unpainted wall separates spaces. A painted wall separates hours.

There are plazas where identical afternoons are stored.

Entering one, a visitor finds workers forever setting the first course of a low wall. The mud remains wet. Hands rise and descend. No one tires, and no brick is ever added. The workers can answer questions, share food, and laugh, but if persuaded to leave, they emerge again from the opposite edge carrying the same first brick. Their relatives bring them shade and speak to them from outside the plaza’s boundary. This is considered an occupational hazard rather than a curse.

Elsewhere stands a grove of ten young palms whose fronds are motionless even when handled. Their leaves bend beneath the hand but return without passing through the intervening positions, as if one shape has simply replaced another. Under those palms are the only known patches of genuine-looking soil, though digging reveals layers of manufactured objects instead: woven mats beneath leaf mold, roof tiles beneath the mats, then another shallow pool with its own bright sky. Sometimes fish are visible down there, swimming overhead.

Despite such failures of causality, the region feels calm rather than unstable. Reality does not convulse. It has settled into its contradictions and keeps them faithfully. The sun remains fixed. The water remains replenished. The lush gardens neither advance nor wither. Buildings are always freshly made, even where they are described as ancient. The animals repeat broad habits without repeating exact movements. Nothing erodes except in workshops, where material is deliberately abraded. A dropped pot breaks; its shards stay sharp forever unless ground down by hand.

Sound is consumed quickly. Voices carry only a short distance before becoming too faint to hear, even across bare courtyards. Hammering reaches the next house as a soft, regular tapping. Birds open their beaks in the brightness and produce little more than dry clicks. There is no wind in the reeds, though their narrow leaves maintain a permanent lean. The loudest common sounds are the sudden slap of a fish, the scrape of a sledge, and the fall of a condensed drop into a cistern.

At night—if night can be said to exist—the brightness does not lessen. People create darkness instead. They unfold black reed cloth over beds, shutter rooms, enter painted vaults, or sit beneath deep awnings where shadow has been thickened by generations of soot. Sleep is treated as travel into a nearby enclosed territory. Dreams are discussed as routes: one may pass under the red arch, follow the crocodile canal, and wake three houses away. Children are taught never to dream beyond the tiled horizon. Those who do sometimes return with mud under their nails, and mud is alarming because no one made it.

Across the far reaches of the wetlands stand colossal half-finished forms: a hand emerging from a rectangular lake, a stair broad enough for a village, the hollow likeness of a seated animal with rooms inside its ribs. Their surfaces bear fresh tool marks. No builder claims them. They are too large for the available methods, yet every part of them has been worked with stone blades, wooden levers, reed ropes, and human fingers. They do not dominate the landscape so much as punctuate its openness, isolated presences separated by miles of shining water and planted pavement.

Travelers use them as landmarks, though landmarks here do not indicate distance. The white hand means fresh water. The hollow beast means frogs. The stair means that a person walking alone may meet themselves descending.

Beyond the last inhabited compounds, the ceramic marsh continues under a blaze of stationary light. Channels branch into exact right angles. Orchards stand like green islands on the white expanse. Birds gather around empty pavilions. Water glitters in places no one has dug for it.

Eventually the pavement reaches what appears to be a raw edge: a long, clean lip from which the whole wet country should look down into whatever lies beneath.

There is no beneath.

Looking over the edge, one sees the same low handmade wetlands extending onward—bright channels, scattered trees, roofless halls, motionless sun—except that everything there is viewed from below. Condensation trembles on the underside of its causeways. Fish cross the ceilings of its pools. Its people occasionally look up through the white glare and see, above them, another country at the edge, peering down.

Neither landscape moves. Neither one ages. Between them hangs a single row of clear drops, always on the verge of falling.
