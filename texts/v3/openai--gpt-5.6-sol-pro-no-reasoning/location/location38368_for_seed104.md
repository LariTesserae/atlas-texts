The sea stands higher than the mountains.

It is not a sea laid over low country, nor a flood with a bottom waiting beneath it. It occupies the upper world: a continent of warm, dark water suspended at altitude, its foundations lost in cloud and blue distance. From below, if below can be reached, it resembles a black weather front that never passes. From within, there is no horizon—only gradients of pressure, dimness, and slow green abundance.

The water is deep enough to make climates of its own. Warm currents rise through colder strata, fold sideways, and return as broad submerged rivers. Invisible fronts advance for months. There are seasons of mineral haze, seasons of clear indigo, seasons when vast regions fill with minute amber spores and the water takes on the weak radiance of old honey. Rain occurs inside it: colder droplets condense in upper layers and fall through warmer water, keeping their shape for leagues. They strike leaves, ledges, and the soft backs of reefs with a sound too faint to travel far.

There is no stable surface. At the highest reaches, water thins into mist without becoming air; farther down, mist thickens again into water. Islands of atmosphere open temporarily in this transition—clear, rounded hollows as large as valleys, filled with warm wind. They drift, collapse, and sometimes divide. Waterfalls pour upward around their margins. Inside one of these air-caverns, vines and fronds hang dripping from every side, growing inward toward an empty center.

The ground, where it appears, is old beyond roughness.

Immense plateaus occupy the dark like submerged mesas, though their cliffs have been worn into long, reclining curves. Ridges once sharp have become backs, brows, knuckles, and then less than those: broad inclinations detectable by the way silt gathers and vegetation leans. The stone is black-green, fine-grained, and slick beneath translucent films. In places it has eroded into dense forests of pillars, each pillar smooth as a river pebble and miles tall. Elsewhere, only shallow basins remain, so ancient that the sediments within them have compacted into new stone and begun eroding in turn.

Some shelves end abruptly over depths that do not behave like distance. Looking down, one may see a floor far below, patterned with pale vegetation. Swim toward it and it recedes. Turn away and it rises close enough to brush the feet. These depths are not illusions. The water has been here so long that direction has softened under its pressure.

Height persists nevertheless. Everything is high, even what lies underneath. Loose objects sink upward toward the world’s remote summit; rooted things grow both down and higher-down; currents descend in spirals and arrive above their beginnings. A traveler can recognize elevation by warmth: the loftiest trenches are almost tropical, while certain exposed peaks—though surrounded by water—carry a chill that numbs the mouth.

Life grows thickly over all firm surfaces.

There are no ordinary trees, but there are groves. Their trunks are braided columns of root and flexible stone, furred in olive cilia. Branches open into broad, dark membranes that catch passing currents. Some crowns extend for miles, overlapping until they form submerged canopies beneath which the water is nearly still. Their leaves are wine-black on one side and faintly silver on the other. Every few hours an entire grove turns its leaves together. No signal can be seen passing between them. One moment the canopy is dark; the next it shines dimly, like scales glimpsed through smoke.

Long grasses fill the ancient basins. They do not wave. Each blade bends a fraction later than the water around it, so that currents become visible as delayed motion traveling across the meadow. Pale mosses climb sheer rock and bridge narrow gaps, building hanging bogs that contain denser water within their tissues. Gardens form inside gardens: cups in the moss hold miniature pools; each pool contains drifting green dust, threadlike reeds, and sometimes another smaller cup of air.

Vegetation is lush but slow. Growth is measured not by length but by repetition. A frond may unfold in a day and then unfold again from the same place without becoming larger. Rings accumulate in open water around old stems. Roots enter cracks, emerge from unrelated cliffs, and braid distant landforms together. If a root is cut—though nothing here seems to cut them—the parted ends do not bleed. A new span of terrain appears between them, already overgrown.

Flowers occur rarely and at enormous scale. One may occupy an entire valley, its petals lying against the slopes like sheets of dim red skin. It opens over several warm seasons, releasing neither pollen nor seed but a change in pressure. For hundreds of leagues the water becomes lighter. Silt lifts. Forest crowns bow. Air-caverns migrate toward the bloom and gather over it like bubbles at a submerged ceiling. When the flower closes, some of those caverns are trapped inside, and warm winds continue moving through its folded petals for centuries.

Animals are scarce.

Now and then, something small disturbs the vegetal density: a clear-bodied swimmer with one black eye; a ribbon that coils around a branch and is gone; a cluster of shell-like organisms walking together so precisely that they resemble a single many-jointed animal. Their movements are careful and brief. There is little grazing. No schools cloud the water, no reefs chatter with mouths. Most visible life belongs to plants, colonies, or categories not cleanly separated from geology.

The great fauna are rarer still. Their presence is inferred from changes in the landscape.

A meadow lies pressed flat for fifty miles.

A deep current stops.

All silver-sided leaves turn inward.

Then, perhaps, an immense pale contour passes behind several layers of forest. It makes no sound. It displaces no water. For a while the objects in front of it become older: leaves spot and fall, stone darkens, silt compacts. Once it has passed, new growth covers the route with unnatural speed. Whether these beings feed on time, carry it, or are merely visible where time is moving remains unknown to everything capable of noticing.

Silence dominates, but it is not emptiness. Water absorbs sharp noises almost at once. The ordinary soundscape is intimate: the tick of mineral grains settling on a membrane, the slight creak of a root changing its grip, the soft collapse of an air pocket caught under moss. On rare occasions, a deep tone enters the water from no discernible direction. It may continue for days. Nothing vibrates at its frequency, yet shadows tremble.

There are scattered signs of building, though not necessarily of builders.

On high stone shelves stand small enclosures made from fitted slabs. They have no doors. Each is only large enough to contain a curled body or a knot of roots. Their interiors remain dry despite the surrounding sea, but the dry space cannot be entered: a hand, fin, branch, or stream pushed through the opening emerges wet on the opposite wall without crossing the room.

Elsewhere, lone stairways descend along cliffs. Erosion has rounded them nearly flat, and vegetation fills the shallow steps. They lead neither to chambers nor terraces. One staircase continues past the foot of its cliff and proceeds unsupported through the water, each lower step older than the one above. Eventually the sequence becomes a trail of rectangular absences in the silt.

There are arches, too—three stones balanced in a form currents should long ago have toppled. Passing beneath an arch alters what is behind. A forest becomes a bare plain, or a plateau acquires a mountain-sized wound. Returning through does not restore it. The arches bear no carvings and show no evidence of tools. Limpets do not settle on them. Roots go around.

Perhaps these forms are not ruins. In a world so deeply eroded, regularity may be the final shape nature approaches. Given enough time, cliffs become walls, fractures become doorways, and riverbeds remember stairs.

The landscape is continually active, though little of it is hurried. Warm upwellings turn whole forests. Mineral storms roll through the trenches, layering exposed surfaces in red, white, and black dust. Mats of vegetation tear loose from upper slopes and drift as moving countries, carrying groves, ponds of heavy water, and sleeping tangles of transparent organisms. Where two mats meet, their roots test one another. Sometimes they fuse. Sometimes both recoil, and the surrounding water darkens with bitter green sap.

At irregular intervals, gravity changes its mind.

Silt rises in curtains. Stones lift from the plateaus and hover among the branches. Air-caverns plunge like hail toward inaccessible depths. The rooted forest stretches, groaning too softly to hear, and holds the soil in place. Then the old direction returns. Everything settles, but seldom where it began. This is the chief agent of erosion now: not current or chemistry, but repeated revisions of down.

The oldest regions have endured so many such revisions that they are nearly smooth in every direction. There are no undersides there. Boulders resemble closed eggs, cliffs resemble swells of water, and vegetation grows evenly over entire forms. These places are called nothing, because names lose orientation in them. Entering one, a traveler may find the path ahead returning through their own memory. Distance becomes dense. An hour can be crossed physically, like a cold boundary in the water.

Light comes from no sun.

It filters through the sea in muted green and violet fields, brightening without apparent source and fading without night. In the deepest groves the only illumination is biological: pinpricks along roots, dull gold within spores, blue pulses moving through the veins of great leaves. Yet shadows sometimes fall upward across open water, crisp-edged and enormous. Their owners are never visible. Plants close beneath them. The rare animals hide. Even currents seem to wait.

There is weather here, but it is weather of pressure, salinity, memory, and depth. There are fronts after which all nearby water tastes of stone. There are warm storms that cause dormant seeds to germinate inside living leaves. There are tides that remove colors one by one—first red, then green, then blue—until the landscape remains visible only as texture. When color returns, some objects receive the wrong shade. A grove may remain white for generations. A stone may turn the exact green of a place that no longer exists.

Nothing suggests that the world is failing. Its impossibilities are too old to be damage. They have weathered into law.

The sea remains aloft. The forests keep their patient motion. Ancient ground travels imperceptibly through dimensions that roots accept as ordinary. Sparse creatures cross between one silence and another. Scattered chambers preserve their dry, unreachable interiors. Far below—or higher, or long ago—the broad plateaus continue to erode toward a final form: not flatness, but a landscape in which every point has become equally deep.
