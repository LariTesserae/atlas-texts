It is a low country, though no one standing within it would call it small.

From horizon to horizon the land lies nearly level, reduced by an age of weather to a dark, shallow plane. There are no young mountains. There are scarcely hills. Every former height has been worn down, spread outward, and packed into the basin until the basin seems filled to its rim with the remains of geography: compressed muds, mineral crusts, shell beds, salt clays, mats of petrified pollen, ancient flood deposits laid one upon another with no clean seam between them. The ground is not empty anywhere. It is fully occupied by its own past.

Water inhabits this density rather than covering it. It gleams in narrow films between grains. It wells through cracks, trembles in footprints that close almost immediately, and hangs in the warm air as a continuous fine condensation. Pools are shallow but immeasurable in extent. Black runnels cross and recross the plain, joining, reversing, and vanishing under skins of silt. Rain is frequent, but often difficult to distinguish from the upward seep of the earth. Sometimes droplets rise from the pools and enter the clouds.

Everything is wet without being lush.

Vegetation persists sparsely and with immense patience. Far apart stand low fans of leathery blades, each plant spread flat to survive the wind. Rust-colored cords of moss follow the margins of brine channels. In protected hollows there are colonies of translucent stalks no taller than a finger, their interiors pulsing slowly with captured water. They flower perhaps once in a century, all at once across thousands of miles, making the plain briefly glimmer with small gray cups. Most of the time the living growth is so thin that one could mistake it for stains.

Yet the place is packed.

The atmosphere itself has thickness. Clouds occupy the sky in overlapping floors, descending until their bellies drag across the mud. Rain curtains lean against vapor columns. Sheets of airborne sediment travel within them, and beneath the surface lie innumerable strata so tightly laid that pressure has blurred the boundary between stone and liquid. There is no true vacant distance. Even the horizon is crowded by mirages, storm fronts, standing waves, and repetitions of the land displaced from their proper positions.

The light comes through all this as a brownish, sourceless dusk. Noon is a diffuse brightening behind miles of cloud. Reflections are more luminous than the sky: the plain shines from below in pewter strips, oily violet pools, and occasional expanses of perfect black. At intervals a pale band passes underfoot, as though daylight were moving through a buried layer. It may take several hours to cross. Nothing above changes while it does.

The noise is overwhelming.

Wind has no obstruction, and so it crosses the basin in continental strokes. It drives the surface water into long, low fronts that slap and hiss against the mineral crust. Thunder rolls almost continuously, but much of it rises from underground. The ancient layers settle, shear, and breathe. Mud chambers collapse with booms that travel for days. Water trapped under pressure screams through capillary fissures. Vast areas of crust chatter like teeth as heat passes through them.

There are other sounds without clear causes: a dense droning from the western flats, rhythmic impacts beneath pools too shallow to hide anything large, and at night a high metallic choir produced when rain touches certain beds of salt. During the great reversals, when channels abruptly exchange their sources and mouths, the whole plain gives a noise like a single immense sheet being torn.

Speech, if there were anyone to attempt it, would be local and temporary. A voice might be heard only by pressing brow to brow.

Storms do not merely cross this country. They manufacture it.

A squall can knead a thousand square miles of flat ground into ridges, erase them, and leave in their place a tessellation of hexagonal pools. Warm vortices descend from the clouds and pull up columns of mud, water, shells, and mineral flakes. These columns sometimes persist for months, migrating slowly over the plain like walking terrain. Where they pass, the strata are inverted: old sediments lie exposed on top, fresh rain is sealed beneath, and fossils of organisms not yet born can be found embedded in the drying surface.

The country is ancient beyond any useful measure, but it is not still. It has reached no peaceful conclusion. Rather, it has been eroded into a final form so sensitive that every small force acts upon the whole. With slopes almost gone, water cannot decide which way is downhill. A storm in one hemisphere may alter the channels of another. Pressure travels farther than rivers. The land is near-flat enough to behave like a membrane, shuddering from edge to edge.

Here erosion has outlived shape and begun to wear at sequence.

Rain sometimes strikes the pools before it falls from the clouds. Channels cut through deposits that will be laid down days later. A person looking across certain flooded reaches might see the same storm at several stages: forming on one horizon, raging overhead, and collapsing on the opposite horizon into clear vapor. The images are not reflections. Birds occasionally fly into one and emerge old, featherless, or not at all.

Birds are rare. So are all mobile animals. A few white, long-legged waders move through the warm shallows, stepping with exaggerated care. They have no calls loud enough for the plain. They communicate by stamping patterns into the mud, and other birds respond days later when ground tremors carry the message. Blind ribbon-shaped swimmers inhabit buried water seams. After heavy rain they are sometimes expelled in their thousands, knotting briefly across the surface before pushing back into the earth.

Larger animals are known only by disturbances. Something displaces water beneath broad regions without breaking the surface. Its passage raises a moving swell perhaps twenty miles wide and only the breadth of a hand high. Sparse plants fold before it. Salt plates tilt. The waders stand still and wait. No body has ever appeared, and it may be that there is no animal—that the entire aquifer occasionally behaves as one.

Life here tends toward the colonial and distributed. Individual bodies are liabilities in a landscape that revises location. The moss cords miles apart may be parts of one organism joined through wet stone. The translucent plants pulse in sequence during pressure changes. Even the scattered birds sometimes turn their heads together, as though one attention were being passed among them.

There are structures, but almost none can be confidently called made.

On isolated firm patches stand low rings of fitted stone, generally three or four courses high. They have no roofs, entrances, or marks of tools. Water inside them remains still during the worst storms. Some contain dry dust despite months of rain; others are filled above their rims without overflowing. Their stones resemble material from strata buried far below and may have risen naturally, though the regularity argues against accident.

Elsewhere, rows of narrow pillars emerge from the flats at intervals so great that their alignment becomes visible only during rare clear hours. They lean in different directions but cast one continuous shadow. Near them the thunder quiets—not completely, but enough for the hiss of breath and the soft activity of mud to become audible. Nothing grows in these silences. Wading birds avoid them.

There are also walls that exist only while it rains: vertical planes where drops stop in the air, accumulate, and run sideways along invisible surfaces. Sediment clings to these planes, sketching rooms, corridors, and enormous curved enclosures before wind strips them bare. No devices lie within. There are no hinges, wires, lamps, or machines. If intention shaped anything here, it did so by persuading water and pressure rather than by using tools.

The warm air smells of iron, wet limestone, old salt, and something faintly organic fermenting at planetary scale. Bare skin would quickly gather a film of mineral moisture. Hair would lift in the charged wind. The mud is often warm at the surface and cold a finger-width down, though in places this arrangement reverses every few seconds. Objects left on the ground sink not by descending, but by becoming progressively older until the local strata accept them.

This is one of the country’s firmest rules: age has weight.

Young things skim the surface. Old things settle. A fallen feather may travel for hundreds of miles, while a stone exposed from a primordial bed plunges through soft ground as though pulled by an immense gravity. During violent storms the rule falters, and whole buried epochs buoy upward. The plain then crowds itself with former surfaces: dead reefs, forests reduced to black lace, beds of shells, red dunes, glacial dust, and slabs bearing the impressions of rains that fell under vanished skies. They overlap without room, each claiming the same elevation.

For a time one can walk across a dozen worlds in a single stride.

Then the storm resolves them. Water presses everything flat again. The reefs soften into clay; the forests become dark streaks; shells grind down to powder finer than smoke. The basin returns to its enduring near-level state, richer by another layer too thin to see.

There is no obvious center. The scale defeats ordinary direction. Weather systems become regions, regions become events, and an event may be large enough to carry its own horizon. Still, travelers—if travelers could persist here—might learn to navigate by sound. The underground thunder has provinces. The salt choir sharpens toward the north. The vast western drone marks an area where the ground vibrates so strongly that standing water forms permanent geometric peaks. To the south, rainfall makes no impact noise at all, although the cloudbursts are heaviest there.

Far out in the central flats is said to be a place where erosion is complete.

No stone rises there. No grain is distinguishable from another. Water lies in a film of uniform depth across a circle larger than a sea, and the clouds descend until they touch it. Between cloud and surface there is no horizon, only dim gray substance in ceaseless agitation. Sound entering this region cannot escape. Centuries of thunder, wind, cracking salt, and collapsing earth have accumulated inside it, packed as densely as the sediments below.

The boundary announces itself as a growing pressure in the bones.

Within, all that stored noise is present at once. It is deafening beyond hearing, a physical medium that shakes water into standing forms and pulverizes anything rigid. Shapes appear in the vapor—coasts, towers, branching organisms, faces vast enough to have weather moving across them—and are destroyed before they become fully visible. The landscape seems to be trying every possible terrain after having worn real terrain away. Mountains rise as acoustical pressure and break without ever becoming stone. Rivers hang overhead. Caverns open in the rain. Brief continents wrinkle across the water film, complete with miniature storms, and sink within seconds.

Perhaps the whole plain is generated there, each layer an abandoned possibility carried outward and flattened by time.

Or perhaps it is the end toward which the basin drains: not a geographical low point, but a final state where matter, weather, memory, and sound can no longer remain separate.

Outside that center, the sparse plants keep low. The white waders stamp their messages into fresh mud. Warm rain beads on the stone rings. Across an expanse too broad to comprehend, shallow waters reverse their flow. The cloud ceiling churns, dragged by pressures rising from under the oldest ground.

Then a buried layer splits.

The rupture runs beyond the horizon in both directions. Water leaps from it in a continuous wall. The roar thickens; the dimness flashes silver from below; ancient sediments rise and crowd the surface once more. For several hours the flat country acquires cliffs, islands, trenches, and black forests of mineral spires.

By morning, if morning can be said to arrive beneath such clouds, nearly all of them are gone.

The land lies low again, saturated and unquiet, packed with every form it has ceased to be.
