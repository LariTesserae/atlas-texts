The place is a hollow inside a single, very old plant.

It lies close to sea level in a cool basin where the bedrock has long since been reduced to a nearly level floor. There are no cliffs left, no fresh cuts, no young stone. What rock remains is buried beneath centuries of root fiber and dark vegetable sediment. The basin is small enough to cross in an hour, though it seldom feels confined. Its boundaries are walls of living tissue: pale trunks fused side by side, rising and curving inward until their crowns interlock above the hollow.

Nothing here was built, but almost everything resembles construction.

Roots descend in straight columns. Branches meet in load-bearing forks. Sheets of bark overlap like shingles, and transparent membranes span gaps high overhead, admitting a steady green-white daylight. Long chambers run beneath the floor where old roots have died and newer roots have grown around the vacancies. Vines braid themselves into cables. Seed cases hang in regular rows. The entire grove has an accidental industrial order, the consequence not of intelligence but of growth repeated without interruption over immense time.

The interior is remarkably empty.

Vegetation fills every surface, yet almost none occupies the open air. The living mass has withdrawn to the perimeter, floor, and canopy, leaving a broad central volume beneath the vault. There are no shrubs, no tangles, no fallen branches. Every loose fragment is rapidly incorporated into the soft ground or lifted away by insects. Across the chamber stand only a few immense stalks, widely separated, smooth and gray-green. Their distance from one another gives them the severity of machinery on a factory floor.

Moisture comes intermittently. The basin has no permanent stream. Rain reaches it after passing through many layers of leaves, by then divided into heavy, widely spaced drops. They strike the membranes, roots, and hollow stalks with deep percussive notes. For hours afterward, condensation gathers under the canopy and runs down grooves in the trunks to shallow black pools at the margins. These pools rarely spread. The plant floor swells, drinks, and seals over them. On dry days only a cool dampness remains, along with beads of water inside flowers shaped like cups.

The machinery of the place is alive.

At regular intervals the great stalks begin to pump. Their walls tighten in slow traveling rings, forcing sap upward through internal tubes. Valves made of dense petals open and close with blunt reports. High in the canopy, broad leaves rotate toward the brightest portion of the sky. Their stems move against fibrous sockets, producing a continuous wooden groan. Seed pods vent excess pressure in sharp bursts. Root nodules knock beneath the floor as fluids pass from chamber to chamber.

No engine was installed here. There are no gears, furnaces, wires, or tools. Yet the grove harvests light, separates water, circulates sugars, regulates temperature, processes waste, and manufactures new tissue on a scale and with a rhythm that feels industrial. It is a factory that has never known an inventor.

The sound is disproportionate to the stillness.

From the entrance comes a constant roar, like distant surf, caused by wind crossing the tight outer leaves. Within, the roar separates into layers: the low pulse of sap columns, the slap of membranes, the creak of loaded branches, the dry rattle of seed husks, and the minute but collective rasp of feeding animals. The basin rarely changes, but it is never quiet. Its equilibrium is noisy.

Animals are abundant chiefly in the surfaces. Beetles move under translucent bark. Mites graze on films of yeast. Pale frogs sit in water cups, each occupying one flower for most of its life. Small birds nest in pockets grown by the plant around their bodies; they enter and leave through round pores in the canopy. Colonies of gray moths cling to the trunk walls so closely that they appear to be scales. Worms, larvae, snails, and root-eating crustaceans inhabit the damp floor in enormous numbers.

In the central open space, however, one may see almost nothing moving. Occasionally a bird crosses from one wall to another, emphasizing the breadth of the vacancy. A cloud of gnats forms above a condensation pool and vanishes when the water is absorbed. Otherwise the fauna remain embedded in the green architecture, busy but concealed.

Predation is mild and constant. Frogs take gnats. Birds probe the bark for larvae. Beetles eat the eggs of other beetles. Nothing dominates for long, and no population erupts. The grove has had too much time to refine its exchanges. Every excess is consumed; every opening acquires a resident. Death occurs everywhere in small quantities and disappears almost immediately into roots.

The air stays cool throughout the year. There is no strong season, only a subtle change in the direction of light and in the spacing of rain. During colder weeks, pumping slows by a barely perceptible measure. During brighter weeks, the upper membranes thicken and admit less sun. The temperature in the hollow remains nearly the same. So does its smell: wet wood, crushed fern, mushroom skin, and a faint mineral odor from the unseen rock below.

Its age is expressed not through ruins but through the absence of accident. Storms have worried the outer grove into a low, rounded profile. Floods have deposited and removed sediment until the floor found its final level. Weak limbs have broken, decayed, and been replaced by stronger alignments. The oldest trunks no longer possess distinguishable centers. They are layers around layers, repairs grown over repairs, an archive without a first page.

Paths through the hollow are not worn by feet. They are maintained by chemistry. Narrow bands of floor exude bitter oils that discourage roots, moss, and burrowing animals, preserving clear routes between pumping stalks and peripheral pools. These lanes look purposeful, but nothing planned them. They are simply the enduring boundaries between neighboring tissues, fixed so long ago that the whole ecology now depends on them.

The chief rule here is that the whole must remain open.

Anything that falls into the central chamber is removed. Leaves curl and drag themselves toward composting seams. Fungi dissolve dead animals where they lie. Young shoots entering the open volume bend back toward the walls or are starved by the canopy. The emptiness is actively produced. It is as much a living feature as the trunks.

A second rule is repetition. Drops fall into the same cups. Birds use the same pores. Sap rises through the same columns. Each animal generation inherits a world almost indistinguishable from that of its ancestors. Even the loud reports of the valves occur in a stable sequence, passing around the hollow from stalk to stalk and beginning again.

Standing at the center, one is surrounded by total vegetation and extraordinary vacancy: a room made entirely of life, preserving a pocket of nearly empty air. It is damp without being lush in the ordinary sense, industrial without artifacts, crowded with animals that are seldom seen, and ancient without appearing ruined.

The grove does not seem asleep. It seems to have completed its experiments.

What remains is operation.
