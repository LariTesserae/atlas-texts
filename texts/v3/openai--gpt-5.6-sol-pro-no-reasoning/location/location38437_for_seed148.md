The city occupies a hollow no larger than a market square.

From above—if above can be reached—it resembles a dark knot of foliage pressed into a shallow depression. There are no suburbs, no roads approaching it, no visible countryside. Beyond its perimeter lies only a cool, vacant distance: flat mineral dusk, unmarked and apparently unfinished. The ground there has the soft gray color of clay before firing. It bears no gullies, no old drainage lines, scarcely even dust. Rain has not worked on it long enough to teach it a shape.

At the hollow’s rim, vegetation begins abruptly.

It does not grow upon the city. It is the city.

Trunks stand in close architectural ranks, slender at their bases and widening overhead into vaults. Some are fibrous and pale, others black and glossy as wet machinery. Their roots are woven into floors, stairs, gutters, conduits, and retaining walls. Broad leaves overlap like roof tiles. Windows are clear blisters in bark. Doors form where paired stems draw apart, then seal behind the entrant with a slow pressure of damp cambium.

Yet for all this living construction, the place feels almost empty. Its buildings are shells around enormous interior voids. A whole factory may consist of six tree-columns supporting a canopy roof over darkness. Streets are narrow but rarely occupied. Bridges span gaps of only a few paces, though beneath them the ground seems to fall much farther than the dimensions of the hollow permit. Vertical shafts open among the roots, breathing cold air from depths that cannot be located.

Nothing here is dead material for long.

Iron braces sprout rust-red tendrils and become vascular. Glass fogs, softens, and develops veins. Brickwork germinates after rain. Cables hang in loose loops from the branches, but when touched they tense like vines; at intervals they pulse with a dim blue current and deliver both electricity and sap. Machines have been grafted into the plant mass so completely that it is difficult to say whether the city industrialized the forest or the forest learned industry.

The largest engine stands near the center, in a chamber called the Mill by the few beings that name things. It is a wheel of dark wood with copper teeth, turning very slowly around an empty axle. No river drives it. Instead, small animals enter its lower compartments carrying seeds, shed shells, bright stones, and scraps of warm metal. Larger animals emerge from the upper apertures, often of different species. The wheel turns once every few days, although its shadow completes several revolutions each hour.

When it moves, the entire hollow responds. Root-pavements flex. Buds open in unison. Somewhere behind the walls, pistons sigh. A distant assembly line starts and stops, producing identical green pods with numbered skins. Most are carried away by beetles before they ripen.

Water is scarce enough to be important and common enough to be everywhere in traces. It beads beneath leaves, collects in the lips of fungal basins, and runs briefly through root channels after the irregular rains. There is one narrow river, but it exists only intermittently—not because it dries, but because it sometimes takes another route through reality.

On certain mornings it crosses the central street, black and ankle-deep, carrying silver pollen. By afternoon it may be found flowing vertically down the side of a trunk. At night its sound comes from inside sealed rooms. Fish appear wherever that sound is strongest, swimming through humid air with careful motions of their fins. They vanish if directly illuminated.

Rain arrives without clouds. Droplets condense in midair at shoulder height and fall upward into the canopy. Mosses turn their cups toward the sky below. Animals gather beneath branches to drink from the upward streams, licking water as it rises past them. The event lasts only minutes, leaving the city cooler and fragrant with crushed fern, ozone, and the mineral smell of newly exposed earth.

There is little erosion. Surfaces have not had time to weather into familiarity. The hollow seems recently made—perhaps still being made. Its edges are soft and provisional. Banks of raw clay bulge overnight, harden by morning, and split open to reveal rootwork already fitted inside. Pebbles hatch from gelatinous sacs. Fresh stone emerges warm from the ground in angular folds, as though geology here is a manufacturing process rather than a history.

Nothing possesses the rounded patience of age. Even the oldest trunks have bark without scars.

Darkness fills the city densely, despite the emptiness of its rooms. Light is an occasional biological secretion: a moth opening luminous wings; a berry glowing when bitten; green sparks passing along a cable-vine; the brief furnace-red eye of some buried mechanism. These illuminations do not push the dark away. They reveal small islands within it, each apparently separated from the others by a much greater distance than the city’s outer dimensions allow.

A lantern carried down one street can sometimes be seen from a window behind the carrier.

The fauna are abundant, though rarely conspicuous. They live in every layer of the plant architecture. Pale lizards graze on the inner walls of chimneys. Furred mites move in herds along handrails. Long-legged browsers crop fresh signs from signposts, preferring vowels. Beetles nest in electrical boxes and close circuits with their bodies. A colony of small, eyeless birds inhabits the ventilation system; each dawn—if the slight whitening overhead can be called dawn—they fly through the ducts and redistribute smells from room to room.

Larger creatures pass less often. There are fox-shaped things made almost entirely of leaves, their bones visible as thin aluminum struts. They hunt the air-swimming fish and sleep wound around warm transformers. A heavy six-footed grazer patrols the city boundary, eating any root that attempts to cross onto the gray plain. Behind it, the severed roots withdraw, knot themselves, and begin designing another route.

Predation is usually quiet. A tremor runs through a patch of foliage. A shadow folds the wrong way. Then one animal is absent and another has acquired its pattern.

Sound carries poorly. Leaves, moss, and fibrous walls absorb it before it travels far. The city is mostly composed of close noises: condensation ticking into cups, mandibles working inside wood, wet cables tightening, the faint rotation of the central wheel. Occasionally an engine starts beneath the roots with a low, orderly throb. It may run for several hours, shaking pollen from the rafters, although no one has found a way to reach it.

There are bells, but they do not ring aloud. When one moves, every animal in the hollow pauses simultaneously and looks toward a different point.

The settlement has infrastructure and, apparently, civic rules. Paths redirect foot traffic by growing thorns behind the walker. Platforms arrive when stood upon and depart when empty. Pipes sort water by memory: rain that has touched an animal goes toward the nurseries; rain that has touched metal feeds the factories; rain caught without contact is stored in transparent fruits high in the canopy. Waste is taken below, where roots separate it into heat, color, odor, and regret. Only the first three are reused.

No inhabitants maintain these systems. Or perhaps every inhabitant does, in small unconscious ways. The beetles regulate power. The browsing mites prune circulation routes. Predators keep certain doors from multiplying. The city may be less a habitat than a collective behavior—architecture produced by thousands of organisms obeying rules none of them understands.

Those rules are unstable.

Distance depends on whether one is hungry. A room entered in fear has more exits than walls. Objects left alone become botanical versions of themselves. Repetition causes duplication: walk the same lane three times and a second lane appears beside it, nearly identical but damp with rain that has not yet fallen. Names function as temporary scaffolding. Once a thing is named too precisely, it begins shedding the features that justify the name.

The city itself resists measurement. From rim to rim it is perhaps two hundred paces. Its central factory alone contains corridors totaling several miles. Both facts remain true unless compared directly, at which point the factory closes its entrances and flowers.

Beyond the vegetation, the bare plain waits.

It is immensely vacant, but not ancient. Here and there, shallow dents appear in the clay as though expecting future hills. Thin seams of stone sketch possible riverbeds and then stop. Nothing moves except the city’s shadow, which reaches far across the plain despite the lack of any visible sun.

Sometimes the shadow germinates.

A dark stalk rises miles away, though miles may not be the correct measure. It branches once, unfolds a single leaf, and begins constructing an empty room beneath itself. Animals at the city’s edge turn to watch. The buried engines slow. Water gathers in trembling beads along every roof-leaf.

Then the whole hollow seems to hold its breath—not in silence, but in a near-silence full of minute work: roots deciding, insects counting, gears tasting sap, stone hardening from possibility into matter.

The new room waits in the distance.

By morning, it may be the next city.

Or it may have always been this one.
