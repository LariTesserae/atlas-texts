At this altitude the city has no ground.

It rests upon a continuous canopy, an old green continent lifted over the mountains on trunks too broad to be recognized as trunks. Their crowns have fused through centuries of pressure and grafting. Bark closes every gap. Roots descend into cloud and stone; branches spread into plazas, embankments, terraces, and long shallow valleys. What appears from a distance to be a high, sparsely settled plateau is the upper surface of one immeasurable organism—or a parliament of plants so thoroughly joined that the distinction has ceased to matter.

The surface is ancient and almost level. Wind, frost, and the tread of generations have worn the bark into broad gray-green pavements. Growth rings emerge underfoot like contour lines, some wide enough to serve as roads. In places, the living wood has weathered down to a final grain: hard, silvered ridges with shallow basins between them, polished by ages of rain. Nothing erodes quickly here. A crack may spend three hundred years becoming a ravine. A fallen leaf can remain lodged in one crease long enough to acquire a district name.

The plateau extends beyond easy sight. Its edges dissolve into cool haze, interrupted by low vegetal hills, tower groves, and the silhouettes of distant industrial wards. The open spaces are immense. The city is urban not because it is crowded, but because it is organized: avenues, reservoirs, conduits, factories, power lines, zoning markers, freight routes, civic bells. Between these things lie miles of bare living pavement, pale lawns of tightly woven moss, and abandoned squares broad enough for weather to develop inside them.

Everything built is also grown.

Houses are hollowed in woody bladders encouraged to swell beside the roads. Their walls are layered bark, their windows translucent membranes stretched across pruned openings. Apartment towers rise as columnar shrubs with room-cells stacked around central sapwells. Bridges are braided roots trained over fissures, tightened each winter by crews with winches and heated collars. Warehouses grow low and broad, their roofs quilted with waxy leaves that fold shut during rain. Even chimneys are vascular pipes, coaxed upward and cauterized at the rim.

The city’s industry depends on persuasion backed by violence.

In the mill districts, slow engines clamp around trunks and pump measured shocks into the cambium. Great pistons thud day and night, forcing sap uphill or redirecting sugars into vats. Fermentation halls breathe through rows of stomata the size of doors. Resin refineries glitter with amber channels. Fiber works strip endless ribbons from sacrificial stems, steam them, press them, weave them into cable, cloth, insulation, and paper-thin structural laminates. No ore is mined nearby. Metal is precious, darkened by repeated use, and concentrated in bearings, blades, boilers, rails, and control cabinets. Most machinery is wooden-bodied, ceramic-jointed, tendon-belted, and loud.

The loudness carries.

Across the open plateau comes the hammering of pump stations, the rhythmic cough of pressure valves, the clatter of freight pods rolling in grooves worn through bark. Wind combs the high leaf towers with a roar like distant surf. Whole districts creak as temperatures change. Beneath all of it is the slow, infrasonic complaint of the canopy: tension traveling through branches hundreds of kilometers long. People cannot properly hear it, but dishes tremble in cupboards, reservoir skins ripple without wind, and sleep becomes crowded with dreams of bending.

Rain is infrequent but consequential. Most days are cool and dry, under an ordinary high-country sky: blue, clouded, or evenly gray according to season. Moisture comes in narrow events. Cloudbanks climb from the unseen valleys and snag beneath the canopy, breathing condensation upward through vents and cracks. At dawn, droplets bead on every hair, wire, leaf margin, and window membrane. Gutters open. Roof foliage tips itself toward cistern mouths. For an hour the entire city ticks and drips.

Actual rainfall arrives as passing curtains. It darkens one ward while another remains sunlit. Water gathers in old scars and pruning hollows, forming scattered ponds with steep wooden shores. Narrow rivers run only along certain grain lines; they may flow for several days, then withdraw into the plant through a million pores. Standing water is guarded because roots are thirsty and the city beneath the city can drink a reservoir empty overnight.

Some pools resist absorption. These are older than the current canopy and reflect skies that do not match the weather.

Around them the rules loosen.

Distance is least reliable near living water. A pond ten paces across may have a far shore blue with remoteness. Workers walking around a cistern sometimes return older, carrying tools discontinued before they were born. Reflections show buildings that have not yet been grafted, or avenues from which every structure has been neatly removed. During condensation fogs, the puddles multiply without increasing the amount of water. Their surfaces appear on vertical walls, under leaves, and inside closed cabinets, each one opening onto the same inverted city.

No one drinks from a pool that reflects stars by daylight.

The plants themselves make up nearly all visible matter. Yet the landscape does not feel lush in the usual sense. Vegetation is total, but forms are sparse. Life has consolidated. Instead of innumerable stems competing for space, there are isolated immensities: one grass blade spanning a public square; a stand of seven black ferns, each as tall as a crane; a lichen field reaching to the horizon with no change in color. The openness comes from scale. A single leaf can roof an industrial quarter. A single flower may occupy a valley, opening once in a human lifetime and halting rail traffic for months.

The old erosion has simplified everything. Fragile forms vanished long ago. What remains is blunt, settled, and difficult to kill. The canopy has survived climates, impacts, infestations, and at least one long era when the sun was reportedly in a different part of the sky. Its shapes are worn but not ruined. The oldest trunks have become smooth mesas. Their former branches lie as low ridges beneath later growth. Dead wood does not rot completely; it mineralizes, becomes hard as masonry, and is incorporated into foundations.

Fauna are rare enough to be events.

There are pale gnats after rain, rising briefly from warm sap vents. Thin-winged pollinators visit the great flowers, though no one knows where they live between bloomings. Root mice occasionally emerge in boiler rooms: eyeless, green-blooded creatures with seedlike teeth. Their appearance can shut down a factory, not from danger but because workers gather to watch.

Birds are more rumor than population. One may cross the plateau every few weeks, a dark speck beating laboriously through the thin air. Sirens announce it. Machines are stopped, reflective surfaces covered, and citizens instructed not to point. Birds cast several shadows here, and only one of those shadows follows the bird. The others run along roads, climb walls, or wait beside doorways. After the animal has passed, sanitation crews mark each stationary shadow with chalk and place a potted cutting over it. By morning, the cutting is usually a mature tree.

There are almost no predators. The place consumes through slower means.

Abandoned objects root. Clothing left damp overnight sends threads into the pavement. Wooden tools graft themselves to workbenches. Paper records develop green veins, revise their own diagrams, and eventually fold into seed cases. Human buildings, though carefully pruned, tend toward forest anatomy. Corridors narrow into vascular tubes. Staircases spiral in patterns no architect specified. Rooms bud behind walls.

The city employs surveyors to oppose this drift. They walk the districts measuring angles, counting doors, and hammering numbered brass studs into stable surfaces. Their maps expire weekly. Some streets lengthen during overcast weather; others become shorter when named aloud. The oldest avenues have settled into such complete stability that they cannot be altered at all. A road may be blocked, buried, cut through, or forgotten, but its route continues to function. Freight placed at one end eventually appears at the other, regardless of what now stands between.

This is the principle behind the industrial transit system. Trains do not move continuously. They enter legacy routes, vanish behind veils of leaves, and emerge hours later from grooves that may lie at a different elevation. Engines are needed not for propulsion but to maintain certainty: huge flywheels, bells, pressure gauges, and synchronized pistons repeating the assertion that departure leads to arrival. When an engine loses rhythm, the train may reach a metaphor instead of a station.

The high air remains cool throughout most of the year. Frost rims the western districts at night, whitening moss and tightening the bridge roots until they sing. Snow is uncommon; when it falls, leaves beneath it remember winter and withdraw their chlorophyll. Patches of the city turn bone-pale. Factories burn resin to keep vital junctions awake. In the sun, the temperature is mild, and the ordinary quality of the light makes the place’s impossibilities more severe. Nothing is hidden by perpetual twilight or theatrical color. The sky is simply sky. Shadows fall cleanly. Workers take lunch beside pipes. Laundry lifts in the wind. Impossible streets extend under calm noon.

Activity comes in pulses rather than frenzy. Dawn opens the stomatal gates. Condensers begin to knock. Freight bells roll across the plateau. Pruning crews climb tower groves, suspended from cables, cutting back new rooms and unauthorized balconies. At midday the sap pressure rises, and entire neighborhoods gently elevate by a meter or two. Near evening, leaves fold across certain roads. Factories shift from extraction to repair. At night, root traffic begins below the pavement—vast movements felt as pressure, migrating from one side of the city to the other.

No one knows what lies under the canopy. Shafts have been sunk, but depth behaves badly there. The first layers are comprehensible: bark, cambium, wet fiber, hollow galleries full of cool vapor. Farther down, the shafts open sideways into forests whose trees grow upward toward the same sun, though they are indisputably beneath the city. Explorers have radioed from these lower crowns while visible through telescopes on the northern horizon.

The municipality has therefore classified “down” as an industrial hazard.

At the outer wards, construction thins. Pump towers stand kilometers apart. Signal posts blink among expanses of silver bark. Roads continue with little traffic, dividing around ancient knots and pools. Beyond them are the uncultivated reaches, though “uncultivated” means only that the plant is shaping itself without civic instruction. There the plateau rises in shallow green swells and falls toward enormous scars. Wind pours through leaf arches. The machinery of the center becomes a low metallic thunder behind the natural thunder of the canopy.

Far away, at the oldest visible edge, stands a row of vertical trunks stripped by ages of weather. They are not dead. Each bears a crown somewhere above the range of sight. Their roots do not descend but stretch horizontally into the sky, narrowing toward points that disappear among the clouds.

On clear days, observers see structures moving along those roots: small, regular silhouettes, like another city’s trains.

The schedule office records their passage. It has done so for eleven centuries. No train has ever arrived.

Still, every evening, the station at the city’s western limit illuminates its platforms. The clocks are wound. The arrival bells are tested. Workers sweep pollen from the empty grooves. Water condenses on the rails, gathers into bright beads, and begins—very slowly—to roll uphill.
