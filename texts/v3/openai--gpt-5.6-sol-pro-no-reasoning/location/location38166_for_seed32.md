The place is hidden inside a wall.

Not behind it, nor beneath its plaster, but within the wall’s substance: a narrow court folded among courses of pale brick. It is scarcely wider than two outstretched hands, though it runs farther than the eye can judge, turning repeatedly around buttresses, blind corners, and close-set arcades. Every surface has been made. There is no bedrock underfoot, no native soil in the joints, no accidental cavity left by settling stone. Floor, walls, ribs, ledges, channels, lintels, and the low vault overhead belong to one continuous construction.

It has no apparent builders.

The bricks are small and densely fitted, cream-white at their faces and gray in their depths. They do not meet in mortar. Instead their edges have weathered into one another, softened by prolonged pressure until the court seems assembled from a single mineral carved to imitate masonry. Every available interval is occupied. Narrow columns stand in ranks against narrower columns. Shelves fill recesses, niches fill the undersides of shelves, and each niche contains a finer array of arches, ridges, stairs, and sealed doors. Even cracks have architecture inside them.

Nothing opens.

The floor lies low and level, a pavement of shallow polygons polished nearly matte. Dust cannot collect because there is nowhere for it to settle. The seams are already packed with a substance like powdered porcelain compressed into stone. There are no drains. Water has never passed through this place. The dry air carries no humidity at all, and exposed material does not crumble so much as slowly round itself: corners blunted, inscriptions reduced to shallow ripples, stair treads cupped by no feet.

This erosion is ancient but complete. It is no longer occurring.

Everything has reached its final worn shape.

The court is brilliantly lit. Light comes from the masonry itself—not as a technological glow, nor as heat or flame, but as an ordinary property of its pale material. Each exposed face gives off a steady, shadowless whiteness. Deeper seams shine less strongly, so the packed geometry remains legible in delicate gray lines. There is no source to approach, no cycle, no dimming. Objects do not cast shadows so much as gather thin outlines around themselves.

The brightness reveals a sparse vegetation rooted in holes no larger than pinpricks. It is doubtful that these growths are plants in any familiar sense. They have no green pigment and seek no sun. Fine black stalks rise from selected joints, each supporting a fan of translucent blades. Their tissues resemble mica, dry horn, or the wings of seeds, though there are no animals here to carry them and no wind capable of dispersal.

Every fan is motionless.

Some occur in orderly colonies, one to each thirteenth brick. Others form solitary crowns on high cornices. Their roots penetrate the built substance without breaking it, following hairline passages prepared for them as precisely as keyways. No dead growth lies below. No new shoot is unfolding. The blades possess minor chips and blunt edges, proof of a forgotten period when the court was less settled, but now each specimen holds its posture indefinitely.

The temperature is cool and uniform. Touching a wall gives the sensation of handling an object kept for years in a shaded room. There are no warm pockets near the light, no colder depths behind the doors, and no breath of moving air. Distance changes neither comfort nor scent. The atmosphere tastes faintly of chalk and old metal, although there is no loose chalk and nothing metallic can be found.

Yet the place is loud.

A continuous, enormous note occupies it.

The sound does not come from machinery. There are no mechanisms, wires, resonant membranes, or moving parts. The whole architecture sounds simply by existing. Pillars hold one pitch; pavement another; the nested arcades introduce close harmonics until the narrow court is saturated with a dense mineral roar. It resembles a bell heard from inside the bronze, except there is no strike and no decay. At certain corners the tones cancel, leaving pockets of near-silence the size of a fingertip. A few inches away, they converge into pressure so strong it seems to give the air a grain.

Nothing vibrates visibly. Dustless ledges remain perfectly still. The translucent fans neither quiver nor bend. The noise has no beginning and spends no energy. It is as permanent as length or brightness—a rule of the enclosed world rather than an event within it.

Movement through the court, if movement were possible, would be mostly a matter of choosing among spaces that almost admit a body. Passages narrow into combs of piers. Stairs rise three steps and end against ornamented ceilings. Galleries loop back inside their own supporting walls. There are plazas only a palm wide, crowded with waist-high monuments; avenues as thin as cuts; towers inverted from the vault until their spires nearly touch corresponding towers rising from the floor.

The density creates strange impressions of vastness. Looking along a row of miniature colonnades, one may imagine a city extending toward a horizon. Looking up immediately dispels the illusion: the roof is close, webbed with luminous ribs, and fitted with thousands of shallow compartments. Each compartment contains a different finished form—an amphitheater, a cluster of domes, a forest of square obelisks, a field of bowls turned mouth-down. None appear symbolic. They repeat too precisely to be ruins and vary too subtly to be decoration.

There is no animal life. No mites inhabit the joints, no pale insects feed on the black fans, and no microscopic grazers soften their surfaces. The court has no food web. Its sparse growths seem less like colonists than integral pieces of the construction, biological clauses in an otherwise mineral grammar. They neither compete nor spread. Each occupies exactly the space allotted to it.

Time is difficult to establish here. The worn steps suggest duration; the rounded reliefs suggest weather; the seamless masonry suggests pressure sustained across ages. But these are conditions, not processes. If the place once changed, that change belongs to a different set of laws. Now no surface loses a grain. No stalk thickens. No temperature drifts. The great chord does not waver by a fraction of a tone.

Even an intrusion would not readily alter it. A footprint could find no dust to receive it. A broken blade, if breaking were somehow achieved, would expose an interior already as old and polished as its outer face. An object left behind would look foreign only briefly; not because the court would consume it, but because prolonged attention would reveal a recess shaped exactly for it somewhere nearby.

The implication is quiet beneath the tremendous sound: this packed, bright court is not unfinished, abandoned, or waiting.

It is full.

It has accounted for every volume, every resonance, every living stem, every permissible absence. The hidden wall contains no room for an event.
