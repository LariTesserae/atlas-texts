The place lies in a shallow cup near the summit of a warm, isolated mountain. It is not much larger than a grove, though its boundaries are distinct: a low ring of weathered basalt surrounds it, rising here to shoulder height and sinking there beneath moss. From outside, the cup appears nearly empty. From within, it seems unexpectedly deep, as if distance has been folded downward instead of outward.

Rain visits often but seldom falls hard. Water arrives as mist, as condensation, as drops released from leaves long after the clouds have passed. The black rock sweats continuously. Its old fractures are padded with fern roots, and clear beads gather along the undersides of ledges until they become too heavy and fall into the basin. The ground is wet without being flooded: dark, springy soil interrupted by pale gravel, shallow pools, and narrow seams of moving water.

At the center stands no tree, stone, or other obvious landmark. There is only an open patch of sedge surrounding a circular pool the width of a sleeping body. The pool is perfectly clear and apparently shallow. Its bottom is paved with greenish stone, yet a hand lowered into it finds no floor. The visible stones retreat at exactly the pace of the reaching hand.

Around this opening, vegetation grows richly but with peculiar restraint. Broad-leaved shrubs occupy isolated mounds, each separated from the next by several paces of wet grass. Tree ferns unfurl above patches of liverwort and red-veined moss. A few small mountain trees lean over the enclosure, their silver bark peeling in soft ribbons, their crowns widely spaced. Vines are abundant only where they find something already living to climb; they refuse the bare basalt entirely. The result is lushness without thicket—a green room furnished sparsely, every plant distinct.

The air is warm enough that rainwater never feels cold. Moisture settles on skin at once. Breath is easy but oddly tangible, and exhalations sometimes remain visible for several seconds as faint, low clouds. Near noon, when the sun clears the rim, the cup becomes bright without becoming harsh. Light rests evenly on the wet surfaces. Shadows are soft-edged, and reflections appear in places where there is no standing water.

The enclosing basalt is ancient and settled into rounded forms. Its faces have been hollowed into bowls, flutes, and overhangs by centuries of patient dripping. Nothing here looks freshly broken. Fallen stones sink slowly into moss and become smooth before they disappear. Channels in the soil are so old that roots have grown along their curves rather than across them. Erosion is not destroying the place; it is the means by which the place maintains its shape.

The loudness comes from water.

Every drop striking the central pool produces a deep sound disproportionate to its size. A bead falling from a fern may ring like a stone dropped into a cavern. Runoff passing beneath the sedge makes a continuous low rushing, although the visible streams are scarcely wide enough to cover a finger. From the weeping cliffs comes a layered percussion: ticks, knocks, hollow wooden notes, and occasional resonant booms that can be felt through the soles of the feet. The sounds overlap slowly, without rhythm, filling the small enclosure until it seems much larger than it is.

Thunder, when it comes, behaves differently here. It does not roll away. It circles the basalt rim, diminishing over many minutes, and sometimes returns from below.

Animals are rare. Tiny copper-colored frogs emerge after rain, always singly, never calling. A long-winged black bird may pass overhead without descending. There are no obvious nests and few tracks. Once or twice in a season, something heavy crosses beneath the moss—the surface lifts in a patient line, then settles unchanged—but nothing is seen. Insects exist mostly as sound: a dry clicking from the ferns at dusk, a solitary hum moving around the pool without a visible source.

There are signs of tool use, though no artifacts remain. Certain nutshells are split with clean, repeated blows and left on flat stones. Fern stems are occasionally cut at the same slant, with edges too neat for browsing teeth. At the eastern rim, hand-sized flakes have been struck from a seam of glassy volcanic rock, but every loose flake is gone. Whoever works here takes everything made and leaves no shelter, hearth, path, or refuse. Perhaps the toolmakers are the rare animals. Perhaps they come only during years in which the pool rises.

The pool rises very slowly.

Its waterline advances no more than the breadth of a leaf in a human lifetime. As it spreads, the sedge withdraws before it, growing in a wider ring. Shrubs migrate upslope by seed. Moss seals abandoned root cavities. Nothing struggles against the change; the entire cup rearranges itself so gradually that it always appears complete. Old waterlines can be found high on the basalt, marked by bands of white mineral and small empty hollows shaped like mouths.

These bands imply that the basin has filled and emptied many times.

Local rules are simple, though not immediately apparent. Water flowing toward the central pool remains ordinary. Water flowing away from it grows warm, then vanishes into the soil. Reflections lag slightly behind the objects that cast them. A fallen leaf may touch the pool before its reflection begins to descend. Sounds have weight: during the loudest dripping, fern fronds bend inward, and the surface of the moss dimples beneath each deep note. Silence, when it briefly occurs, makes the whole enclosure rise by an almost imperceptible amount.

At dusk, condensation thickens into strands between widely separated leaves. These strands resemble spider silk but consist only of suspended droplets, each holding a tiny inverted image of the pool. They persist through the night. By morning they collapse all at once, releasing a soft rain beneath a clear sky.

There is no ruin here, no road, no monument. The mountain cup has not been abandoned because it was never occupied in the usual sense. It is a complete, old mechanism made from basalt, roots, water, and resonance, working at a pace too slow to reveal its purpose. It gathers rain. It deepens sounds. It teaches plants where to stand. It keeps an impossible depth beneath a shallow surface.

And, over ages, it appears to be listening for something loud enough to answer.
