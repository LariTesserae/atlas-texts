The city lies in a vast depression, lower than the surrounding country and perhaps still sinking. From the rim it appears as a dark green basin filled almost to capacity: roofs, trees, reed beds, stone walls, ponds, lanes, and pale threads of moving water pressed together with scarcely any bare ground between them. Cold mist rests over it in the morning. By afternoon the mist lifts, but the air remains wet enough to bead on bark and masonry.

No one built this city. It has the plan and density of one, but not the workmanship. Its avenues are animal tracks braided into broad corridors; its walls are young shelves of stone, newly raised by frost and the slow settling of saturated earth. Groves stand like districts. Dense thickets occupy the blocks between them. Water passes everywhere through gutters of root and gravel, gathering in courtyards of black mud before slipping beneath another wall.

The basin was formed recently. Its sides are still sharp in places, with raw cuts showing blue clay, shattered slate, and veins of ocher gravel. Springs have begun to weep from these wounds. There has not yet been time for rain and thaw to soften every edge, though plants are already attempting it. Moss advances over exposed rock in thick, luminous pads. Liverworts glaze the shaded banks. Willow and alder roots descend through fresh rubble and bind it before the next hard rain can move it.

The ground is cold even in the warmest season. Water stands close beneath the surface, so that a hoofprint fills almost as soon as it is made. In low quarters, peat floats underfoot. It bows and trembles rather than breaking. Pools lie among sedges, their surfaces dark, clear, and often filmed at the edges with a skin of new ice. Rocks sweat continually. Drops gather under ledges and fall at long intervals into the moss, each impact startlingly distinct.

The city’s tallest structures are trees, though few are ancient. White birches rise from raised islands, densely spaced and pale as clustered columns. Spruces occupy firmer ridges. Alders form tangled galleries along the waterways, their roots making chambers where trout shelter and frogs sleep through winter. Fallen trunks do not create openings for long. Ferns lift through them, fungi cover them, and seedlings pack every seam in the decaying wood.

Nothing here remains unoccupied. Lichens divide the exposed stone into gray, yellow, and green territories. Beetles inhabit the lichens. Mites inhabit the beetles’ discarded shells. Water striders patrol puddles no wider than a hand. Beneath the ponds are drowned grasses, caddisfly cases, snail tracks, fish, larvae, roots, and buried leaves compressed into future soil. Even the air seems populated: gnats drift in sheltered places, and tiny spiders travel on threads too fine to see except when condensation catches them.

Yet the basin is quiet.

Its abundance does not produce a great clamor. Cold slows the wings and voices of its inhabitants. Birds call singly from the thickets: a short whistle, an answer from far across the water, then silence again. Deer step carefully through flooded lanes. Otters surface without splashing and leave strings of bubbles beneath the duckweed. Foxes use the stone shelves as dry roads. In the upper reeds, voles move with a faint papery friction, while owls pass over them as softly as shadow.

Most sound comes from water, and even that is subdued. There is the thin running of springs through gravel, the occasional collapse of a muddy bank, rain ticking on leaves, and the hollow drip beneath overhangs. In thaw weather, ice makes small internal cracks. Wind rarely reaches the bottom of the basin with force. It crosses the rim, combs the crowns of the tallest trees, and descends in weakened currents that move the mist without disturbing the ponds.

The waterways provide the city’s order. Narrow channels join broader ones, converging toward a marshy central lake. Their banks resemble quays, though they are built only of layered roots and stones stranded by floods. Beaver dams interrupt several channels and redirect them through new districts each year. Behind the dams, drowned woods become ponds; beyond them, old ponds drain and turn to meadows. Thus whole neighborhoods appear and vanish without catastrophe. The city does not preserve its map.

There are crossings everywhere but no bridges made by hands. Roots arch over cuts in the ground. Logs lodge between banks. Stones crowd shallow runs closely enough for foxes and deer to pass. Smaller animals use tunnels beneath these crossings, and fish use the water below, so that several roads occupy the same place at once.

Rain is frequent and deliberate. It arrives first as a thickening of the air, then as droplets shaken from leaves before the clouds themselves begin. For hours the basin darkens under fine, steady precipitation. The exposed clay gleams. Channels rise by a finger’s depth. Moss swells, fungi straighten, and salamanders emerge onto the black stones. When the rain ends, every surface continues raining onto the surface beneath it. The canopy drains to shrubs, shrubs to herbs, herbs to moss, and moss to soil. It can take half a day for the last drops to reach the ground.

In winter, snow gathers deeply but unevenly. Warm springs open round windows through it, exposing green moss and dark water. Animals concentrate around these places. Their tracks overlap until the snow is written over completely: hare, fox, deer, mouse, grouse, otter. By late winter there is almost no untouched surface. When thaw comes, meltwater moves through the packed basin in many shallow fronts, lifting dead leaves, rotating stones, and cutting fresh channels into the young earth.

Because the basin is new, its life has the urgency of settlement. Seeds arrive on fur and in droppings. Reeds advance into open water. Willow stems root wherever floodwater lays them down. Landslips expose mineral soil, and within weeks the first green films appear. Predators follow prey along the expanding edges. Beavers alter watercourses; watercourses determine groves; groves shelter birds that distribute more seeds. Nothing directs the process, yet the result has streets, wards, boundaries, reservoirs, crowded dwellings, and constant maintenance.

From within, the basin feels much larger than it appears from above. Dense growth interrupts every view. A traveler may walk a short distance and pass from birch colonnades into a flooded alder quarter, then climb a bare ridge of fresh slate and look across miles of reed roofs silvered by rain. Beyond them rise dark spruce islands and the pale cliffs of the enclosing rim. There is always another district past the visible one.

No smoke rises. No metal rings. No wheel has cut these lanes, and no blade has trimmed the hedges. The closest thing to masonry is frost-broken stone stacked by gravity at the foot of slopes. The closest thing to a door is a gap beneath roots, polished by generations of bodies. The closest thing to a lamp is open water reflecting the ordinary sky.

At dusk, the basin does not become strange. It simply loses detail. Birches gray into the mist; pools retain a little light; reeds turn black at their bases. Frogs give a few low calls from the warmer springs. An owl moves between groves. Somewhere a beaver strikes the water once, and the sound travels farther than expected through the cold, packed landscape.

Then the place settles into its many small motions: seepage, respiration, hunting, rooting, freezing, decay. Almost nothing is still, but almost everything is quiet.
