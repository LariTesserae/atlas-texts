The high country is covered by a forest that has forgotten the distinction between organism, ground, and weather.

From the rim of the plateau it appears almost level: a dark green province extending between bare, frost-whitened peaks. Yet there is no soil beneath it, and very little stone. What looks like a valley floor is the upper surface of an immense vegetative accumulation—a woven depth of trunks, runners, root cables, corky bladders, leaf scales, and old compressed growth. The living layer fills ancient ravines and climbs their walls, spanning some narrower chasms outright. In places it is only a few meters thick. Elsewhere it descends through its own dead and sleeping strata for hundreds of meters before touching bedrock.

Nothing has been built here. Nevertheless, the landscape has an industrial character.

Across the plateau stand tall, leafless-looking growths with chambered stems. They draw air through slotted crowns and compress it as the mountain wind bends them. Pressure passes downward through woody valves, producing slow internal strokes. Vast root knots tighten and release. Sap is pumped uphill. Seed husks are stamped into identical hexagonal plates and shed by the thousand. Groves synchronize over distances, so that an entire slope may flex, blanch, and return to green in a single traveling wave.

There are no engines separate from the plants. The engines are anatomical.

The plateau is cold enough that exposed moisture freezes at night in every season. Rain comes infrequently, usually as brief diagonal showers driven in from the western escarpment. The forest captures nearly all of it. Leaves overlap like scales and guide droplets into hanging reservoirs buried within the canopy. Hollows that seem solid underfoot may contain black water beneath a skin of interlaced roots. Condensation is more dependable than rain: each morning, pale filaments rise from the foliage and comb moisture from passing fog. Their tips pearl with water, bow, and empty into channels no wider than veins.

Consequently the place is damp without seeming wet. Water is present everywhere in small custody—sealed inside translucent pods, trapped under bark, running through capillary grooves, frozen in the fur of stems—but open water is rare. A few long pools lie where the living mat has sagged into old river cuts. They are smooth, tannin-dark, and almost perfectly still even when the vegetation around them is in violent motion.

The surface has been weathered into stable forms over immense time. Prevailing winds have planed the western groves into ramps and wedges. Ice has split old boles along the same seams for generations, producing stair-like terraces of pale wood. Root rivers follow inherited courses through the plateau, their bends as settled as those of ancient streams. The forest is turbulent in the present but conservative in the long term: every convulsion repairs an old outline. What collapses into a hollow becomes the scaffold for the same ridge that stood there before.

Crossing it would be difficult, though not because it is impenetrably thick. There are open corridors between the denser stands—long, cold avenues floored with springy leaf felt. The difficulty is that these avenues move.

At irregular intervals, pressure builds beneath the mat. A distant region of canopy rises as if taking breath. Root cables pull taut; broad plates of vegetation tilt and overlap; fissures open, travel for kilometers, and close behind themselves. Stands that were separated meet and interlock. Others peel apart. Whole slopes creep downslope in abrupt increments, shedding curtains of old bark into the gullies. During the largest shifts, the green plain resembles a stormy sea viewed from above, though each “wave” is a hill of wood and foliage advancing with crushing patience.

Despite this activity, the country is quiet.

The moving growth does not crack or roar. Its fibers are supple, its joints padded with pith, its great internal machinery lubricated by resin and water. Close by, one can hear tiny sounds: condensation tapping into a cup; the faint tick of frost leaving a stem; a papery friction as millions of leaves turn their undersides to the wind. Farther away, turbulence becomes nearly soundless. Valleys alter shape without thunder. Acres fold over each other with only a deep pressure felt in the ribs, below the threshold of hearing.

Wind itself is muffled. The canopy receives it in layer after layer until it emerges as a cold breath smelling of crushed needles, minerals, and bitter green sap.

Animal life is scarce. There are a few white-bodied mites in the warm valve chambers, and thin burrowing things that spend years moving through the porous deadwood below. On the dark pools, one may occasionally see an insect standing motionless on the surface, its legs spread wide enough to distribute its weight. Birds visit only at the plateau’s margins. They do not nest. The forest’s pollination is accomplished by pressure pulses, airborne dust, and direct grafting whenever neighboring groves collide.

Many structures that resemble animals are, on inspection, temporary organs of the landscape. Pale knots crawl slowly along root cables, carrying concentrated sugars from one district to another. Tall jointed stalks uproot themselves during frost and step several meters toward better light before anchoring again. In storms, black seed vessels travel in coordinated streams through the canopy, giving the impression of herds crossing a distant ridge. None possesses an independent life. Severed from the greater tissue, each becomes inert within minutes.

Light is ordinary here, which makes the place stranger. Under clear midday skies, shadows fall cleanly and colors remain sober: fir green, lichen gray, old-wood silver, the dull burgundy of cold-stressed leaves. There is no pervasive glow and no supernatural haze. The sun reveals the landscape precisely enough to show that the hill on the horizon has changed position since morning.

At night, the plateau contracts.

Cold closes the air channels. Pumping groves slow and lock. Flexible surfaces stiffen under films of ice. For several hours the whole region approaches true stillness. Moisture gathers on every exposed filament, and the stars are reflected in thousands of small black reservoirs hidden among the leaves. This is the safest time to move, though the cold becomes severe and the frozen skin is treacherous.

Dawn initiates the daily upheaval. Sunlight reaches the eastern crownlands; chambers warm; trapped gases expand. A sequence of silent impulses travels westward. Leaf plates lift, condensation spills, stems uncoil, and the internal pressure network wakes in stages. What appears from a distance to be sunlight moving across the plateau is also the plateau responding to sunlight: green ridges standing up, valleys tightening, temporary clearings closing like wounds.

There is an order to it, though no fixed map can show that order. The place is divided not into territories but into processes. There are districts where water is harvested, districts where dead growth is digested, cold uplands devoted to seed manufacture, and deep central basins where heat from fermentation keeps the inner tissues from freezing. These zones migrate. Material circulates between them along thick vascular trunks laid over the surface like pipelines, branching and reconnecting whenever stress breaks the network.

Occasionally, excess pressure produces a bloom.

A dome rises over several days, kilometers across. The canopy stretches thin over it until light passes through the leaves, turning the entire swelling amber. Then the dome opens along radial seams. Warm vapor pours out, carrying spores, fragrant oils, and scraps of feather-light tissue high into the frozen air. For an hour the plateau is full of motion: streamers racing east, seed plates spinning, leaves reversing, distant groves bending to receive what comes. Then the cavity collapses. Vegetation slides inward, layering itself over the opening. By evening only a broad circular depression remains, already filling with condensation.

These events are the landscape’s factories, storms, and seasons at once. They dismantle one part of the forest to manufacture another. No overseer directs them. No artifact survives them. Their precision belongs to an ecology old enough to have turned repetition into anatomy.

At the outer escarpments, the living mat thins and its hidden depth becomes visible. Layers of former forests hang in bands: green above, then gold, brown, black, and finally pale compressed fiber hard as shale. Water seeps from these bands and freezes into short blue curtains. Root ends protrude into the open air, blindly flexing. Pieces occasionally detach and fall into the empty valleys below, where they shatter without echo in the snow.

From those lower valleys, the plateau looks like an elevated continent of vegetation—dense, calm, and permanent.

Only after watching for a long time does one notice that its horizon is breathing.
