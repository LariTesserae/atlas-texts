The plateau stands high enough that the country below has become an abstraction: a pale distance beyond escarpments, visible only where the rim has not been eaten back into coves and long, shallow bays of air. From above, the plateau seems nearly level. On foot, it is revealed as a dense congregation of old surfaces—stone tables, low ribs, blind channels, packed pillars, and shallow basins—every shape worn past sharpness.

Nothing here looks freshly broken. Even fallen blocks have rounded shoulders and dark, settled undersides. The rock has been exposed for so long that erosion no longer seems an active force. It has become a condition of the stone, as grain is a condition of wood. Thin beds of ocher limestone lie over purplish basalt, yet both have been reduced to similarly patient forms: domes low as sleeping animals, pavements crowded with cups, and columns narrowed at their middles by winds that have blown in much the same manner for ages.

Warm air rests over it all. At this elevation the sun is clear but not brutal, and the persistent upland wind draws heat away from exposed skin. The light is ordinary daylight—white at noon, honey-colored toward evening—but the age of the surfaces makes it appear unusually deep. Stone does not shine here. It absorbs brightness into skins of rust, black varnish, chalk, and gray-green lichen.

There is little vegetation, though the place never feels empty. Tough silver grass grows in protected seams, each clump isolated and combed in one direction. Succulents no larger than coins seal themselves into cracks. In the basins stand occasional crooked shrubs with papery bark and root systems far larger than their crowns. Their leaves remain folded most of the year. After rain they open within hours, turning scattered hollows briefly green, then bronze, then bare again.

Moisture seldom arrives as a generous storm. It condenses at night on the high stone and gathers under overhangs, where the temperature falls fastest. Before dawn, beads of water line the rims of shallow cups. Some cups overflow into others through grooves so old and subtle they resemble veins. By noon, most are dry.

A few deeper cavities retain water for weeks. These are dark, steep-sided pockets hidden among the stone thickets, their surfaces covered by a translucent film of pollen, shed wings, and mineral dust. The water beneath remains unexpectedly cold. Around each pool is a narrow ring of black mud crowded with tracks.

The animals are everywhere.

At first they are difficult to perceive because almost none of them moves quickly. The stone fields appear mottled only by weathering. Then a patch of lichen closes an eye. A rounded pebble unfolds six pale legs. A hairline crack proves to be the dark back of a long, jointed creature fitted exactly into the channel it occupies.

Small shelled grazers cover the shaded faces of the rocks by the thousands. Their shells reproduce the colors beneath them—red on ironstone, cream on limestone, soot-black beneath the varnished ledges—and they advance so slowly that a whole colony seems merely another mineral crust. They scrape invisible films from the rock. In still weather, if one kneels close, the combined feeding of multitudes can be heard as a faint dry hiss, like sand settling in a jar.

The open pavements belong to broad, low-bodied animals armored in overlapping plates. They bask with their limbs tucked away, indistinguishable from weathered slabs until their shadows lengthen. Each carries a shallow depression along its back. Dew collects there. Tiny flies drink from it; translucent mites breed along its edge; narrow-billed birds visit and pick delicately through the living debris. The armored animals neither encourage nor resist them. Some individuals are so old that lichens have spread across their plates and silver grass has rooted in accumulated dust near the base of the tail.

Between the pillars hang colonies of membrane-winged creatures, packed one beside another in curtains. Their wings are opaque and rough, more like dry leaves than skin. During the day they remain folded and silent, warmed by stone. At dusk, whole curtains detach almost imperceptibly and begin to drift through the channels. They do not beat their wings except to turn. The rising warmth of the plateau carries them. From a distance their migration resembles ash moving sideways through the air.

There are heavier beings as well. In the broad, shallow valleys, herds of tall animals proceed on cushioned feet. Their bodies are narrow, but each bears a branching crown of porous bone perforated by hundreds of holes. Wind passing through these crowns makes low, breathy tones. Because the animals seldom hurry and the winds are mild, the plateau’s most characteristic sound is not a call but an occasional chord: a herd standing among the stones, each head angled differently, the air sounding through them.

Even this is rare. Most hours are nearly quiet.

Predators lie in the basin rims, flat-bodied and sand-colored, eating only at long intervals. They do not chase. Their mouths are fringed with damp, bright tissue that smells of fresh water. Thirsty creatures approach, sometimes for days, pausing nearer each time. The predator waits until a muzzle touches the fringe. After feeding, it may remain in the same hollow for an entire season, gradually sinking under dust.

Nothing in this country appears to need urgency. Eggs take years to hatch in dry crevices. Larvae wait inside mineral sheaths for the vibration of rain. The tallest grazers may spend a month crossing a valley visible from end to end. Death is similarly unhurried. Bodies desiccate where they fall and become shelters, salt sources, nesting chambers, and finally pale outlines pressed into the ground by generations of feet.

The density of life is not expressed through lushness, but through occupancy. Every protected volume is inhabited. The undersides of flakes of stone hold sleeping coils. Hollow stems are divided into chambers by waxy partitions. Pools have layers: skaters on the surface, red threadlike swimmers below, blind shelled things in the silt, and beneath them porous mats of organisms that retreat deeper into the rock whenever the water shrinks. Even the wind-worn holes in the herd animals’ crowns are occupied by pale beetles that migrate from host to host when the crowns touch.

Scattered through the plateau are shapes that might, from a distance, be mistaken for ruins. Low walls cross some saddles. Ring-shaped enclosures occupy sheltered terraces. A few towers, heavily rounded and no higher than a tall animal, stand where valleys converge.

They were not built by hands.

The walls are accretions made by generations of mud-bearing insects. Each carries a grain or a pellet after rain, adding it to ridges whose original purpose—if purpose is the right word—has long disappeared. The rings are the exposed rims of immense subterranean nests, abandoned in their centers but still maintained at their edges by descendants that no longer enter the old chambers. The towers are fused cases left by colonial larvae, layer upon layer mineralized until weather can scarcely distinguish them from bedrock.

No tool has ever struck the plateau. No fire has been kindled deliberately. There are no roads, though regular migrations have polished certain passages smooth. There are no monuments, although living processes repeatedly produce monumental forms. The landscape has architecture without builders, paths without travelers who understand paths, reservoirs without keepers, and borders enforced only by scent, shade, inherited behavior, and the slow availability of water.

Near the plateau’s center rises a maze of close-packed stone fins. It is the densest and strangest part of the region. The fins stand only a few body-lengths apart, yet extend across many miles. Their tops have been worn down to a common height, so that seen from afar the maze appears to be a single dark plain. Below that false surface is a world of narrow corridors, chambers, vertical slots, and bridges.

Here nearly all life moves along the walls rather than the ground.

Snails travel in vertical migrations following the band of condensation. Small furred animals sleep adhered beneath ledges with their backs downward. Pale reptiles flatten themselves across warm faces and drink by pressing their bellies to wet stone. Dense clusters of chrysalis-like sacs hang in places untouched by direct sun. When the rains come—perhaps only several times in a year—the corridors fill with descending threads of water and awaken so many concealed organisms that the rock itself seems to loosen.

Yet the awakening is almost soundless. There is no riot of birdsong, no roaring flood. Water whispers down worn grooves. Membranes open. Mouthparts begin their work. Thousands of soft bodies emerge from seams and remain close to the surfaces that preserved them. Over several days the maze changes color, gray and ocher disappearing beneath films of violet, brown, pearl, and living black. Then the water withdraws. The creatures seal themselves away. Dust returns.

Along the western rim, shallow streams sometimes form after distant rain. They flow not across open ground but beneath rafts of animals gathered shoulder to shoulder in the channels. These dark living carpets trap silt, slow evaporation, and shelter aquatic young beneath their bellies. When the streams break into isolated pools, the rafts divide with them. Months later, when the last water is gone, the animals disperse uphill carrying eggs stuck in folds along their flanks. Thus the river survives by walking away from its bed.

The plateau is old beyond visible catastrophe. Whatever mountains once stood here have passed into sediment and wind. Whatever deep rivers cut the maze have diminished into dew and brief runoff. The surviving forms are not relics waiting to vanish, however. They are the final, intricate inhabitants of a land that has had immense time to remove everything poorly fitted to it.

At sunset the escarpment casts a shadow across the lower stone fields. The shadow advances slowly enough that winged colonies lift from one pillar after another in sequence. Armored backs darken. Grazers turn their perforated crowns into the cooling wind. A chord passes across the plateau, breaks, and reforms farther away.

Then condensation begins in the shaded cups.

One drop gathers, swells, and falls into a basin already crowded with waiting mouths.
