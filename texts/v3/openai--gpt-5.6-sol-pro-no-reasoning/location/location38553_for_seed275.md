Below the world’s mineral crust lies a city grown into an old, air-filled stratum.

It occupies a cavity so broad that its far side is usually lost in blue-gray distance. The ceiling hangs low only at the margins; toward the center it rises into a shallow dome, ribbed with pale stone and ancient fault lines. Nothing here has been worn smooth. The cavern is geologically young, or has only recently opened: planes of rock meet at sharp angles, crystals remain needle-edged, and whole terraces look freshly split from an unfinished block. Dust lies in the hollows but not in deep drifts. There has never been rain, never a stream, never the patient polishing of water.

The city compensates for that absence with life.

Its ground is not soil but a continuous, cool vegetal mat: densely braided roots, cork, fungal-looking plant tissue, and tough translucent membranes stretched over the raw rock. It gives slightly underfoot. In open districts the mat is only a hand thick, revealing the shapes of rubble beneath it; under buildings it descends through fissures in cables as thick as train tunnels. Roads are broad strips of hardened bark, repeatedly compressed, scored, and resin-sealed. Sidewalks are pale cambium. Curbs rise and heal. Where a road is damaged, its exposed fibers slowly draw together unless wedges are driven into the wound.

Although plant matter covers almost every surface, the city does not feel lush. There is no wetness, no green haze, no crowded forest. Growth is distributed through great areas of emptiness. A pillar-tree may stand alone in a plaza a kilometer wide, its crown spread flat beneath the cavern ceiling. Rows of narrow stems define abandoned industrial lots. Immense leaves, each the size of a roof, are spaced along overhead vines with dozens of meters of bare cable between them. The dominant colors are chalk white, old ivory, soot-black, muted violet, and the cold green of oxidized metal.

These plants do not use liquid water. Their chemistry is based on dry mineral salts, ammonia trapped in waxy tissues, and gases drawn from deep vents. Fine root hairs enter cracks in the rock and exchange crystals with it, dissolving nothing. They loosen individual ions by electrical charge and pass them inward through branching conductive fibers. A cut stem does not bleed. It exhales a white powder and seals beneath a film of amber resin.

The cavern is lit as evenly as an overcast afternoon. High on the dome, colonies of thin reflective fronds turn toward banks of industrial lamps. The lamps themselves are housed in black iron galleries, fed by generators deep below. Their light is redistributed through miles of living optical tissue: translucent trunks, leaf mirrors, and taut strands that shine softly along their length. There are few shadows except under machinery. Day and night are municipal decisions. At the end of each work period, the ceiling fades through gray to a dull plum twilight, district by district.

Buildings rise from the vegetal floor in organized but unfamiliar forms. Some were constructed in the ordinary sense: steel frames, ceramic walls, riveted ducts, freight elevators. Others were trained. Tower trunks were bound into scaffolds until their tissues thickened around the beams; now the beams remain inside them like bones. Apartment blocks consist of stacked seed chambers, their partitions grown from fibrous panels. Warehouses have roofs of overlapping shell-leaves. Narrow factories hang beneath load-bearing branches, and their chimneys pierce the cavern ceiling to reach gas pockets in higher strata.

The city is extensive but discontinuous. Dense knots of architecture gather around power stations, transit exchanges, and root wells, while large tracts between them remain nearly empty. From an elevated railway one passes lit residential tiers, then several minutes of open bark plain, then a lone refinery wrapped in white vines, then another settlement glowing under the ribs of the ceiling. The spaces are deliberate. Plant growth must be kept apart to prevent incompatible root systems from recognizing each other as competitors and beginning a slow subterranean war.

Machinery is everywhere, though seldom loud.

Factories comb mineral dust from the air, sort it by electrostatic charge, and pack it into dry nutrient bricks. Presses laminate bark into structural panels. Long walking engines prune the underside of the ceiling, moving on six padded legs so they do not injure the living roads. Conveyor vines, stimulated by timed electrical pulses, carry crates by muscular contraction. Their movement is almost too gradual to see; cargo simply appears farther along whenever one looks away.

The loudest events are infrequent: the clank of a freight coupling, the cough of a gas turbine, or the deep wooden report of a tower expanding inside its steel braces. Otherwise the city is filled with restrained noises. Leaves tick as they rotate toward the lamps. Dry seed husks skitter across plazas. Root-switches click beneath the ground, directing nutrients to one district or another. In industrial quarters there is a low electrical hum, more felt in teeth than heard. Voices travel far in the cool, still air, so inhabitants speak softly.

Animals are abundant without being conspicuous. Most are small, pale, and adapted to open distances. Six-legged grazers move in scattered files across the plazas, shaving films from the bark with black glassy mouthparts. Paper-thin flying creatures coast between light towers, steering with broad tails rather than wings. Warm-bodied burrowers inhabit vacant seed chambers and emerge at artificial dusk to gather salt nodules. On walls, slow predators imitate knots in the wood. They may remain motionless for weeks, then unfold all at once around passing prey.

Larger animals inhabit the transit margins. Long, sparsely furred browsers stand beneath the ceiling fronds and strip them with forked tongues. Their bones contain buoyant gas chambers, allowing them to balance on four delicate feet despite their size. Maintenance crews tolerate them because they eat diseased tissue. Packs of smaller hunters follow at a distance, communicating through vibrations transmitted into the living floor. One can see them converge without hearing a sound.

There is weather, though there is no rain. Each artificial morning the lamps warm the upper cavern and drive a slow convection cycle. Cold gases descend at the perimeter and move inward as a shallow, powder-bearing wind. By midday, vertical drafts climb around the towers. At evening, volatile compounds released by millions of leaves form a faint aromatic fog—not moisture, but suspended oils and crystalline pollen. It smells of cedar, hot wire, and stone struck with a hammer. Static discharges flicker blue between high branches. Occasionally a pollen storm passes through, whitening machinery and stopping exposed gears.

The city’s engineers are also gardeners, and the distinction has largely vanished. Every utility is at least partly alive. Power cables contain conductive sapwood. Ventilation shafts open and close with seasonal growth rings. Public clocks are flowers whose numbered bracts unfold in sequence. Waste is fed into pale digestion orchards, where heat and useful gases are extracted over months. Fire is treated with particular fear: without water, crews fight it by collapsing air bladders, releasing smothering powders, and ordering nearby streets to shed their combustible outer bark.

Expansion is continuous. At the eastern frontier, the cavern is still being made habitable. Stone-cutting organisms are seeded into hairline faults, where they grow mineral wedges and force the rock apart grain by grain. Behind them come bracing trees, then gas scrubbers, then lamps. New districts begin as sparse lines scratched across sharp rubble. A road takes root; a station swells beside it; scaffold frames arrive on silent trains. For years the place remains raw, with fresh stone glittering between patches of living substrate. Only later do walls thicken, roofs overlap, and the municipal root web close beneath the streets.

This is why the whole landscape feels both ancient and unfinished. The plants may be older than the city, but they are always manufacturing it. Buildings add rings. Foundations crawl. Neighborhood boundaries drift by centimeters each year. Surveyors hammer ceramic markers into the ground, only to find them swallowed and displaced by spring growth.

Far out in the open districts, beyond the last regular tram stop, the lights continue. They diminish into the cavern’s blue distance in orderly constellations: a pumping house, a root nursery, a lone switching tower, the red eye of a kiln. Between them stretch miles of pale living ground and untouched angular stone. Herds cross these spaces. Freight vines pulse. New cracks open in the ceiling with small, clean sounds.

The city does not stand inside the cavern as something separate.

It is the current shape taken by the cavern’s vegetation: a cool, dry bloom of streets, engines, habitats, and patient mineral hunger, still opening beneath a world that may never know it is there.
