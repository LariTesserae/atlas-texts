The city grows in a low, warm basin where no ground is visible.

There may once have been soil beneath it, but that is conjecture. Every surface now belongs to the vegetation: braided roots underfoot, leaf vaults overhead, trunks pressed into walls, walls budding into stairs and terraces. There is no masonry, no worked wood, no artifact that can be separated cleanly from the living mass. Even the broad avenues are plant tissue—pale, firm ribbons of rind that have flattened themselves between districts of towering stems.

From a distance the basin looks like an unbroken green-black plain interrupted by swollen towers. Up close, it resolves into a dense city of botanical architectures. Buttress roots form plazas. Hollow trunks open into chambers stacked dozens high. Branches inosculate across the streets, fusing into bridges with balustrades of thorns. Curtains of aerial roots divide courtyards and arcades. Great leaves overlap as roofs, their gutters channeling brief rain into translucent bladders suspended beneath the eaves.

Nothing here was built with tools. The city nevertheless has neighborhoods, intersections, drainage channels, public spaces, and structures resembling gates. It grows as though urban planning were an instinct shared among thousands of species—or as though the entire basin were one plant thinking in incompatible styles.

It is deafening.

Sound comes first from growth. Fibers strain and part with reports like splitting timber. Seedpods detonate continuously in the canopy, firing clouds of dust and winged seeds down the avenues. Vines tighten around columns with a long, rope-deep groan. New shoots drive through older tissues in sharp, percussive bursts. Vast leaves snap open one after another, their unfolding passing over the city like thunder rolling across canvas.

Beneath these noises lies the hydraulic roar of sap. In some trunks it pulses slowly enough to count, a concussion every few breaths. In others it rushes without pause, making whole districts hum. Flowers shaped like bells amplify the pressure changes inside their stems and produce booming notes too low to be heard cleanly; they are felt through the ribs and teeth. When rain comes, even lightly, the city erupts. Millions of cupped leaves drum at different pitches, water cascades through petal chutes, and swollen pods whistle as air is displaced from them.

There are no birds, no insects, no grazing things, no worms beneath the root mats. Nothing crawls, swims, or flies except what plants cast off. Pollination is accomplished by violence: flowers clap together in the wind, or launch packets of pollen across open spaces, or extend long, searching stamens that touch distant blooms. Dispersal belongs to pressure, heat, rain, gravity, and the city’s own convulsions. The absence of animals does not make the place still. It makes the plants seem to occupy every ecological role at once.

Some flowers hunt shadows. Some roots migrate.

Moisture is intermittent. Most days are warm, bright, and heavy, with a thin haze rising from the transpiring basin. Condensation gathers in shaded interiors and runs down the waxy walls in slow threads. Water collects in scattered cistern-plants: deep cups large enough to resemble ponds, green-rimmed and dark inside. Their surfaces are empty of life. No larvae twitch there. No drinking creature disturbs them. They reflect the shifting architecture until the reservoirs contract and seal themselves against the heat.

A river crosses the basin, though not always in the same place. It does not flow over stone. It travels through a trough formed by roots that continually loosen, knot, and redirect it. In dry periods the river is drawn entirely into the city. The channel becomes a long crease of damp bark, warm to the touch, smelling of minerals and bruised leaves. After rain, water bursts back through pores and root mouths, sometimes emerging miles from its former course. Streets flood ankle-deep; plazas become shallow mirrors; then thirsty tissues lift the water away so quickly that droplets can be watched climbing hairlike capillaries against gravity.

The city is young, despite its immensity. Its edges are raw. Towers stand in fresh splits of their own discarded husks. Root terraces end in sharp, pale faces not yet stained by weather. New districts heave upward overnight, breaking the low plain into ridges and jagged folds. There has not been time for decay to soften anything. Dead growth is rapidly enclosed, digested, or used as scaffolding. Where a tower has fallen, its ruin remains recognizable only for minutes before runners stitch across it and leaves turn its cavities into rooms.

The urban pattern is therefore temporary. A boulevard visible at dawn may narrow by noon as its bordering trunks inflate. A bridge becomes a wall when its branches descend and take root. A chamber that admitted sunlight closes like a fist; elsewhere, a trunk tears itself open and reveals a bright atrium ribbed with wet wood. There are processional ways leading to no permanent center. Doorways migrate around buildings. Stairs alter the height and number of their steps while being climbed—not in response to a traveler, for travelers do not belong here, but as a consequence of relentless internal revisions.

The city has no inhabitants distinct from itself.

Its dwellings may be reproductive organs. Its plazas may be exposed membranes for exchanging gases. The long arcades may exist to distribute strain when the basin shifts. What resembles ornament—spirals of red tendrils, facades plated in overlapping scales, rows of pale nodules—is functional in ways that refuse ordinary categories. A balcony flowers. A tower sheds its upper stories as seeds. Whole blocks turn their leaves at once, redirecting the daylight into neighboring blocks until the streets glow with a green, submarine radiance.

Then the impossible changes begin.

Perspective is unreliable in the deeper districts. An avenue may appear a few hundred paces long but contain an afternoon of distance. Two parallel walls meet repeatedly without converging. Courtyards open inside leaves smaller than the entrances through which they are reached. Looking upward between the trunks sometimes reveals not the sky but a second basin hanging overhead, its root towers extending downward, trembling just beyond contact with those below.

These are not illusions created by heat. The vegetation does not merely occupy space; it produces and prunes it.

Growth rings pass outward through the air as visible distortions. When one crosses a street, nearby structures acquire additional histories: a sapling becomes an ancient pillar for several seconds, layered with scars from seasons that never occurred, then snaps back to translucent youth. Seeds fall upward into previous versions of their flowers. Shadows detach from leaves and continue photosynthesizing on the ground. At certain intersections, rain arrives before the clouds, droplets rising from dry surfaces and hanging overhead until the weather catches up.

The city tolerates contradictions as readily as a tree tolerates branches.

In one district, all stems lean away from a central vacant lot. Nothing grows within the lot, yet the emptiness has roots: thick negative forms that push through surrounding walls, visible only where living matter bends around them. The roar there is muted, not through quiet but through an excess of sound that cancels itself. A fallen leaf crossing the boundary vanishes from sight while remaining physically present; the roots beneath it bow beneath its weight.

Elsewhere, a forest of columnar blossoms continuously constructs and destroys a skyline. Each flower takes only moments to rise. Its stem telescopes upward, hardens, opens into a many-roomed crown, releases a flare of luminous pollen, and collapses into mulch. Hundreds do this at once. The district boils with architecture. Streets appear between falling towers and are buried seconds later. Through it all, runner-vines move across the debris in coordinated bands, laying down provisional bridges before the next growth surge tears them apart.

The tallest structures are visible from across the basin. They are not trees so much as negotiations among trees: thousands of trunks fused into tapered, perforated mountains. Their surfaces carry hanging gardens dense enough to be landscapes in themselves. Cloudlike masses of spores collect around their summits. In the afternoon warmth, the towers flex. When one bends, the skyline bends with it. Distance puckers; neighborhoods are drawn suddenly close, then released. The resulting shock travels through the root plain in waves, making every leaf flash its pale underside.

At intervals, the entire city performs a change too large to understand locally.

The warning is a deepening of the sap-roar. Reservoir plants seal. The river retracts into its roots. Leaves turn edge-on to the sun, and for a brief moment the dense basin becomes strangely transparent, revealing layer behind layer of trunks stretching toward the horizon.

Then the root mat contracts.

Plazas buckle into ridges. Towers slide past one another without breaking, their tissues liquefying at points of contact and fusing afterward. Streets braid together. Whole districts migrate slowly across the basin, propelled by waves of root growth. What had been east becomes enclosed beneath what had been west. The overhead basin, if it is visible, twists in the opposite direction. The noise exceeds thunder: a continuous, world-filling crackle of splitting fibers, explosive pods, rushing water, and leaf surfaces slamming together.

Rain often follows, though it may fall sideways or in local columns bounded as sharply as walls. It washes the exposed tissues, fills the cups, and gathers briefly in warm pools among the roots. Within hours the city drinks nearly all of it. Steam lifts through the avenues. New green skins spread over the wounds.

Afterward, the place is recognizably itself but impossible to map. The same towers may remain, though their interiors lead elsewhere. The same bridges may cross different gaps. A familiar plaza may now stand vertically along the side of a trunk, its fountain-shaped flower spilling pollen into the sky.

Near sunset the turbulence does not cease, but light makes it legible for a while. The low sun enters beneath the upper canopy and catches every vein, tendril, hair, and drifting seed. The city becomes translucent in layers: amber sap behind red membranes, green chambers behind violet bark, pale roots moving under the rind of the streets. There is beauty in its impossible density, but no invitation. No path has been left for feet. No fruit has evolved to be eaten. No flower seeks an eye.

Night is warm and almost dark. A few blossoms shine with stored daylight, enough to show the avenues closing and reopening. Condensation begins to tick from the high leaves. Towers murmur with fluid pressure. Somewhere beyond sight, a district overturns itself with a sound like an ocean collapsing.

By morning there will be more city.

Not expansion exactly. The basin is already full.

There will simply be more of it inside the same space.
