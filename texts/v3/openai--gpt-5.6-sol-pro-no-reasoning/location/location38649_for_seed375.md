The city occupies a shallow basin no wider than a hamlet, though the word *city* remains inescapable. It has avenues, courts, covered passages, towers, thresholds, and districts nested inside districts. Yet nothing here was built by hands.

Its walls are columns of pale mineral that rose recently from the ground, extruded in rows as precise as streets. They have not had time to soften. Their corners are keen; their surfaces retain flow lines like cooling wax, and where a column split during formation, the fracture shines black and glassy. Narrow blocks stand between empty lanes. Archways have grown where neighboring columns leaned together and fused. Thin roofs span some alleys, not laid there but precipitated across the gaps one crystalline layer at a time.

The whole settlement could fit inside a great cathedral. Its tallest spires rise perhaps thirty meters, while most structures reach only twice the height of a person. Beyond its last wall lies a level salt-gray waste without landmark or apparent end. Thus the smallness of the city feels deliberate: a dense thought surrounded by an almost perfect absence.

There is no water. There has never been rain in the basin, nor frost, dew, buried ice, or dampness sealed in stone. The chemistry of the place is carried instead by a heavy, invisible vapor that seeps upward through hairline faults. It is not quite air. It moves reluctantly and pools in low courts, where it reacts with exposed minerals and causes them to swell.

This is how the city grows.

Growth comes in episodes. For long intervals everything is still. Then the basin floor tightens with no audible tremor. A curb rises along an avenue. Six-sided tiles push out of bare ground. A blank wall acquires shallow recesses resembling doors, all too narrow to enter. Sometimes an entire arcade appears over the course of an hour, each pillar emerging simultaneously, pale and sharp, lifting a roof that hardens above them. No debris is produced. The new architecture simply displaces empty space.

Despite this ceaseless construction, the city is nearly vacant. Its breadth is mostly hollow chambers and unoccupied streets. One can look down a long avenue and see nothing moving, though movement is taking place everywhere in increments too small for the eye: crystals lengthening, seams closing, facades bowing inward, courtyards slowly changing shape.

The temperature remains cool, with a dry cold that never bites deeply but enters whatever stands still. Light comes from the sky as a diffuse violet-gray illumination. There is no visible sun. Shadows are weak beneath open air and unexpectedly dark inside arches, as though the stone consumes more light than its color should allow. At irregular intervals the vapor in the lower streets becomes faintly luminous, sketching the road plan in tarnished green. Whole districts then seem submerged in radiance, though there is nothing liquid about it.

Life persists, but sparsely.

Along north-facing seams grow low fans of black tissue, neither leaf nor fungus in any familiar sense. Each fan is thin as paper and ribbed with metallic salts. It feeds on the difference between the cold wall and the reactive vapor passing over it. The colonies are widely separated: one beneath a lintel, three in a sunken court, a dark fringe around a vent. Their edges curl and uncurl through a cycle lasting several days.

Elsewhere stand the spindle groves. These are clusters of ivory stalks, seldom more than a dozen together, rooted directly into fresh mineral. They have no branching limbs. At their tips, translucent bladders inflate when the underground vapor rises and collapse when it retreats. In the dimness they resemble small lamps that have forgotten how to shine. Their spores are dense grains, and because there is almost no wind, they do not drift. The stalks fling them. At rare intervals a bladder snaps inward and shoots a single seed across a plaza. It strikes a wall with a tiny click.

That click may be the loudest ordinary sound in the city.

There are no footfalls unless something has come to make them, no rustle of leaves, no running water, no insects humming in the air. Even the geological activity is silent. Walls rise without grinding. Cracks advance without report. The vapor passes without a sigh. Silence here does not signify inactivity; it is the medium in which activity occurs.

The animals are few and difficult to distinguish from pieces of architecture. Flat, jointed creatures cling inside cornices, matching the white stone. They eat the black fans by lowering threadlike mouths, leaving neat circular absences in the colonies. When threatened, they release their grip and fall rather than flee. Their bodies are so light and the vapor so dense that they descend slowly, turning once or twice before settling on the street.

Something hunts them.

Its tracks sometimes appear as paired cuts in newly formed tiles, proceeding from one sealed doorway to another. No one sees the maker crossing open ground. In deep courts, one may find a shed sheath: clear, tubular, longer than an arm, with too many folded joints. The sheath begins to mineralize almost immediately. Within a day it resembles decorative molding. Within a month it is part of the building.

This incorporation is the city’s central rule. Nothing remains foreign for long.

A fallen stalk becomes a buttress. Spores trapped in a fissure determine the shape of a future window. Animal sheaths turn into ridges, ribs, or stairs. Even the rare dust drifting in from the outer waste is gathered electrostatically along walls and sealed beneath a new glaze. The city does not decay so much as edit itself. Because it is young, its revisions remain legible: pale additions over dark foundations, sudden corridors that end in raw stone, stairways whose lower steps have not yet emerged.

Some streets are passable in the violet phase of the sky and closed by the next dim cycle. Alleys narrow as their walls feed. Rooms split into smaller rooms. Door-shaped apertures migrate from one side of a court to another, not by moving but by sealing behind and opening ahead. There is no stable map.

Nor is the city expanding evenly. Its outer perimeter stays almost fixed while the interior becomes more intricate. It builds inward, subdividing absence. Each new wall creates two emptier spaces from one. Each colonnade organizes a vacancy. From above, the settlement might look like a compact mineral labyrinth attempting to use up all the open space within itself.

At its center is the largest unbuilt place: a circular court about forty paces across. The paving there is dark, fresh, and perfectly smooth. Nothing grows on it. Vapor does not pool there, and the dim green luminescence halts at its rim. Surrounding towers incline very slightly away from the circle, although elsewhere every structure leans toward its neighbor.

The court changes more quickly than any other district, but only at its boundary. Each cycle, a new ring of fine teeth rises around it. Then the previous ring folds outward and becomes walls, passages, arcades, whole miniature neighborhoods. The open circle does not shrink. It generates architecture while preserving its emptiness.

In the deepest dimness, the paving lifts by the breadth of a finger and settles again. The movement travels across the court in patterns: first a spiral, then branching lines, then a geometry like streets seen from impossible height. No sound accompanies it. The sparse organisms retract into seams. The pale hanging animals flatten against the stone. The unseen hunter ceases leaving tracks.

Then, throughout the little city, thousands of narrow apertures open at once.

Cold reactive vapor rises from them in straight columns. Black fans spread to full width. Spindle bladders inflate. Fresh walls bloom with crystals. Arches thicken, chambers divide, and new towers begin to push above the roofs. The whole place enters motion without ever breaking its silence.

Beyond the perimeter, the salt waste remains unchanged and bare. Inside, the city builds furiously around an emptiness it cannot fill—or is careful not to.
