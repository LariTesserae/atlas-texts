The city hangs beneath the warm sea.

It occupies the drowned summit of a mountain not yet fully born: a broad, rising dome of pale stone whose upper ridges stand thousands of fathoms above the abyssal floor, though still far below the moving skin of the ocean. From the distant deep it resembles an island arrested before emergence. Its peaks are blunt and soft-looking, split by fresh seams, with terraces that have lifted faster than currents could carve them. Nothing here has had time to weather. Cliffs show no scree and valleys hold no old sediment. The mountain’s surfaces are raw—cream, sulfur-yellow, faintly pink—laid down in swollen folds as mineral-rich water seeps through them.

Warmth rises continuously from below. It does not erupt in black towers or violent vents. It comes as a pervasive fever in the rock, warming the surrounding water until the whole submerged highland lies in a mild, dim bath. At certain cracks the sea shimmers, warped by slow gradients of heat and salt. Clear fluids bead from pores in the stone and creep upward like oil, carrying minerals that harden into translucent rims. New ground is always appearing by increments: a ledge thickening during a generation, a chimney growing the width of a hand in a year, a stair sealed under milky deposits and remade beside itself.

The settlement covers several adjoining heights and the shallow saddles between them. There are neighborhoods, reservoirs, cultivated groves, roadways, shrines, quarries, and empty ceremonial squares. Yet almost nothing is built from blocks. The inhabitants possess no furnaces hot enough for true metallurgy, and underwater mortar would be of little use. Instead they persuade the mountain to build for them.

Walls begin as fences of bone pegs and woven fiber erected around a mineral seep. Over years, pale stone accumulates along the weave. The builders prune it with shell adzes, redirect the flow through clay tubes, and add new latticework where they want arches or chambers. A mature house is therefore half masonry and half reef: swollen columns, soft corners, doorways narrowed by deposition and repeatedly cut open. Tool marks remain as pearly scratches. Some rooms are no older than their residents; others have been growing for centuries and contain earlier rooms entirely engulfed in their walls.

The city’s principal avenues are buoyant causeways. Ropes as thick as trunks stretch from pinnacle to pinnacle, supporting woven mats, suspended gardens, baskets, lamps, and small dwellings. The causeways bow gently in the currents. Travelers pull themselves along them hand over hand, or hook on with carved toggles and let the water carry their bodies horizontally above the streets. There are no wheels. There are sledges for heavy stone, paddled barges for porous cargo, and harnesses for towing, all made from wood, shell, hide, fired clay, and braided plant cord.

Vertical distance matters more than horizontal distance. Addresses are spoken as depths. Wealthier houses occupy the warm middle terraces, close enough to the mineral springs for comfort but far enough that deposits do not choke their doors. The highest district is cooler and dimmer in a bluish way, exposed to broad currents sweeping over the summit. Below the lowest retaining walls, streets end abruptly above open black water.

The sea presses everywhere, but it does not feel empty. It is densely furnished.

Forests grow upside down from rafts anchored over the city. Their plants have broad, dark leaves filled with gas, each leaf straining upward while pale roots hang beneath in curtains. Other groves cling directly to warm rock: feathered fronds, waxy red cups, black ribbons with gold-veined edges, and thick translucent bulbs that pulse slowly as internal bladders adjust to pressure. The gardens are lush without being green. Most vegetation is burgundy, soot-brown, ivory, or the deep violet of bruised fruit. Green appears chiefly near the summit, where a residue of daylight filters down on clear days.

Long strands cross the open plazas from wall to wall. Gardeners tie edible bulbs along them, making lines of suspended produce that resemble strings of dim lanterns. Root curtains divide courtyards and trap drifting nutrients. In some alleys they have become so dense that passage requires pushing through warm, rubbery folds. Pollen does not fly here. It uncoils in threads, drifts a few arm-lengths, and knots itself around receptive branches.

Almost all plant life participates in the architecture. Fibrous species brace growing walls. Spongewood is cultivated for beams and tools. Hollow reeds carry fresh hot seep-water through colder districts, while braided root pipes draw foul water downward into beds of filter organisms. Seed chambers are built into public towers so that buoyant seeds, when released, rise through a sequence of nets and can be sorted by size.

The city has few animals.

Tiny pale grazers stipple the gardens, and minute silver things flash inside root masses, but they are easy to mistake for bubbles or loose flakes of mineral. Larger creatures arrive only rarely. Once in a season, perhaps, a broad shadow crosses above the summit and every suspended garden leans in its wake. Once in a lifetime, something from the abyss ascends along the mountain flank: blind, slow, and luminous at the joints. Such visitors are neither hunted nor approached. Bells are muffled. Doors are tied shut. People remain inside until the lights descend again.

Because fauna are scarce, there are few familiar sea sounds. No chorusing shoals, no constant snapping of shells, no distant calls. The city is mostly quiet. Sound travels too well for carelessness. A hammer blow may pass through three districts. A falling jar announces itself across a valley. Conversation is conducted at close range, with low throat tones and signs of the hands.

The persistent noises are soft and structural: rope fibers creaking under tension; gas leaves rubbing together far overhead; the delicate crack of mineral crust thickening around a warm spring. In the oldest houses, one can hear walls growing—a faint succession of clicks as crystals settle into place. Currents hum through perforated towers. At intervals, a pressure door flexes with a deep wooden groan, and the whole neighborhood pauses to listen for a second one.

Artificial light is scarce. Fire cannot live here, and luminous animals are too rare to domesticate. People cultivate slow-glowing films in clay bowls, feeding them ground root and mineral salts. The films produce amber, rust-red, or weak green light, enough to reveal faces but not ceilings. Important streets are marked by polished white stones rather than lamps. Travelers navigate by touch, by gradients of warmth, and by the taste of water passing across the tongue.

Public buildings are identified through texture. The archive has ridged walls. The healers’ chambers are covered in smooth overlapping scales of ceramic. The council house can be found in absolute darkness because its columns have spiral grooves turning in opposite directions. Maps are woven as knot patterns and carried around the waist. Children learn the city first as a sequence of handholds.

Pressure and buoyancy alter every ordinary rule of settlement. Floors are useful but ceilings are equally inhabited. Shelves surround rooms in complete rings. Beds are nets stretched through the center of chambers. Loose objects must be tethered. A broken vessel does not fall; it spills upward, downward, and sideways according to the density of what it held. Dust does not gather in corners. It hangs in dim clouds until filter fans—broad mats worked by hand—draw it away.

The people shape clay in weighted frames and fire it in sealed geothermal kilns, where hot rock and carefully trapped gases harden small batches over many days. Their ceramics are precious, dark, and uneven. Stone tools are common, especially glassy blades quarried from young seams along the mountain’s eastern shoulder. Metal occurs as native flecks beaten cold into hooks, needles, and ceremonial plates, but never in sufficient quantity for walls, weapons, or machinery.

Their greatest technology is patience.

A canal may take thirty years to grow. A civic tower begins as a design laid out by one generation and is occupied by the grandchildren of the builders. Doors are deliberately made too large, because their mineral rims will close inward. Reservoirs start as cages of woven root that slowly calcify into basins. Construction plans include not only shape but maturity: how a building should look in ten years, in fifty, and at the point when it must be abandoned to stone.

Nothing erodes quickly here. The water is warm and persistent but strangely gentle, and the new rock has not yet acquired scars. Instead of wearing down, the landscape accumulates. Steps blur under crystal skins. Canals narrow. Statues become faceless not because they are rubbed away but because they are covered. The past survives inside successive shells.

At the heart of the city lies the Unfinished Square, an expanse almost level, bounded by twelve low mineral mounds. Warm water seeps through thousands of pinholes in its floor. Fine white deposits gather wherever a foot, tool, or basket interrupts the flow, recording each temporary presence in a delicate crust. Every few months the citizens scrape the square bare with shell blades. No building is allowed there. It is maintained as an absence, a place where the mountain’s intention remains visible before it is redirected into walls.

Above the square hangs a vast cultivated canopy. Its buoyant trunks cannot rise because they are anchored to stone pylons, so they bend and spread sideways, forming a suspended woodland over the district. Roots descend in columns but stop short of the ground. Dim glows move through the tissues in rhythms lasting days. No one is certain whether these are chemical tides or something like thought.

The farther one travels from the center, the less clearly the city ends. Formal roads become garden cords, then quarry lines, then lone handholds hammered into fresh stone. Outlying houses merge with mineral terraces. Beyond them stand experimental structures: empty lattices placed around new seeps, awaiting walls that do not yet exist. Some resemble towers, some shells, some enormous rib cages. Their builders may never see them finished.

Past the last lattice, the mountain slopes away through forests of dark ribbons. Below, warmth fades and the water opens into immeasurable night. Above, the summit rises toward a remote brightness that no living citizen has reached. Bubbles released from the highest vents travel upward for hours and disappear.

The city’s oldest belief holds that the mountain is still deciding whether to become land. Its slow uplift can be measured against carved markers. Each generation finds the summit fractionally nearer to the sunlit surface, though the distance remains enormous. Some say that one day towers will break into air, gardens will collapse under their own weight, and the inhabitants will have to learn fire, falling, weather, and the terrible thinness of sky.

Others consider this a childish fear. To them, the ocean is not something covering the world. It is the substance in which the world is being made. The young mountain, the growing houses, the hanging forests, and the citizens themselves are temporary arrangements inside one warm body of water—dense with unfinished forms, rising so slowly that motion can be mistaken for permanence.
