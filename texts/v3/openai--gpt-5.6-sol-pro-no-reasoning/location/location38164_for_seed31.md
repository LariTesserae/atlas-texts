The city occupies a high, broad shelf beneath a line of newly risen mountains. The peaks are not yet softened into familiar forms. Their ridges remain sharp, with exposed planes of dark stone and pale fractures that catch the daylight. Whole slopes look freshly split rather than weathered. Loose scree gathers beneath them in angular fans, but little has traveled far. There are no deep gorges, no rounded boulders, no mature river valleys. Water has found the land only recently and still moves over its surface as if testing possible routes.

The shelf itself is nearly level, an open expanse several valleys wide. Low swells of rock rise from it, separated by shallow basins filled with clear water, reed beds, and dark soil. Rain falls often, usually without violence. It comes as a warm, steady curtain from a ceiling of gray-white cloud, beads on stone, and drains along the joints of the mountains. Even when rain is not falling, water remains everywhere: trembling on broad leaves, weeping from rock faces, collecting beneath paving slabs, condensing along cool walls. The air smells of wet mineral dust, clay, and crushed green stems.

Across this plateau stands a sparse city.

Its districts do not press together. Each cluster of buildings occupies a dry rise or a tongue of exposed stone, with stretches of marsh, meadow, shallow water, and bare pavement between. Seen from the mountain slopes, the settlement resembles a loose arrangement of islands linked by causeways. There are perhaps enough structures for a city, but enough empty ground to make it feel contemplative rather than crowded.

The buildings are made mostly of fitted stone, clay brick, timber, woven reed, and lime-colored plaster. Metal is rare and used only in small blades, hooks, and pins. Doors turn on wooden pivots. Roof beams are lashed or pegged. Water channels are lined with flat slabs chosen by hand, their seams packed with moss and clay. Nothing depends on hidden machinery. The city’s complexity lies in patient arrangement: gradients measured with bowls of water, walls aligned by cord, foundations raised one stone course above the known wet line.

Most buildings are low, with thick walls and wide roof overhangs. Their roofs are tiled in fired clay or densely thatched with marsh reeds. Courtyards open to the ordinary sky. Rain jars stand beneath carved spouts, and narrow gutters pass through walls into communal cisterns. On the highest rises are long public halls with pillared fronts, storage houses, open markets, and square platforms where fires can be lit beneath rain shelters. A few stepped towers rise above the rest, built not for defense but for sighting storms and surveying the waterways.

The roads are broad but few. Near inhabited clusters they consist of fitted slabs, glossy with moisture and slightly uneven. Farther out they become raised earthways faced with stone. Small bridges cross channels only a pace or two wide. Larger sheets of water are traversed on causeways pierced with culverts, so that the marsh remains connected on either side.

There is no wheel traffic. Goods move on human shoulders, on poles, in baskets, or aboard shallow reed boats pushed with long staffs. With no animals in the world—or at least none in this region—there are no hoof marks, dung, leather harnesses, birds on the roofs, insects above the water, or fish disturbing the pools. This absence is not dramatic. It is simply complete.

The vegetation has adapted to that quiet vacancy. Grasses grow without grazing. Seed heads remain intact until rain or wind knocks them down. Low trees stand widely spaced along the better-drained ridges, with smooth pale trunks and dense crowns of dark leaves. Their fruit, where they bear it, falls untouched and slowly dissolves into the soil. Ferns gather under retaining walls. Reeds occupy every basin deep enough to hold water, yet they do not form impenetrable wilderness; people cut them continually for mats, roofs, cordage, baskets, and fuel.

Because no creature pollinates the plants, most flowers are small, wind-served, or self-fertile. The landscape is green without being florid. Color comes in restrained fields: silver grass, rust-red moss, black wet bark, yellow clay, blue-gray stone. In sheltered gardens, people cultivate tubers, gourds, climbing beans, broad-leaved greens, and grainlike reeds. The gardens are carefully spaced and hand-tended. Ash, composted vegetation, and dredged marsh silt renew their soil.

The city is warm, though never oppressive. Moist air softens the high-altitude sun. Daylight is plain and diffuse, bright enough to reveal distance without making the stone glare. At noon, mist often remains against the mountain faces while the plateau lies clear beneath it. At night, condensation thickens on walls and roofs, feeding little chains of drops that continue until dawn.

Despite all this water, the place barely sounds alive.

There is the faint spill of channels into cisterns, the soft tapping of drips beneath eaves, the occasional whisper of wind over reeds. Human sounds do not carry far in the saturated air. A mason’s hammer may strike three or four times and then stop. A group crossing a causeway speaks quietly, their bare or sandaled feet making dull contacts with the wet stone. There are no calls from the marsh, no wings, no distant barking, no nocturnal chorus. Even leaves seldom rustle; the wind tends to move in broad, slow breaths.

The water itself is unusually calm. Springs emerge at fixed points in the mountain and flow at nearly constant rates. Pools rise to stone lips and spill into channels without noticeable seasonal change. Rain comes so regularly, and evaporation proceeds so evenly, that the whole plateau seems held in equilibrium. Fields neither parch nor flood. Marsh edges do not advance. Water stains on buildings remain at the same height from one generation to the next.

This steadiness governs the city’s habits. Reservoir gates are simple stone shutters, rarely moved. Farmers plant by count and custom rather than by signs in weather. Buildings are repaired, but expansion is cautious. A new wall changes drainage; a new causeway divides water; a newly cleared field alters the balance of moisture. Every project begins with weeks of watching bowls, stakes, and channels. Construction is less an act of conquest than a negotiation with levels that have remained unchanged longer than anyone remembers.

There are no ruins beneath the city and few stories of previous landscapes. Everything feels first-built. Quarry scars are crisp. Steps have not yet been hollowed by centuries of feet. The mountains retain the severe geometry of their formation, and the city answers with its own young geometry: straight embankments, square courts, fresh terraces, clean joins between clay and stone.

Yet it does not feel temporary. The freshness belongs to the world itself, not to a passing historical moment. Cracks do not widen. Channels do not deepen. Mountains shed no great landslides. Moss grows only so far across a wall and then holds. A cut bank remains a cut bank. Even abandoned paths persist beneath a thin green veil, their edges still legible.

At the outer margin, the city becomes progressively more open. Houses give way to garden compounds, then to water stairs and reed-cutting platforms, then to isolated survey towers on stone knolls. Beyond them spreads the plateau: a patchwork of wet meadows and still basins reaching toward the sharp mountains. Thin threads of water shine between them. Here and there, solitary trees occupy raised ground as precisely as monuments.

From a tower, one can see almost the entire inhabited region. Smoke rises in sparse vertical lines from sheltered hearths. Roofs, courtyards, canals, and fields repeat at generous intervals. The settlement has no enclosing wall; marsh and distance provide its boundaries. Nothing moves except people, smoke, falling rain, and water passing through channels so quietly that its motion is visible before it is heard.

The overwhelming impression is not desolation, though the animal silence can seem desolate to someone unused to it. It is an inhabited stillness: a young stone world already settled into permanence, wet without storm, green without wilderness, urban without crowding. The city stands high above any unseen lowlands, open to the mild sky, its handmade order distributed across the patient water.
