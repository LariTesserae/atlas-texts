The place is a narrow cleft near the summit of a young mountain, scarcely larger than a cathedral nave but so steeply enclosed that its true boundaries are difficult to judge. Basalt walls rise on either side, black and angular, their fracture planes still sharp. Little has had time to round them. Whole shelves look newly broken from the mountain, showing gray interiors beneath skins of volcanic glass.

No structure has been built here. Nevertheless, the cleft feels industrial.

Its machinery is geological: pressure, gravity, water, and cold operating at full capacity.

A stream enters from somewhere above but never becomes a proper river. It arrives in erratic discharges, thrown over the lip in white ropes that strike the ledges, vanish into foliage, and emerge lower down as spray. At other times the fall stops altogether. Then the cleft continues dripping from its accumulated moisture. Condensation beads along the rock, gathers on every leaf tip, and falls in a dense, irregular percussion. Pockets among the blocks hold dark water for an hour or a day before draining through fresh cracks.

The vegetation is improbably abundant given the altitude. It fills almost every recess without softening the place. Mats of liverwort and moss adhere to the walls in thick green-black nap. Ferns crowd the ledges, their fronds stiffened by cold and repeatedly flattened by sudden gusts. Dwarf shrubs grow in compressed tangles, woody stems braided through fallen stone. Pale grasses hang from seams overhead, tossing loose strands into the air. Where mineral seepage stains the rock orange, small fleshy plants make dense red rosettes, each cupping a thumbful of rain.

There are no trees in the ordinary sense. A few mountain birches have rooted near the upper opening, but wind and scant soil have reduced them to low, contorted meshes. Their trunks run almost horizontally along the rock before dividing into hundreds of flexible twigs. In darkness they resemble wire scrub, an organic thicket engineered to survive repeated loading.

Almost no sky reaches the floor. The walls lean inward, and foliage seals much of the remaining gap. Day appears only as a dim gray seam high overhead. Lower down, color is swallowed. Green becomes black; wet stone holds occasional dull reflections. During rain, every surface shines for a few seconds whenever the clouds thin, then subsides again into obscurity.

The cleft is never quiet.

Wind crossing the summit is forced into the narrow opening and accelerated. It descends in pulses, not as a steady current but as blows of compressed air. Leaves turn inside out all at once. Ferns thrash against the walls. Water is driven upward or sideways. Loose gravel races over ledges with a dry metallic hiss, followed by the deeper knock of stones dropping into unseen hollows.

Beneath that is a continuous low vibration. The stream passes through chambers inside the mountain, and its pressure makes the floor hum. Where water narrows between basalt slabs it produces a hard, repetitive hammering like a distant piston. Jets vent through cracks with the shriek of steam, though they are only cold water and air. Larger surges set buried boulders knocking together. The effect is of a factory in operation behind the walls: turbines, belts, crushing drums, and exhaust ducts, all suggested by natural processes and all impossible to inspect.

Nothing here was manufactured. There are no cables, pipes, foundations, cut surfaces, or discarded tools. The industrial resemblance comes from concentration. Too much work is being done in too little space.

Weather changes the cleft by the minute. A cloud striking the summit turns the air opaque. Condensation forms instantly on stone and leaf, swelling the moss until every foot of wall begins to drain. Then wind tears the cloud apart and the water supply diminishes. A pool empties through a newly opened fissure; another appears where a mat of roots blocks a channel. Pebbles accumulate behind stems, overburden them, and spill downslope. Fronds are buried, uncovered, and buried again. Small sections of vegetation detach intact and slide across the wet basalt like sod on a tilted roof.

The mountain itself is still settling. Frost enters hairline fractures each night. In warmer intervals the ice releases, and thin blades of rock peel from the walls. Most falls are minor—a snap, a ricochet, a rain of chips—but now and then a shelf gives way. The collapse is violent in the confined space. Air punches outward, leaves flatten, and black dust briefly overwhelms the smell of wet plants. Within days, moss fragments and rootlets begin to occupy the new rubble, though nothing has yet had time to conceal its raw edges.

Despite the lushness, there are no animals. No insects rise from the pools. No birds enter from the summit. The moss is untroubled by grazing, the leaves unperforated, the mud without tracks. Flowers, where they occur, are small and wind-pollinated, releasing dry clouds of pollen into the violent air. Spores do better: they pour from fern undersides and fungi hidden in the root mats, spreading wherever turbulence drives them.

This absence is not mysterious. The cleft is too unstable, too cold, too enclosed, and too poor in enduring food. Anything mobile would spend more energy resisting wind and flood than feeding. Plant life survives because it can be broken and continue, buried and regrow, drowned and wait. Its richness is made from repetition rather than longevity. A square yard may contain dozens of species, but few individual stems persist for many seasons.

The smell changes with each disturbance. Ordinarily it is dark and mineral: wet basalt, crushed fern, cold humus, the faint iron scent of seepage. After a fall, newly opened stone smells dry and dusty even in the rain. When root mats tear, they release the richer odor of peat. There is little decay. Dead matter is either washed away, sealed into cracks, or held cold enough to darken slowly.

At the cleft’s lowest point, the water enters a slot scarcely wider than a hand. The surrounding rock is polished only for a short distance; downstream of it the mountain remains angular and new. The slot draws air with each surge, producing a heavy inhalation followed by a concussive gulp. Moss around its rim streams continuously toward the opening, every filament aligned by suction.

Nothing can follow the water. The drain is the cleft’s only exit, and it seems to consume the whole landscape piecemeal: rain, soil, leaves, shattered basalt, spores, and darkness. Yet material keeps arriving from above. Fresh rock falls in. Clouds refill the pools. Roots spread over each new surface.

The place is therefore both lush and unfinished—a dense green chamber suspended near the top of a bare mountain, where creation has not slowed enough to become peaceful. It is small enough to cross in minutes, but there is no safe moment to cross it. Every ledge is temporary. Every pool is passing through. The walls are still becoming walls.
