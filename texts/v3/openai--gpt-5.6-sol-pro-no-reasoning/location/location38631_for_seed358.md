The basin lies below the surrounding plain, not dramatically—a long, shallow subsidence rather than a crater—but low enough to gather everything the country releases. Rainwater, warm runoff, sediment, insects, broken reeds, engine oil, and the noise of distant machinery all settle here.

From the rim, the landscape appears open. Its flatness carries the eye for kilometers, across pewter water and low islands of dark mud, toward a horizon blurred by vapor. Yet at ground level there is almost no empty space. Water occupies every hollow. Silt fills the water. Snails cover the silt. Midge larvae cloud the margins. Mats of eggs cling beneath stones and sheets of rusted metal. The basin is packed less with objects than with layers, each presence fitted closely over another.

The land is young. It has not yet learned the shapes of gullies, banks, or stable channels. Water spreads wherever it can, in films and shallow sheets, seldom gathering enough momentum to carve. New mud rises in soft lobes around submerged debris. Mineral crusts begin along the edges of pools and are drowned again before they harden. Footprints last for months, broadening slowly as their walls slump inward. Even the few drainage cuts made by machinery look provisional: straight incisions with raw sides, already filling from below.

The ground cannot decide whether it is land. A dull skin of clay rests over warm, saturated sediment, strong enough in places to bear a person and weak enough elsewhere to open around the ankle without warning. Pressing a boot into it releases strings of bubbles and the odor of iron, wet ash, and vegetation preserved before it could properly decay. Below that skin are older deposits—sand, powdered limestone, black organic bands—but they remain close to the surface, exposed by augers, animal burrows, and the collapse of shallow pits.

There is little vegetation in the usual sense. No continuous marsh grass softens the basin. Salt, disturbed soil, and constant inundation keep growth scattered. Rushes survive in isolated clumps on slight rises, their stalks yellow-green and widely spaced. Liverworts darken the shaded sides of concrete footings. Here and there a willow has established itself on a spoil mound, but none grows tall. Their leaves hang limp in the warm air, silvering only when machinery sends a pressure wave across the water.

The lack of plants does not make the basin barren. Animals are everywhere.

Frogs occupy the flooded tire tracks and call from beneath corrugated scraps. Water beetles scribble overlapping paths through rain films. Small fish move in drainage trenches scarcely wider than a hand, flashing whenever the dim sky brightens. Worms knot in the mud around leaking pipes. Pale crabs—or creatures near enough to crabs—shelter under slabs along the brackish western margin. Birds gather in numbers too large to count: stilts, terns, ibises, gulls, swallows, and blunt-billed waders that walk steadily behind the dredges, taking whatever the disturbed ground gives up. Their feeding is so constant that the mud seems to twitch.

The basin’s warmth is close and damp rather than fierce. Moisture condenses on railings, cable housings, tool cabinets, and the undersides of roofs even when rain has not fallen. Metal sweats. Concrete sweats. At night, low clouds sometimes meet the vapor rising from the pools, erasing the horizon entirely. Dawn brings no clean illumination, only a gradual whitening of the dark. Daylight passes through haze, exhaust, and suspended droplets until it reaches the ground gray and diffuse. Shadows are rare and shallow.

Across this low country stand the scattered parts of an industrial operation whose whole plan is difficult to see. There are pump houses on pilings, each no larger than a cottage. Narrow service roads cross the wet ground on beds of pale aggregate. Culverts emerge from embankments and discharge continuously, though the water level on either side appears the same. Rusted marker posts form long geometric lines across otherwise shapeless mud. A conveyor rises alone near the center of the basin, carrying dark sediment from one shallow excavation to another. It moves so slowly that its motion is first noticeable in the changing position of the birds perched along its frame.

Farther out are settling ponds bounded by low levees. Their water differs subtly from the surrounding flood: one is green-gray, one opaque beige, another black enough to reflect the sky without showing what lies beneath. Pipes connect them in circuits. Valves turn by remote instruction. Old gauges tremble in locked cabinets. The site may be extracting something, or washing it, or merely separating the basin into increasingly precise kinds of wetness.

The machinery makes the place deafening.

Pumps hammer beneath the ground with a force felt through the soles before it is heard. Diesel motors maintain a heavy, unbroken roar. Fans whine inside vent stacks. Loose sheet metal rattles on abandoned sheds. At intervals, pressure releases somewhere beyond sight, producing a vast exhalation that sends birds upward in simultaneous clouds. Over these industrial sounds lie the cries of thousands of animals: frogs pulsing in ditches, gulls quarreling around outfalls, insects forming a high electrical veil beside the ear.

Because the basin is flat and damp, sound travels strangely well. A chain dropped three kilometers away may seem to fall behind one’s shoulder. Engines beyond the horizon add their frequencies to engines nearby, until direction disappears. Silence exists only inside certain concrete chambers, and there it feels artificial, almost pressurized.

For all this noise, change is slow. The pumps run day and night, yet water levels shift by centimeters over a season. The conveyor works continuously, yet its spoil mound grows no faster than a natural bank. Sediment settles grain by grain. Levees subside. Mineral stains descend the walls of tanks in thin annual bands. Whole colonies of birds arrive, breed, and depart while the same puddle remains beneath the same ladder.

Construction and geology proceed together. Gravel dumped for a road sinks until only its coarsest stones remain visible. Foundations tilt before buildings are completed. New concrete is laid over old mud, and new mud wells up through its joints. The industrial works do not dominate the basin so much as participate in its formation. Pipes become ridges. Culverts become caves. Broken pallets catch sediment and establish islands. A stranded barge, left when a channel shifted, has become the hard center of a broad mudflat crowded with nests.

There are rules here, though most are practical rather than mysterious. Never trust a surface made smooth by rain. Never stand between a pump outlet and open water when the warning lamps begin to turn. Do not walk toward a sound, because the basin carries it from elsewhere. Follow the posts, even when the road between them has vanished. If birds are feeding, the mud is shallow; if they are standing motionless, it may not be.

At the lowest part of the basin, water covers the land in an uninterrupted sheet. It is rarely more than knee-deep, but it extends beyond sight, broken by valve towers, marker poles, nesting platforms, and the upper halves of drowned fences. Warm rain stipples it for hours at a time. Each drop briefly clears a circle in the oily surface film, then disappears among millions of others.

Nothing in the basin is finished. It is neither marsh nor lake, neither mine nor treatment works, neither ruin nor active construction site. It is a landscape being assembled under load: water laid over clay, machinery laid over water, animal life packed into every seam the machinery leaves open. Its future forms are present only as slight tendencies—a channel beginning around a footing, a reed clump holding its first handful of silt, a levee lowering almost imperceptibly toward failure.

From the rim it looks empty and gray.

Below, it is full to the point of overflow.
