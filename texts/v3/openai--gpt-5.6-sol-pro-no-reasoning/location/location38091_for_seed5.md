The city is hidden inside a fallen statue.

Only the statue’s face remains above ground: a pale stone brow rising from the cold flats, one blind eye filled with sedge, the bridge of the nose worn low by centuries of grit. Nothing moves on the plain except brief veils of snow. No road approaches it. From any distance it appears to be one more weather-softened ruin, too shallow to shelter anything.

The entrance is a crack behind the lower eyelid.

It leads down only a little way, through stone polished by shoulders and baskets, into the Hollow—an enclosed city occupying the statue’s skull. The place is no larger than a market hall, yet every available surface has been claimed. Houses crowd the inner curves of the cheeks. Stairs climb over roofs and descend through chimneys. Niches have been deepened into rooms, rooms divided by woven walls, walls made useful as racks, dovecotes, shelves, seed beds, and sleeping ledges. Narrow bridges cross the empty center from temple to granary, from granary to the communal ovens set along the upper jaw.

The old teeth stand at the bottom as smooth white towers. Some have been hollowed into storehouses; others support platforms of horn, bone, and frost-hardened timber. Around their roots runs a shallow street scarcely wide enough for two people to pass. Its paving consists of countless small black stones pressed into clay by generations of bare soles and felt boots. Drainage grooves follow the seams between them with such patience that a spilled cup empties into the cisterns before it can freeze.

There is no metal here except a few blunt needles, green with age, and a single copper bowl kept in the house of measures. Everything else is fashioned from what the city can gather or grow: flint blades, antler picks, bone hinges, gut cord, fired-clay lamps, wicker pipes sealed with wax. Doors are layered hide. Window shutters are thin mica held in carved wooden grids. The oldest beams have become black and glossy from hands.

Despite the cold, the Hollow is brilliantly lit.

Day enters through the statue’s damaged eyes. Light strikes shelves of pale crystal embedded in the skull and divides into clean, steady brilliance. Every lane and chamber receives some portion of it. Ceilings shine with reflected white; wet leaves glitter as if varnished. There are few true shadows, even beneath stairs. At night the crystals continue to glow faintly, releasing what they gathered during the day, so darkness arrives only as a soft reduction of detail.

The inhabitants believe the light is finite. No one is permitted to cover a crystal, chip one free, or place a mirror where it might hoard brightness from a neighbor. These are not religious prohibitions so much as civic laws, as practical and old as the rules governing water.

Water is scarce in quantity but present almost everywhere as dampness. The cold stone sweats when the outer air changes. Condensation pearls along the high vault, gathers at the tips of carved curls, and falls into a network of leaf funnels. Rain entering through the eyes is slowed by mats of moss and braided roots. It travels down woven channels, through settling jars, into cisterns cut below the teeth.

Nothing flows quickly. Nothing is allowed to.

Each drop is passed through the city several times: first for drinking, then for cooking, then for washing roots or softening clay. At last it enters the low gardens, where beds of charcoal, sand, and powdered bone cleanse it before it seeps into the foundations. Small pockets remain in shell-lined basins throughout the Hollow. Their surfaces are perfectly still. A person bending over one can see their face with the old statue’s mineral veins crossing it.

The vegetation is astonishingly dense. The city has less open ground than a forest and more leaves than a field.

Moss carpets parapets, thresholds, roof ridges, and the shoulders of carved figures. Dark kale grows in stacked clay troughs. Dwarf ferns spring from cracks in the stone. Pale vines loop from beam to beam, bearing translucent gourds no larger than knuckles. Lichens are cultivated in geometric panels for dye, medicine, and food. Low berry shrubs occupy the brighter ledges beneath the eyes; their roots are trained through drilled channels and wrapped around porous stone.

Nothing grows high. Height belongs to paths, houses, and reservoirs. Plants spread sideways, downward, or into one another. A household may own a branch but not the roots beneath it; those belong to the ward, because roots hold the walls together.

In the warmest pockets—warm only by comparison—mushrooms rise from compost chambers. Their caps are blue-gray, pleated like closed fans. They are harvested by touch so that unnecessary shadows are not cast over the seedlings. Beds are never cleared all at once. One leaf is cut from each plant, one fungus taken from each cluster, one basket gathered from each wall. The gardens therefore seem perpetually full, poised at the same moment before harvest.

That sameness governs the whole city.

Its population does not grow. Every dwelling has a fixed number of sleeping recesses, and every cradle is carved beside an ancestral niche. The people say the Hollow has been full since the first memory. Birth, adoption, and immigration are balanced by departure according to customs discussed in murmurs and enacted without ceremony. No new building may narrow a passage, dim a garden, or increase the demand on a cistern. A collapsed room is reconstructed to its former outline. Worn steps are replaced with stones of the same height. Even repairs aspire not to renewal, but to continuity.

The city changes in its details and remains identical in its shape.

Erosion has already done nearly everything it can do. Edges are rounded to comfortable forms. Channels have found their final routes. The stone has shed its loose layers and arrived at a hard, dense core. Where feet pass, the floor is concave; where hands turn corners, the walls are hollowed into grips. These marks are so old and so perfectly suited to use that they no longer appear damaged. The Hollow has been worn into agreement with its inhabitants.

Animals fill the remaining spaces.

White moths rest beneath leaves with wings like chips of lime. Thumb-sized frogs live in the cistern mouths, moving only when insects come near. There are beetles in the compost, pale fish in the deepest reservoir, seed mice in the warm walls, and small blue-black birds nesting among the upper crystal shelves. The birds do not sing. Their throats pulse, their beaks open, but they produce only a faint stirring of air.

Almost every animal in the Hollow is silent.

The frogs catch flies without croaking. Mice communicate by touching whiskers. Moths beat their wings with no audible flutter. Even the beetles’ shells seem padded. The people speak softly because louder sounds die strangely here: a shout is not echoed but extinguished, as if taken into the stone. Hammers are wrapped in hide. Grain is ground in deep wooden mortars. Doors settle onto felt. The city’s loudest regular noise is condensation falling into water, one drop at a time.

Yet the Hollow is not lifelessly quiet. It possesses an intimate layer of near-sounds: breath through nostrils, cloth against stone, the minute click of a seedpod opening, the wet separation of moss beneath a knife. In crowded lanes people announce themselves by tapping another’s wrist. News passes through hand signs, knotted cords, and small tablets of clay marked with reeds. Conversation exists, but it rarely travels farther than the table around which it began.

At the center hangs the Balance, the strangest relic in the city.

It is a stone sphere suspended over the open court by cords of braided root. The sphere is the size of a child’s head and contains a droplet of water in a natural cavity. No one can see the water, but when the sphere is held close to the ear, the droplet may sometimes be heard shifting within.

It has not shifted in living memory.

The cords that hold it remain green though they have neither leaves nor visible soil. Gardeners trim them to an established pattern. Surveyors measure the sphere’s height each morning with a notched bone rod. It always hangs at precisely the same level. The city’s calendar is founded upon this immobility. Years are not counted by change, but by successful returns: the cistern restored to its line, the berry beds pruned to their former shapes, the same number of seed jars sealed, the same rooftops made ready for frost.

Outside, weather crosses the low plain. Snow comes and thins. Rare rain gathers in the statue’s eroded mouth. Frost enters cracks and leaves them again. But within, fluctuations are absorbed by a thousand practiced acts. Extra water is stored. Failed seedlings are replaced. A cold chamber is narrowed with curtains; a warm one is opened into the lane. The citizens do not resist the world so much as distribute its pressures until none remain noticeable.

From the entrance behind the eyelid, the entire city is almost visible at once: its stacked houses, crowded gardens, tooth-towers, bridges, ladders, pale basins, and innumerable small lives, all held in bright suspension. It appears less constructed than fitted together. There is no vacant lot, no wilderness separate from habitation, no firm boundary between building and organism. Moss seals masonry. Roots serve as ropes. Birds warm seed jars. Human breath feeds the gardens, and the gardens return the moisture to the stone.

Deep in the foundations, water seeps away through the statue’s buried throat. The quantity is exact. Above it, drop for drop, the cold ceiling gathers more.

The Balance does not move. The lanes remain full. The silent birds open their beaks in the unwavering light.
