The Low Works have no ground beneath them.

Everything underfoot was placed there: brick laid upon tile, tile upon beams, beams upon older vaults whose builders are unnamed. Wells descend through masonry for hundreds of cubits and find only more masonry—arches crushed into chambers, chambers packed with potsherd and clinker, roads roofed over by later roads. Even the mud is manufactured, a black paste ground from charcoal, lime, powdered ceramic, and the dust of worn walls. When the inhabitants require stone, they do not quarry downward. They dismantle the past.

The land is low and broad, a shallow basin of construction extending farther than torchlight or memory. Its horizons are serried silhouettes: kiln cones, retaining walls, stepped reservoirs, collapsed arcades, scaffolds knitted from crooked poles. Nothing stands alone. Every building braces another; every stair is wedged between older stairs; bridges pass over roofs that have themselves become streets. The place feels crowded even where no one lives, dense with columns, pulley frames, drying racks, buttresses, ladders, cistern mouths, gutters, and empty doorways.

There is no open soil and no plant life. No moss greens the wet brick. No weed prises apart the paving. Rot is almost unknown. Wood, where it exists, is precious and repeatedly reused until it becomes a polished assemblage of pegs and bindings. Roof poles may be older than the houses they support. Rope is braided from hair, gut, narrow leather strips, or mineral fibers teased from fire-softened slag. Mats are woven from beaten copper wire or ceramic links.

The Works are cool throughout the year. Their warmth comes locally and violently—from kilns, cook pits, smelters, and banks of coals sheltered in clay housings—but the prevailing air is chill. It pours through low passages and settles in courtyards. Condensation beads on glazed ceilings. Metal sweats. Breath appears pale in the lower galleries.

Water comes irregularly.

For weeks the gutters may carry nothing but grit. Then rain arrives as a hard, brief drumming over the basin. It is heard before it is felt: millions of impacts on tile, copper, fired clay, and stretched hide, merging into a roar so complete that speech becomes useless. The whole city catches the fall. Roofs tilt toward channels; channels feed stone throats; stone throats pour into jars, troughs, settling courts, and stepped cisterns. At every level, water crosses other water. It drops through grates in silver ropes and emerges far below stained gray or red by the layers it has traversed.

The rain never touches a natural surface.

In the days afterward, the Low Works change character. Pools occupy sunken rooms. Narrow canals wake between buildings. Ceramic floats carry salt, charcoal, tools, and blind livestock from one quarter to another. Water wheels turn in ducts too cramped for animals, setting hammers to strike and bellows to breathe. Then, gradually, the reservoirs are sealed, the channels dry, and damp retreats into wall joints, vaulted undercrofts, and the interiors of cooling pipes.

Erosion has scarcely begun, though the city is ancient.

This is because it is always being made faster than it can be worn away. A stair rounded by feet is faced with fresh tile. A cracked gutter is packed in clay and fired in place. A leaning tower receives a buttress assembled from dismantled rooms. Surfaces do not so much decay as become buried inside repairs. The few places where water has been allowed to work unattended seem shocking: tiny scallops in mortar, shallow runnels on bricks, pale fans of deposited salt. People gather around these marks as if watching a new craft being invented.

Some say the Low Works were built all at once, perfect and unweathered, and that erosion only recently entered the world.

Others say there never was a first city. Construction is the substrate of existence. Given enough digging, one will not find bedrock but the underside of another roof.

No one has dug far enough to decide.

Technology here belongs to the hand, lever, wedge, wheel, and flame. There are no great engines, though the total city often behaves like one. Its mechanisms are dispersed among thousands of workers who pull ropes in rhythm, stamp clay, carry baskets, turn capstans, sort broken tile by sound, and listen for changes in the strain of walls. The most sophisticated devices are built from simple parts: counterweighted lifts climbing through twenty floors; water clocks that release gates in sequence; stone whistles mounted in ventilation shafts; clay valves that seal more tightly as pressure rises.

Metal exists, but sparingly. Hammered copper edges tools of bone and ceramic. Soft iron is found in old fastenings and guarded like a relic. Most cutting is done with glassy slag, chipped into short black blades. A skilled knapper can turn the by-product of a communal kiln into saw teeth, awls, skinning flakes, or surgical points.

Fire is the principal architect. Whole districts are shaped from wet clay around temporary cores, then baked room by room. During a firing, nearby passages are sealed, inhabitants relocate, and the walls glow faintly through the dark. Cooling may take months. Cracks are expected and interpreted. A clean vertical crack demands a doorway; a branching crack becomes a shrine, drainage tree, or map. Builders claim they do not impose form upon the city. They negotiate with shrinkage.

There is little light. Flames consume valuable fuel and are used where work requires them. Elsewhere, people move by touch and memory. Doorframes bear carved patterns identifying streets, families, hazards, and depths. Floors change texture before drops or junctions. Strings run through the most crowded corridors, each route distinguished by knots.

Dim illumination also comes from salt lamps: shallow bowls in which brine reacts with paired scraps of metal, producing a frail blue shimmer. In the reservoir wards, colonies of non-plant life inhabit damp ceramic pores. They resemble chalky films or translucent blisters rather than fungi, and emit a dull violet light when disturbed. No one agrees whether they are alive. They spread only on certain ancient glazes and die when removed.

The fauna of the Low Works is scarce, hidden, and highly adapted.

Pale six-legged mice nest inside warm wall cavities and drink condensation from copper pipes. They have broad incisors suited to scraping lime. Small eyeless eels appear after rain in the lowest cisterns, though every channel leading there is screened. A long-bodied animal called a gutter hound moves through drainage ducts and is seldom seen whole: one hears its claws, glimpses its narrow muzzle at a grate, then finds a cache of ceramic shards arranged by size. Whether it collects them deliberately is disputed.

Larger animals are almost unknown. Once or twice in a lifetime, someone hears a deep knocking from beneath the lowest occupied galleries. The blows travel through columns and floors, regular enough to resemble work. During such events, all hammers in the district are silenced. The answering sound sometimes continues for hours.

The Works are never quiet.

Even without rain, they clatter and bellow. Kilns draw air with a throatlike rush. Mallets strike molds. Carts with solid wheels hammer over seams in the paving. Water ticks inside walls. Chains of ceramic cups chatter as they descend into wells. Wind crossing the roofscape sounds through thousands of pots, vents, and cracked chimneys, making the basin into a vast, discordant instrument.

Certain towers are built expressly for this wind. Their openings are tuned with slabs and resonant jars. When pressure changes over the basin, they produce low warning tones. A rising moan means rain. A pulsing note means contraction in the eastern vaults. A shriek from multiple towers means that some deep support has failed and weight is migrating through the city.

Then the Works become urgently active.

Doors are bricked shut. Arches are thickened. Families carry their possessions along prescribed routes while wall-listeners kneel with cheeks against tile. Crews dismantle upper rooms to lighten the load and use the material to reinforce lower ones. Nothing is wasted, and there is no rubble in the usual sense. A fallen house becomes a quarry before its dust has settled.

This continual movement gives the landscape a strange, almost metabolic life. Streets close and reopen elsewhere. Courtyards deepen as new stories rise around them. Reservoirs become plazas; plazas are roofed and made into workshops; workshops are flooded to cure clay. A person returning after ten years may find their childhood lane still present but enclosed inside a public bath, its old doorways serving as niches beneath the waterline.

There are laws governing this change.

Nothing may be built without carrying something else.

No void may be filled until its echoes have been recorded.

Rain belongs first to the level on which it falls, except during fire.

A wall found warm without explanation must not be pierced.

Any stair uncovered beneath a floor must be descended before it is dismantled.

And no one may claim the lowest chamber.

The last rule has practical force. Ownership descends only three levels below one’s dwelling, while deeper spaces belong to all. In those shared depths are the cool halls, settling cisterns, salt stores, communal kilns, burial niches, and the old unlit roads. Bodies are not returned to soil, for there is no soil to receive them. They are dried, wrapped in clay, and incorporated into non-load-bearing walls. Their names are pressed into the wet surface with patterned rollers. As generations accumulate, the memorial corridors become thick, crowded books.

Beyond the dense central wards, the construction opens but does not end. Low causeways cross fields of fitted plates. Empty scaffolding forests stand over shallow catchments. Lines of waist-high chimneys exhale mist from unknown chambers. In the far dark, isolated furnace towns glow dull orange, separated by tracts where no one now lives but where every handspan remains fabricated.

These outskirts are considered young. Their bricks still have sharp edges. Rain has not yet found stable routes through them, so storms produce sudden waterfalls, burst walls, and lakes where maps show markets. Builders go there to study beginnings. They watch raw plaster craze as it dries and debate which cracks will become rivers in a thousand years.

At the basin’s outermost known boundary rises a wall with no gates. It is not monumental; it is simply continuous, gently curved, tiled in black ceramic that returns almost no light. Rain striking it runs upward.

The upward water gathers along the wall’s rim and disappears over the other side.

People have raised ladders, towers, ramps, and human pyramids to look beyond. All have failed in different ways. Ladders lengthen but never reach. Towers settle as they rise. Ramps curve imperceptibly until they return to their own beginnings. Thrown stones strike the wall, fall, and are later discovered embedded in old floors deep within the city.

So the Low Works remain enclosed—not like a prison, but like the interior of a vessel whose purpose has been forgotten.

Under darkness, amid hammering and rushing air, its inhabitants continue to build. They add rooms to a world already solid with rooms. They catch the intermittent rain. They repair surfaces barely touched by time. They listen to the city’s weight moving through the columns and to the measured knocks far below.

And everywhere, on cold tile and glazed brick, the first thin marks of water are only beginning to appear.
