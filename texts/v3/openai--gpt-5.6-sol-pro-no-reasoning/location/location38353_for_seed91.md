Beneath the northern plain lies an excavated basin so broad that its farther walls disappear into darkness. It is not a cavern in the natural sense. The roof is a slab of frozen earth supported by pillars of uncut stone, old concrete, and living roots. Shafts rise through it at long intervals, admitting narrow columns of winter daylight. From below, they look like pale wells drilled into another world.

The basin was once a lignite mine. Its stepped workings remain: black shelves, haul roads, drainage trenches, and angular faces still too young to have softened. Frost has preserved every machine cut. Teeth marks from bucket excavators stripe the walls. Broken stone lies where it fell, sharp-edged and unstained. Here and there, the coal seam shows through as a dull, dark band.

Plants have inherited nearly all of it.

They do not form a conventional forest. There is too little soil, too little light, and the temperature remains close to freezing throughout the year. Instead, vegetation occupies the mine as a continuous low fabric. Moss spreads over the spoil terraces in thick, resilient mats. Liverworts seal the drainage channels. Pale grasses root in coal dust, their blades lying flat beneath the cold movement of air. Willow roots descend from the surface through cracks in the roof and branch overhead, making suspended groves with no visible trunks. In places those roots have grown together into walls, ramps, and broad shelves strong enough to walk upon.

Much of the apparent ground is plant matter. Beneath a dark crust of moss lie generations of compressed stems and roots, frozen before they could decay. A footstep sinks a little, then meets the fibrous resistance of older growth. The basin is slowly filling itself with peat, though the process may take millennia. From the upper galleries, the vegetated floor resembles a green-black tide caught halfway through rising.

Open distances remain between its denser colonies. Long flats of bare shale extend under the roof, interrupted by isolated islands of sedge or by a single knot of roots hanging from above. The sparseness is deceptive: even where plants seem absent, fungal threads and fine root hairs penetrate the cracks. Life is not abundant in height but nearly complete in coverage, a thin occupation spread over an enormous area.

Water is present without being plentiful. Condensation gathers on the cold roof and swells into drops. Most fall singly, punching small holes in soft moss. Others follow root filaments downward and feed clear pockets held among leaves. After rain or thaw on the plain above, the shafts begin to drip in earnest. Trenches fill, shallow streams run briefly along the old haul roads, and every low terrace shines. Within days the water drains into deeper workings or freezes in place.

There are ponds, but they are scattered and seldom large. Their surfaces are black, edged with ice, and startlingly still. Filamentous algae live under the ice in translucent green veils. Around the largest ponds, horsetails rise in sparse rows like jointed wire. The water tastes of iron and coal, though beds of moss have filtered out much of the mine’s older acidity.

The cold comes not as wind or weather but as a constant property of the stone. Breath hangs close to the mouth. Metal burns bare fingers. Every pool carries a skin of ice, even in summer, and the tallest grasses are permanently furred with rime. In winter the shafts exhale downward and the temperature falls further; in summer they admit a faint warmth that rarely reaches the basin floor.

Despite this stability, the place is overwhelmingly loud.

Ventilation machinery still operates in chambers around the mine’s perimeter. Great fans turn behind steel grilles, driven by motors installed decades apart. Some are new, their housings clean and bright. Others survive through replacement parts, welded braces, and layers of grease. Their combined pressure keeps the roof’s accumulated gases moving toward exhaust shafts. It also keeps the underground vegetation alive.

The fans produce a roar beyond ordinary industrial noise. It fills the basin continuously, too deep to hear only with the ears. Root shelves tremble. Pond surfaces quiver. Loose bolts creep across abandoned platforms. Near the main ducts, the air itself seems solid, hammering at clothing and flattening grass in permanent arcs. Conversation is possible only face-to-face, by signs or through headsets.

Other sounds exist within the roar but arrive stripped of context: the slow percussion of pumps, the shriek of an idling bearing, chains striking a distant housing, water rattling through a pipe. At regular intervals, louvers open somewhere in the dark and the entire pressure of the basin changes. A long metallic boom passes beneath the roof from one end to the other. Moss releases dust. Birds, if any are present, go silent.

The machinery is dispersed rather than concentrated. A pump house occupies one island of concrete. A transformer cage glows blue behind another. Narrow maintenance huts stand at shaft bottoms, their doors painted for visibility and their roofs covered by moss. Cable trays cross open ground on low legs. Insulated pipes vanish into curtains of roots and emerge hundreds of metres away. Sodium lamps mark the principal route, but many have failed, leaving only occasional pools of amber light.

Between them the mine is dim. Electric lamps illuminate fragments rather than spaces: wet rail, a white-coated valve, grass trembling under forced air. The shaft light is colder and less dependable. Around noon it reaches certain terraces and turns the frost briefly silver. The rest remains in charcoal tones, with green visible only at close range.

Most of the former mine infrastructure is gone. Excavators were dismantled, tracks lifted, workshops stripped to their frames. What remains looks less abandoned than reduced to necessity. The ventilation system, pumps, power lines, and inspection roads are maintained because the roof cannot safely be allowed to flood or fill with gas. Everything else has been surrendered to plants.

Old structures persist as skeletons inside the growth. A conveyor bridge crosses one drainage hollow, its belt removed and its trusses woven through with willow roots. Concrete footings rise like low tombs from moss. The cab of a haul truck sits half buried in peat, windows opaque with frost, its interior packed with ferns. Survey marks and hazard stripes are still visible where condensation has not peeled them away.

People come through in small crews. Their movements are prescribed by the machinery: lit routes, numbered gates, timed inspections, zones where hearing protection is mandatory and zones where no one enters alone. They carry lamps, gas meters, thermal clothing, and radios that transmit through buried repeaters. At a distance they resemble isolated sparks moving through a region too large for them.

No settlement occupies the basin, though there are rest rooms at the main access incline and an emergency shelter stocked with blankets and oxygen. The nearest thing to civic space is a junction called the Square, where five haul roads meet under a cluster of lamps. A map board stands there behind scratched plastic. Someone has placed a bench beneath it. The bench has rooted: moss covers its seat, and pale shoots emerge through the drainage holes.

Animals are uncommon. The ventilation flow, cold, and ceaseless vibration discourage most larger species. Small pale moths gather near warm electrical boxes. Springtails darken the margins of meltwater pools. A few beetles live in the peat, slow-moving and glossy as drops of oil. Occasionally a vole descends along a root mass and leaves a brief script of tracks in the frost.

Birds are rarer still. A wren may enter through a shaft during a storm and survive for a season among the maintenance buildings, feeding on insects around the lamps. Its song cannot compete with the fans; one sees its throat moving but hears nothing. There are stories of a fox living in the western galleries, though the tracks attributed to it are often weeks old.

Nothing here changes quickly. The fans rotate fast, water falls, motors vibrate, yet the landscape itself advances by fractions. Moss closes a wheel rut over twenty years. Roots thicken around a handrail until metal and wood become one support. Peat rises against concrete steps one dark layer at a time. Frost opens the youngest cuts slowly, separating chips from the walls but never rounding them. The mine retains its fresh geometry under its vegetal covering.

Even failures tend to unfold with patience. A pump weakens over months. Water gains a centimetre in a remote trench. Sedge follows it. Ice expands inside a seam. Engineers notice the trend on a graph and reroute a line before the change becomes an event. The basin is governed less by catastrophe than by maintenance: a continuous negotiation among pressure, water, cold, stone, and growth.

The plants participate in that negotiation without design. Roots stabilize faces that would otherwise shed rock. Moss filters runoff before it reaches the pumps. Peat clogs some culverts and seals others. The ventilation that protects human access brings carbon dioxide and spores deep into the basin; the vegetation, in turn, changes humidity and traps dust. Industrial systems and living systems have become mutually entangled, though neither depends entirely on the other.

At the farthest edge, beyond the maintained lamps, the mine narrows into new cuts abandoned shortly after excavation. These are the sharpest places: clean black walls, pale spoil cones, drill holes still visible in rows. Vegetation reaches them in scattered patches. A mat of moss occupies one ledge, then nothing for fifty metres; a root finds a fracture and descends alone into the dark.

Here the fans are heard as a single immense note rather than as machines. Condensation gathers overhead. Drops fall through the beam of a lamp and vanish into coal dust. The ground slopes downward toward workings no longer pumped, where black water has risen through the galleries and stopped.

Along that shore, the plant world ends in a thin green line. Beyond it is cold water and the drowned continuation of the mine. Pipes, stairways, signs, and younger excavation faces descend intact beneath the surface. They appear when a maintenance light is lowered: angular, close, preserved.

Then the light swings away. The submerged levels disappear, the roots overhead tremble in the driven air, and the roar continues—constant, mechanical, almost geological—while the basin, leaf by leaf and fibre by fibre, very slowly turns itself into ground.
