The place occupies a shallow saddle near the summit of a warm, bare mountain. It is no larger than a walled garden, though no wall encloses it. Beyond its rim the slopes fall steeply into darkness, and farther ranges appear only when the dust thins: black teeth under a copper sky.

There is no water here. There has never been rain in any shape the inhabitants would recognize. The ground is old volcanic ceramic, crazed into interlocking plates and polished along their edges by centuries of airborne grit. Pale salts bloom from the cracks, but they are not residues of vanished seas. They rise from mineral vapors exhaled by the mountain and harden upon contact with the air. Underfoot, the saddle feels hollow. Now and then something deep below strikes it, and the entire garden rings.

Its lushness is deceptive.

Plants grow everywhere, yet sparsely: great individual forms separated by generous tracts of open stone. Each has room to display its full architecture. There are bronze fans taller than a person, leaf-blades held edgewise to the dim light; low forests of jointed ivory rods; black cushions soft as fur; and branching organisms like antlers cast in green glass. Nothing has roots in the ordinary sense. The plants clamp themselves to cracks with ceramic claws and feed upon warm gases, dust, static charge, and the faint metallic effluence of the rocks.

Their tissues contain no sap. Fine powders circulate through chambers in their stems, driven by flexing membranes. When cut, a plant does not bleed—it pours a brief ribbon of fragrant smoke, then seals the wound with a bright glaze.

The tallest growths are the bell-trees. Their trunks are hollow, ridged columns of reddish fiber and silica. From them hang thin, overlapping leaves resembling hammered metal. The leaves are always moving, though the air may seem still. They strike one another with a ceaseless, disorderly clamor: clangs, rattles, deep gong notes, sudden volleys like stones cast down a stair. Every plant has its own pitch. Together they make the saddle audible from far below.

This is only the ground note.

Warm updrafts surge over the ridge without rhythm. They arrive carrying ash, pollen, glittering mineral needles, discarded wings, and dense flocks of small animals. The garden bends, shuts, opens, and rearranges itself under each blast. Fan-plants fold into spears. Rod colonies retract beneath the crust. Bell-trees twist their crowns and become screaming wheels. Minutes later the air drops dead and everything unfolds again, abruptly, as though released from a fist.

The animals exploit these transformations.

Thousands inhabit the saddle, although at any moment the landscape may look nearly empty. Most are hidden within stems, under plates, or in the warm cavities of the mountain. When the wind rises they pour out.

Six-legged sailers leap from the rim and spread skins veined with amber. They circle the garden in tight, shrieking spirals, feeding on mineral dust caught in their comb-like mouths. Thumb-sized hammerbacks run across the ceramic flats, striking the ground with hardened foreheads to stun burrowing prey. Long, light-bodied things cling in rows to the lee sides of bell-trees; when a particular note sounds, they all shed their outer skins at once. The husks fly upward like pale birds while the new animals, black and glossy, vanish into the trunk.

There are also crawling wreaths composed of many separate creatures. Each animal grips the next by a hook at the tail, and the completed ring rolls erratically across the saddle. If threatened, the wreath explodes into individuals; if food is found, they reassemble around it and turn slowly while eating. Their rolling makes a dry, jubilant clatter.

Nothing drinks. Animals obtain reactive powders from plants and lick condensates of sulfur, ammonia, and oily hydrocarbons from the coolest stones. Respiration is more important than feeding. Many species carry bright inflatable sacs with which they harvest passing gases. In calm weather they become torpid. During storms they are frantic, brilliant, and loud.

Thus the garden’s abundance is measured not in mass but in event. Empty stone becomes, within seconds, a bazaar of wings and mouths. A gust departs, and the crowd disappears so completely that only tracks in the salt show it was ever there.

Near the center stand three small structures.

They were built from fitted slabs of the mountain’s natural ceramic, lashed with braided plant fiber and sealed with black resin. No structure is larger than a sleeping chamber. One has collapsed into a neat fan of tiles; another leans heavily against a bell-tree that has grown through its roof; the third remains intact, though its doorway is only a narrow triangular slit.

The builders had simple tools. Chips of glassy stone lie beside an old work block. Grooves on the slabs were cut patiently with abrasive cords. Pegs are bone or fired fiber. Inside the intact shelter are low shelves, clay masks with perforated cheeks, and a rack holding half a dozen tuning forks made from native copper. Soot coats the ceiling, but there is no hearth. The soot came from burned incense-plants whose smoke was food, medicine, and perhaps speech.

No one now lives in the houses permanently. A few masked gatherers climb to the saddle during the quiet intervals. They carry baskets with rigid lids, stone scrapers, hooked poles, and little hammers wrapped in felt. They move carefully from plant to plant, never stepping across a crack that is exhaling.

They do not harvest leaves or fruit. They harvest sounds.

A gatherer touches a tuning fork to a bell-tree, listens through the bone of the jaw, and searches for a branch with the same note. That branch is bound before it is cut. If allowed to ring freely after severing, it attracts predators from the cliffs: broad, eyeless animals that navigate by vibration and arrive by hurling themselves blindly from ledge to ledge.

The people say each useful substance has a tone, and each dangerous change announces itself as a mistake in the garden’s music. A flat ringing underfoot means a crust plate is about to tilt. A rapid silver chatter means the air will become poisonous. Three low beats from no visible source warn that the mountain is preparing to reverse its breath.

Whether this is religion or fieldcraft is not clear. Here, the distinction may never have arisen.

The light remains subdued even at midday. High clouds of metallic dust spread the sun into a dull red disk, warm but weak. Shadows are soft and brown. Certain plants compensate by carrying their own illumination: dim green seams, violet pores, rows of orange blisters that brighten when touched. At dusk the garden does not darken so much as change ownership. Day colors vanish, and internal colors appear.

Then the larger fauna emerge.

They are thin enough to have hidden in cracks no wider than a finger. Kite-shaped hunters unfold joint after joint until they stand above the shelters. Their bodies are frames rather than volumes, lattices of bone and taut membrane. They step across open ground without touching it often, planting one needle-foot at a time. Smaller animals go silent at their approach, but the plants grow noisier, shaking their leaves despite the still air.

The hunters do not pursue flesh. They pursue motion. A rolling wreath, a flickering lamp-organ, even a loose cord on the ruined house may provoke a sudden strike. After pinning movement to the ground, the hunter waits for the mountain’s warm breath to pass through its prey and extracts the altered gas. The body itself is often left untouched.

At irregular intervals the whole saddle changes.

A pressure front climbs from the abyss. Salt flowers burst simultaneously. Cracks flare blue. The warm stone heaves—not violently, but with the slow certainty of a creature turning in sleep. Plates overlap; old paths close; cavities open where none existed. Seeds like flat black coins spin out of the fissures. Animals descend upon them. Plants tear free and cartwheel into the sky. New mineral foam rises around the buildings and hardens before it finishes swelling, leaving delicate, impossible arches.

These storms may last a minute or most of a night. They destroy little in the conventional sense. Instead, they revise arrangements. A grove wakes on the opposite side of the saddle. A shelter gains a room of natural stone. A familiar bell-tree rings in an unfamiliar voice. The terrain keeps its age while refusing to keep its exact shape.

This is why the saddle appears both weathered and unfinished. Every surface bears long use: rounded corners, polished hollows, old repairs, generations of claw marks. Yet no map remains accurate. Stability belongs to patterns, not positions.

The garden will always contain three shelters, though not necessarily the same three. There will always be a clear patch near the center where nothing grows, even if the center moves. The bell-trees will always become silent just before the strongest upheaval. Something will always wait beneath the largest plate.

The gatherers have a rule never to look beneath it.

The animals have no such rule. During the loudest storms they gather around that plate in extraordinary numbers—sailers, hammerbacks, wreath-creatures, lantern mites, lattice hunters—all facing inward while dust and leaves whirl above them. The entire saddle rings. The plate rises by the width of a hand, showing not darkness below but a dim, crowded radiance, as if another garden lies inverted under the mountain.

Then it settles.

The creatures scatter. The plants open. The small houses creak on their fiber lashings, still standing or standing again, and the warm wind carries the uproar down the barren slopes.
