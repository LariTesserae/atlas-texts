There is no ground here.

The world is a drowned vertical garden built along the sides of an abyss. Its upper boundary is lost in black water, and its lower boundary is lost the same way. Between them rise immense pillars: not mountains exactly, but constructions of root, shell, compressed fiber, and pale mineral secreted by plants. Each pillar is many kilometers high. They stand far apart, leaving broad reaches of open water between them, and are joined only occasionally by bridges grown from braided trunks.

Everything solid has been made. There is no visible rock, mud, or sand. The oldest surfaces are layered with leaf-stone: dead vegetal tissue compacted over ages until it has become hard, dull, and finely banded. Arches, terraces, hollows, overhangs, and long ascending galleries have been shaped from it. Yet no builder can be separated from the building. The architecture is what remains of growth repeated so slowly and for so long that intention and ecology have become indistinguishable.

Each pillar began, perhaps, as a single plant. Its body forked, enclosed water, thickened around empty chambers, and laid down buttresses against currents that no longer vary. Daughter growths rooted into the parent rather than into any external earth. Later generations filled most of the available surface. Their tissues now form floors, ceilings, stairlike ribs, vents, wells, hanging walls, and spars reaching outward into the dark.

The structures have been eroded almost to their final shapes. Constant water has rounded every edge that could be rounded. Weak spans fell ages ago; unstable chambers collapsed and were sealed by new growth; loose material was eaten, dissolved, or caught in the living fabric. What remains is smooth, load-bearing, and immensely old. Nothing here looks ruined, though all of it bears the soft contours of ruin. Grooves run down walls like melted inscriptions. Great door-shaped apertures open into chambers that were never meant to be entered. The water has polished exposed rootwood until it resembles black bone.

Vegetation fills nearly all solid space but does not make the world feel crowded. Growth lies in enormous, economical masses. A single curtain-leaf may hang for a kilometer, thin as skin and broad as a district. Stands of rigid fronds occupy terraces hundreds of meters apart. Vast ropes descend alone through open water, each wrapped in short dark cilia. Gardens are not collections of individual plants; they are solitary organisms whose parts are separated by distances that would elsewhere divide forests.

Most vegetation is black, iron-blue, or colorless. Light is too scarce for green. The plants feed upon mineral traces, upon the slow chemistry of the water, upon one another’s shed tissue, and upon a dim pressure-born radiance concentrated inside their cells. In places, veins beneath translucent bark show a bruised violet glow. The glow never brightens or fades. It is less illumination than evidence: enough to reveal a curve, an approaching wall, the comb of a distant frond, but never enough to disclose the whole.

The temperature is cool and perfectly even. There are no warm currents, no seasons, no shallow waters heated from above. The liquid has the steady chill of a deep reservoir sealed before weather began. It is dense, slightly bitter, and breathable to most of the animals. Tiny suspended spores give it a faint silken resistance. Movement through it feels like passing through an endless cold infusion of leaves.

Despite the darkness, the place is loud.

The sound comes first from the structures. Water presses through millions of narrow pores in the pillar walls, producing a permanent organ-note: low, immense, and without rhythm. Large hollow trunks answer one another across the open distances. Some hum below hearing and are felt in cartilage and cavities of the body. Others emit clear tones where water passes through slotted leaves or through the mouths of ancient chambers. The whole abyss sounds engineered, though there are no mechanisms and nothing moves enough to count as machinery.

Animals add layers to this continuous voice. Long-bodied grazers rasp the pillar skins with circular mouths. Shoals of small shelled creatures snap their hinged fins to hold position, making a ceaseless dry crackle. Blind swimmers announce themselves with blunt pulses that travel astonishingly far through the water. Their calls return from terraces and bridges as dense fields of echoes, giving the darkness an audible shape.

Fauna is abundant, but rarely seen in crowds. The open water is too large, and the visible range too short. Life arrives one presence at a time.

A raylike grazer may emerge from blackness, wide as a house and almost transparent, its stomach full of luminous plant fibers. It passes close to a hanging leaf and removes a strip with the delicacy of a page being cut. Farther out, herds of slender animals hang upright and motionless, arranged around resonant root-cables. They listen for vibrations conducted through the plant body. Predators resemble loose bundles of fronds until they unfold their jaws. Small creatures occupy every pore of the architecture: pale mites in bark seams, fan-tailed filterers inside vents, shell-backed animals walking slowly along ceilings, and ribbon-thin scavengers threaded through the galleries.

Nothing chases for long. Nothing blooms suddenly. There are no migrations, storms, tides, or outbreaks. Birth and feeding continue, yet the total pattern does not alter. For every strip of leaf consumed, an equal strip thickens elsewhere. Every animal that leaves a chamber is replaced by another entering through a different opening. Bodies decay, but decay feeds roots at exactly the rate new bodies are formed. Even the suspended debris remains constant: the same density of husks, scales, spores, and minute drifting eggs throughout the water.

This equilibrium gives the place its strangest rule. Events occur, but conditions do not.

A bridge can lose fibers without weakening. A colony can divide without becoming larger. An animal can die without increasing the amount of death in the world. There is no visible hand correcting these exchanges; balance is simply the medium’s oldest law. Perhaps time passes locally, inside flesh and appetite, while the landscape as a whole stands outside it.

The extreme elevation is sensed rather than surveyed. Animals orient not by a surface or bottom but by the pillars’ grain. Their roots point downward, though no one has found what they point toward. Buoyant spores ascend, though none return with evidence of an upper sea. Pressure should increase with descent, yet after a certain depth it remains unchanged, as if the abyss has reached a final depth and continues downward only in distance. A falling shell sinks for years and is sometimes encountered again higher on the same pillar, still falling.

Here and there, the built character becomes unmistakable. Terraces repeat in measured intervals. Openings align across gaps too wide for branches ever to have touched. Some pillars contain vertical shafts of exact proportion, each lined with overlapping scales that admit animals of one size and exclude all others. Bridges bow with identical curves. Chambers resonate in complementary notes. Yet all these forms are grown, healed, grazed, and inhabited. There are no tools, no cut marks, no metal, no relics of constructors. If a design exists, it is inherited as growth behavior. The blueprint is sap.

One region opens into a wide gulf between seven pillars. They stand in a ring beyond the reach of any ordinary sight. Their positions can be inferred from seven continuous tones, each at a different pitch. Between them hangs a web of sparse root-cables. Enormous animals move along these cables in darkness, touching them with long sensory limbs and sending bass vibrations through the entire ring. Below hangs an inverted forest: trunks descending from a bridge canopy, branching downward into finer and finer twigs until they end in clouds of stationary leaves.

Elsewhere, a single leaf forms a plain. Its surface extends beyond the dim biological glow, gently ribbed, covered by a thin layer of colder water. Animals cross it in slow files, feeding from hairlike growths. Along its edges the leaf curls upward into walls, then closes overhead, turning the plain into a vast tubular hall. The hall carries sound for hundreds of kilometers. A scrape made at one end may be heard at the other as a soft roar.

There are places where the plants have withdrawn from their own architecture. These are the emptiest spaces: ancient vaulted cavities several kilometers across, enclosed by smooth vegetal stone. Nothing grows on the walls. Few animals enter. The permanent hum becomes purer there, stripped of clicks and grazing noises. In the center of each chamber floats a loose knot of roots that touches nothing. It neither rises nor sinks. Small blind creatures circle it but never land.

No one knows whether these voids are rooms, organs, wounds, or simply the inevitable emptiness around which a living structure learned to endure.

Beyond them, the garden continues. Pillars alternate with open water. Bridges appear, vanish into blackness, and emerge against other masses whose distance cannot be judged. Pale fauna pulse once and are gone. A curtain-leaf trembles, though the water is still. The seven tones of one region fade into the many-toned pressure-song of another.

There is no dawn to reveal the whole, no storm to rearrange it, no collapse that has not already been accounted for. The landscape has spent its age finding the only form it can permanently hold.

It is alive everywhere.

It is made everywhere.

And in all its feeding, sounding, drifting multitudes, it never becomes anything other than what it has always been.
