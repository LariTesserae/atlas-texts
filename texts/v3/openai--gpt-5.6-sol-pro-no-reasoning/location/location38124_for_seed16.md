The city occupies the inside of a single stone bell.

It is not a bell made by hands, though generations have cut stairs, chambers, and streets into it. The bell is a hollow basalt blister buried beneath a salt waste. Its interior is broad enough for districts, but from any balcony the opposite wall remains visible through the dim red air. Everything belongs to one enclosure: the black vault overhead, the sloping walls, and the low basin where the city gathers around an emptiness.

There has never been water here.

Heat rises through cracks in the basin floor. It comes in breaths, each deep enough to stir the whole cavity. During the long exhalations, red mineral dust fountains from narrow vents and hangs aloft for hours. On the inhalation, cooler air is pulled down through hidden chimneys leading to the desert above. The pressure change makes the stone sing. Grooves in the walls catch the moving air and produce low organ notes; shutters chatter; suspended ladders strike masonry. At the height of the cycle, conversation becomes impossible.

The inhabitants call this weather.

The city is low and spread thinly around the basin, despite the enclosing vault. Its neighborhoods are separated by great intervals of bare stone. A shrine, six houses, and a kiln may stand together under one shelf; then there is nothing for a hundred paces but dust, abandoned paving, and the scars of old foundations. Bridges reach across gaps where no buildings remain. Roads end at bricked apertures. The emptiness does not suggest recent ruin. It has been designed around, repaired, inherited. The city has always contained more absence than habitation.

Its oldest structures are carved directly from the inner shell. Their corners have been rounded by ages of dust-laden wind until façades resemble worn teeth. Later buildings are assembled from fitted basalt slabs, red ceramic, salt brick, and a pale, porous stone quarried near the upper vents. There is little metal. Doors pivot on stone pins. Roofs are ribbed with fired-clay beams and sealed with mineral pitch. Cords, mats, baskets, and hinges are made from the city’s plants.

These plants are lush in their own dry fashion.

Dark gardens occupy warm fissures and the undersides of streets. Their growths do not resemble forests so much as masses of folded leather, coral, and hair. Thick crimson fans spread from cracks, collecting sulfurous condensates and windborne minerals. Rope-vines hang in curtains from the upper ledges, drinking gases through thousands of silver pores. In the deeper heat, colonies of translucent bladders inflate and collapse with each subterranean breath. They store no water. Their sap is oil, their tissues packed with salts and waxes that remain fluid in the heat.

Where a vent has been carefully terraced, vegetation can swallow an entire courtyard. Black fronds arch over ovens. Orange nodules cluster on walls. Pale threads crawl nightly across masonry and retreat before the hottest exhalation. Gardeners guide them with ridges of ash, chipped channels, and patterns of shade. They prune using obsidian flakes and cauterize cuts with hot stones. Nothing grazes the growth. Nothing pollinates it. There are no insects hidden in the folds, no lizards on the walls, no birds in the vault.

There is no animal life at all.

The plants propagate through pressure. When the cavern’s breath falls into a certain rhythm, mature bladders rupture with sharp reports, firing dust-fine spores into the circulating air. For several hours the whole city glitters with them. People wrap cloth over their mouths and seal food jars, while streets and towers vanish behind bronze haze. By morning, every sheltered crack bears a faint red nap. Most of it dies. Some finds hot stone and begins to thicken.

The absence of animals gives the place a peculiar stillness beneath its noise. Nothing rustles by intention except people. There are no incidental cries, no scratching in walls, no wings startled from roofs. When a loose pebble moves, someone moved it. When a hanging garden sways against the direction of the air, someone is concealed behind it—or a new vent is opening.

The city’s loudness is mechanical and geological. Basalt booms as it cools. Clay vanes clack in the chimneys. Kilns roar continuously, fed with pressed cakes of resinous plant tissue. Stone hammers strike wedges in the quarries. Grain-like mineral nodules are ground in broad mortars. At intervals, warning gongs of thin ceramic are sounded from the upper wards, not by metal clappers but by weighted cords snapping taut in the wind.

Most labor follows the cavern’s respiratory cycle. During intake, crews climb toward the chimneys to spread collecting cloths in the descending air. Dust from the outer salt-field brings useful trace minerals and, rarely, splinters of glassy stone. During exhalation, they descend, closing shutters behind them. Kilns draw best then. Resin is rendered, bricks fired, pigments fixed, and food-bulbs roasted in sealed clay.

The people cultivate broad beds of ash-root beneath porous tiles. Ash-root grows as interlocked plates, slowly digesting volcanic gases and mineral salts. Its inner layers are fatty and bitter. Other crops provide starch in chalky stems, spice from blistered skins, and a dark syrup distilled from wax-sap. All agriculture is a form of chemistry practiced by hand. Gardeners test heat with the palm, acidity with strips of dyed fiber, and poisonous drafts with delicate fronds carried in ceramic cages.

There are no beasts of burden. Everything is dragged, rolled, lifted, or carried. The principal streets descend gently so heavy sledges can travel under their own weight, restrained by teams holding braided ropes. Returning uphill takes whole neighborhoods. This has shaped the city more powerfully than any ruler. Kilns stand below quarries. Storehouses stand below kilns. Refuse slides toward the basin, where it is sorted, burned, dissolved in caustic plant oils, or packed into new bricks.

At the city’s center lies the Quiet Floor, a wide oval of polished black stone without building or garden. The name is comparative. Here the songs of the walls cancel one another, leaving only the hiss of vents and the occasional deep knock from below. No one has dug into it successfully. Tools soften, stone wedges split, and workers become confused about distance. A person may walk toward its center for an hour and find that the rim remains only thirty paces behind.

Because space behaves imperfectly there, the city avoids building inward. Its emptiness is maintained by law. Markets form around the perimeter, their awnings facing away. Processions circle it but never cross. Children are taught that the floor is not a place but the underside of a place: a patch where the world has worn thin from long use.

The rest of the cavern is deeply weathered and stable. Its cracks are named. Its collapses are recorded across centuries. Certain pillars are renewed grain by grain, patched with ceramic collars and plant resin. Wind channels cut by ancient exhalations have reached smooth, settled curves. The city does not fight erosion; it tunes it. Masons widen some grooves so they sing warnings before pressure surges. They blunt other edges to keep destructive harmonics from gathering. Entire towers serve as instruments, changing tone when their foundations shift.

Light comes mostly from heat. The basin vents glow dull red, and the gardens shine faintly where mineral films form on their skins. Households burn wax-sap in shallow covered lamps, conserving oxygen and keeping soot from the crops. Ceramic mirrors, glazed bone-white, pass small amounts of light down stairs and around corners. The upper vault remains nearly invisible. Sometimes, during the strongest intake, a remote chimney admits a blade of white daylight from the salt-field. It cuts through the cavern like a solid pillar.

People stop work to watch dust rotate inside it.

Then the breath reverses. The blade narrows, reddens, and disappears. The city shutters itself. Gardens inflate along the walls. The stone organs begin their enormous note, low enough to be felt through floors before it is heard. Across the empty districts, clay towers answer one another. Ropes tighten, furnaces brighten, spores erupt, and every hanging vine leans toward the central dark.

The bell sounds, though nothing has struck it.
