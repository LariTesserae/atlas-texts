In the cradle of a newly birthed landform, where the earth itself was only yesterday scraped from its deep slumber, lies the Domain of Verdigris Hollow—a place where the very air seems to hum with a low, mournful song, as though it were weeping for the world that birthed it.

The terrain is low, almost knee-high at its highest points, rising gently in folds of damp earth and spongy moss. It’s a landscape that feels young, freshly shaped, sharp in its ridges and valleys, like a sculptor’s clay untouched by time. Each ridge is a jagged spine, carved clean into the earth by rivers that have yet to know the slow patience of seasons, their waters young, full of life and energy. The surface is packed with materials: clay, gravel, and dark loam, each layer telling a story of its own making. The ground here is saturated but not stagnant; water flows through cracks in the rocks and pools in hollows, creating small marshes that shimmer faintly under the dim, diffuse light. Weeping rocks stand like sentinels, dripping with a fine mist that mingles with the condensing atmosphere, giving the air a hazy, ethereal quality.

Vegetation is sparse but present, a scattering of hardy plants that cling tightly to the fragile soil. These plants seem to have adapted specifically to this environment—small and low to the ground, their roots digging deep into the wet earth. Some grow in clusters, their leaves broad and waxy, glistening with moisture, while others appear almost alien, their forms twisting into strange shapes that might belong to another world entirely. They are saturated within the bounds of what this place can support, tightly packed together in groves or small, self-contained gardens that seem to thrive despite the constant dampness.

Temperature is cool, lending a sense of stillness to the hollow. The air is heavy, thick with moisture, and carries the faint scent of iron and wet wood. It’s cool enough to make breath visible in the mornings, the kind of cool that seeps into your bones but doesn’t quite chill you to the core. This temperature, combined with the high humidity, creates a constant mist that clings to the edges of everything, softening the outlines of objects and muting sounds in a hazy whisper.

Erosion here is a constant process, the land still in its infancy, its features sharp and defined by the forces that shape it. Water carves small channels through the earth, leaving behind streaks of clay and sediment. Rivers flow swiftly, their paths uncertain, carving new channels as they go. The ground itself seems to shift slowly, as though it is still adjusting to its own form, the surface alive with movement beneath your feet.

The scale of this place is intimate, small enough that you could traverse it in a single day if you hurried, yet large enough that it feels like a world unto itself, with its own rules and boundaries. At any given moment, you might feel as though you are wandering through a grove—or perhaps a single, massive room whose ceiling is the sky, veiled by clouds. The landscape is packed, with no room between presences. Each element, whether natural or built, seems to exist in perfect harmony, as though they were always meant to belong here.

Beneath this land, built upon it, and woven into it is an urban presence of sorts, though it is primitive and ancient by today’s standards. Settlements here have emerged organically, structures carved from the earth itself, using simple tools and techniques that speak to a time before metal was forged from ore. The buildings are low, squat, almost subterranean, their surfaces blending seamlessly with the landscape. Roofs are made of thatch and leaves, held together by ropes of dried vine, while walls are composed of compacted earth and stone, their forms irregular but functional. Infrastructure is minimal, but layers upon layers have been built over time, suggesting a history of resilience and adaptation. Roads, if they can be called such, are barely visible, worn into the ground by the passage of countless feet.

Technology here is primitive, yet ingenious. Tools are crafted from wood and stone, with occasional hints of polished bone or copper. Simple machines, like levered carts and waterwheels powered by tiny streams, suggest a people who understand the power of their surroundings and are adept at harnessing it. Construction techniques are rudimentary but effective, using natural forms and materials to create structures that seem almost alive, as though they are growing out of the land itself.

The atmosphere is one of quiet tension, as though the world is still holding its breath, caught between its beginnings and its unknown future. Sounds are muted, mostly quiet except for the occasional rush of water, the gentle patter of rain, or the distant call of unseen creatures. Yet beneath this quietness lies a sense of dynamism, a subtle energy that permeates the air, a reminder that this place is alive with motion, even if it is not immediately visible.

Ecology here is fragile and unusual. Fauna is rare, but where it exists, it is adapted to this wet, young environment. Small, glowing insects flit about in the dim light, their bodies luminescent, while burrowing mammals, almost furred, make their dens deep within the earth. Birds with broad wings and soft plumage swoop low over the hollow, their calls echoing faintly in the stillness. The vegetation, while sparse, supports a delicate balance, feeding the few creatures that inhabit this land, their populations tightly interwoven with the rhythms of the environment.

Weirdness permeates the hollow, not in the overt sense of magic or horror, but in the subtle, inexplicable qualities of its existence. Rocks seem to shift their positions overnight, rivers carve paths that defy logic, and the light seems to change subtly, as though it were alive and responsive to the world around it. It is a place where the rules of nature are still being written, and where the familiar gives way to the strange, inviting curiosity but also a sense of caution.

This is Verdigris Hollow—an ancient yet young place, alive with the potential of what it might become. It is a place of wetness and motion, of saturated life and primitive ingenuity, where the boundaries between the natural and the built blur into a single, cohesive whole. It is a place of quiet mystery, where the sounds of the world are muted but never still, and where the air whispers secrets of its own making.
