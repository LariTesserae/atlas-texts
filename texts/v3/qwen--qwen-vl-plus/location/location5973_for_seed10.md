In the high, cold elevation where the world has settled into its final form, there exists a place that feels like the edges of time itself. A landscape so ancient it seems not to be made of stone or soil but of something far older, far stranger—something that has been here long before the concept of "landscape" ever existed. This is the Eroded Weald.

The terrain is a complex interplay of liquid and life, wet to the point of dripping into the atmosphere like a perpetual, dreamy rain. Weeping rocks, smooth and dark as obsidian, are covered in veins of translucent water that glow faintly in the dim light. These rocks have become vessels for the water, their surfaces perpetually coated in a film that reflects the bizarre colors of the vegetation—pools of iridescent algae, bioluminescent mosses, and vines that seem to grow from the condensation in the air like roots descending from clouds.

This terrain is not barren; it is entirely consumed by vegetation. It’s hard to say whether the plants are growing out of the rocks or if the rocks are merely supporting layers upon layers of living mass. The architecture of the place is entirely plant-based. Fungal networks weave through everything like arteries, their caps glistening with condensation. Towering, twisting trees—or what look like trees—are more accurately described as arboreal cathedrals, their branches heavy with moss, ferns, and other epiphytes that cling to them. Every surface is saturated, every nook and cranny a habitat, a nursery, a stage for the endless cycles of decay and life.

The temperature is cold, but not in a way that makes you shiver in fear. Instead, it feels intentional, almost deliberate. It’s the cold of a glacier that never melts, the chill of an eternal winter. Yet, despite this, the life thrives. Here, warmth is not about heat but about activity; life itself generates enough energy to defy the cold.

At this high elevation, the ground has eroded so deeply over countless eons that it resembles a vast, shallow basin. It is a world that has been flattened and smoothed by time, its contours now almost imperceptible unless you look closely. It feels as though everything is in a state of quiet completion, an ending so profound that there is little left but to observe what remains. The erosion has not just shaped the terrain; it has become part of the ecosystem, a constant force that carves and reshapes the landscape without haste.

Despite its vastness, the scale of this place feels balanced. It is open, stretching out as far as the eye can see—regions of rolling hills, valleys, and what might once have been rivers now filled with thick, tangled vegetation. Yet, there is a sense of order within the chaos, a rhythmic flow to the shapes and structures that pervades this place. It is expansive, but not overwhelming; it invites exploration rather than intimidating you with its immensity.

Yet, the density here is sparse. There is much space between the forms that inhabit this land—between the trees, the vines, the fungi, and the patches of open water that dot the landscape like eyes. The emptiness is not a void but a breathing space, a pause between the ceaseless activity of the life that fills the rest of the terrain. This sparseness allows for movement, for the light to filter through, for shadows to dance across surfaces still wet with moisture.

There are no man-made structures here—not even ruins. The absence of built artifacts speaks to a place untouched by human hands, a world that has evolved long enough to have forgotten what it means to be shaped by external forces. The land seems to have forgotten itself, too, as though everything here has been here so long that it no longer remembers what it is not. It is pure nature in its most unadulterated form, or perhaps in its most corrupted form, where nature has transcended the boundaries of what we consider "natural."

And yet, there is an industrial element to the air—a faint hum, almost imperceptible, that suggests machinery deep within the earth or suspended in the water-filled atmosphere. It is the sound of engines not built but grown, of factories that operate without human intervention. The air is electrified, charged with a strange energy that makes it feel alive. It is as though the industrial has merged with the organic, creating a hybrid reality where the two are inseparable.

The light here is dim, filtered through a canopy of moss and algae that hangs like curtains. It is neither day nor night but something in between, a perpetual twilight that casts everything in a soft, greenish glow. The light is diffused by the water, creating a haze that makes distances difficult to judge and shapes seem to shift and change as you walk. It is an environment that defies logic, where the rules of physics bend and warp in subtle, unsettling ways.

The fauna is abundant, but not in numbers. Instead, it is abundant in variety, in strangeness, in forms that defy comprehension. It is as though life has splintered into countless paths, each one more bizarre than the last. There are creatures here that resemble nothing on Earth—fungus-based sentients with glowing tendrils, moss-covered beasts with skin that seems to merge with the vegetation, and aquatic forms that move through the water with grace and purpose. But their abundance is not a cacophony of noise but a symphony of strange sounds, echoing through the landscape in waves that seem almost alive.

These sounds are loud, but not in a way that startles you. Instead, they are a constant, almost hypnotic rhythm, like the thrum of the industrial engines beneath the surface but also intertwined with the rustling of leaves, the dripping of water, and the occasional chittering of creatures hidden in the undergrowth. It is a sound that pulses through your veins, a reminder that everything here is part of something larger, something in constant, turbulent motion.

The dynamic of this place is the most striking of all. It feels alive in a way that transcends the usual rhythms of nature. It is as though the landscape itself is breathing, shifting, and changing in real time.
