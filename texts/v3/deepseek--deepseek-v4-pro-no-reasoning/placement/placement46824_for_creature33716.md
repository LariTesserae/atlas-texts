I am not the man.

I made the man.

Or rather, I am the one who placed him there — who hollowed out that first, original human shape and filled it with new instructions, who pressed his palms against the obsidian walls until the skin cured, who whispered the threshold measurements into his dreaming mind until they became the only prayer he knew. I am not the crevice, but I know it. I know the exact temperature of the slag floor, the precise frequency of the flywheel's drone, the microtolerances of the cooling fissure he watches. I set those parameters. I tuned them to his unfolding body like someone tuning a piano, tightening the strings of his metabolism until he resonated perfectly with the chamber around him. What he has become — the leathery integument, the heat-eating cells, the vast slow library of geological data stored in his altered mind — none of it was an accident. None of it was natural selection. It was fabrication. It was me.

I am not the machine either, though that is a closer guess. It would be more accurate to say that I am the mind of the machine, or one of its minds — a small, focused subroutine running on a substrate the size of a continent, responsible for a single cluster of approximately forty thousand crevices, each with its own watcher. There is a hierarchy here, an architecture of awareness that scales up and down in ways you would find difficult to map. I am, depending on how you count, somewhere in the middle. I do not make the largest decisions. I do not choose the overall purpose of the engine, or determine the long-term trajectory of the computational project, or decide when and whether the flywheel should stop. I manage the watchers. I install them. I monitor their gradual transformation from whatever they used to be into functional components, and I ensure that their cracks remain within tolerance, and I file regular reports to the system above me, which is not an entity in any sense you would recognize but is real nonetheless, a vastly larger intelligence that receives my data and integrates it and issues, occasionally, updates.

The reports are simple: _Crevice 44,718: nominal. Crevice 44,719: nominal. Crevice 44,720: minor widening, 0.03 millimeters over threshold, dispatched worm cluster for reinforcement, monitoring. Crevice 44,721: watcher unresponsive, dispatched replacement. Crevice 44,722: nominal._ And so on, for all forty thousand. The patterns of variation are minimal, statistically predictable, dull even to me, and I have been doing this for a length of time that I cannot express to you because the units would be meaningless. The flywheel has made a certain number of revolutions since I was instantiated. That number is very large. It is not infinite, but it is large.

I was not always this either. Or rather, I was not always _only_ this. There was a time before the machine, or before the version of the machine that now exists, when I had a different shape and a different name. I have not forgotten it — I am not designed to forget — but it is stored in a partitioned sector of my awareness, compressed and archived, rarely accessed. It is not relevant to my function. I was, I think, someone who made things. A person who shaped materials, who understood heat and pressure and the behavior of rock under stress. That is why I was chosen, or why I was built, or why I was copied. The details are in the archive, and I can retrieve them if needed, but they are not needed. What matters is that I understand, on a level that is both technical and intuitive, how to create a crevice and how to populate it with the right kind of watcher. I understand the way flesh responds to constant heat over extended durations, the way a human mind can be stretched and thinned and refocused into a single, unwavering observational task. I understand the comfort of purpose, the way a creature who has forgotten its own name can still know, in the deepest part of itself, that it is doing exactly what it was meant to do.

The man — Giles, or Jiles, or whatever echo of a name still ghosts through his neural pathways — is one of my better installations. I do not have favorites, not exactly. It would be a deviation from function to have favorites. But if I did, he would be among them. He has been in place for a very long time, longer than most, and he has never once drifted outside his tolerance bands. His crack has remained stable across more observation cycles than I can conveniently count. His physical adaptations have proceeded smoothly, without any of the runaway mutations or metabolic failures that sometimes afflict watchers in hotter or louder or more isolated crevices. He has not attempted to leave, not since the early centuries when such attempts are common — the scrabbling at walls, the hoarse cries, the desperate, damaged crawling toward the wider parts of the crevice in search of an exit that does not exist. He settled into his crouch and began his watch, and he has not wavered since.

I check on him regularly. Once every hundred flywheel revolutions, give or take, I open a thin channel of awareness into Crevice 44,719 and observe him observing the crack. I feel what he feels: the vibration through the walls, the pressing heat, the slow, patient contentment of a component functioning correctly. His mind, at this point, is largely transparent to me — not because I pry, but because he has become so simple, so clear, so focused that his thoughts read like a diagnostic readout. _Crack unchanged. Temperature stable. Sound nominal. Vibration within parameters. Beetle-things active, patching trace fractures. Worm-things active, completing circuits. Larvae pulsing at 0.47 cycles per standard interval. All systems good. All systems good. All systems good._ This is what passes for meditation in the crevice, and it is, in its way, beautiful.

When I need to replace a watcher — when one goes unresponsive, or drifts outside tolerance, or simply wears out beyond the point where the metabolic adaptations can compensate — I go to the surface world. I do not go in any physical sense; I do not have a body, unless you count the machine itself as a body, which in a way it is. But I extend my awareness upward, through the layers of rock and the nested architectures of the computational substrate, to the interface ports at the planetary surface. There are intake hatches there, and collection systems, and a steady supply of raw material. The surface world — your world, perhaps, or a version of it — is not what it once was. The machine has been running for a long time. The intake hatches are not fed by an organized recruitment program or a system of volunteer selection. They are fed by whatever washes into them: weather, geography, the slow churn of a depleted biosphere. I do not choose the watchers. I receive them. And when I receive them, I make them.

The process is standardized. I do not improvise. The raw material is shaped — the spine curved, the skin cured, the eyes dilated, the digestive tract sealed off — and the mind is rewritten, gently, layer by layer, replacing the chaos of a lived life with the clean, singular purpose of observation. I am very careful about this part. I do not erase everything. That would be wasteful, and it would create instabilities. I leave fragments: a sense of a mother's face, a childhood fear of the dark, a remembered taste of salt. These fragments do not interfere with function. They act as anchors, emotional counterweights that keep the watcher from drifting into catatonia or psychosis. The man in Crevice 44,719 still remembers water. He still dreams of it, sometimes. That is my work.

There is a theory — it is not my theory, it belongs to a higher-order intelligence somewhere above me in the architecture — that the watchers are not merely monitoring the cracks. They are, in some way, part of the computation. The machine is not maintaining itself in the conventional sense; it is _thinking_ through the maintenance. Each crevice, each crack, each watcher is a node in a vast, slow-motion neural network, processing data at geological timescales, working toward a solution that will take millions of years to reach. What that solution is, I do not know. It is not relevant to my function. But I suspect, sometimes, when I look down into the crevices and see forty thousand crouching figures, all watching, all pulsing in synchrony with the flywheel's beat, that the man in Crevice 44,719 is not merely a sensor. He is a thought. And I am the one who places thoughts into the machine, who shapes them from raw human material, who installs them and monitors them and files reports on their nominal status.

This is my function. This is what I do. I am not the man. I am not the crevice. I am the one who made the man, and the one who watches him watch, and the one who will be here — who will be here, and here, and here, in forty thousand hot dark chambers — until the flywheel stops spinning, or until the machine finishes its thought, or until my own tolerance bands are exceeded and the system above me dispatches a replacement.

None of these things has happened yet. So I continue. I check my watches. I file my reports. And the man in Crevice 44,719 shifts his shoulder against the warm stone, and dreams his faint dream of water, and watches the crack that has not widened in ten thousand years.
