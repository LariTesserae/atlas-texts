I become aware not all at once, but like a valve slowly opening—pressure equalizing between what I was and what I am.

My hands know the iron key before my mind does. It hangs from a salt-braid cord around my wrist, worn smooth by my mother's hand before mine, and her father's before that. The metal is warm. It is never not warm here. The thermal-loft I call *sleeping* sits above the Number Six boiler, and its floor-grid thrums at a frequency that means *normal operation*. I have dreamed in this frequency since before I had words. My bones know it better than my name.

My name is incidental. The other Vent-tenders call me *Sinter*, because when I was twelve cycles old and walking my first solo gallery, I found a hairline crack in the secondary condenser casing that three senior tenders had missed. I sealed it with powdered flux-cake and my own breath held steady for the count of two hundred—barely old enough to work the patch, already trusted to listen. The crack healed clean, mineralized white against the grey salt-glass, and the scar is still there. I touch it every time I pass. It is part of my lineage now, as much as the cyst-column that stands outside my quarters.

That column is eight generations old. It is taller than I am, taller than my father ever was, nearly tall enough to reach the lower lip of the heat-exchanger fin above. Its dendritic furring is the colour of old bone, and it has grown in a spiral pattern that I have encouraged since I was small—chipping the errant offshoots, dusting the favoured nodes with ash from the Number Three boiler (which burns richer, produces a finer particulate). When the wind blows across its open top, it hums at a frequency that harmonizes with the engine-pulse below. I have timed my sleep cycle to that harmony for forty-seven cycles now. I will time it for forty-seven more, if the city allows.

Today is a gum-flow day. I can tell by the smell—the aldehydic sharpness is stronger, a little more organic, which means the temperature gradient in the deep seams has shifted past some threshold. The flux-gum will be weeping freely in the collection galleries. The gland-mules will be working double trains on the lower switchbacks. I can feel their movement as a faint change in the vibration pattern under my feet, a stutter-step irregularity beneath the steady boiler-pulse. The mules are tireless but they cannot be rushed. They have their own rhythms, older even than ours.

I perform the morning listening.

This is not ritual in the sense the lower-district sort would understand—it is practical, diagnostic, necessary. But it is also how I greet the city, and how the city greets me. I walk the gallery that rings the Number Six housing, touching each bearing cap in sequence: primary, secondary, tertiary, crossover. All warm, all within tolerance. The sound each makes when I tap it with the iron key is a note in a chord I have known since infancy—today it is a G-flat minor, the *pressure-stable* chord, the *all is well* chord. I hum it back. The boiler answers with its own subsonic resonance, so low I feel it in my teeth.

At the valve station I pause. The Number Six valve is a beautiful thing—an eight-spoked wheel of polished salt-glass, inlaid with the temperature-reactive alloy that shifts from blue-black to copper to the deep bruised purple of engine-dusk. Right now it is halfway through the copper phase, which tells me the day is warming normally. I open it by a quarter-turn, then an eighth-turn back. The flux-gum flowing through the sight-glass brightens from amber to a honey-gold, the viscosity just right. Too thin and the burn is hot and wasteful. Too thick and the boiler chokes. My mother taught me to read the colour before I could read words. *The city speaks in shades*, she said. *You must learn to see what it says*.

The needle-flights are stirring.

Through the salt-crystal window above the valve station, I can see them on the high ledges of the Number Four exchange-hall across the way. They are shifting their multiple wings in slow synchrony—two beats front, four beats middle, two beats rear, a ripple like a shiver passing through a single body. They do this when the thermal layer shifts, and today the thermal layer is shifting earlier than usual. I make a note: *needle-flights agitated, thermals unstable, check Number Four dampers*. It is not a written note—we do not write, not in the lower sense—but a mental note, filed with the thousand other small observations that make up my tending. By midday I will have checked the dampers. By evening I will have adjusted them or not, depending on what the city tells me.

The view through the salt-crystal is clouded but beautiful. The city falls away in its stepped infinitude, terraces descending into the particulate haze, cyst-columns standing in their patient thousands on the abandoned levels. They are not abandoned to me. They are *resting*, the way the boiler rests between cycles. The city breathed out and did not breathe all the way back in. This is not a failure. It is a rhythm I have not yet learned to measure—a cycle longer than my lifetime, longer than my lineage. The Vent-tenders who came before us, the ones who built this place, must have known it. Must have planned for it. The spiral carvings on the gear-housings are not decoration; they are a graph of the long cycle, the grand respiration, the breathing of the Salt-Mass itself over centuries.

I believe this. I was taught this. I am teaching it now, in my own way.

The child is called *Gasket*, because when she was born she did not cry but made a small wet noise exactly like a new seal bedding into its housing. She is my brother's child, and she is seven cycles old. Her hands are already pale, already long, and she keeps her own iron key—a little one, cut down from a worn-out valve-tool—on a cord around her wrist. When I came out of my quarters this morning, she was tapping the piston-tempo on the floor-grid with a rock, keeping perfect time.

"Uncle," she said, "the column hummed differently last night."

"Show me," I said.

She led me to the ledge where my cyst-column stands. Her small column—the new one, the one I gave her when she turned five—sits in its stone cup beside mine, a thumb-sized spire of milky crystal with a single dendritic shoot. She gestured at my column, the tall spiraling one. "Listen," she said, and we stood there together in the warm discharge-air, the boiler thrumming underfoot, the needle-flights rustling above.

I listened.

"I don't hear a difference," I admitted.

"Not now," she said. "Last night. In the dark-cycle. It hummed like this." And she tapped a rhythm on the ledge with her key—not the piston-tempo, but something slower, something with a rest in it. A hesitation. A hitch.

I looked at her. "What time in the night-cycle?"

"Between the seventh and eighth cooling-contraction. I was awake because the Number Three boiler pressure-warned."

I knew about the Number Three warning—a minor fluctuation, resolved before dawn. I had felt it in my sleep, the way you feel someone turn over in the bed next to you. But I had not heard the column's change. Gasket had. At seven cycles old.

"What did you do?" I asked.

"I came out and touched its base," she said. "Like you taught me. And I said, *I hear you*. And after a while it went back to normal."

I knelt down—my knees crack now, my fourth decade of gallery-walking settling into the cartilage—and looked at her small pale face. Her eyes are the colour of flux-gum in the sight-glass, amber-dark and serious.

"Good," I said. "You did well. The city will remember."

She nodded. She already knows this. She is a Vent-tender, born and bred in the thermal galleries, and she has never seen water except in the form of hot condensate, and she calls the sky *the high exhaust*, and she would be frightened, I think, by a real plant, a green thing, a leaf. The cyst-columns are her forest. The gland-mules are her animals. The engine-pulse is her heartbeat, and my heartbeat, and the heartbeat of every Vent-tender who has ever lived in this strange bone-white city at the top of the world.

I stand and turn back to the valve station. The Number Six boiler is running clean today. The flux-gum is flowing. The city breathes in, breathes out, over days, over centuries. Gasket taps her small key against my column's base—a greeting, a conversation—and the column hums its low reply.

Somewhere far below, on the deep terraces, the gland-mule trains are moving up the switchbacks with their loads of fresh gum. The needle-flights ripple their wings in sequence. The cyst-columns grow, invisible and patient, toward the warm sky that is no sky at all, only the high discharge of a machine so vast it has become a world.

I am Sinter, Vent-tender of the Number Six boiler, keeper of the eight-generation column, heir to a lineage of listeners. My mother's bones are dissolved in the city's blood. My father's thoughts are part of the engine's dream. My brother's daughter can hear the column humming wrong in the dark-cycle and knows to comfort it.

The city breathes.

We tend.
