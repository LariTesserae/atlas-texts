She moves through the sump like something the sump itself dreamed into being.

Mae—though she rarely thinks of herself by name anymore, has become simply *I* in the darkness, a point of awareness moving through the hum—descends the metal staircase from her alcove with the care of long practice. Her left knee protests the first ten steps, then settles into its familiar ache. The handrail vibrates against her palm. The frogs are chorusing in their layered rhythms, and she reads their voices like a status board: sump three population stable, sump seven's big male still dominant, the juveniles near her alcove piping their reedy calls without distress. Nothing has entered. Nothing is wrong.

The north pipe gallery first. Always first.

Her knuckles find the first flange without her eyes needing to direct them—a hollow *tock* that resonates up her finger bones. Good. Still good. She moves along the pipe run, tapping as she goes, a human sonar mapping the interior of iron arteries she will never see. *Tock. Tock. Thud.* She stops. The flange just before the pressure-reducing valve, the one that sweats more than the others, has gone dull since yesterday. Not critical—not yet—but the corrosion is advancing inside, eating the iron from within like a secret. She makes a mental note: *monitor, check again in twelve cycles.* She would write it down, but the logbook pages won't hold new ink in this humidity, and anyway, she will remember. She always remembers.

The big pressure gauge on the main feed line reads 2.4 bar, the needle trembling in its usual arrhythmia. She taps the glass with her fingernail—a different tap than the flanges, lighter, almost tender—and the needle jumps, steadies. The gauge is older than she is. Its brass face is fogged with internal condensation, the numerals faded to ghosts, but she can read it as clearly as her own palm. Some part of her brain has been calibrated to these instruments for so long that she no longer translates numbers into meaning; she simply *knows*, the way a sailor knows the wind has shifted.

"Morning, Henry," she says.

Big Henry rumbles at her. The pump housing is warm under her palm, a steady heat that radiates through the cast iron. She checks the grease fitting, finds it dry, and pulls the grease gun from her belt. Three pumps of the handle. The bearing sighs into silence. Henry has been overheating lately—nothing catastrophic, just a degree or two above its historical baseline—and she has been greasing it more often, compensating for wear with attention. She'll need to adjust the packing soon, maybe replace the seal. She has spares somewhere, salvaged from decommissioned units, stored in a crate near the electrical room. She's been saving them for when Henry really needs them. The definition of *really needs* has shifted over the years, stretched and deferred, and she knows one day she will stretch it too far, and Henry will seize, and that will be that.

But not today. Today, Henry runs.

She climbs to the catwalks. The narrow grating sags slightly under her weight, a familiar give she adjusts for without thought. Up here, near the ceiling panels, the light is fractionally better—the fiberglass sheets are filthy with decades of mineral deposits, but the grey sky still bleeds through, a diffuse amber that feels almost like daytime. She walks the high route, checking for pooling water, for new rust blooms, for fern fronds pushing through the grating. She finds a cluster of ferns near vent shaft four, their pale green fingers reaching up toward the light. They've grown since last week, spreading through the gaps in the metal.

She crouches—the knee protests, she ignores it—and pulls them out by the roots. The fronds come away wet, trailing dark soil that has accumulated from nothing, from dust and dead insects and the patient labor of decay. She holds one for a moment, examining it. The green is almost luminous in this place, a color that belongs to another world. Part of her wants to let them grow, to see how far they'd spread, whether they'd eventually colonize the whole catwalk, the pipes, the pump housings, a green tide reclaiming the industrial for the living. But they'll trip someone eventually. A maintenance worker, a drifter, herself in the dark. She tucks the fern into her pocket. She'll add it to the moss bed near her alcove, where it can grow without endangering anyone.

The catwalk ends at a junction platform, and she descends again, three flights of stairs, past the Twins (running in tandem, perfectly synchronized, their twin hums braiding into a single chord), past Old Lady (chugging along with the slightly off-kilter rhythm that means her impeller is still slightly unbalanced, has been slightly unbalanced for seventeen years, will outlast them all). The light dims as she moves deeper, away from the ceiling panels, into the spaces where only the hanging bulbs hold back the dark.

The sumps are waiting.

She checks them in order. Sump one: level stable, clarity good, the grey shapes of fish drifting in their slow circuits. She counts them—seven visible, which means probably twelve total, accounting for the ones that stay deep. One of them, the pale carp with the dark spot, rises when her shadow falls across the water. She breaks a corner off the bread she keeps in her pocket, presses it into a pellet, drops it. The carp takes it with that slow dignity, that ancient patience. She's been feeding it for fifteen years. She doesn't know how long carp live. She doesn't want to look it up.

Sump three: the big male frog is in his usual spot, a warm concrete ledge where a pipe coupling leaks a constant trickle of tepid water. He's enormous for his species—she's measured him against her hand, twice the size of the others—and his call is a deep stutter-croak that she can feel in her chest when she stands close. He watches her approach with one gold-rimmed eye. She doesn't feed him; the frogs have no need of her food, thriving on the insects that hatch from the water in endless abundance. But she pauses, as she always does, and they regard each other in the dimness. He has been here for at least eight years. She doesn't know how long frogs live either.

The ozone sump is last.

She saves it for last not out of fear—she tells herself this, and mostly believes it—but out of respect. It's the deepest sump, the one where the floor slopes down to meet the raw earth of the depression, where the steel sheet piling bleeds rust into water that smells different from the others. The smell is hard to describe, even to herself: a sharpness, a cleanness, underneath which is something organic, something that triggers a response she cannot name. Her hindbrain knows this smell. Her hindbrain does not like it.

The light here is nearly nonexistent. She carries a flashlight on her belt, a heavy black cylinder with a rubberized grip, and she clicks it on as she approaches. The beam cuts through the mist, illuminates the surface of the water. It's still, stiller than the other sumps—the agitation system was never installed here, or was removed, or failed and was never repaired. The water doesn't churn. It reflects the flashlight beam like dark glass.

She stands at the edge and watches.

The level gauge is mounted on the sheet piling, a simple float-and-arm mechanism that moves a needle across a faded scale. She checks it. Level stable. She shines the light across the surface, looking for—what? She doesn't know. She's seen something here twice, and both times she wasn't looking for it, and both times it wasn't there when she looked again. A long pale shape, sinuous, silent. Not a fish. Not a carp, not any species in her guidebook. Something that broke the surface and submerged without a ripple.

She doesn't believe in monsters. She believes in sumps. She believes in the tendency of water to accumulate things—nutrients, minerals, life, debris. A sump is a collector by definition, a low place where gravity brings everything that falls. And this place has been collecting for decades. Who knows what washed in, what was introduced, what evolved in the dark warm water, generation after generation, adapting to the mineral balance and the constant vibration and the perpetual twilight?

She doesn't believe in monsters. But she checks the ozone sump last, and she doesn't linger, and she doesn't feed whatever lives in it.

She turns away, clicks off the flashlight, and climbs back toward the light.

---

The man with the electrical panels comes today. She knows before she hears his footsteps, because the frogs stop. All at once, the chorus cuts off, that sudden silence that drops like a curtain, and she straightens from where she's been adjusting the packing on a secondary pump, and she listens. Footsteps on the metal stairs. Heavy boots, not the cautious shuffle of a drifter. The frogs know the difference. She knows the difference.

She meets him at the junction platform, where the main electrical trunk splits into branches that feed different sections. He's young—they're always young now—with a hard hat that looks new and a high-visibility vest that gleams obscenely in the dimness. He's carrying a tablet, which means the authority has upgraded their equipment since last time, which means someone topside still cares. The thought is almost painful.

"Mae?" He squints into the murk. "They said you'd be down here. I'm with maintenance. Electrical inspection."

"I know what you are," she says. Her voice comes out rougher than she intended—she doesn't speak much, days can pass without her using her voice for anything but talking to the fish—and she clears her throat. "The panels are this way. Watch your step. The grating's loose past the next junction."

He follows her, and she can feel his gaze on her back, the way he's taking in her patched coveralls, her grey hair, the grease gun on her belt, the way she moves through the pipes without hesitating. She remembers being looked at like that, back when she was the one squinting into dimness, back when the old-timers were the ones who knew the sump's secrets. Now she's the secret.

"Christ," he says, ducking under a low pipe. "How do you see anything down here?"

"Practice," she says.

She shows him the panels, stands back while he runs his diagnostics. The tablet glows pale blue, its screen impossibly bright in the amber dimness, and he taps and swipes and frowns at readings she can't see. She could tell him which panels run hot, which breakers trip in humid weather, which conduits have been drawing more current since the big storm three winters ago. But he doesn't ask, and she doesn't offer. You learn, in a place like this, not to volunteer information to people who won't be here long enough to use it.

"Everything looks good," he says finally, which means he's checking boxes on a form, which means he doesn't actually know. "You need anything? Supplies?"

She gives him the list. Canned food. Coffee. Batteries for the flashlight. Grease cartridges, if he can find the right size. A new logbook—the old one is down to its last swollen pages. He types it all into his tablet, and she wonders if the list will survive the journey topside, if it will be translated into a purchase order, if anyone will fill it. Sometimes they do. Sometimes they don't. She's learned to live with both outcomes.

"Anything else?" he asks.

She hesitates. There is something else. A question she's been holding for years, waiting for someone to ask, or waiting for the courage to ask herself. The young man with his hard hat and his tablet is not the person she imagined asking. But he's here. And the frogs are still silent.

"The authority," she says. "They still exist?"

He looks at her strangely. "Yeah. Of course. Why wouldn't they?"

"What do they do? Now? What does this place do?"

The question hangs in the damp air. It's the question she's been avoiding, the one that lurks at the edges of her rounds, the one she drowns out with the tapping of flanges and the greasing of bearings and the endless patient rhythm of maintenance. What does this place do? What has it ever done? The water moves through, and something happens to it, and then it moves on. Treatment, or transfer, or something else. She's never known, not really. She's never needed to know.

The young man shrugs. "Water treatment, I think. Groundwater remediation, maybe. Some kind of industrial legacy thing. Honestly, I just do the electrical inspections. You'd have to ask someone in operations."

"There is no one in operations," she says. "There's me."

He doesn't have an answer for that. He looks uncomfortable, the way they all do when they realize what she is—not an employee, not a caretaker, but something closer to a resident. A woman who lives in the machinery like a hermit crab in a shell. He packs up his tablet, mutters something about the stairs, and she leads him back to the exit, the junction platform, the long climb toward the surface. At the top, where the stairs meet the hatch, he pauses.

"You should come topside more often," he says. "Get some sun. This place..." He gestures at the dimness, the pipes, the dripping water. "This place can't be good for you."

She almost laughs. She's been down here for twenty-three years. The sump is in her bones, in her lungs, in the rhythm of her heartbeat. She doesn't know who she would be without the hum.

"I'll think about it," she says, which is not a lie, exactly. She does think about it. She thinks about it every day, and every day she goes back to her rounds.

The hatch closes behind him. The frogs, after a long moment, resume their chorusing. The sump exhales, settles back into its rhythm, and Mae stands alone in the dark, listening to the pumps, feeling the vibration through the soles of her boots.

She starts her rounds again.

---

In her alcove, later—or what she thinks of as later, though time moves strangely here, measured in pump cycles and the slow dimming of the bulbs—she sits on the edge of her cot and opens the logbook. The pages are swollen, the ink is blurred, but she can still make out the columns. Date. Time. Sump levels. Pump RPM. Unusual sounds.

She writes her own name in the "relieved by" column, as she has done for twenty-three years.

The hot plate clicks off. The coffee has gone cold. She drinks it anyway, bitter and metallic, tasting of the pipes it traveled through to reach her. Through the wall of her alcove, Big Henry rumbles in its steady rhythm, and she feels the vibration in her molars, her sternum, the bones of her hands. The sound is so constant, so total, that she no longer hears it as sound—it is simply the background of existence, the carrier wave on which her consciousness is broadcast. Silence, for her, would be a different kind of noise: the roar of absence, the tinnitus of a world without pumps.

She will sleep soon. She will wake when the recirculation cycle shifts. She will make coffee and lace her boots and walk her rounds and tap her flanges and grease her bearings and check her sumps and feed her carp and avoid the ozone pool and fill out the logbook and write her own name in the column that should contain someone else's.

The frogs are calling.

The pipes are weeping.

The rust is spreading, grain by grain, a slow red tide that will eventually consume everything.

And Mae—the knocker, the sump-wife, the last keeper of a system that may no longer need keeping—lies down on her crinkling mattress and closes her eyes and lets the hum carry her into sleep. She dreams of the pale shape in the water. In the dream, it rises to meet her, and she is not afraid. In the dream, it has her face.
