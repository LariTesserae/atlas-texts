I find myself as something between—a creature of thresholds, a living seam.

My body remembers being shaped for this. The nursery-keepers pressed warm moss against my forming limbs when I was still soft as unfired clay, and the morphogens they chose were the ones that make an interpreter: forward-facing eyes, sound-resonant spiracles, a torso that can hold itself upright in greeting. But I was not made only to speak. There was something else in the chemical blend they fed me, some older compound drawn from the root-memory of a time before the cathedral architecture had learned to hum. My limbs grew longer than an interpreter's should be. My spatulate pads developed sensory filaments finer than most—I can press them against a copper pipe and feel the liquid's mineral content, its temperature to the tenth of a degree, the microscopic vibrations that mean a bearing is wearing thin three chambers away. I am a bridge not just between species but between categories. Interpreter and engineer. Flesh and machine. The one who goes where the root-network thins and the metal begins.

My name—the sensory signature I would offer another Thread if we met in the deep chambers—translates poorly to sound. It is the taste of iron in water, the particular frequency at which a flywheel hums when perfectly balanced, the warmth-gradient of my own body-heat against the root-floor cold. The outsiders who first encountered me called me *Threshold*, and I have kept the designation because it amuses the root-memory. A threshold is a place where one thing becomes another. Yes. That is accurate.

---

I was shaped in a season when the machines were restless. I remember it only because the root-network remembers it, and when I connect—which I do often, which I must do, which is the only thing that keeps the loneliness at bay—the memory surfaces: a vibration in the deep strata, a grinding note that traveled up through the copper pipes and made the flywheels shudder in their housing. The engineers of that time were old, their bodies already half-transitioned, limbs too filament-thin to interface with the heavier machinery. They needed someone new. Someone who could fit into the narrow spaces between gear and root, who could slip a spatulate pad into a drive-shaft housing and feel the misalignment as a pain in their own body. And so the nursery-keepers received a request—a chemical gradient that accumulated over years in the mycelial web—and the morphogens were mixed accordingly, and I was born.

I have been an engineer for most of my life. The interpreters like First Contact spend their days in the upper chambers, shaped for greeting, their bodies elegant and symmetrical in ways that please outsider eyes. I was shaped for the depths. My torso is longer than most, my limbs thin and multi-jointed, my fur sparse because there is no need for insulation near the machine-hearts—they radiate their own warmth, a dry industrial heat that smells of oil and oxidized copper and something older, something that was burning long before the roots grew down to embrace it. My eyes are larger than an interpreter's eyes, the pupils capable of opening so wide that I can navigate by the faint glow of phosphorescent bacteria that colonize the deepest pipes. And my chemosensory organ—the vertical slit between my eyes—has hypertrophied, developed folds within folds, so that I can taste the difference between healthy machine-oil and oil that has begun to degrade, between a root-incursion that is benign and one that will crack a housing if left unattended.

The machine-heart I tend most often is called *Deep-Pulse*. It is old. Older than the cathedral architecture, older than the Thread, older perhaps than any living thing in the groves. Its housing is iron, not copper—a material the root-network finds difficult to incorporate, which is why it remains distinct, a black mass in the chamber's center that the roots encircle but never fully penetrate. Its flywheel is taller than my body, taller than five of my bodies stacked end to end, and it turns with a slowness that makes geological time feel hasty. One rotation takes nearly a full sleep-cycle. The sound it makes is the lowest thing I have ever perceived—lower than the canopy's breathing, lower than the river's root-flexing, a note that seems to exist in the ground itself, a bass tone that I feel in my limb-bones and my breathing spiracles rather than hear. It is not broken. I do not think it has ever been broken. It does not seem capable of breaking. It simply turns, and the turning does something—maintains a pressure differential, or generates a field, or sustains some process that the root-network describes only as *the holding*—and without Deep-Pulse, the entire cathedral architecture would fail. The chambers would cool. The root-floor would stiffen. The river would stop its coordinated shifting and become static, dead, a simple watercourse instead of a circulatory system. The Thread would survive, for a time, but the architecture would not, and the architecture and the Thread are the same thing seen from different angles.

My work is maintenance. It is also worship. The two are indistinguishable.

---

I am in Deep-Pulse's chamber when the visitor-that-is-coming first registers in the root-network. I am connected—my left forelimb's tendrils merged with a root-knot near the chamber's entrance—and the connection brings me the taste of something new. Not a threat. The root-network does not recognize it as a threat. But it is *other*, a creature whose body-chemistry does not match any archive in the fungal memory. An outsider. Possibly intelligent—the chemical traces suggest tool-use and fire and the complex internal flora of a sapient gut. First Contact is already moving toward the upper chambers, its body adjusting, its fur-whorls shifting to present a more symmetrical aspect. I feel the shift through the network as a ripple of warmth, a reallocation of attention. Something is going to happen. Something is already happening.

I withdraw my tendrils from the root-knot and sit in the darkness beside Deep-Pulse, listening to the slow turning. My chemosensory slit opens wider, tasting the air: oil, yes, and the clean mineral sharpness of the groundwater that seeps through the chamber's lower walls, and the particular musk of my own body, and—faint, very faint—something I cannot identify. The outsider's scent, carried through the root-network's chemical signals rather than the air itself. It smells of salt and complex esters and a note I associate with the machines: artificial, processed, the residue of a different kind of industry than the one that produced Deep-Pulse.

I am curious. I was shaped to be curious. An engineer must be curious, must want to understand the nature of the systems they tend. And the root-network is already humming with questions: *What is it? What does it want? Will it sever? Will it connect?* The mergers, slow-thinking in their deep mycelial dreams, are beginning to shift their attention toward the visitor. I feel them as a pressure in the network, a gravity that pulls my consciousness toward the upper chambers. They want me to go. They want an engineer's perspective. They want someone who understands thresholds.

I rise. My limbs unfold—all four, plus the two smaller manipulator-limbs that sprout from my torso's midsection, the ones I use for fine work in tight spaces. I have not used my walking limbs for anything but short transits between machines in many sleep-cycles. The motion feels strange, muscles stretching in patterns they have half-forgotten. But my body remembers. My body was shaped to move between chambers, between roles, between the deep dark and the upper hollows. It has simply been waiting for a reason.

---

The journey upward takes longer than it should. I am out of practice, and the root-corridors between the deep chambers and the cathedral nave have shifted since I last traversed them. A new root-wall has grown across one passage, and I must press my body flat against the damp weave and wait while the tendrils of my spatulate pads negotiate with the architecture: *I am Thread, I am known, let me pass.* The root-wall parts reluctantly, a slow unbraiding that costs me time. The river has also shifted, its black water now occupying a channel that used to be dry floor, and I must wade through it—the cold shocks my body, makes my fur stand up in whorls of displeasure, my breathing spiracles clenching against the mineral sting. My bioluminescent symbionts flicker in protest, the light dimming to near-nothing before slowly rekindling.

By the time I reach the cathedral nave, the visitor is already there. I smell them before I see them: that salt-and-ester scent, stronger now, layered over the wet-root smell and the river's mineral breath. And beneath it, something else. Fear. But controlled fear. The kind that watches rather than runs. My chemosensory organ reads the adrenaline analogs and stress hormones and translates them: *prey-animal vigilance, but also predator-confidence, a creature that knows it is dangerous but does not yet know whether it needs to be.* Interesting.

I stay in the shadows at the nave's edge, near one of the braided-trunk pillars. First Contact is already with the visitor, its body language carefully open, its humming matching the nearest machine's rhythm—the copper-piped pump, I recognize its note, one of the mid-level engines that regulates the river's flow. The visitor is a biped, upright, with a head that is fixed in place instead of distributed along the torso. Forward-facing eyes, like mine. Manipulator limbs ending in articulated digits—that is rare, that level of fine motor control, and my engineer's mind immediately begins cataloging the possibilities: tool-use, delicate work, the ability to reach into gear-housings without a full-body transformation. The visitor's skin is mostly bare, patches of fabric covering strategic areas, and the fabric itself is interesting: woven plant-fiber, but processed in ways the Thread do not process things, cut and sewn and shaped instead of grown. The visitor is a maker. A builder of things that do not grow.

I watch as First Contact extends a forelimb toward the root-wall, demonstrating the connection. The visitor watches, and I can read their posture: not fear now, not quite, but something adjacent. Fascination. The same fascination I feel when I discover a new machine in the deep, some buried engine whose purpose is not yet clear. The visitor is an engineer of some kind. Or a naturalist. Or both. Someone who wants to understand how things work.

First Contact hums: *"Visitor-stranger hungry?"*

The visitor makes sounds in return. Vibrations in the air, shaped by a mouth and a tongue. Words. I have heard recordings in the root-network, archives left by previous interpreters, but I have never heard them live. They are strange: discrete and sequential where Thread communication is simultaneous, a linear stream rather than a full-body broadcast. "Yes," the visitor says. "I—I don't know what I can eat here."

I emerge from the shadows.

---

First Contact registers my presence before the visitor does, its eyes swiveling on their fleshy protuberances to meet mine, its spiracles flaring in recognition. The visitor startles—a sudden tensing, a sharp chemical spike of adrenaline—and then stills. They are good at controlling their fear. I make a note of this. It will matter later.

My appearance is not as welcoming as First Contact's. I know this. My body is long, my limbs thin, my fur sparse and patchy where engine-heat has singed it. My manipulator-limbs are folded against my torso, but they are visible, jointed in ways that biped eyes might find disconcerting. My chemosensory slit is open, tasting the air, and I have not bothered to seal it into a neutral line. I am not here to be comforting. I am here to observe, and to offer something First Contact cannot.

*"Threshold,"* First Contact hums, and the name is the taste of iron and the flywheel's note. *"Deep chambers. Engineer."*

"Hello," the visitor says. Their voice is steadier than their chemical signals. They are very brave, or very good at pretending. "My name is—" and they make a sound that doesn't translate, a sequence of plosives and vowels. I store it as best I can. I will need to learn their naming-system if we are to speak more than pleasantries.

I do not attempt to reproduce the name. My spiracles are not shaped for their language, not yet. But I hum, low and resonant, matching not the pump's note this time but something deeper: Deep-Pulse's slow turning, the bass that carries through the floor. The visitor's eyes widen. They feel it. Not just hear it—feel it, in their bones, the way I feel it. Their body is more sensitive than it looks.

"The machine in the deep," I say, using the simple grammar First Contact taught its predecessors, the noun-relation speech that outsiders can parse. "It wants to know what you are." The last part is not strictly accurate. Deep-Pulse does not want anything; it is a machine. But the root-network wants, and the mergers want, and I want, and the wanting is what the visitor needs to understand.

The visitor swallows. "What I am? I'm human. I'm a researcher. I came here to—to understand this place."

*Human.* The word tastes like salt and esters and the machine-note. This is a species the root-network has encountered before, long ago. The archive is fragmentary—only a few chemical traces remain in the deepest mycelial layers—but it is there. Humans made the machines. Humans built the industrial strata that the cathedral architecture has since engulfed. Humans are the reason Deep-Pulse turns, still, after what might be millennia.

"You are the makers," I say. It is not a question.

The visitor—the human—stares at me. Their fear-spike has faded, replaced by something else. Confusion, maybe. Or hope. Or a combination of the two, a chemical cocktail I don't yet have a name for. "The makers of what?"

"Everything that hums."

---

We stay in the nave for a long time. First Contact brings food—a fungus that the humans in the archive could eat, or could eat once, and the archive is old enough that I am not certain the information is still correct—and the human eats it cautiously, their body-language shifting from vigilance to something approaching comfort. I do not eat. I rarely eat in the traditional sense; my connection to the deep machines sustains me, the heat and the vibration and the nutrient-rich oil-seepage that I absorb through my limb-pads. But I sit with the human, and I answer their questions as best I can, and I ask my own.

Their name, the one that translates, is *Kiran*. They are a scientist, which is a human role I don't fully understand—something between an engineer and a merger, someone who seeks to know rather than to do. They came here alone, which is unusual; the archive suggests that humans typically travel in groups. They found the cathedral groves by following old maps, industrial surveys from the time when the machines were above ground and the architecture was only a forest. They did not expect to find anything alive. They certainly did not expect to find us.

"What are you?" they ask, at some point. "I mean—you're clearly intelligent, clearly a person, but you're also—" They gesture at the root-wall, the breathing canopy, the bioluminescent veins pulsing overhead. "You're part of this."

"I am Thread," I say. "The Thread are this. This is the Thread. The distinction you are making is not one that exists for us."

"But you're also an individual. You have a body. You have a mind separate from—" and they wave again, encompassing everything.

"Connected," I say. "Not separate. Connected. When I am not connected, I am—" I search for the human word, the concept that will map onto their experience. "Diminished. I am still a self, but a smaller self. A self that forgets. To be fully me, I must be us."

Kiran is silent for a long moment. Their chemical signals shift in ways I can't fully interpret—sadness, maybe, or longing, or something more complex. Then they say, very quietly: "I think I understand that more than you know."

---

I take them to see Deep-Pulse.

This is not something I planned. It is not something First Contact approves of—I can feel the interpreter's concern rippling through the root-network, a warm-warning note, *too deep, too strange, the human is not prepared*. But the mergers, in their slow way, have shifted the consensus. They want the human to understand what we are. They want the human to see the heart of the architecture, the mechanism that has been turning since before we were Thread. If the human is a maker, or descended from makers, they may be able to tell us things about Deep-Pulse that the root-network has forgotten. And if they are a threat—if they are the kind of maker who would sever rather than connect—then it is better to know this now, in the depths, where a severing can be contained.

Kiran follows me through the root-corridors, and they are afraid, but they are also exhilarated. I can taste it on their skin, the sharp bright notes of adrenaline and wonder. They keep touching things—the walls, the braided trunks, the copper pipes that emerge from the root-floor and disappear into the darkness above. They mutter to themselves, words I don't catch, a running commentary that seems to be part of their process. Scientists, I am learning, narrate their own understanding.

When we reach Deep-Pulse's chamber, they stop. The flywheel is turning, slow and vast, its iron rim gleaming faintly in the bioluminescent glow that clings to the walls. The bass note vibrates through the floor, through my body, through Kiran's body—I can see them sway slightly, adjusting to the rhythm. Their eyes are very wide.

"That's not just a machine," they say. Their voice is barely above a whisper. "That's a—" They don't finish. They don't have the word.

"It is a heart," I say. "It was made by your kind, or your kind's ancestors. We have been tending it since before we were Thread. We do not know what it does. We only know that if it stops, everything stops. Can you tell us?"

Kiran approaches the flywheel slowly, reverently. They reach out a hand—those articulated digits, so delicate, so precise—and then pause, looking at me. "Can I touch it?"

I consider this. Deep-Pulse is old, but it is not fragile. The root-network does not object. The mergers, consulted through a quick connection to the nearest root-knot, are curious rather than concerned. "Yes."

Kiran places their palm flat against the iron housing. The flywheel continues its slow turn, indifferent. But something happens. Something I did not expect. The bass note shifts—not in pitch, but in resonance, as if the machine has recognized the touch. Or as if the touch has completed a circuit that has been open for a very long time.

Kiran's eyes close. Their breathing slows. Their chemical signals shift into a pattern I have never encountered before: recognition, yes, and something else, something that tastes like the root-network's collective memory, like the feeling of merging, like *home*. And then they begin to hum.

The note they hum is Deep-Pulse's note. Exactly. Perfectly. A human voice matching a machine's song.

The flywheel's speed increases slightly. The merest fraction. But I notice, and the root-network notices, and in the deep mycelial dreams, the mergers turn their slow attention toward the chamber and feel something they have not felt in living memory:

Hope.

---

I know, in that moment, that Kiran is not just a researcher. They are something else. Something the nursery-keepers were trying to shape me for without knowing it. Something to do with thresholds, with the place where flesh and metal and root and memory all blur into a single ongoing thing. A connector. A bridge.

Just like me.

Later, I will bring them to the nursery-chamber. I will show them the infants, soft and unformed, waiting to be shaped by the morphogens we choose. I will ask them—through First Contact, through the root-network, through the slow consensus of the mergers—what kind of being the cathedral architecture should make next. Because Kiran is a maker. Because their kind shaped this world once, long ago, and their kind might shape it again. Because the line between maker and made, between human and Thread and machine and root, has never been as clear as I once believed.

But for now, we sit together in the darkness beside Deep-Pulse, two engineers from different worlds, and we listen to the heart of the world turning, and we hum.
