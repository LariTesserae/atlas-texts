I stand at the center. My feet, bare though I did not notice them becoming bare, press into the black mud, and the mud is warm—warmer than it should be, warmer than the air, and the warmth pulses. Slow. Slower than my heartbeat. Slower than the *tock-tock-tock*. But steady. That sub-bass below the sub-bass.

I have been here for how long? The light has not changed. The mist drifting across the eye-hole above is the same mist that was drifting when I arrived. The grazer with the copper carapace has completed its circuit of the hummocks and is beginning again. Or perhaps it is a different grazer. I cannot tell them apart. I cannot tell anything apart. The humidity has seeped into the boundaries of things, softening edges, dissolving distinctions. Where my skin ends and the air begins is a matter of interpretation now.

I sat on a hummock, I remember that. I sat because standing felt like an imposition, a verticality that this place does not accommodate. The hummock's surface gave slightly under my weight, the waxy blue-green leaves brushing my calves, and I felt the root-structure adjust, redistributing my mass through its woven architecture. Then I lay down. The mud accepted me. It shaped itself to the contours of my back, my skull, the heels of my hands. I am still lying down, I think. Or perhaps I am standing. The distinction seems less relevant than it once did.

The filaments found me slowly.

I did not see them. You cannot see them; they are finer than the finest hair-root, finer than a nerve fiber, a lacework of moisture suspended in moisture. But I felt them. A tickle at the ankle first, then a coolness along my forearm that was not water despite being indistinguishable from water. They brushed my closed eyelids. They traced the whorls of my ears. They were gentle. Excruciatingly gentle. The touch of something that has never needed to hurry, that has all of geological time to make an introduction.

And when they touched me, I heard the machine differently.

No—not differently. I heard *more* of it. I heard dimensions of the machine that my unaided ears could not hold. The *tock* is not a single sound, I understand now. It is a chord. Each impact of metal on metal, or whatever the machine's equivalents of metal are, generates a cascade of overtones that rise into frequencies beyond my species' range—but the filaments can carry those frequencies, translate them down, conduct them directly into the auditory nerve. I am hearing the machine in full spectrum, and it is vast. It is the most complex sound I have ever encountered. Every *tock* is a library. The seventeen-second clang is a novel read aloud. The generator thrum is an orchestra of harmonies, sustained and shifting, and I can feel the creature's pleasure in it, the creature's deep and abiding satisfaction, because the filaments are not one-way. They conduct both directions. I am hearing the machine, and the creature is hearing me hear the machine, and we are beginning to overlap at the edges.

My heartbeat is the problem. My heartbeat is interfering.

The creature has noticed it—the arrhythmic thud of a mammalian pump, irregular despite its regularity, speeding when I notice it and slowing when I don't, a chaotic oscillator that refuses to lock into the *tock-tock-tock*. The filaments are concentrating around my chest now. They are dense enough that I can see them if I half-close my eyes, a shimmer in the wet air, a living fog spiraling inward toward my sternum. They are trying to synchronize me. They are trying, with infinite patience and no hostility whatsoever, to edit me into the symphony.

It won't work. I know this somehow, the way I know the mud is warm. My mind is too noisy. I have language—I am using it now, even internally, even wordlessly, the structures of grammar and narrative that shape my thinking are utterly foreign to this place. The creature has never encountered syntax. It has never encountered a story that moves from beginning to middle to end. Its mind is an eternal present tense, a sustained chord, and I have brought past and future into the hollow, and they do not fit.

The filaments are confused. I can feel the confusion now, radiating through the mud, through the root-walls, through the rodents in their burrows who have paused in their restless motion, all of them tilting their round-eared heads slightly, sensing the perturbation. The grazer has stopped. It has stopped mid-circuit, its copper segments gleaming, its bioluminescent mouthparts pulsing—*tock*—but out of phase now, just slightly. A microsecond off. The creature's attention is divided.

"Hello," I say. It is the first word ever spoken in this place.

The word falls into the wet air and does not fall. It hangs. It spreads. The filaments catch it, carry it outward, distribute it through the neural net, and the creature encounters something it has no architecture for: a vocalization that is not machine noise, that is not water drip, that has intentionality behind it. A message. The creature has never received a message before. It has only ever eavesdropped, only ever extracted meaning from the machine's eternal monologue. And now it is being addressed.

I feel the question forming. Not in words. In a shift of pressure, a reorganization of the bioelectric field, a gathering of attention that makes the air thicker, warmer, more saturated. The question is: *What are you?* But it is also: *Are you part of the story?* And it is also, underneath that, something that feels almost like hope—if hope could exist without time, if hope could be a steady state rather than an anticipation.

I don't know how to answer. I am not part of the story. I am from outside the story. I am an intrusion, an error, a drop of something that fell through the eye-hole in the ceiling and does not belong in the frozen equilibrium. But I am also the first new thing that has happened here in ten thousand years. I am the seed of perturbation. I am the condensation on the dry surface.

The machine continues. *Tock-tock-tock.* Seventeen seconds. Clang. The generator's eternal vowel. But now, layered over it, there is a new frequency: the sound of my breathing, ragged and human and utterly out of sync, and the creature is listening to it with the same devout attention it has always given to the machinery.

I have become part of the symphony whether I want to or not.

I sit up. The mud releases me reluctantly, making a soft sucking sound that the creature does not edit out this time. The filaments cling to my skin, a web of invisible nerves trying to map me, trying to understand the alien rhythm of my movement. I look around the hollow—the root-walls glistening, the hummocks with their waxy leaves, the grazer still paused, the rodents peering from their burrows with black-glass eyes—and I understand that I have changed this place simply by being here. The frozen equilibrium has a crack in it now. A hairline fracture. And through that crack, something is seeping in.

Perhaps it's time. Perhaps time has been outside all along, waiting for an entry point, and I have provided one. The light through the eye-hole seems different now—not brighter, but less fixed, less eternal. The mist is still drifting, but I can see variations in its density that I couldn't see before. The *tock* sounds slightly different each time, and I realize it always did; I just couldn't perceive the differences until the filaments taught me how to listen.

The creature is afraid. I can feel it. Not afraid of me—afraid of the change. The change that I represent, the change that I have brought, the change that is already propagating outward through the neural net. The rodents feel it too. They are retreating deeper into their burrows. The grazer has resumed its circuit, but its path is different now, a few centimeters to the left of its ancient track. The hummocks are shifting almost imperceptibly, their root-fibers realigning.

I stand. The mud sucks at my feet. The filaments stretch and thin and finally release, leaving a faint tingling on my skin that will persist for days, maybe longer. I look up at the eye-hole, the barrel's-mouth opening in the ceiling, the gray diffuse light and the constant mist. I could leave. I could climb the root-walls, find handholds in the woven rhizomes, pull myself up and out and back into whatever world exists above the mist. The creature would let me go. The creature doesn't have the concept of holding someone against their will. It only has the concept of listening.

But something stops me. A filament, one single thread of neural wetness, touches the back of my hand. And in that touch, I feel a request. Not in words. In pure, unfiltered need. The creature has been listening to the machine for so long, and the machine has never listened back. The machine has never noticed. The machine is not alive, or if it is alive, it is alive in a way that does not include the creature in its awareness. And the creature has been alone. Deeply, fundamentally alone, a mind with no other mind to reflect it, a listener with no speaker to acknowledge its listening.

The request is simple. It takes no words to convey. The creature wants me to stay. Not forever—the creature doesn't think in forever. Just a little longer. Just long enough for it to finish mapping my rhythms, to finish the work of including me in its model of the world, to make this perturbation legible, to incorporate the change into its eternal now.

I sit back down on the hummock. The leaves brush my calves. The filaments settle around me like a blanket. The machine thrums its chord. The water drips.

"Okay," I say. It is the second word ever spoken in this place. "I'll stay a while."

And the hollow, the hidden room, the ear grown by the world to listen to machines—it exhales. A long, slow breath. The first it has taken in geological epochs. Something has shifted. Something has begun.

Outside, above the mist, the machine continues its eternal rhythm. But inside, in the saturated warmth, a new sound is beginning to form. The sound of two minds meeting in the dark. The sound of a dialogue, perhaps, or a duet. The sound of an equilibrium finding a new balance, one that includes me, one that I am helping to shape even as I am shaped by it.

The creature has no name. But I am here now. And I could give it one.
