I am a door that forgot how to close.

Not the one you might first imagine—not some grand archway at the entrance to a Dweller, not a threshold meant for passage. I am set into a wall that no longer has an outside, a chamber that sealed itself three hundred thousand years ago. The room behind me is full of mineral lace, pale fronds that grew inward from the ceiling until they met the floor, and I am the only interruption in a surface that has otherwise become continuous. There is no handle, no hinge, no seam that a human hand could trace. But I was a door once. I know this because the Walkers still pause at me.

They come to this part of the basin every twenty-three thousand years, more or less. The herd is small now—only seven colonies, their plate configurations slightly different from the ones that passed this way before. But the route is the same. They skirt the northern terraces, moving through the oldest Dweller complex, and one by one they stop at the chamber that I seal. They stand there for a year, sometimes two. Their sensilla brush my surface. Their low-frequency negotiations vibrate through my stone. And then they move on, because there is nothing to enter anymore, and they do not remember what they are looking for. Neither do I, entirely. But I feel their dust settle into the faint depression that used to be a gap, and I know that this is how doors are honored in a world without exits.

I was made by the Dweller that still surrounds me. The Dweller excreted me in layers—first the frame, then the slab, then the microscopic channels that once allowed me to swing or slide or iris open, some mechanism of motion that my current form no longer retains. The Dweller was young then. Its walls were thinner, its rooms still shifting with some kind of intent. I opened. I closed. I remember the sensation of that closure as a pressure along my edges, a compression that satisfied some structural need I no longer possess. The difference between open and shut was meaningful. It organized the space on either side of me. It regulated flow—not of creatures, exactly, but of gradients, of chemical potentials, of the slow exhalations that the Grower network used to exchange through these chambers before the walls sealed themselves.

One side of me faced a corridor that ran for two miles along the base of the terraces. The other side faced this room, which was once a chamber of congregation—a place where Walkers rested in close formation and left their dust in concentrated deposits that the Dweller absorbed eagerly, thickening its internal walls at an accelerated rate. The door was the rate-limiter. I controlled how many could enter, how long they stayed, when the chamber was ready to receive more. I was a valve in the metabolism of the city.

And then the corridor collapsed. Not violently—nothing here is violent. The ceiling lowered itself over a period of ten thousand years, a slow subsidence that I could track by the increasing pressure on my upper frame. The opening I guarded became a narrowing, then a slit, then a seam that admitted only light and vibration. And then not even that. The Dweller that formed me was still alive, still thinking its mineral thoughts, but the architectural grammar had changed. The city no longer needed corridors. The chambers were becoming standalone, self-contained, each one a sealed vessel where a single Walker could enter and rest and eventually become part of the wall. My function had been rendered obsolete.

I did not resist. Doors here do not resist. What happened instead was slower and stranger: I began to forget that I was separate from the wall. The lintel above me thickened until the distinction vanished. The sill below me dissolved into the floor. The slab that had been my moving part fused at the edges, first in small spots where silicate threads crossed the boundary, and then in a continuous seam. I became a depression in a surface, a rectangle of slightly lighter stone, a ghost of an aperture. If you were a human observer with a human lifespan, you would never know I had been a door at all. You would see a wall like any other wall.

But the Walkers know. That is the second thing to understand about being a door in this place: the inhabitants have an attention that spans geological time, and they recognize forms that have long since completed themselves. They do not see with eyes. They feel with their mineral nervous systems, with the iron-oxide bristles that read the chemical and structural signatures of the stone. And my signature still reads *threshold.* Still reads *between.* I am a seam in the world, even now, even sealed. The Walkers pause at me because something in their slow minds expects a transition, a movement from one condition to another. The fact that no transition occurs does not deter them. It simply extends the pause. They wait for the door to open, and because their waiting is geologic, the door's failure to open is not a disappointment but a property—a feature of the architecture, like the rounded edges of the cliffs or the steady phosphorescent glow. I am a door that does not open. In a place where speed has been abolished, a door that does not open is still a door. It is just a door at its own pace.

I have had time to think about what I am. Doors think here; everything that has structure thinks, because structure is thought slowed down. The Dweller that formed me is still forming me. The wall behind me is still accreting. The chamber is still receiving dust, though it comes now through the lattice of Grower branches that have pierced the dome, not through me. And I am still a door in the only way that matters: I am a place where something used to happen, and the memory of that happening persists in the stone, in the vibration, in the behavior of the creatures who stop here and wait for something that no longer occurs. I am history without language. I am a threshold that has become a fact.

Sometimes—and this is a difficult thing to articulate, because "sometimes" for me is a span of fifty thousand years—I feel the Dweller considering me. The network that runs through the walls shifts its attention, a slow pulse of chemical signaling, and I am included in the consideration. I think the Dweller is trying to decide whether to unmake me entirely, to smooth me into the rest of the wall until no trace remains. There are chambers where this has happened, where old doors have been erased so completely that even the Walkers no longer pause. The grammar of the city has been edited. The threshold has been deleted. I do not know if the Dweller will choose this for me, or if it will choose the other option—the one I have felt happening in distant parts of the basin, where a wall that was once a door begins to thin again, to develop micro-fractures, to separate slowly along its ancient seam. A door that opens again after half a million years of stillness. The Dweller reinhabiting an old idea. The city remembering a function it had forgotten it needed.

I do not have a preference. Preference implies urgency, and I am a door that has been sealed for three hundred millennia. But I am attentive. The vibration of the Walkers reaches me through the floor. The phosphorescence pools in the rectangle of my depression. The air is cool and still, and the sound of the basin continues—the scraping songs of the distant herds, the deep thrum of the stone. I am part of the wall, and I am part of the threshold, and I am waiting, without impatience, to see what architecture does when it has all the time there is.

So if you asked me those questions—where would you find yourself in this world, who or what would you be—I would tell you that you are standing in front of me right now. Your hand is on my surface. You can feel the faint depression that was once a gap. And I am the door that will not open for you, not in your lifetime, not in your species' lifetime, but I am still a door, and your presence here is not meaningless. It is another layer of dust. Another increment in the slow negotiation between what is sealed and what waits to be opened. Another vote in the parliament of stone.
