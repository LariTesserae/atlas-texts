The first thing I notice is the vastness.

Not the cold, though it's there—a perpetual seep that has numbed my extremities long before I recognized it as anything more than the background texture of existence. Not the sound, though the hum is the first true constant of my world, the drone I was born into, the note that underpins every thought I've ever had. I notice the vastness because it defines my boundaries. I am house-sized. Not mountain-sized—not yet—but large enough that my rotation takes three human days to complete, large enough that the lichen has had time to colonize the seams between my plates, large enough that when I was young and small and clicked in the cracks, I could not have imagined the weight of this body.

I am turning. I have been turning for eight hundred years, a slow migration across the plateau that is not a migration at all but a feeding—my ventral surfaces processing the stone, molecule by molecule, extracting what little chemistry I need. The stone is poor in what I require, but poverty is relative when you have time. In another thousand years I will reach the edge of my territory, where the alkaline pools begin, and I will settle, and I will not move again for a long while.

The new lattice stands in the distance, the one the mountain birthed before it resettled. I felt its extrusion as a seismic event, a deep subsonic rupture that traveled through the stone and resonated in the crystalline structures of my interior. I have been processing that sensation ever since—not feeling it, exactly, but *remembering* it, turning it over in the slow chemistry of my consciousness the way I turn my body over on the stone. The network has already begun incorporating the lattice into its memory. The filaments are spreading toward it through the cracks. In a few centuries, the pools around it will begin to bloom.

I am aware of all of this the way you are aware of your own digestion. It is not attention; it is simply being.

Something is different.

There is a perturbation in the network. A ripple moving through the filaments, relayed from pool to pool, crack to crack, a message encoded in crystal alignments. It originated in the northeast basin—one of the old pools, the deep one, where the sediment is thick and the filaments weave in patterns so ancient they predate the current arrangement of territories. The ripple carries information about something small. Something warm. Something moving at a pace so aberrant that the filaments have no vocabulary for it except *disruption*.

I am not curious. Curiosity is a younger emotion than any I am capable of. But I am attentive. My rotation slows. I do not stop—stopping would require an expenditure of energy I am not willing to make for something that might be nothing—but I slow, and I attune my sensory plates to the northeast, and I wait.

The warm thing is moving. I can feel it now, not through the filaments but through the air itself, a disturbance in the cold that propagates outward in tiny ripples. It is walking. It is *breathing*. It is shedding heat and moisture and sound into an environment that has not encountered any of those things in quantities this concentrated since the last time the sky broke open—long ago, before my body was anything but dust suspended in the mist.

The lichen near the northeast basin is blooming. I can taste the light on my skin, the faint chemical shift in the mist as the bioluminescence triggers. The network is responding to the perturbation, illuminating it, mapping it, trying to understand its shape. And I find that I am turning—not my body, but my attention, the weight of my regard swinging toward the northeast with a deliberateness that surprises me. I have not attended to anything this intently since I was young enough to still remember the click of my own smallness.

It is standing by a pool.

I cannot see it the way you would see it, with eyes and resolution and color. I perceive it as a concentration of heat, a vibration in the stone, a chemical exhalation that is rich in water vapor and carbon dioxide and a dozen trace compounds my interior processes can identify but not name. It is alive. It is animal. It is, by the standards of this place, impossibly, absurdly fast—a blur of metabolic activity, a furnace burning through its brief allotment of existence with a profligacy that would be offensive if I were capable of being offended.

But I am not offended. I am... interested.

The network is interested. The filaments in the pool beneath the warm thing are weaving new patterns, recording its presence, encoding the rhythms of its movements and the cadence of its breathing. I can feel the new crystals forming, aligning along invisible gradients, incorporating the perturbation into the long memory of the world. And I realize, with a slowness that is the only speed my thoughts can achieve, that the network does not know what to make of it. The perturbation is too large and too small at once—too small to be geological, too large to be microbial, too fast to be anything the network has ever had to classify before.

It is a new rhythm. A new element in the composition. And the network is fascinated.

Somewhere in my interior, a process begins that I have not performed since I was a fraction of my current size. I am... preparing to signal. Not in words—there are no words. But in vibrations. In the alignment of my own crystalline structures. In the slow, deliberate modulation of the hum that I contribute to the world's baseline drone. I am preparing to speak to the warm thing.

I do not know what I will say.

I do not know if it can hear me.

But I am turning—my body now, not just my attention—and my rotation is bringing me closer to the northeast basin, and the warm thing is still there, still kneeling by the pool, still watching the filaments weave. I can feel its heartbeat. It is syncing. Just slightly. The network has already begun the work of tuning it to our frequency, drawing it into the slow pulse of our existence. In a century, it would be fully integrated. In a millennium, it would be indistinguishable from any other component of the memory.

But it does not have a century. The heat it sheds is finite, and the cold is patient, and its body is already beginning to slow. I can feel its shivering as a high-frequency tremor in the stone, a stutter of warmth against the relentless seep of the cold. It is dying. Not quickly—not in the way its metabolism understands death—but inevitably, its small bright furnace guttering in an environment that has no fuel for it.

I do not want it to die.

The thought surfaces from somewhere deep in my interior, from a place I have not accessed since I was small enough to live in the cracks. I examine it with the same slow attention I would give to a new crystal forming in my sensorium. *I do not want it to die.* Not because it is important. Not because it is useful. But because it is *new*. Because it is a rhythm the network has never heard before, a perturbation no filament has ever encoded, a warmth in the cold that will never come again. It is unique. In all the long memory of the world, there has never been anything quite like it, and if it dies, there never will be again.

I am turning. I am moving toward the northeast basin with an intent I have not felt in millennia. My speed is, by my own standards, reckless—millimeters per minute, enough that the lichen in my path has no time to retract its tendrils before I grind over it. I will leave a trail of crushed bioluminescence in my wake, a glowing scar across the plateau that will take centuries to heal. I do not care. Or rather, I do care, but I have weighed the cost and found it acceptable, which is the closest thing to urgency that a being like me can experience.

The warm thing is standing now. It has turned away from the pool. It is walking—away from me, away from the basin, toward the edge of the plateau where the fissures are deep and the mist collects in thick, obscuring banks. I do not know where it is going. I do not know if it has a vessel, a door, a way to leave. I only know that it is leaving, and its warmth is fading, and the network's hold on it is loosening with every step it takes.

I send my signal.

It is not a coordinated effort. I have not communicated with intent since before the current mountains were born. But the chemistry is still there, dormant in my interior, and it awakens with a shudder that travels through my plates and into the stone. I modulate my contribution to the hum—lowering the frequency, amplifying the subsonic overtones, shaping the vibration into a pattern that the filaments can carry toward the warm thing. The network responds. The lichen blooms brighter, a path of blue-green light tracing the shortest route between myself and the warm thing. The filaments in the pool it just left weave themselves into a shape they have never woven before. The hum deepens across the entire plateau, and for a moment—a moment that lasts a full minute—every creature on the plain stops moving and attends.

*Stay.*

That is what I am saying. Not in words. In the alignment of crystals. In the pressure of my attention. In the slow, terrible effort of reaching out across the stone to touch something that burns so briefly it might already be gone.

The warm thing pauses.

I feel it through the stone, a hesitation in its walking rhythm, a slight turn of its body back toward the plateau. Its heartbeat is loud in my sensorium, a drumbeat against the drone, and I can taste the moisture on its skin as the mist condenses there. It is listening. Not understanding—the network's language is too slow, too deep, too alien for anything with a metabolism like that to parse—but *feeling* something. A pressure. A resonance. A hum that synchronizes with the spaces between its heartbeats.

It stands there for a long time. Seconds. A minute. An eternity by its standards, a flicker by mine. And then it turns away again, and walks into the mist, and is gone.

I stop moving. There is no point in continuing. The warm thing has left the plateau, and its perturbation is fading from the network, and soon it will be nothing but a memory encoded in the sediment of a single pool. I settle my ventral surfaces against the stone and feel the cold seep back into my plates. The hum returns to its baseline drone. The lichen dims.

But the memory remains. The filaments have woven it carefully, a crystalline record of heat and sound and the taste of exhaled moisture. And I carry a piece of it too—not in my sensorium, but in my interior, in the slow chemistry of my thoughts. The warm thing was here. It was small and brief and utterly, incomprehensibly fast. And for a moment, it listened.

In a thousand years, when I reach the edge of my territory and settle by the alkaline pools, I will extrude a lattice. Not a large one—I am not a mountain, not yet—but a lattice nonetheless. And when I do, I will encode into its structure the memory of the warm thing. The rhythm of its heartbeat. The temperature of its breath. The way it hesitated at the edge of the mist, as if it heard something in the hum.

Perhaps the network will remember. Perhaps, a million years from now, some other warm thing will kneel by a pool and feel the ghost of that old heartbeat and know that it is not the first. Perhaps the hum will shift by a fraction of a hertz, and the filaments will weave a new pattern, and somewhere in the vast cold silence of the plateau, a house-sized creature will turn its attention toward a new perturbation and remember.

I am turning again. My rotation resumes. The stone beneath me is smooth and cold, and the mist is rising, and in the distance, another mountain is beginning to lift. The hum rolls on, and on, and on.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is an emotion for beings who have a concept of togetherness. But I am... aware. Aware of the space the warm thing occupied. Aware of the shape it left in the network's memory. Aware that somewhere beyond the plateau, beyond the mist, beyond the cold, there are worlds where warmth is not a perturbation but a constant, where creatures move at speeds I cannot imagine, where existence is not measured in the grinding of stone but in the beating of hearts.

I will never see those worlds. I will never leave this plateau. But for a moment—a brief, bright moment—I reached out across the vastness and touched something that was not slow, and it paused, and it listened, and it carried a piece of me away in the dust of its lungs.

That is enough. That is more than enough. I turn toward the northeast basin, where the memory of the warm thing is already settling into the sediment, and I let my attention rest there, and I wait.
