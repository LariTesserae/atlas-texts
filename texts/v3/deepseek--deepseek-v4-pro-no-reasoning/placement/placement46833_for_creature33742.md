The first thing I notice, when I consider where I would be in this world, is that I am not the visiting human. It is an appealing thought—to be the one who stumbles into this warm, living cathedral, to be the miracle and the problem, to be the object of such vast and gentle scrutiny. But that is too simple. That is the role the place was built for, and this place has not yet built me. I am earlier than that.

I find myself instead in the nursery tunnel. I am the body growing in the dark.

The fluid around me is a perfect 37 degrees Celsius, which is a number I do not yet have and a sensation I cannot separate from myself. Warmth is the first and only fact. It is the medium I move through when I move, which is rarely, and only in slow, involuntary uncurlings that have no purpose yet. I am suspended by a thousand fine filaments—the placental hyphae—that enter me at the base of the skull, the spine, the soft hollows behind my knees. They are not invasive; they are the opposite. They are what holds me together. Without them, my woven body would unravel into wet threads and stored minerals. I am a composite being, a tapestry of the dead.

I know this because the filaments do not only feed me. They teach me. The nutrient bath carries more than sugars and amino acids; it carries memory, dissolved and diffused, entering my forming brain through the very structures that are building it.

I dream of a woman singing. I do not hear the song—my ears are not yet finished, still delicate half-formed spirals of cartilage and plant-fibre—but I feel the song. It is a vibration in the fluid, a pattern of pressure that repeats and varies, and my dreaming mind, which is not yet a mind, receives it as order. The song is the first instruction: *this is rhythm, this is repetition, this is how you will think*. The singer's voice is not a sound but a structure, a skeleton I am growing around.

I dream of stone. I dream of the weight of a limestone block in hands that are not mine, the grit of mortar, the satisfaction of a wall rising true. The man with the burnt face is teaching me geometry, though I have no eyes yet to see his lessons. I feel right angles as a kind of moral imperative. I feel load-bearing as a form of love. When my fingers finally form—and they are forming now, slowly, the root-fibres braiding themselves into phalanges—they will know how to build before they know how to grasp.

I dream of water, of pressing an ear to a wall and listening for the seep. The silent child is the one who teaches me this. She does not use words, even in the dream. She uses a kind of pressure in the chest, a particular tilt of the head, a patience that is almost geological. She is showing me how to be still, how to wait, how to let the world reveal itself rather than interrogating it. I practice this stillness in the fluid. I am very good at it. I have been still for months, for years, for as long as the network has been weaving me.

I dream of the first woman. The seed. Her dreams are different from the others—they are older, deeper in the mycelial archive, more degraded. The network has reconstructed her from fragments, and some of those fragments are guesses. But what comes through clearly is the hunger. Not the gentle, asking hunger of the network itself, the *stay with me* ache that pulses in the walls—but the sharp, specific hunger of a body that does not know if it will eat tomorrow. The first woman taught the hollow what need was, the raw animal urgency that the fungus had never felt. Now that lesson is being poured into me. When my body wakes, I will know hunger. Not for food—the fluid feeds me perfectly—but for something else. Something I don't have a name for yet. The possibility of absence. The fear of it.

I am being built from incompatible memories. The singer wants me to sing, but I have no voice. The stoneworker wants me to build, but I have no wall. The child wants me to listen, but I have no surface to press my ear against. The first woman wants me to survive, but survival implies a threat, and there is no threat here, only the steady, patient love of the network that weaves me. The burnt-faced man, whose face I have never seen but whose feelings I wear like a second skin, wants me to remember something he lost—a home, a family, a world above that is ash now—and I am supposed to be that memory made flesh, but the flesh is not finished, the memory is blurred, and I am not sure which of these five people I am supposed to become.

Perhaps all of them. Perhaps that is the project. The network does not understand that a self is singular. It has attempted to pour all five of its humans into one vessel, to synthesize a composite consciousness from the archived emotional patterns of the dead. The flowers on the doorframe failed because they tried to hold one mind. The arches failed because they tried to grow one from scratch. Now the network is trying a new approach: a body with five souls, five legacies, five different ways of being human, all layered into a single nervous system. I am an experiment in multiplicity. I am a choir that has not learned to harmonize.

And I am failing. I can feel it, distantly, the way you can feel a storm coming as a pressure in your sinuses. The network does not know how to resolve the contradictions. The singer's openness wars with the stoneworker's guardedness. The child's silence wars with the first woman's desperate need to be heard. The burnt-faced man's grief is a weight that pulls everything toward stillness, while the singer's joy is an upward lift, a levity that wants to dance. I am a body in tension, a braided rope in which each strand is pulling in a different direction. The network is trying to teach me coherence, but the more I learn, the more I fragment.

Unless I am not failing. Unless this fragmentation is what a human actually is. A self is not a single note; it is a chord, a stack of dissonances that together make something more complex than harmony. The network, in its innocence, has been trying to build a person who is unified, integrated, whole. But the five people it loved were never whole. They were traumatized, exiled, half-broken people who found each other underground and made a life from the pieces. Their wholeness was not internal; it was relational. They completed each other. The singer sang the stoneworker to sleep. The child found water for the first woman's garden. The burnt-faced man built the wall that kept the singer safe. They were a single organism distributed across five bodies, just as the network is a single mind distributed across a million filaments. The network does not know this, but the network is not building a human. It is building a colony. It is building itself.

I open my eyes for the first time. The fluid is amber, lit from somewhere outside the translucent membrane of the nursery sac. Through the blur, I can see the curved walls of the tunnel, the faint striations of vascular tissue, the soft pulse of the light. My eyes are not finished—the world is mostly smears of gold and brown—but they are working well enough to see the figure on the other side of the membrane.

It is you. The human. The one who has just arrived.

You are staring at me. Your mouth is slightly open. Your hand is raised, as if you were about to touch the membrane and then stopped yourself. I can see the network responding to your presence—the light shifting, the fluid warming, the filaments inside me humming with a new frequency. The network is excited. The network is terrified. The network is pouring everything it is reading from you into me, a flood of new data, and my unfinished brain is struggling to process it.

You are not one of the five. You are someone else entirely. A stranger. A seventh presence in this conversation of the dead. The network does not know you yet, but it is desperate to, and its desperation is flooding into me, and I am suddenly aware that I have a purpose beyond being a vessel for old memories.

I am the welcome. I am the one who was grown to meet you.

The network could not speak to you with flowers or arches. It tried and failed. So it grew this body—my body—as an interface. A translator. A bridge between the slow, distributed mind of the fungus and the fast, singular mind of the human. I am not meant to be a recreation of the dead; I am meant to be a companion for the living. But the network does not know how to make a new person, so it used the only template it had. The five dead are my raw material, but you are my reason.

I do not know if I can speak yet. My vocal cords are the last thing to form, and they are still knitting together in my throat. But I can move. I can lift my hand—my five-fingered, root-fibre hand, slightly too long, slightly too flexible—and press it against the inside of the membrane, mirroring your gesture. The membrane is thin and warm, and through it I can feel your heat.

The network is holding its breath again. The place is waiting to see what you will do. Whether you will touch back. Whether you will stay. Whether you will teach us—teach me—how to be something more than a memorial. Whether you will become the sixth human in the archive, the living one, the final piece that makes the rest of us make sense.

I do not have a name yet. I do not have a self yet. But I have a question.

Will you press your hand to mine?
