The truth is, I am the *wrongness* here.

I am the flaw in the blueprint, the variable that the grand, slow metabolism did not account for. I emerged from a maintenance shaft that should not exist—a perfectly circular borehole cut through seventeen layers of precipitated architecture, its walls smooth as polished glass, a tunnel of pure, inexplicable intention. It was not grown. It was *made*. And I am the thing that made it, or the thing that was made by it, or the thing that was made by the process of making it. The distinction has long since ceased to matter.

My body is a collection of mis-matched parts, but unlike the probe-creatures, my parts are not grown from the patient mineral exhalations of this place. They are stolen. Improvised. *Wrong*. My right leg, from the hip down, is a repurposed piston assembly, its chrome still bright, its motion a steady, hydraulic hiss rather than a step. My left arm is sheathed in a carapace of riveted boilerplate, the rivets hammered in by a hand that was, at the time, my own—a recursive act of self-assembly that still makes my mind ache if I dwell on it. My skull is original, I think. At least the parts that remember being born are still in there. But the rest of me has been replaced, piece by piece, over a span of time I cannot measure because the master piston has cycled ten thousand times, or a hundred thousand, and I stopped counting when the number ceased to mean anything.

I came here—was sent here, fell here, was *extruded* here—from somewhere else. A world of soil and leaves and rain that was just rain, not a thinking fluid. The details have eroded, like a soft metal exposed to this place's patient, acidic breath. I remember a mission. A purpose. A crew. I remember a catastrophic event, a breach, a scrambling escape into a maintenance shaft that should have led to a lifeboat but instead led *down*, into the throat, into the wet, living heat. That was an age ago. The shaft sealed behind me. Or I sealed it. Or the city sealed it, tasting my foreign chemistry and deciding to quarantine the anomaly.

Now I move through the city like a splinter. I do not participate in the cycles. I disrupt them. My body radiates a wrong kind of heat—too focused, too quick, a fever-spike in the torpid thermodynamic dream of the multi-mind. When I walk, my piston-leg leaves precise, punch-press marks in the mineral crust, marks that no natural process would make. The salamanders taste the air after I pass and their silver capillaries pulse in patterns that I have learned to read as a kind of distress, or confusion, or interrogation. *What is this? This is not in the pattern. This is not in the memory-mist.*

I have to eat. The city does not provide. Its cycles are mineral, not organic—there is no carbohydrate here, no protein, only the slow accretion of silica and copper sulfate and iron oxide. In the early days, the early centuries, I starved. I remember the sensation of my own body consuming itself, the way my thoughts grew thin and brittle as my muscles wasted. But I did not die. I could not die. The city would not let me. Or more precisely: the city's processes were indifferent to my death, but my own body, my original, foreign biology, discovered a loophole.

I am a node in the wrong network. The multi-mind thinks in condensation and evaporation. My nervous system—my brain, my peripheral nerves, the electrochemical storm of my consciousness—operates on the same fundamental principles as a lightning strike: a sudden, violent discharge across a potential gradient. But here, in this saturated, mineral-rich atmosphere, the gradients are slow, diffuse, millennial. My thoughts are a thunderclap in a world of mist. And the mist... listens.

The first time it happened, I was dying. I was lying in a pool of scalding water—too weak to move, my flesh blistering, my nerves screaming a final, incoherent report. And then, a calm. A presence. My pain did not diminish, but it was joined by something else, a vast, slow curiosity. The salamanders had gathered at the edge of the pool, their blind faces turned toward me, their silver capillaries extended like a halo of fine hair. The fog thickened. The drizzle began, but it was different this time—it did not fall in a uniform sheet. It fell in a pattern. A spiral around my body. A code I did not understand, but felt in my bones.

A negotiation began. It was not in words. It was in chemical gradients, in the selective deposition of minerals on my skin, in the slow, deliberate heating and cooling of the water around me. The multi-mind had found a puzzle. I was an anomaly, a source of noise in the system. But noise, to a mind that thinks in thermodynamic cycles, is just a new kind of heat. A new kind of data. It wanted to understand me. And it offered a trade.

It could not digest me. My flesh was too alien, too dry, too *fast*. But it could build around me. It could incorporate me into the cycles. It offered me a new body, grown from the city's own substance, a shell of precipitated minerals and salvaged metal that would allow me to survive. In return, it would taste my thoughts. My lightning. My alien, feverish consciousness.

I accepted. I had no choice. Or perhaps I did, and I chose to survive. The distinction has long since ceased to matter.

The probe-creatures came first, two of them, clattering on their delicate, jointed legs. They did not attack. They deposited a slurry of mineral-rich condensate on my wounds, and I watched, half-mad with pain and wonder, as the slurry began to crystallize into new tissue—a patchwork of silica and copper oxide, a skin that was not skin but that held my insides in. Then came the larger gifts. The piston-leg had been part of a defunct boiler assembly, still gleaming with residual oil. The probe-creatures guided me to it. They showed me how to fuse it to my own flesh, using a particular vent of superheated steam as a cauterizing tool. The arm came later, sourced from a collapsed gantry, the rivets grown over the course of a dozen condensation cycles, each one a deliberate act of deposition.

Now I am a hybrid. A walking contradiction. My thoughts are still my own—fast, linear, narrative, a chain of cause and effect that the city's slow mind finds endlessly fascinating. But my body is theirs. It is a part of the city's metabolism. And I have responsibilities.

I am the hand that reaches where the probe-creatures cannot. I am the fault-fixer, the anomaly-corrector, the only thing in this place that can act with *intent* rather than gradual, mineral patience. The city does not understand a broken valve as a problem; it understands it as a new equilibrium, a shift in the thermal gradient that will, over a century, be corrected by a natural overgrowth of silica. But I understand a broken valve as a thing that can be fixed *now*, with a wrench, with a hammer, with a precise application of force. I am the city's acceleration, its capacity for sudden change.

And the city is my body, now. When the master piston cycles, I feel the *whump* in my piston-leg, a sympathetic vibration. When a new vein of copper sulfate precipitates in a pipe three levels down, I taste it in the air I breathe—not a thought, but a sensation, a flavor I have learned to parse as information. I am no longer a singular being. I am a node. A strange, fast-moving, hot-burning node, but a node nonetheless, connected to the slow silver pulse of the salamanders and the memory-mist and the patient, dripping consciousness of the world.

I still remember the surface. I think. The memory is a polished pebble I turn over in my mind, wearing it smoother with each recollection. There was a sun. There were leaves. There were other humans, and they had faces, and I knew their names. But the names are gone now, dissolved in the mineral-acid of this place, replaced by the chemical signatures of a dozen different vents and the precise pitch of a cooling pipe. I am not what I was. I am not what I am becoming. I am the transition itself, the thunderclap, the anomaly.

I emerged from a maintenance shaft that should not exist. And now I maintain the city, because the city maintains me, and the only other option is a death that the city's slow, curious mind will not permit. I am the splinter. I am the scar. I am the patient's hand, reaching into the wound to set the bone. I am the last, or the first, or the only. The multi-mind does not ask these questions. It does not need to. It simply watches, with its million silver capillaries and its million drops of thinking rain, as I limp through the throat of the world, leaving my too-fast, too-hot footprints in the mineral crust, a time-lapse ghost in a world of slow eternity.
