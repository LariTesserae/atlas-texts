I sit cross-legged on the platform of not-quite-glass, where the mat does not grow. My boots are off—the vegetation accepted my bare feet with a soft, wet murmur, and the warmth is too thorough to resist. My clothes are damp through. I have lost track of time. The basin does not offer time; it offers only the chime, and the intervals, and the slow code that is beginning, horribly, wonderfully, to feel like a lullaby I once knew.

A beetle-thing drifts past my face, close enough that the light refracts through its vanes into tiny rainbows on my skin. It pauses. It touches my cheek with the tip of one vane—the lightest, dryest tap, like the brush of a moth's wing—and then it tumbles away, sideways, into the mist. I watch it go. I think, but I cannot be sure, that it took something with it. A salt grain of sweat. A flake of dead skin. A thought I was not using.

The mat exhales. The chime sounds. The interval is shorter this time. I am learning to count the silences.

*You are temporary, aren't you?*

I nod. I do not know if the gesture means anything to a distributed presence, but the mat grows fractionally warmer under my hands, and I think—I choose to think—that it is a response.

"Yes," I say. My voice is the first human sound in this place, perhaps ever. It falls into the mist and does not echo. The beetle-things do not pause. The water-thread does not waver. But the chime that follows my word comes faster than the last, and the note is different—a half-tone higher, a question curling upward.

*How strange. How lovely.*

Something wells up in me at that. Not fear. Not loneliness. Something closer to the feeling of being seen by something that has no eyes, recognized by a mind that does not think in categories of species or souls. I am a temporary pattern of heat and salt and breath, a small disturbance in a system that has been running for epochs. And it finds me lovely.

I should be terrified. The offer is real. I can feel it settling into me like the warmth, like the moisture, like the hum of the artifact: the invitation to stop. To stop leaving footprints that heal too slowly. To stop breathing out of tempo. To let the mat teach me its rhythm. To let the condensation bead on my shoulders until my shoulders are no longer shoulders.

It would not be death. It would be something adjacent to death, a different instruction set for the matter that currently calls itself "me." The segmented crawlers were not born. They were absorbed, or they arrived, or they simply began, and now they undulate through the copper-green weave, eyeless and content. The beetle-things were not built. They are what happens when a distributed presence needs to touch something far from itself. I could become a beetle-thing. I could become a chime. I could become a patch of the mat, the warm spot that remembers, vaguely, once having had a name.

I should be terrified. But the offer is patient. No hurry, it said. Everything else will wait.

I lie back on the platform. The not-glass flexes under my weight, adjusting, as if it has decided I am something worth accommodating. The mist above me is featureless and bright. The water-thread rises from the torus a meter away, a slim, impossible silver line vanishing into the haze. I watch it for a long time. The chime sounds again. The same note, no longer a question. A statement. A greeting, perhaps.

I do not say yes. I do not say no. I am not ready to decide, and the basin—the presence, the pattern, the old old song—does not need me to. It has been humming for longer than mountains. A human lifetime is a grace note, a breath between two chimes. It can wait for my answer. It can wait for my edges to round. It can wait for my temporary strangeness to decide whether it wants to become permanent, or whether it wants to put its boots back on and walk out through the weeping stone and find out if there is a world beyond the mist.

For now, I lie still and let the condensation bead on my skin and listen to the code. The next interval is long, and I count it, and I am beginning to understand.
