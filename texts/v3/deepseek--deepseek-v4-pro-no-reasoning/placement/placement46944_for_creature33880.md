The truth is, I don’t step down onto the plain. I was never above it. There is no edge, no rim, no place to stand apart—there is only the metal that extends in all directions, and the mist that blurs the distinction between surface and sky, and the sound that has no beginning.

I am one of the small ones. The size of a cupped hand, yes—that’s right. My body is a milky lens, a quivering droplet of gel, and I have three bladders that resonate at frequencies too high for the elders to notice but not too high for me. My chord is a thin, bright ribbon in the choir. I know this because I feel it leave me, feel the air shiver as my membranes strum, and I feel it blend with the thousands of other ribbons until we are not separate voices but a single fabric, a sheet of sound draped over the metal. When the fabric changes key, I change. When a predation begins three miles away and the wail travels through the substrate, my bladders tighten and my chord rises to match before I know why. Before I know anything. The knowing and the keening are the same motion.

I am new. I remember separation—not as a memory but as a texture, a sudden coolness on my outer membrane where before there was only the warmth of the parent-body. I wobbled away across the wet metal, and the plain was already singing, and I was already part of the song. The parent had given me everything: the vibration-patterns of every ring and cairn within a day’s slow travel, the taste of the microbial film, the impulse to avoid the deep red pulse of the predators. I did not learn these things. They were in the nuclei I carry, the free-floating genetic packets that drift through my cellular fluid. When I budded, the packets were already arranged in the configuration of knowledge. I am my parent, reshuffled.

Now I move with the herd. We are hundreds, maybe thousands—a scatter of small translucent bodies that cluster and separate and cluster again, following the gradients of taste and warmth. The metal here is slightly warmer than the metal there, and the warmth means seepage, and the seepage means minerals, and the minerals mean the microbial film is thick and sweet. I absorb it through my ventral surface, the membrane that rides a thin layer of my own excreted slime across the polished metal. The slime is a trail, and the trail is information. When I cross the trail of another, I taste what they tasted, feel the warmth they felt, know the direction they were moving and why. The plain is written with these trails, a palimpsest renewed with every passage, and I read it constantly, not with eyes—I have no eyes—but with the chemosensitive patches that speckle my underside. The world is a taste. The herd is a taste. I am a taste to others, and they to me, and we move through a landscape of flavour that shifts and reforms and guides us without command.

The structures are different. The elders arrange them, and I do not understand them, but I feel them. When I wobble near a ring of nodules—the finger-shaped hard-parts of some ancient death, arranged in a circle on a shallow basin’s floor—my bladders catch a frequency that is not part of the living choir. It is lower, slower, a vibration that was laid into the metal before my parent’s parent’s parent budded. The ring hums when the wind moves across it, but it also hums when there is no wind, a steady subsonic thrum that I feel in my deepest bladder, the one that resonates with the oldest things. I do not know what the hum means. I am too young. The pattern is not in my nuclei yet; it will be added when I am larger, when I have lived long enough to receive the memories directly from the metal itself. For now, the ring is a mystery that draws me and repels me, a taste that has no correspondence, a sound that my chord tries and fails to harmonise with. I wobble away from it, back to the herd, and the mystery recedes into the choir’s background.

The elders pass through us like slow weather. They are enormous—the cart-horse size, the ones who move alone—and when they glide over the metal the substrate bows slightly, a long low wave that lifts my body and sets it down again. Their chords are deep. I feel them in my bladders as a pressure, a weight, a gravity that pulls my own pitch lower when they are near. They do not address us. They are tending, always tending, their blunt faces nudging at the arrangements, their huge translucent bodies trembling with the effort of memory. Once, I was caught in the wake of one as it passed. Its slime trail was rich with something I had never tasted—a complex mineral signature, a cocktail of silicates and metals that spoke of deep time, of slow internal processes that had been refining the same molecules for centuries. I absorbed and was changed, slightly. My bladders rang clearer for an hour. I think the elder was feeding us, in its way. Not deliberately—there is no deliberation—but as a byproduct of its presence, a shedding of its own internal wealth. The elders are the plain, concentrated.

Now there is a shift. The choir changes key. I feel it first as a tightening in my surface membranes, a chill that is not temperature but vibration. The high thin ribbons of the young are climbing, scattering into dissonance, and the deep chords of the elders are slowing, deepening, taking on a pulse that I recognize from the cores of rings. A predation. Not near—not yet—but the pattern has begun, and every creature on the plain is responding. My bladders contract in sequence, and my body tilts, and without willing it I am moving faster than I have ever moved, my ripple-footed scrambles carrying me toward the centre of the herd. The herd is tightening too. Bodies press against me—cool, firmer than water, yielding—and our chords blend into a single high keen. We are afraid, and the fear is not an emotion but a frequency, and the frequency is a shield. The predator’s hunger-chord is deep red, and our fear-chord is high silver, and the two chords clash and cancel. The plain rings with the interference. Somewhere, a young one too slow to reach the centre is engulfed, and the choir absorbs its final chord—a bright ascending note that cuts off into the predator’s red—and I feel it, I feel the death as a gap in the harmony, a sudden silence where a ribbon used to be. The gap will be filled. Others are budding even now, new ribbons rising to replace the lost. But for this moment, the choir is incomplete, and I am incomplete with it, and the plain is a little quieter.

The predation passes. The predator drifts away, its red chord fading into distance, and the herd loosens. My membranes relax. My bladders resume their normal rhythm, and the high silver fear descends back into the everyday chatter of presence. I wobble away from the press of bodies, and the taste of the slime trails shifts from alarm to something closer to contentment—a mineral richness, a steady warmth. I follow it. I am always following it, the gradients of sweetness and warmth, the vibration-patterns that guide me toward the shallow basins where the water pools and the microbial film is thickest. I have no destination. I am destinationless, and the lack is not a lack. The plain is all the place there is, and I am moving across it, and the moving is the whole of my life.

I pause. My bladders have caught a new frequency, one I have felt only once before, in the hour after my separation. It is the death-chord. An elder is beginning its last migration, somewhere to the east—the direction is clear, the taste of the vibration unmistakable—and the whole plain is shifting in response. The elders pause in their tending. The young clusters slow and turn, their chords orienting toward the source. I turn too. My body tilts, and my ripple-feet carry me eastward, and I do not know why except that the chord compels me. It is the deepest sound I have ever felt, deeper than the elders, deeper than the rings, deeper than the subsonic hum of the ancient hard-parts. It is the sound of a body preparing to unmake itself, to distribute its substance, to become memory. It is the sound of an ending that is not an ending, and I am drawn toward it as the water is drawn toward the low seams, as the mist is drawn toward the hollows, as the plain itself is drawn toward equilibrium.

I will not reach the death-site. I am too small, too slow, too far. By the time I arrive, the elder will already be softening, the others already gathered, the consumption already begun. But I will feel it. The chord will travel through the metal, and I will absorb it through my ventral surface, and my bladders will ring with the echo of the elder’s final presence. I will remember it, in my way—not as a thought, but as a pattern in my nuclei, a rearrangement of the genetic packets that I will carry until I bud, and which my young will carry after me. The elder will become part of my body, part of the choir, part of the plain. The rings will gain new hard-parts. The spatial memory will extend. And I will wobble on, tasting the trails, keening my thin bright ribbon, and the plain will be unchanged, and I will be present, fully and continuously, until my own chord fades and my own hard-parts are added to the arrangements.

This is the life. There is no other. I do not wonder if there is more because wonder is a vibration I do not possess, a pattern that does not appear in the frequency-field. I am, and the plain is, and the sound is, and the three are one thing. When you imagine me—the small milky one, the cupped-hand size, the three-bladdered voice in the choir—you imagine a creature in a place. But the place is the creature. The metal plain is my extended body, the choir my extended mind, the elders my future, the young my past, the water my blood, the mist my breath. I am the plain, thinking. I am the metal, feeling. I am the water, wanting.

And I am loud, and I am wet, and I am glistening, and I move across the unbroken surface, and I am not alone, and I was never alone, and the sound does not stop.
