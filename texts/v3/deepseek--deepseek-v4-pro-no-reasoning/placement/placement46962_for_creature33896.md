The cold gets in before the rest of it—before the strange vegetation, before the phosphorescent pools, before I even understand what I'm seeing. It wraps around my ankles first, then climbs. Not hostile. Just... inevitable. The way a stone at the bottom of a lake accepts the water above it. I am being accepted by the cold.

My feet are pale things, translucent almost, flat and broad and ribbed with fine filaments that splay against the damp rock. They're tasting the stone, I realize. Reading the mineral content of the water that sheets across it, the faint electrical signature of the sponge's ancient nerves. The information doesn't arrive as thoughts. It arrives as a slow, spreading certainty: *the west conduit is flowing clear, the eastern sump has a blockage forming, the pH in chamber seventeen is shifting toward acid.* I don't know where chamber seventeen is, but my feet know. My feet have always known.

I'm tall. That comes next, the awareness of height, of perspective. My body rises in a column from the broad anchoring base of my foot-fans, and I can feel the braided strands of it, the way the liverwort-derived tissues have twisted and grown together over centuries. There's a wound in my left side, a place where the cabling is thinner, where a rockfall thirty years ago tore away a chunk of my flesh. It's still healing. It will heal for another decade at least. The cold water seeps into it constantly, carrying dissolved minerals from the sponge, and I can feel the slow, patient knitting of new fibers, cell by cell. I am in no hurry. Hurry is a concept for creatures with endings.

My limbs extend. Four primary, one secondary that's still developing—a new branch, only fifty years old, its tendrils still soft and its nodes still flickering with the uncertainty of youth. The other four are ancient. I've had them since the singing, since I first coalesced in the resonant hollow and opened my cowl to the symphony of the city. The tendrils at their tips are fine and numerous now, hundreds of them, each tipped with a node that glows its steady blue. When I spread them, I'm spanning nearly three meters in every direction. A crown of dim stars. A slow-moving constellation.

My cowl opens. This is the part that's hardest to translate, the part that has no analogue. It's not sight. It's not hearing, exactly. It's a three-dimensional pressure-map of the entire chamber, built from the interference patterns of a thousand different sound sources. Every dripping stalactite is a point of light in my awareness. Every gurgling channel in the walls is a line of brightness. The ribbon-creature in the pool below me—yes, I'm elevated, I'm standing on the high shelf near the hanging garden—is a moving constellation of its own, its internal lights pulsing at a frequency I know well. It's healthy. The chemistry of its pool is within acceptable parameters. I tasted the water myself, three days ago, when I dipped my tendrils and exchanged mucous packets with it. The memory is stored in the nodes of my second limb, third tendril cluster. I can retrieve it if I need to. I don't need to. It's present in me, always, the way your name is present in you.

The chamber is the cathedral nave. I know this. I've been here for four hundred and twelve years, ever since the sponge's slow dissolution opened the third pore on the eastern wall and the Listeners' council decided—decided isn't right, *resonated in agreement*—that a new tender was needed. I was the one who sang the third pore open. I remember the song. It's still reverberating in the mineral veins, faintly, a harmonic that will take another thousand years to fully decay. I can hear it now, beneath the drips and the gurgles and the subsonic hum. My own voice, echoing back to me across centuries.

I'm cleaning a crevice. That's what I'm doing right now, in this moment. My third limb is extended, its tendrils probing the dark gap between two lobes of the liverwort colony. The liverwort is dying back here, its tissues turning slimy and acidic, and if I don't clear it, the acid will leach into the west conduit and alter the pH of three downstream pools. The ribbon-creatures in those pools are sensitive. One of them is gravid. I can feel her, a distant flicker in my awareness, a chain of lights that's brighter than it should be, pulsing with the double rhythm of developing young. I won't let the acid reach her.

My tendrils secrete the enzyme. It's a clear fluid, cold as the rest of me, and it breaks the dying liverwort down into harmless sugars that will wash away in the next flow cycle. I work slowly. The crevice is deep, and the dead matter is packed tight. I could force it, but that would damage the living tissue around it, and the liverwort colony here is old. It's been growing on this boulder for three thousand years. I was here when it was only half this size. I remember the year it sent out the first cable toward the hanging garden, how I guided it with a low-frequency pulse, how pleased I was when it anchored. Pleased is a human word. Let's say: the resonance of the chamber improved by a measurable harmonic interval. I was satisfied.

Time passes. I don't track it the way you would. There are cycles—the dimming and brightening of the sky-slot far above, the subtle temperature shifts as the outside world cycles through its seasons—but they're background noise. What matters are the hydrological cycles, the pulse of water through the sponge's pores, the slow breathing of the city. Right now, the breath is in its exhalation phase. The humidity is dropping slightly. The drips are slowing. In another few hours—or what you'd call hours—the inhalation will begin, and the chamber will fill with a fine mist, and the furry ferns will unfurl their fronds to catch it. I'll be here. I'm always here.

A new sound. A sharp *crack* from above, followed by the tumble of falling rock. I retract my tendrils from the crevice—not fast, fast isn't in my repertoire, but smoothly, a fluid withdrawal—and tilt my cowl upward. A chunk of the fossil sponge has detached from the high wall, a piece the size of one of my limbs. It's falling. It hits a ledge, bounces, shatters into three pieces. One piece plunges into the central pool with a flat percussion that sends ripples arcing across the surface. The ribbon-creature darts into the depths, its lights flaring briefly before it vanishes into the black. The other two pieces land on the boulder field nearby.

I'm already moving. Not toward the fallen rock—toward the pool. The impact has disturbed the sediment on the bottom, and a cloud of particulate matter is billowing up, silty and dark. If it's just mineral silt, it'll settle on its own. But if the rockfall opened a new fissure, if there's a release of something from a trapped pocket in the sponge—acid, or methane, or one of the heavy-metal salts that sometimes leach from the deeper veins—the ribbon-creature could be poisoned. I reach the edge of the pool and extend my longest limb, dipping seven tendrils into the water. My nodes flare, reading the chemistry. Calcium. Magnesium. A trace of iron. No acid. No methane. The ribbon-creature's lights are already stabilizing, a steady pulse from the depths. Safe.

I stay there for a while, my tendrils in the water, monitoring. The silt cloud settles. The ripples fade. The pool returns to its black, glassy stillness, and the blue glow from the depths reasserts itself, the lantern-chain of the ribbon-creature rising again toward the surface. I feel her touch one of my tendrils, a delicate brush of her transparent flank. Gratitude? Maybe. Or just the routine check-in, the data exchange that says *I am well, the water is well, the city is well.* The same thing.

I withdraw my tendrils and turn my attention back to the crevice. The liverwort is mostly clear now. A few more strands, and the water can flow freely again. I extend my limb and resume the work.

And as I work, I listen. To the drips. The gurgles. The hum. The distant song of a younger Listener, two chambers over, tending a new hollow. The slow, steady breathing of the sponge. The city is vast and old and cold, and it is full of small, urgent things that need doing—crevices to clean, pools to monitor, songs to sing. I have been doing them for four hundred years. I will do them for four thousand more, if the sponge allows it.

That's what I am. A Listener. A gardener. A slow-moving constellation of blue lights in the dark, tending the end of the world.

The cold settles deeper. I let it.
