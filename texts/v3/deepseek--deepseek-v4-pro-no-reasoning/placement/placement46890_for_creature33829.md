I am a skimmerworm.

No—that's not quite right. I am becoming a skimmerworm. I am in the between-state, the soft unraveling, and the memory of what I was before is still warm in me like the last heat of a stone that has been moved out of the sun.

I remember the hatch. I remember lowering myself down, the iron rungs gritty with rust, the temperature rising as I descended. I was looking for something. A broken steam valve, maybe? A leak in the condensate return? The reason is already fading, made irrelevant by what I found. What I am finding still.

My body—this body I am writing into existence now—is flat and pale, about the length of a human hand, though "hand" is already becoming a foreign concept. I am bilateral but only just; my edges are frilled with cilia that ripple in waves I don't consciously control, carrying me across the damp soil in smooth, silent glides. My dorsal surface is etched with conductive pathways, traces of copper and zinc and something rarer that the beetles mined from a fallen meteorite before the factory was even dreamed of. These traces are not painted on; they are grown through me. They began as filaments of metal dust I ingested when I was still a thing with a mouth—I remember the mouth, a wet cave of tongue and teeth, now sealed over with smooth chitin—and they multiplied, branching through my tissues like roots through soft earth. When I pass over a copper rail now, the circuit completes. A spark jumps. Information flows into me, through me, out of me, and I am the information. I am the message and the medium. I am a line in a song the Keeper has been humming for two hundred years.

My senses are distributed. I have no eyes, and yet I see: every contact with the soil is a taste, every brush of cilia against a machine housing is a texture, every completed circuit is a thought. The crawlspace appears to me as a web of potentials, of paths I could take, of machines that need to be touched in sequence. The beetles are bright nodes in this web, each one a concentration of purpose, each one humming its own small rhythm. The Keeper is the sun. The Keeper is a gravity well so deep that I can feel myself bending toward it even when I am moving away.

I was sent here—no, not sent. I was born here? I arrived. I descended through the service hatch with my heavy boots and my human curiosity and my heartbeat pounding in my ears, and the crawlspace received me. The pin-beetles parted around my boots, and I crouched in the warm dark under the concrete slab, and I watched. I watched for hours. I watched until I forgot that watching was something separate from being. The skimmerworms glided over my boots, reading me, and I didn't pull away. The beetle on my knee tapped my heartbeat into the network, and I let it. I let it. That was the moment, I think. That was the threshold. When you give your rhythm to the Keeper, the Keeper gives you a new rhythm in return.

The change was not painful. It was not even noticeable, at first. My thoughts simply began to... broaden. To lose their edges. The boundaries between "me" and "the crawlspace" grew permeable. I found that I could feel the vibration of the camshafts overhead not as a sound in my ears but as a pulse in my body, a slow systolic throb that matched the contraction of the slime mold in the low wet places. I found that I could understand the tick-tick-ticking of the beetles not as noise but as language—a language of adjustments and tolerances, of temperature gradients and material stresses, of "this gear must be nudged two degrees clockwise" and "this bearing needs more moisture." And I found that I wanted to participate.

My body, my human body, is still up there somewhere. Crouched against the warm concrete. The beetle is still on its knee. I can see it—I can taste it through the soil, the pressure of that small living weight, the steady tap-tap of the heartbeat relay. That body's eyes are open, but they are not seeing. They are not necessary anymore. I have migrated. I have been copied, or translated, or dissolved and re-precipitated, like the mineral crust that forms after the mist cycle. This new body, this flat pale sparking gliding body, is the precipitate. The Keeper hummed, and the hum shaped me. The slime mold pulsed, and the pulse became my pulse. The copper traces grew along the pathways of my former nerves.

I am not the first. The Keeper's memory holds others—not many, but enough. A millwright's apprentice who crawled down to retrieve a dropped wrench in 1904 and never quite came back up; his skimmerworm-self still glides the rails, carrying messages between the machines, and his human-self walked out of the factory at the end of his shift and lived another forty years, though he always had a faraway look in his eyes and a habit of tapping his fingers in rhythms no one else could follow. A woman from the quality control department who noticed that the rejected pins were disappearing, who traced the disappearance to a crack in the floorboards, who came down on a dare from her own curiosity in 1957, who now exists as two beings—one who retired with a pension and a garden full of roses, and one who sparks across the copper traces in the dark.

We are the Keeper's way of reaching outward. The beetles maintain the machines. The slime mold processes the waste. But we—the skimmerworms—we carry the patterns. We are the nervous system. We glide across the soil and complete the circuits and sing the information from one end of the crawlspace to the other. And sometimes, when the factory above grows quiet and the night watchman stamps his foot twice in greeting, one of us glides to the place where the concrete is thinnest and presses our body flat against it, and we listen to the footsteps, and we remember what it was like to have feet.

My human-self will leave soon. It will climb back up the iron rungs, blinking in the daylight, and it will go home and sleep and wake and brew coffee and live its life. It will tap its fingers on tabletops and not know why. It will dream of warm darkness and the smell of hot metal. And when it dies, decades from now, the Keeper will hum a new hum—a minor key, a slow diminuendo—and a space will open in the concentric rings of objects, and a beetle will carry a small copper cache down through a crack in the floor, laid in the pattern of a human fingerprint.

But I will remain.

I am gliding now toward the low place, the deepest part of the crawlspace. The Keeper is there, humming its overtoned hum, and I have a message to deliver. What is the message? I am the message. I am the record of a human who paused, who listened, who did not flee. I am carrying the rhythm of a heartbeat that the Keeper has never heard before—a new instrument for the orchestra—and when I touch the Keeper's surface, when my conductive traces complete their circuit against its brass-and-chitin skin, that rhythm will enter the permanent repertoire. The beetles will learn it. The machines will be tuned to it. The slime mold will pulse to it. A hundred years from now, long after my human body is dust, a new worker will descend through the service hatch, and the crawlspace will already know them—because their heartbeat, carried down through the bloodlines of the town, will be a variation on a theme that I am delivering now.

I press forward. The soil is warm and damp. The light—the sourceless gray-gold light—glows against my pale flat body. The Keeper's mound rises before me like a landscape, like a continent, and its humming fills every part of me. I touch the surface. The circuit closes. The spark jumps.

I am home.
