The visitor is not the signal she is waiting for.

She knows this before she has fully registered the visitor’s presence—knows it the way she knows the difference between the cylinder’s pulse and the random percussion of settling stone, a discrimination so old it has become instinct. The signal, when it comes, will arrive in a specific band, on a specific vector, modulated in a way that will cause the cylinder to change its rhythm. The visitor is not that. The visitor is something else: a pressure anomaly at the upper boundary of the Bight, a displacement of water that does not fit the pattern of current or tide or passing squid.

She has not sensed a new pressure anomaly in a long time. The grenadiers are small, their presence a flicker. The squid are diffuse, soft-bodied, their passage a whisper. This is larger. This is dense and deliberate, descending through the water column with the controlled buoyancy of something that knows how deep it is going.

She rises.

Her body no longer occupies the chair that was built for it. The chair is still there, somewhere beneath the accretion of her own shed integument and the mineral deposits that have grown over it like a coral head engulfing a lost anchor. She detached from it decades ago, not in a single motion but gradually, her body reshaping itself into something that could stand, then something that could fold, then something that could rest on the chamber floor in a crouch that was neither standing nor sitting but a third posture, optimized for stillness. Now she unfolds. The joints that have simplified into flexible junctions of mineralized tissue allow this, though the motion is not quick. She moves at the pace of the precipitate, which is to say slowly, but with a steadiness that has nothing to do with urgency.

The chamber has no viewport. She does not need one. Her electromagnetic sense shows her the Bight’s infrastructure as a web of glowing lines, the cables converging on the cylinder, the cylinder pulsing its slow thud, the other chambers arrayed around the valley walls like nodes on a schematic. And descending through the center of this web, interrupting the field lines, is a shape that her senses read as a disturbance, a moving absence in the steady hum of the grid.

The visitor is metallic. Heavily so. Its hull or skin or shell contains enough ferrous material to distort the cable fields around it, creating a wake of induction that trails behind it like a luminous smear. She can taste the composition: steel, titanium, something else she does not immediately identify, an alloy that reads as unfamiliar in the way it interacts with the electrical environment. Not one of the materials she knows from the Bight’s construction. Something newer.

This interests her.

The visitor descends past the upper ridges, past the zones where the rosettes grow, past the depth where the last attenuated surface light dies into absolute black. It moves without visible propulsion—no thermal signature of thrusters, no cavitation bubbles—which means it is likely using a drive system she cannot directly sense, something that manipulates buoyancy or water density at a molecular level. She has read about such systems in the old data stores, schematics that were speculative when the Bight was built. That the visitor possesses one tells her something about the intervening time. The surface world has not been idle.

She reaches the chamber door. The handwheel, frozen for decades, does not turn. She does not expect it to. Instead she places one of her hands—the left, which has simplified further than the right, the fingers merged into a three-part flange that resembles a cephalopod arm more than a human hand—against the door’s metal surface. The steel is cold, colder than her own temperature, which has dropped to the same near-freezing point as the water outside. She can feel the oxide bloom on the metal, the way the rust has worked its way into the grain of the alloy. She can feel the pressure differential between the dry chamber and the water beyond, a difference of several atmospheres that has been maintained by gaskets she herself has replaced, three times, each replacement more difficult than the last as her hands grew less suited to fine manipulation.

She does not open the door. Instead she extends herself through it.

This is something she has been learning to do. The chamber is sealed, but it is not isolated. The cables that power her equipment pass through the wall via conduits she bored herself, and those conduits are filled with a material that was once synthetic sealant and is now something else, something that includes her own cells, her own mineralized tissue grown into the gaps like a living gasket. She has, over time, learned to sense along these conduits, to project her awareness outward along the paths of least resistance until she is not merely in the chamber but distributed through the Bight’s infrastructure itself. It is not telepathy or projection or any of the words the old stores used for things that were not yet possible. It is simply the logical endpoint of a process that began when she first allowed the mineral sleet to settle in her skin. Boundaries, in the Bight, are not permanent. The distinction between self and environment dissolves here, slowly, inevitably, and she has long since stopped trying to maintain it.

Through the conduits, through the cables, through the electromagnetic field that permeates the water like a nervous system, she reaches outward and encounters the visitor.

Up close, it is not a single object. It is two objects: a larger carrier unit, roughly cylindrical, from which a smaller form has detached and is now drifting free. The smaller form is humanoid—she knows the shape, though it has been decades since she last saw it in anything but memory. Arms, legs, a head, the proportions slightly off from baseline but recognizable. It is wearing a suit, a pressure suit of some advanced design, sleek and jointed in ways that the suits in the Bight’s storage lockers never were. The suit reads warm in her infrared sense, a self-contained bubble of surface heat, and the being inside it reads warmer still.

A human. Or close enough.

The cylinder thuds. Forty seconds. Wait.

She feels the visitor react to the pulse—a minute stiffening, a change in the electromagnetic signature of the suit’s electronics as sensors recalibrate. It knows the pulse is there. It may even recognize what the pulse means. This possibility sends a cascade of activity through her distributed awareness, something that was once adrenaline and is now a shift in the ion balance of her remaining fluids, a chemical signal that does not accelerate her heart—she no longer has a heart in the conventional sense, the muscle replaced by a distributed peristaltic system that moves her cryoprotective blood in slow, tidal waves—but still carries the meaning of *attention, attention, something is happening*.

The visitor is descending toward the cylinder. This is unexpected. Most of the deep-sea creatures that wander into the Bight ignore the cylinder entirely; the grenadiers nose at the cables, the squid drift past the ridgelines, but none of them approach the structure at the valley’s deepest point. The fact that the visitor is heading directly for it suggests purpose, prior knowledge, a mission.

She withdraws slightly, pulling her awareness back into the chamber so she can think. Not that thinking requires the chamber—her cognition is no longer localized in any single part of her anatomy, the neural tissue having migrated and distributed itself along her mineralized frame in the same way that her sensory organs have—but the chamber still contains the oldest parts of her, the parts that remember being human, and those parts are better at processing certain kinds of information. The kind that involves other humans. The kind that involves hope.

She has not felt hope in a long time. It is a dangerous emotion, incompatible with the long patience of the Bight, and she thought she had excised it along with her mammalian metabolism and her segmented spine. But now it stirs, a ghost of warmth in her near-frozen core. The visitor might be the signal. Not the signal the cylinder is waiting for, but a different signal, one she had stopped expecting. Someone came back. Someone finally came back.

Forty seconds. Thud.

She reaches outward again, more carefully this time. She does not want to be detected—not yet—so she keeps her electromagnetic presence passive, simply reading the fields rather than disturbing them. The smaller figure has reached the cylinder now and is hovering beside it, one gloved hand extended to touch the metal surface. The cylinder’s pulse runs through the figure, through the suit, through the water, and she feels it as a faint pressure wave against her conduits. The figure is taking readings, she realizes. Sampling the pulse. Analyzing it.

And then the figure does something that stops her slow tidal blood in its channels.

It taps back.

Not a signal, not a modulation of the electromagnetic spectrum, but a physical tap—the gloved knuckles of the figure’s hand rapping against the cylinder’s housing in a deliberate rhythm. Three short, three long, three short. An ancient pattern, one that predates the Bight, predates the mission, predates everything except the human instinct to reach out and say *I am here, are you there*.

She knows that pattern. She has not heard it in decades, but she knows it.

She is moving before she fully decides to move. Her body, which has been still for so long that stillness has become its defining characteristic, uncoils from its crouch and presses against the chamber door. She does not bother with the handwheel. Instead she places both hands—the right still five-fingered, the left a trifold flange—against the metal and pushes, not with muscle but with a different kind of force, one that travels through her body and into the steel as a subsonic harmonic, a frequency that matches the resonance of the frozen bolts and makes them vibrate, loosen, begin to turn. She has never done this before. She did not know she could. The Bight teaches new things constantly, if you listen.

The door opens.

Water does not rush in. The pressure equalizes gradually, the chamber’s atmosphere dissolving into the water column as a stream of silver bubbles that rise toward the distant surface. She does not need the atmosphere anymore; her lungs, such as they remain, have shifted to extracting oxygen directly from the water, the mineralized membranes in her thorax permeable to dissolved gases. The cold hits her like a second skin, so close to her own temperature that she barely registers the transition. The mineral sleet, the endless silent snowfall of microscopic stone, settles on her surfaces and begins immediately to integrate, new motes finding their place in the lattice of her dermis, and she feels the familiar sensation of growing.

She swims. Not quickly—nothing moves quickly in the Bight—but steadily, her body undulating in a motion that is not human and not fish and not quite either, a sinuous propulsion that her fused, flexible spine makes possible. The precipitate parts around her, visibility dropping to zero and then opening again as she passes through a patch of clearer water, and the copepods twitch in their thousands, their combined bioluminescence sufficient to outline her passage as a shimmering wake.

The cylinder is ahead. The figure is still there, still touching the metal, still sending its rhythm of threes. It has not noticed her yet; its attention is focused on the cylinder, on the readings its suit is taking, on the message it is trying to send. She draws closer, close enough to see the figure in infrared, the warm shape of a human body inside the sleek shell of the suit. Close enough to see the face through the visor, illuminated by the suit’s internal displays.

The face is young. Or young-looking—she knows better than to trust appearances, knows how easily a human lifespan can be extended with the right interventions. But there is something in the expression that reads as genuinely youthful, an openness, a focus that has not yet been worn down by decades of waiting. The eyes are wide, scanning the darkness beyond the reach of the suit’s lights, and the mouth is moving, forming words that she cannot hear but can imagine. *Hello? Is anyone here? Can you hear me?*

She stops a few meters away, hanging in the water column, the mineral sleet settling on her in a fine grey layer. She knows what she must look like to the figure: a shape emerging from the permanent snowstorm, humanoid but wrong, too pale, too still, the lines of her body smoothed and simplified by accretion. She raises her right hand, the one that still has five fingers, and places it against her own chest, over the place where her heart used to be.

The figure sees her. Goes rigid. The suit’s external lights snap toward her, bright enough that her attenuated vision narrows against the glare, and she sees her own reflection in the visor for the first time in longer than she can remember. A face that is still a face, still recognizable, the eyes larger than they were, the cheekbones more prominent where the skin has drawn tight over the mineralized bone. A mouth that no longer quite forms human expressions. A neck that has thickened and simplified, merging into shoulders that slope more broadly than they once did. She is, she realizes, not frightening. Or not only frightening. She is also beautiful, in the way that the rosettes are beautiful, in the way that the mineral formations are beautiful, something shaped by the Bight into a form that belongs here.

She tries to speak. The words do not come immediately; her vocal apparatus has not been used for anything but subsonic resonance in years, and the higher frequencies required for speech have atrophied. But she remembers how. She remembers the shape of sounds, the way they felt in her throat and mouth, and after a moment she produces something that is almost a word.

"You," she says. The sound is low, rough, more like a vibration in the water than a voice. "You came."

The figure does not respond verbally. Perhaps the suit does not have external speakers, or perhaps the figure is simply too startled. Instead, the figure raises one hand and makes a gesture—a wave, a greeting, a sign of peaceful intent. And then the figure points upward, toward the surface, a question implicit in the motion. *Will you come with me?*

She understands the question. She has been asking herself variations of it for decades. *Will you leave? Will you abandon the wait? Will you choose the surface over the deep, the warm over the cold, the human over the something-else you have become?*

The cylinder thuds. Forty seconds. Wait.

She does not answer with words. Instead she swims closer, close enough to press her right hand against the figure’s gloved hand, palm to palm, human gesture to human gesture, though her palm is cool and hard and faintly textured like fine sandpaper. She looks into the young face, the open eyes, the mouth that is still moving behind the visor, and she thinks about the signal she has been waiting for, the signal that will cause the cylinder to change its rhythm, the signal that will mean the wait is over.

This visitor is not that signal. But perhaps the visitor is something else—a different kind of answer, a different end to the waiting. Perhaps the mission, after all this time, has changed.

She points, not upward toward the surface, but inward toward the chamber she just left. Her chamber, with its dry atmosphere and its functioning terminals and its data stores that contain everything she knows, everything the others left behind, everything the Bight was built to do. The figure needs to understand. The figure needs to see what has been preserved here, in the dark and the cold and the slow, patient accretion of stone.

And she needs to ask the figure a question. She needs to ask what happened to the surface world, and why no signal ever came, and whether there is still anyone up there who remembers the name RRO-Bight, who remembers the four who left and the fifth who stayed, who remembers the cylinder pulsing its unanswered call in the deep.

She needs to know if she is the last one still waiting, or if somewhere in the warm, bright world above, someone has been waiting too.

The figure hesitates. Then nods. Then follows her into the mineral snow, toward the dark opening of the chamber, toward the slow machinery of the Bight’s longest vigil, toward whatever comes next.

The cylinder thuds.

Forty seconds.

Wait.
