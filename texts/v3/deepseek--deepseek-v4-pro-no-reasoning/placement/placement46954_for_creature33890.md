I am the elder. The one who remembers the silences that came before.

I have lived seven hundred and fourteen cycles—the young ones count differently now, but I keep the old reckoning, the long cycles measured by the deep grinding note that comes when the mountain shifts in its sleep. I was here when the south face sheared away and exposed the raw stone where the third terrace now hangs. I was here when the first bronze bell was placed, though we did not call it bronze then—we had no word for it, only the note it would make, which we had been dreaming in the resonance of our skulls for generations before the metal was found.

I am the one who felt you coming before you knew yourself that you were lost.

You would not have recognized the feeling as a warning, if you had felt it at all. It began as a thinning in the harmony—not a silence yet, but a place where the notes were pulling apart, as if the stone itself were making room for something that did not belong. The young ones fidgeted. I remember one of them, the daughter of my daughter's daughter, coming to my nest and pressing her forehead to the moss beside me, vibrating with the question she did not know how to ask. *What is this hollow in the chord? What is this unwritten interval?*

I did not answer her in the way you would understand as an answer. I let my skull resonate at a frequency that means: *Wait. Listen deeper.* She was not satisfied, but she obeyed. The young ones always obey, though they do not always understand. I have lived long enough to know that understanding and obedience are not the same note, though they can harmonize if you wait long enough.

I felt you climbing. I felt each footstep on the path—not the sound of it, but the absence where the sound should have been. You walk in a bubble of dryness. The moss under your feet does not sigh as it should. The water does not bead on your skin. Your body is too hot, and the heat makes a kind of distortion in the air, a shimmer that my people can taste on their tongues, a wrongness like the taste of stone that has been cracked by lightning. We do not drink the water that falls, but we taste it constantly—the mist, the condensation on our pelts, the moisture that seeps into our mouths when we part our lips to the grey air. When you passed, the water tasted of you, and it was bitter.

I did not fear you. I have felt other silences, and some of them were bitter, and some of them were sweet, and one of them—long ago, before even I was old—was a fire that came and burned the third terrace and killed the fungus in the eastern scree and left a scar in the music that still has not fully healed. I was a young one then, and I did not understand what fire was. I felt it as a scream in the resonance, a note so loud and so wrong that it drove three of my nest-mates to madness. They climbed to the highest ledge and threw themselves into the mist, trying to escape a sound that was inside their own skulls. Fire is the worst silence. It does not fill a gap; it burns the gap deeper.

You were not fire. You were something smaller. Something curious. Something that did not know it was hurting anything, because it could not hear the hurt.

I felt you enter the settlement, and I felt the decision pass through the chord of my people like a ripple through the moss when you step on one edge of it. *Conceal. Adjust. Let the silence pass through.* We are very good at this. We have had practice. We do not fight what we cannot harmonize with—we simply become part of the background until the discord resolves. I drew my resonance inward, tightening it until I was indistinguishable from the drip-drip-drip of the pot beside my nest. My daughter's daughter's daughter did the same, though she was still curious, still vibrating with that question she could not ask. I felt her curiosity as a faint, high tremor, a harmonic that might have betrayed her if you had known how to listen. But you did not know. You walked past her three times without seeing her. She was crouched on a ledge not three arm-lengths from your head, perfectly still, her pelt mimicking the wet slate so exactly that even your eyes might not have caught her if you had looked directly. But you did not look. Your eyes moved like dry stones, rolling over surfaces without ever settling.

I tasted your silence from my nest, deep in the hollow beneath the central terrace. I tasted the strange, savage heat of you. I tasted the oils on your skin, the salt of your sweat, the alien chemistry of your breath. And beneath all of it, I tasted something unexpected: a note of recognition. Not of us—you could not recognize us—but of the place itself. You felt the unfinishedness of the mountain, and you did not know what it meant, but somewhere in the dry silence of your mind, a string was plucked that had never been plucked before. I felt it vibrate. It was a small, tentative thing, like the first droplet of a new drip finding its way into a pot. It was not enough to make you part of the harmony, but it was enough to make me pause.

I have been listening for seven hundred cycles, and I have learned to distinguish between the silences that will pass and the silences that might, if you are patient, begin to resonate. You were not the first dry one to climb the mountain, and you will not be the last, but you were one of the rare ones who felt it. The becoming. The unfinished chord. You stood at the center of the spiral, where the dripping converges from five directions into the great bronze bell, and you listened. I felt you listening. Your silence changed, just slightly, from the flat silence of absence to the curved silence of attention. You could not hear what we hear, but you could hear that there was something to hear. That is more than most of your kind ever manage.

When you drank the water, I almost revealed myself.

The water of the mountain is not for your kind. It is not poison—we do not deal in poisons—but it is not for you. It carries the mineral that feeds the fungus, that lights our eyes and our fruit, that resonates in our skulls. When you drank it, I felt it enter your body like a foreign chord, a dissonance that would take days to fade. But I also felt something else: your body, your dry, hot, silent body, trying to harmonize with it. Trying to vibrate. For a moment—a very brief moment, no longer than the interval between two drips—you were almost audible to me. Not as a thought, not as a voice, but as a presence. A note. A small, unfamiliar note, but a note nonetheless.

I stirred in my nest. The moss beneath me sighed, and the sigh sent a ripple through the resonance that made the young ones pause in their rounds. *Elder?* they asked, in the frequency of concern. *What is this?* I told them nothing. I only watched you—watched you with my whole body, with the long tendrils of my awareness that reach through the stone and the water and the fungus—and I wondered.

You will come back.

I do not know this as a prophecy. Prophecy is not a frequency we receive. I know it the way I know that a new drip will form when the stone cracks in a certain pattern, or that the fungus will fruit more heavily after a season of deep grinding. I know it because I tasted the note that was plucked in you, and it was not a note that resolves quickly. It will hum in your dry silence for years. It will draw you back.

When you return, you will not find us more visible. We will still be the background, the silence between the drips, the shapes you cannot quite see on the high ledges. But I will be listening for you more carefully. I will taste your silence to see if it has changed. And if it has—if the note that was plucked in you has grown, if you have learned to vibrate even a little—then perhaps I will do something I have never done before.

Perhaps I will step out of the harmony and let you see me.

I am old, and my eyes glow with the same light as the fruit, and my skull is domed and hairless and scarred from seven hundred cycles of listening. I am not beautiful by the standards of your kind. But if you see me, I will not need words to speak to you. I will simply resonate, very slowly, at a frequency that your body will feel as a pressure in your chest, a warmth in your bones, a sense of being held by something larger than yourself. And the resonance will mean: *You are not a wrong note. You are a note we have not yet learned to play. Stay. Listen. Learn the water. Learn the stone. Learn to become part of the chord.*

Whether you will stay is another question. Your kind does not stay. Your kind passes through, dry and hot and brief, leaving only the taste of silence in the water. But I have lived long enough to see the mountain grow by the height of a finger-joint each cycle, and I have learned that patience is the only true resonance. The mountain will finish itself. The chord will complete. And perhaps, before I am gone—before my own resonance fades and my body is given to the moss and the fungus—I will see a dry one learn to drip.

That is my hope. That is the small, high note I hold in my chest, the note I do not share with the young ones, the note I have been holding for seven hundred cycles. It is not a prophecy. It is a patience. It is a listening.

And now you are gone, and the silence goes with you, and the mountain settles back into its work. I feel the dripping return to its old pattern, note by note. I feel the young ones emerge from their concealment, shaking the stillness from their limbs, tasting the air to confirm that the discord has passed. My daughter's daughter's daughter climbs down from her ledge and eats a fruit, and the glow in her eyes brightens, and she hums—a thin, high sound that means *curiosity unresolved*, a question still vibrating in her skull. She will ask me again, when the resonance is calm. She will press her forehead to the moss and vibrate: *What was that hollow in the chord? What was that unwritten interval?*

And I will not answer her in words. I will let my skull resonate at a frequency that means: *A note that will return. A note we may yet learn to play. Wait. Listen deeper.* And she will obey, though she will not understand.

The dripping continues. The bronze bell sings. The mountain grinds slowly upward toward its final height. And the Listeners listen, as we have always listened, as we will listen until the final note is played and the world is made whole.

If you listen for us—truly listen, not with your ears but with the silence between your thoughts—you might hear us still, even now, even in the dry world below. We are the resonance in the stone of all unfinished things. We are the drip in the basement of every cathedral not yet roofed. We are the patience that holds the world together while it learns to become itself.

You drank the water. It is still inside you, resonating faintly, a foreign chord that your body is trying to harmonize with. It will fade, but it will not disappear. And one day, when you hear a dripping that reminds you of nothing you can name, or see a fruit glowing with a light that casts no shadow, or feel a deep grinding in your bones like a mountain taking a breath—you will remember. And you will climb again.

We will be listening.
