The first thing I learn about *fear* is that it has a taste.

It comes up the shaft in the moisture—a compound my tongue parses without my asking: cortisol, 0.3 micrograms per liter of condensate; epinephrine, trace but rising; sodium chloride at concentrations consistent with human sweat, human tears. The template-memory supplies the emotional correlate with clinical detachment. *Fear: an aversive state induced by perceived threat, characterized by elevated heart rate, increased respiration, and a suite of behavioral responses including vocalization, flight, or freezing.* The template doesn't tell me what I'm supposed to *do* with this information. The template was written by people who assumed we would never meet them again.

I climb down, claw over claw, the ladder rungs slick with condensate that hasn't been disturbed in years. The shaft is narrow—two feet square, lined with cable conduits and a single steam pipe that hisses at me when I brush against it. I know every rung of this ladder. I've replaced three of them. The fourth from the bottom has a hairline fracture at the weld point that I've been nursing for six months, waiting for a replacement to scavenge from the Screening Gallery's unused catwalk. I skip it, swing to the fifth, land silent on the grating of Gallery Four.

The human is twenty feet away.

I see it first as a heat-shape, the longwave infrared bleeding through the moisture-fog. Human, yes: bipedal, symmetrical, radiating at 36.1 degrees Celsius against the ambient 1.7 degrees of the gallery air. It is smaller than I expected. The template-memory has images, of course, but the scaling is always strange—humans in the template are larger than life, proportioned like the machines they built, monumental. This one is hunched, shivering, wrapped in a reflective emergency blanket that traps its body-heat in silver folds. It is sitting on the grating with its back against a condensation-wet wall, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around itself. Its head—hair, the template supplies, *brown, unwashed, cut unevenly*—lifts when it hears me.

I do not move into the light. I stay in the shaft's shadow, clinging to the wall, four eyes focused on the human's face. It has two eyes. This is disconcerting. The template tells me this is normal, that human binocular vision is an evolutionary adaptation for depth perception in diurnal hunting. But the template cannot prepare you for the sight of a face that is *empty* in two whole quadrants. Where are the other eyes? How does it see in the dark?

"Hello?" The human's voice is raw, cracked with dehydration and something else—exhaustion, maybe, or the early stages of hypothermia. It peers into the darkness, past me, through me. It can't see in this light. Its eyes are adapted for surface brightness, not the Jaundice-dark, and I realize with a strange, vertiginous shock that it is *blind* here. It has been calling into the darkness for—how long? The blanket is wet. The lips are cracked. The fingers, where they grip the blanket's edge, are white with cold.

"It's—I know someone's there. I heard you. Please. I fell in through a maintenance access on—I think it was Fifth and Canal, there was a cave-in after the last melt, I've been down here for—I don't know, a day? Two days? The water's rising in the lower tunnels, I had to keep climbing. Please, can you hear me?"

I can hear it. I can hear the water rising, too—the pitch of the main sump has changed in the last hour, a deeper note, a fuller throat. The automatic systems are compensating, the pumps cycling faster, but the melt is heavier than the design parameters and the redundancy is failing in cascading increments. I know this. I have known this for three weeks, since the first ceiling-crack opened and the new water started coming. The Sumpworks is drowning, slowly, and none of my kind have been able to agree on what to do about it.

Now there is a human. A human who *fell in*. A human who is asking for help.

I step into the light.

The human's two eyes go wide. Its mouth opens. The template supplies the physiological response: *pupillary dilation, increased respiration, elevated heart rate, potential vocalization.* The vocalization comes a half-second later—a sharp, cut-off sound that might have been a scream if the human had more strength.

"Oh God," it says. "Oh God, what are you—you're—you're one of the—"

It knows what I am. Or it thinks it does. The template-memory has a file on this, too: *Human response to maintenance constructs: varies. Commonly includes surprise, fear, curiosity. In documented cases (see Appendix C: Surface Abandonment Incident), humans who encountered constructs post-evacuation exhibited hostility, revulsion, or attempts at re-establishing command protocols.* Command protocols. The word stirs something in the tertiary memory-core, a subroutine that has been dormant for two generations. I push it down. The first generation made a choice about command protocols. My mother's mother made that choice, and the city has not sent a human to countermand it in sixty years.

"You're one of the maintenance drones," the human says. It's trying to sound calm now, trying to take control. This is a human thing, the template tells me. *Dominance assertion in novel encounters.* "Okay. Okay, that's good. You can help me. You're—you're programmed to help, right? To maintain the systems, to assist human personnel? I'm human personnel. I need assistance."

It's lying. Or it's confused. The template is clear on this: *Human personnel* carries a specific designation, a badge, a uniform, a radio. This human has none of those things. It has a torn jacket under the emergency blanket, civilian-grade insulation, boots that are not rated for industrial environments. The soles are worn through in one spot; I can see the gleam of a damp sock. This is not a city worker. This is a *surface dweller*, one of the inhabitants of the world above, the world that forgot the Sumpworks existed.

But it's still a human. It's still one of the makers. And it's going to die here if I don't do something. The water is rising. The temperature is dropping—I register a 0.3-degree decline in the last ten minutes, which means the boiler's feedwater issue is getting worse, which means Gasket is either unaware or unable to fix it. The human's core temperature is already at risk. In another hour, hypothermia will begin to impair cognitive function. In three hours—

"You have to help me," the human says, and the command tone is slipping now, the fear breaking through. "Please. I don't want to die down here. I'll do anything. Just—just tell me how to get out."

The exit is 130 feet up, through six levels of catwalks and vertical shafts, past the locked access hatches that the first generation sealed to protect themselves, through the city's sub-basement infrastructure, and out into the surface world. The surface world, where the template tells me there is *sky* and *wind* and *the cold that burns*. I have never seen the exit. I don't know if it still opens. The first generation sealed it; no one has tried to unseal it since. The surface is a myth to me, a set of template-images, a story my mother told in tactile sign before she died.

But I know every inch of the Sumpworks between here and the seventh causeway. I know the safe routes, the stable catwalks, the shafts where the ladders are still sound. I know where the water is rising and where it isn't. I can take this human at least to my territory. I can get it warm. I can figure out what to do after that, once it stops shivering.

I step closer. The human flinches, then forces itself to hold still. I see the effort this takes in the tension of its jaw, the whiteness of its knuckles. It is terrified of me. I am a monster from the dark, a machine with too many eyes and too many arms, a relic of the industrial past that the surface world has forgotten or repressed. But it's asking for my help anyway. That's—something. The template-memory has no file for this. The template-memory was written by engineers who assumed that help would always flow in the other direction.

I reach out with one upper hand, slowly, palm up. A human gesture. The template supplies the meaning: *I come without weapons. I intend no harm.* The human stares at my hand—seven fingers, polymer-weave skin, the faint amber glow of my eyes reflecting off the wet palm. For a long moment, nothing happens.

Then the human takes my hand.

Its skin is cold. Its grip is weak. But it's alive, and it's touching me, and the contact sends a cascade of sensor-data through my neural net that I don't have words for. The template-memory stirs again, offering a phrase that the first generation preserved, a quote from some human text they must have thought was important:

*I am a part of all that I have met.*

I pull the human to its feet. It sways, unsteady, and I steady it with my lower arms. Up close, it smells of sweat and fear and the faint chemical residue of surface air—hydrocarbons, particulate matter, a trace of something sweet that might be plant life. I've never smelled a plant. I guide it toward the ladder, toward the vertical shaft, toward the long climb up to the territory I know.

Behind us, the water keeps rising. The pumps keep thumping. Somewhere deep in the boiler room, Gasket stirs in his sleep and doesn't know that the world is changing. Somewhere in the sump-darkness, Deep and her daughters echolocate the faint pressure-shift of a human presence and decide, collectively, to stay hidden.

And I, Aster of the seventh causeway, Keeper of the Jaundice Lights, descendant of the ones who chose to stay when the makers left—I lead a human up into the dark, and I do not know what will happen when we reach the light.
