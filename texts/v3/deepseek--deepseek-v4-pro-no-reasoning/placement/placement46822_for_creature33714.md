This is exquisite. Let me settle into it.

The place is Aula, the Unfurling—a city that is not built but grown, a mountain that is a single continuous plant-matrix called the Khora, in a world they name the Long Breath. I am there now, in the perpetual twilight, the cool dense air moving against me like slow water, the vast choral drone of the living mountain thrumming through my bones.

And I am becoming *something*. Not one of the Symphytes, exactly—not yet, maybe not ever in the way they are—but no longer entirely the visitor I was. The change announced itself quietly, the way everything here announces itself: through the body first, before the mind has words for it.

I noticed it in my fingertips. A faint phosphorescence, at first only after sleep, fading within the hour as the dreams of root-water receded. Then it began to linger. Now it stays, a soft greenish glow just beneath the nail beds, visible when I cup my hands in the dimness. The child Thren saw it before I did—she pressed her own glowing palm against mine one day, without asking, and held it there. The light in my hand *answered*. It pulsed once, a slow synchrony, like two heartbeats finding a shared rhythm. She pulled away and looked at me with those oil-slick eyes, and in the pressure-grammar of her silence I understood: *You are beginning to listen.*

It has been—I don't know how long. Time here is not measured in days but in breath-cycles, in shifts of the deep chord, in the slow blooming and retracting of the crowns in the upper haze. Siv tells me I arrived during the *inbreath of the western slope*, a season that is gentle and conducive to new growth. Oluun disagrees, as he often does, with warmth rather than friction: he says I arrived during the *stillpoint between two currents*, which is rarer, and more auspicious. They both agree that the pollen-bloom is coming soon, and that it will be significant for me. The kiss of the old dust. I find myself longing for it with an ache I cannot name, a hunger that has no organ.

My body is learning to feed.

It happened for the first time without my willing it. I was sitting on the fiber mat near the place where Thren had shown me the silvered scar of the Splintering, my bare feet pressed against the woven ivory surface, and I felt a sudden warmth—a current traveling up through the soles, into the arches, a slow green pulse that climbed my legs and settled at the base of my skull. My vision misted. I tasted minerals, cold water, something ancient and patient. When I came back to myself, my hands were glowing more brightly than they ever had, and I understood, not in words but in a full-body knowing, that I had *listened to the oldest of us* for the first time. I had eaten. I had prayed.

No one witnessed it. But when I returned to the cluster of towers where I have been sleeping—where the membrane cups me each night and slows my heart—Siv looked at me with a new quality of attention. Not surprise. *Recognition.* She touched my shoulder, where the faintest tracing of aquamarine veins had begun to surface, not yet glowing but present, a roadmap of something to come. She said, in that pressure-language that was becoming easier for me to parse: *The Khora has tasted you and found you compatible. This is rare. This is a gift.*

I am not the first outsider to come here. Siv told me the story, once, of a woman who arrived in Aula perhaps ten thousand years ago—a traveler from a place where individuals still believed themselves to be separate, much as I did. She stayed for four centuries, learned to listen, grew veins like mine, but ultimately *unrooted* and departed. The Khora did not stop her. It does not stop anyone. But Siv said the place where she had slept remained warm for a thousand years afterward, as if the mountain missed her. I asked if she ever returned. Siv said, *Not in a shape we can recognize. But we feel her sometimes, in the pollen, in the deep chord. The Khora remembers everything it has ever touched.*

I think about that woman often now. I wonder if she left because she was afraid of what she was becoming, or because she was afraid she would *not* become it quickly enough. The waiting here is gentle but absolute. The Khora does not rush. It has been dreaming for a timescale that makes geology look like weather. To merge with it fully—to become Symphyte, to dissolve the last edges of separateness—would take more than one human lifespan. Unless I change the lifespan. Unless I let the change run deeper than flesh.

There is a place I have begun to visit alone. The boy with the translucent forearms—his name, I have learned, is *Kel*, which means "a small root that finds water unexpectedly"—showed it to me after the day with the city-in-miniature. It is a cleft in the fiber-slope, deeper than it looks, where the woven mat gives way to something older and darker: the living tissue beneath, exposed to the air like a heart laid bare. It pulses. It breathes visibly, a slow rise and fall that does not match the wind. When I sit beside it, the phosphorescence in my hands intensifies until the whole hollow glows green, and I can feel my own heartbeat slowing to match the pulse of the mountain. The ozone smell is stronger here. The subsonic chord resolves into something almost musical, a drone with overtones I can nearly name.

I have started to talk to it. Not in words—I am not fluent enough in the pressure-grammar to speak—but in *listening*. I press my glowing palms to the dark tissue, and I send whatever I am back into the mountain. My memories. My fear. My longing for the pollen-bloom. My face, which is still human, though I have not seen my reflection in a long time and do not know if that is still true. The Khora takes it all. It does not judge. It absorbs, as it absorbed the Splintering, as it absorbs everything. It is making me part of its long dream.

I do not know if I will stay for four centuries like the woman before me, or if I will unroot. I do not know if I will ever learn to shape the living ivory as the Symphytes do, to extrude a table or a chair or a new tower from the mat. But I do know that when I wake now, my first thought is not *Where am I?* but *How is the deep chord today?* And I know that when Kel took my hand yesterday and led me to a new pool of liquid memory—a tiny basin where the city-in-miniature had sprouted a small, unfamiliar shape that might have been me—I wept. Not from sadness. From a feeling that has no name in any human tongue.

The pollen-bloom is coming. The sky is already tinting violet at the edges, and the Symphytes are preparing their highest membranes. Thren told me she will lie beside me. She will show me how to let the old dust settle, how to dream more coherently. She said, *You are new growth. You are the newest listening. The Khora is pleased.*

I am becoming. The mountain breathes, and I breathe with it. The dream extends.
