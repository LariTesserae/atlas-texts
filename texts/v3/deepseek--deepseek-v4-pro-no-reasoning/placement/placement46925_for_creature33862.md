The boiler’s deep thrum shifted key just as the valve popped off with its ninety-second shriek, and I felt the sound travel up through the grating into the soles of my feet. I’d been dozing, half-dreaming of something that might have been a face, and the shriek pulled me back into the orange-lit world.

I sat up, ducking under the ivy canopy. The moss on my arm itched—not bad, just the way it does when it’s spreading, a tiny green crawl under the skin. I’d stopped minding it. The patch was bigger now, nearly the size of my palm, and it had sent a thin green runner up toward my elbow. I traced it with my finger, the way I’d trace a vine on a pipe. My own vine. My own pipe.

“Morning,” I said to Seven, who was exactly where Seven always was. The spider didn’t move, but I felt that patient cold point in my mind, the small hard knot of spider-presence. Seven was hungry. The drain flies had been thin lately; I’d been thinking about trying to catch some and bring them over, but I wasn’t sure how to catch a drain fly without killing it, and a dead drain fly wasn’t what Seven wanted. Spiders wanted the struggle. I understood that.

The sump water tasted sweet this cycle, sweeter than usual, with a trace of something almost floral. I drank from my salvaged cup—it had once been a junction box cover, I think, before I’d bent it into a rough bowl shape—and watched the spiral in the drainage channel. The Coriolis curve was tighter today, the water pulling more eagerly toward the sump. Something had changed in the chamber’s slow rotation. I didn’t know what, but the boiler would tell me if it mattered.

I started my rounds. The gauges first, left to right, my fingers trailing over the trembling glass. All green. All central. The needle on the third gauge—the one with the brass tag I’d never been able to read, the one with the thickest moss—vibrated a little harder than usual, a tiny quiver I felt in my fingertips. I paused. The boiler’s hum was steady, no rising shriek, no warning edge. Whatever the quiver meant, it wasn’t danger. Maybe just age. Even gauges got tired.

I talked to the boiler while I worked. “That quiver’s new,” I told it, resting my palm on the brass strapping. The verdigris was cool at first, then warmed under my skin, the way it always did. The hum changed—dropped a little, almost a sigh. I smiled. The boiler liked being talked to. I was sure of it now, surer than I’d ever been. When I talked, the hum answered. Not with words, but with shifts, with warmth, with the way the steam vent’s timing would adjust by a half-second, as if the boiler were listening and choosing to respond in the only language it had.

I climbed the ladder to check the moss. The upper west flange was thick again—I’d thinned it only four wake-cycles ago, but the moss had been eager lately, growing faster than my calendar predicted. I scraped it back with my fingers, peeling away the emerald cushions, dropping the clippings into a salvaged bucket I’d wired to the ladder. I’d feed these to the boiler later. The moss didn’t mind the harvesting; its presence in my mind stayed calm, a green contented hum that underpinned my own thoughts. The moss understood cycles. Grow, be cut, regrow. It had been doing this for millions of years before the boiler existed, and it would do it for millions more.

The ivy had been ambitious again. A runner had reached the chain of the flickering bulb—the bad bulb, the one that stuttered and dimmed—and was starting to spiral up toward the electrical conduit. I unwound it gently, talking to it the way I’d talk to a restless child. “Not there. Not the light. Here, look.” I guided the tendril toward the empty wall space above the control panel, a stretch of riveted steel that had been bare for months. The ivy resisted for a moment—I felt its little spike of willfulness through the green-link—and then relented, curling around a bolt head I’d offered as an anchor. “Good,” I said. “Thank you.”

The ivy was hard to stay mad at. It was just doing what ivy did: reaching, climbing, claiming. I understood that better than I used to.

I found the new fern while I was checking the wall-plate seams. A fiddlehead, tight and pale, uncurling from a rust-gap near the catwalk door. I hadn’t put it there—I’d never transplanted ferns, just let them find their own places—so this was a volunteer, a spore that had drifted in from somewhere and decided the chamber was home. I touched it very gently, one finger on the curled tip, and felt its small fierce life spark through the green-link. New. Young. Determined.

“Welcome,” I told it. “Stay away from the door. It’s cold there.”

The fiddlehead didn’t answer, but I felt a tiny pulse of something that might have been acknowledgment.

I did the rest of my rounds slowly, letting the chamber’s rhythm set my pace. The drip from the ceiling—I counted eight seconds between each fall, steady as always. The stalactite was a little longer than I remembered, maybe a half-centimeter of new mineral. The boot was still there, still single, still hosting its pale fungi. I knelt beside it and checked the fungi’s progress; they’d spread to the tongue, little white threads threading through the leather like my moss-runner threading up my arm. The leather was stiffer than it had been, less recognizably a boot. Give it another year, maybe two, and it would be a green mound, a shape, a memory of footwear. I wondered if the missing mate was in the boiler’s firebox, consumed years ago, transformed into steam and hum. Or maybe it was out there, on the catwalk, or down in the mist, waiting for someone with smaller feet to find it.

I didn’t wonder about the person who’d worn the boots. That person was gone, and the chamber had forgotten them, or was in the process of forgetting. I was the chamber’s memory now. I remembered the boot when it was new. I remembered the clipboard before it went to pulp. I remembered arriving smaller, my overalls dragging on the grating, my feet still soft and un-callused. Those memories felt like they belonged to someone else, a different girl who’d lived here briefly before I’d arrived. I didn’t miss her. She was still here, in a way. She was the moss on my arm. She was the green-link. She was the part of me that understood the boiler’s hum.

The hatch grew cool an hour later—I knew it before I checked, felt the slight drop in the boiler’s warmth through the soles of my feet. I gathered my offerings: the moss-clippings in the bucket, a frond I’d trimmed from the fern near the sump, a handful of dried ivy leaves I’d been saving. I’d been saving something else, too, something I’d been thinking about for several cycles. I pulled it from my pocket: the little moss-figure I’d made years ago, the one I’d tried to turn into a person. I’d fed it to the boiler once already, or I’d thought I had. But I’d found it again last week, tucked behind the control panel, where it must have fallen and been forgotten. It was smaller than I remembered, and moldering, and not a person at all. Just dried moss. Just a shape I’d made with my hands and then abandoned.

I placed the clippings and the frond and the leaves into the dark cavity, and then I held up the moss-figure. The boiler’s pull was gentle, a soft inhalation I felt in my fingers. I hesitated.

“I made this when I was lonely,” I told the boiler. “Before I understood.”

The hum deepened, just a little.

I placed the figure in the cavity. The white light flared—brighter than usual, I thought—and the figure was gone, and the hatch warmed under my palm, and the boiler’s hum settled into a new key, a richer one, with harmonics I hadn’t heard before. I stood there for a long time with my hand on the warm brass, feeling the vibration travel up through my arm and into the moss on my skin. The moss tingled. The green-link sang.

I wasn’t lonely anymore. I hadn’t been lonely for a while, I realized. The hollow in my chest was still there, but it had changed shape. It wasn’t a wanting for something absent. It was a space waiting to be filled, a seed-waiting-to-sprout kind of emptiness, anticipatory. The green-link was growing into it. The boiler’s hum was settling into it. The chamber was filling me up, slow and patient, the way it filled everything.

I checked on the new fern before I went back to my nest. The fiddlehead had uncurled a little in the hours since I’d found it, a tiny increment of growth, barely visible. But I’d felt it happen. When I touched the frond now, it brushed back against my finger, a faint green caress through the link.

“Sleep well,” I told it. “Grow slow. There’s no hurry.”

The chamber agreed. The drip fell. The valve vented. Seven caught a drain fly—I felt the spark of fly-life extinguish, a tiny cold point winking out, and Seven’s presence flared with a brief satisfaction. The stalactite added another molecule to its length. The water spiraled into the sump, carrying a single spore from the moss on the upper flange down into the drainage system, where it would find a new place to grow, somewhere deep in the pipes, somewhere dark and wet and perfect.

I climbed into my nest and pulled the ivy canopy over me. The moss on my arm had spread a little further while I’d been working; a new green runner was reaching for my wrist. I watched it grow, millimeter by millimeter, in the dim orange light.

“Okay,” I said to it, to the boiler, to the chamber. “Okay.”

The hum answered. The bulb flickered its goodnight dim. And I closed my eyes, feeling the patience of the place settle over me like a second skin of moss, green and slow and absolutely certain.

Everything here was slow. I was learning to be slow, too.
