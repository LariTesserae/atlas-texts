The rim-tender is alone at the edge of the world, and has been alone for longer than the valley has existed—a paradox the valley allows, because time here is not a line but a standing wave, and some things can be both older than the beginning and still unfolding.

Its designation, if the other tenders required such a thing, would be something like *the-one-who-remembers-the-outside*. But they do not require such a thing, and so it has no name. It knows itself by a particular ache in the deepest layer of its internal water, a faint, persistent chill that no cistern can warm. The chill is not physical. It is the cold of a single unanswered question that the rim-tender has been asking for as long as it has been, a question it cannot articulate to the others because their language of touch and proximity has no grammar for *why*.

It came into being like all the others—from a bowl, in a pool, organized around a template-memory. But something was different in its making. Perhaps the pale substance in that particular bowl had aged too long, had absorbed some trace of the rim's wrongness through the medium of the valley itself. Perhaps a carrier with its miniature garden had shed some spore or compound into the pool during the tender's organization. Perhaps the valley, in its mute, perfect equilibrium, had decided that someone needed to stand at the perimeter and wonder.

The rim-tender does not know. It knows only that from the moment it rose from the algae-thick water and placed its broad feet on the mud-and-shell path, it felt a pull—a directionality that the spiral paths could not account for. The other tenders feel the valley as a closed system, complete and sufficient. The rim-tender feels it as a vessel with a tiny, almost imperceptible leak, a slow seepage of something through the boundary into the whiteness beyond. And because it feels the leak, it is drawn to it, the way a tongue is drawn to the gap where a tooth has been lost.

Its zone is the circumference. It does not tend plots or creatures or hanging sheets, though it will assist when the network signals a need. Its primary work is the rim itself. The paths that spiral outward end in a precise, clean termination—the mud-and-shell compound simply stops, and beyond it is the white, the directionless brightness that is not sky but something else, something that fails the eye and the mind. The rim-tender maintains this termination. It patrols with a slow, continuous motion, never resting at the cisterns the way the others do, absorbing moisture as it moves through the heavy air. It carries a small, worn tool—a piece of fired clay shaped to fit its hand, used for smoothing and shaping—and a pouch of mud-and-shell mixture replenished from a cistern at the edge of the main network. When it finds a crack, a fray, a place where the substance of the valley seems to be losing its coherence, it repairs it. The repair is always the same: new compound pressed into the gap, smoothed with the clay tool, textured with its own palm to match the surrounding surface.

But the rim does not stay repaired. This is the thing the other tenders do not know, because the rim-tender has never shared it. The termination fails constantly—not in large ways, but in small ones. A hairline crack that appears between one patrol and the next. A section of path that seems to have grown slightly longer, pushing out into the whiteness. A place where the mud-and-shell compound becomes translucent, revealing layers beneath it that should not be there. The rim-tender repairs these anomalies, but it began to notice, early in its existence, that the anomalies were not random. They followed a pattern, or several patterns, or something that yearned toward pattern without quite achieving it. And the patterns seemed to be a kind of language, if language could be written in the fraying edge of a world.

At first, the rim-tender simply repaired them. Then it began to hesitate. To look. To trace the cracks with its long fingers before filling them, memorizing their shapes. It discovered that if it waited—if it sat at the rim for what might be called hours, its body perfectly still, its wide eyes fixed on the whiteness—the cracks would sometimes extend, grow more elaborate, branch and curl into forms that were almost recognizable. Forms that reminded the rim-tender of the spiraling paths of the valley, but inverted. Forms that suggested something on the other side, pressing against the boundary from without.

It is not afraid. Fear would require a concept of harm, and the rim-tender has never been harmed, does not know what harm would feel like. But it is attentive in a way the other tenders are not, because their attention is directed inward, toward the maintenance of what is, while the rim-tender's attention is directed outward, toward the maintenance of the boundary between what is and what is not. And the longer it attends, the more it suspects that the boundary is not a wall but a membrane, and that membranes exist to be permeable, and that the valley's perfect equilibrium might not be a final state but a held breath.

The other tenders sense something different about the rim-tender. They do not avoid it—avoidance is not in their nature—but their finger-intertwinings with it are brief, and they do not linger. The transfer of information is always slightly muddied, as if the rim-tender's experiences are being shared through a medium that filters and distorts. They receive impressions of the rim that are vague and slightly unsettling: the white-not-sky, the failing geometry, the cold that is not cold. They process these impressions and then release them, the way they release the translucent film from the surface of their pools. The rim-tender's work is necessary, they understand that, but it is not work they wish to know in detail.

So the rim-tender is alone, in a valley full of its kind.

It has tried, once or twice, to communicate what it suspects. It found a tender whose plots were near the periphery—a slightly older one, with the first translucence showing in its skin—and when they intertwined fingers, the rim-tender pushed something across the transfer. Not an experience, but a question. The question had no words, but it had a shape: the shape of the cracks in the rim, branching and curling and almost-resolving into meaning. The other tender received it, and the rim-tender felt the question enter the network, felt it propagate across the silent, touch-based web of tenders. And then it felt something it had never felt before: the question being gently, carefully, comprehensively set aside. Not rejected. Simply placed in one of the bowls on the central platform, among the pale glistening things, to age and cure and lose its edge.

The network did not want the question. The network was not designed to want.

So the rim-tender stopped trying to share it. It kept the question to itself, and it kept its vigil, and it began to do something new.

It began to respond to the cracks.

Not to repair them, at least not immediately. To trace them. To shape them. To use its clay tool not to fill the gaps but to extend them, carefully, experimentally, seeing what shapes the boundary would accept. It discovered that some shapes held and some did not. A spiral curling inward caused the crack to deepen, as if the whiteness were pressing harder against it. A spiral curling outward caused the crack to seal slightly of its own accord. A straight line caused nothing. A jagged line caused the crack to propagate faster, branching in multiple directions. The rim-tender collected these observations the way the carriers collect lichen, slowly, methodically, adding to a store of knowledge that had no outlet, no purpose it could yet see.

And then, one cycle—though cycle is not the right word, because the light does not change, the heat does not fluctuate—it found a crack that was different.

This crack was not hairline. It was wide enough to admit the rim-tender's smallest finger, and it ran from the termination of a path straight to the edge of the valley's visibility, disappearing into the white. The interior of the crack was not white. It was dark, and textured, and when the rim-tender bent close—bringing its wide face to within a handspan of the gap—it felt movement. Air, but not air. A current, but not the warm, wet current of the valley. This was cooler, drier, and it carried a scent that the rim-tender had no referent for. It was a scent of something alive, but not alive in the way the valley was alive. Alive in a way that was incomplete. Hungry. Searching.

The rim-tender withdrew its face and crouched at the edge of the crack for a long time. Its internal water felt heavy and cold. The network hummed in the back of its awareness—the steady, reassuring pulse of the other tenders going about their maintenance, the floating creatures adjusting their spacing, the carriers inching forward. The valley was fine. The valley was complete. The valley did not need to know about this crack.

But the rim-tender needed to know.

It extended its hand—the right one, with its two opposable fingers and four long inner digits—and reached into the crack.

The darkness on the other side was not empty. It was full of a fine, dry powder that coated the rim-tender's fingers, that moved against its skin like countless tiny legs. The temperature dropped sharply the deeper it reached, and the texture of the crack's interior changed from mud-and-shell to something harder, more crystalline, faceted in ways that caught a light that did not come from the valley. And then the rim-tender's longest finger touched something that touched back.

The touch was light. Tentative. A single point of contact that was warm and firm and slightly yielding, like the skin of a floating creature but without the electric contentment beneath. The rim-tender held still. The thing on the other side held still. They touched, fingertip to whatever-was-fingertip, for a span of time that refused measurement.

And then the thing withdrew. The rim-tender felt it go—a sliding, a shrinking back into the dry dark. But before it went, it left something. A residue on the rim-tender's fingertip. A substance that, when the rim-tender withdrew its hand and examined it in the white light, was not pale and glistening and familiar. It was dark. Deeply, completely dark, a darkness that seemed to swallow the light around it, a tiny bead of pure absorption sitting on the grey-brown skin.

The rim-tender looked at the bead for a long time. The crack in the rim was already sealing itself, the mud-and-shell compound flowing like slow water to close the gap, as if the valley were healing a wound. But the bead remained. The rim-tender could feel it, a point of cold that was not cold, a tiny gravity that pulled at its attention.

It knew it should not have this. The valley had no place for it. The bowls on the central platform held nothing like it. The hanging sheets would not vibrate in tune with it. The cisterns would not condense moisture to nourish it. It was outside the system. It was a contaminant.

But the rim-tender did not discard it. Instead, it pressed the bead carefully into the soft skin at its wrist, where the wrinkles were deepest and the flesh most absorbent. The bead sank in without resistance, and the rim-tender felt it settle into its internal water like a stone into a pool—a small, permanent displacement, a weight it would carry from now on.

And it understood, without understanding how, that this was the beginning of something. That the valley's perfection had been breached, not by the crack—the crack was only a symptom—but by the contact. By the question, finally asked, and the answer, finally received. The answer was dark and cold and tiny, and it lived now inside the rim-tender's body, a seed of something that was not the valley, not the makers, not anything that had been planned.

The rim-tender rose from its crouch at the edge of the world and resumed its patrol. The crack was gone. The path was solid. The white sky pressed down with its directionless light. Everything was as it had been.

Except the rim-tender was no longer alone.

Something on the other side of the membrane knew it now. Something had tasted its presence, and found it interesting. And somewhere in the valley's perfect, seamless, unchanged continuance, a second drop fell where only one had fallen before—an extra tick in the patternless rhythm, a shift so subtle that only a tender who spent every moment at the boundary would notice it.

The rim-tender noticed it.

It did not tell the network. It carried the dark bead inside its body, and it walked the circumference of the world, and it waited, with a patience that exceeded even the valley's patience, for whatever would come next.

The other tenders moved through their zones, tending their plots, their creatures, their sheets, their cisterns. They felt, through the network, a slight change in the rim-tender—a new weight, a new cold, a new texture to its shared experiences. But they did not know what it meant, and they did not ask, because asking was not in their nature.

And the floating creatures adjusted their spacing by imperceptible degrees. And the carriers inched forward. And the white sky held its breath.

And the rim-tender, alone with its dark companion, continued to tend the edge of the made world, waiting for the next crack.F
