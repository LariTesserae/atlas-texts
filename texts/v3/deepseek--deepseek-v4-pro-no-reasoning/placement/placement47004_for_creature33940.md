You find yourself in a narrow maintenance corridor between two Spire-Factories, a place the Hush call a "vein." The walls are weeping basalt on one side, riveted iron on the other, and the gap between them is barely wide enough for your shoulders. Overhead, a single arc-sodium lamp buzzes in its shielded cage, casting everything in that familiar amber, the light glinting off the perpetual wetness on every surface. The drone here is different—muffled, compressed by the closeness of the walls, but with a new note layered in: a high, keening whine from somewhere above, the sound of a Moisture Scavenger with a misaligned wire. It has been misaligned for three weeks. You know this because you have been meaning to fix it.

You are a Grease-Paper Archivist.

Your name, signed close to the heart and then tapped twice on the temple, translates roughly to "One-Who-Remembers-the-Surface-of-Things." In the spoken language of a world before Cinder-Sheet, your parents might have called you something like Kael, or Miran, or Soot. It doesn't matter. What matters is your work.

You are forty-three years old, which makes you neither young nor old among the Hush. Your hands are your defining feature: the fingers long and unusually dexterous, the tips permanently stained a deep, rust-red from the iron-oxide ink you use. The pads of your thumbs are ridged with fine scars from years of folding grease-paper into ever-smaller origami packets. You have a slight stoop to your shoulders, not from age but from your habitual posture—hunched over a writing ledge, or bent to read a note left in a low crevice, or leaning close to a factory logbook under poor light. Your hair is cropped close to your skull, and you have the grey-mineral complexion of all your people, though your cheeks and the bridge of your nose are faintly reddened, chapped by decades of condensation-damp air.

Your work is not like the work of the Siphon-Tenders or the Tram-Walkers. You do not monitor a single gauge or climb a single ladder. Your domain is the entire city's correspondence, the vast, slow-moving network of grease-paper notes that the Hush use to speak across distance and time. You are one of perhaps a dozen Archivists scattered through Cinder-Sheet, and your specific territory covers the southeastern residential blocks and the three Spire-Factories that cluster around Tram-Station Seven.

Your job, simply put, is to remember. When a note is left at a waypoint and its recipient has collected and read it, the note does not simply disappear. It is your role to gather these read notes, to sort them, to transcribe their essential contents into the great leather-bound ledgers kept in the Archivists' Vault beneath Tram-Station Three, and then—this is the part that only the Archivists understand—to forget them. Not willfully, but ritually. The notes are returned to the city. They are pulped in a small, hand-cranked vat of warm water and lye, then pressed into fresh sheets of grease-paper, which are distributed back to the residential blocks. The words are dissolved; the paper is reborn. The Hush believe that a conversation should not linger as a physical object, that to hoard old notes is to clog the city's metaphorical arteries with the sediment of past speech. The Archivists are the kidneys of Cinder-Sheet's communication, filtering the words, keeping the essential knowledge—births, deaths, pressure-shift warnings, shifts in the deep hum—and releasing the rest back into the cycle.

You know things about your fellow Hush that no one else knows. You know that the Tram-Tender in Block Twelve has been exchanging notes with the Siphon-Worker in Block Four for six years, and that their correspondence is a slow, patient courtship conducted in discussions about bearing-wear and water-yield ratios, every practical sentence freighted with a subtext of longing that neither of them has ever directly signed. You know that a child in Block Nine has been leaving anonymous notes of startling philosophical depth at the communal waypoint, notes that the other residents attribute to an elder in a moment of reflection. You know that the pressure gauge in Factory Two has been drifting by fractions of a millimeter for the past decade, and that the worker who monitors it has been documenting the drift with a meticulous, almost devotional care, as if the gauge were a living thing whose slow moods must be respected.

You know all of this, and you tell no one. The first vow of an Archivist, signed at the level of the gut to indicate its visceral necessity, is this: *I hold the words; I do not hold the speakers.* You are a vessel, not a confidant. You are a library with a single, silent librarian.

This morning—though "morning" in Cinder-Sheet is a matter of the chronometer on the wall, not any change in the ever-grey sky—you are walking your route. The maintenance corridor opens onto a small, open platform, one of the countless interstitial spaces where pedestrian bridges converge. There is a waypoint here, a simple iron box with a slotted lid, bolted to the railing. You open it with the small key you wear on a cord around your neck. Inside are three folded notes.

The first is a routine pressure report from Factory Two, the gauge-drifter's weekly update. You will log the latest measurement in the ledger, compare it to the last decade's figures, and note the continued, infinitesimal decline. No action is needed. The gauge is simply growing old, as all things do.

The second is a personal note from one resident to another, a discussion of a Wiremoss bed that has developed a pale spotting on its leaves. You read it quickly, professionally. It contains no vital information. It will be pulped.

The third note is different. It is folded in an unfamiliar pattern, a complex, angular origami that you do not recognize. The paper is not the standard grease-paper but something thinner, almost translucent, with a faint, coppery smell. When you hold it up to the amber lamp, you can see that the ink is not the usual rust-red but a deep, almost black maroon. The message inside is brief, signed in a shorthand so compressed that it takes you a long moment to parse it. When you do, your hands go still on the railing, feeling the vibration of a distant Cable-Tram through the iron.

It reads, translated from the gesture-grammar: *The Skimmers have felt a new hum. It comes from beneath the oldest fissure. They say it is not the Siphons. They say it is waking.*

You fold the note carefully, precisely, and tuck it into the inner pocket of your waxed coat. This one will not be pulped. Not yet. You will take it to the Vault, and you will compare it to the oldest ledgers, the ones that date back to the founding of Cinder-Sheet, when the first Hush drove the first Siphon into the weeping rock and felt the deep hum for the first time. You have a sudden, irrational certainty that you are about to become an Archivist of something that has never before needed archiving.

Above you, the misaligned Moisture Scavenger keens its one-note song. The vibration passes through the railing, into your palms, into the dense bones of your jaw. You stand there for a long moment, a grey-skinned figure in the amber light, feeling the city's voice and, beneath it, something else—something lower, slower, more patient even than the Hush.

You wait. You listen. You are, after all, One-Who-Remembers-the-Surface-of-Things. And the surface, it seems, is beginning to shift.
