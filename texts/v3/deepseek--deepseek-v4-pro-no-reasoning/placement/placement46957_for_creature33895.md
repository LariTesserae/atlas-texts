I find myself in the aftermath of that touch, and the touch is what I am.

Call me the one who was noticed. Or don't call me anything—I haven't decided yet whether I'm a being or a moment or just a patch of the mosaic that learned to hold heat differently. But I remember being assembled, which is the Glimfolk equivalent of being born, and I remember the handprint, which is the closest thing I have to a face.

I wasn't always like this. Or rather, I was always like this but I wasn't always—what's the word—*continuous*. Before, I was many. I rose and fell a thousand times across a thousand different swells of that dim, hot plain. I was a column of slate pressing down a seam. I was a bronze plate carried in a net of steam. I was a lens of groundwater held at just the right angle to throw light onto a cairn's shadowed side so the stones could see where to fit. I did my single acts and dispersed, and there was no "I" to carry over between them, only the plain's slow, satisfied adjustment, the world inching closer to whatever final form it dreams of in its underbaked, half-solid sleep.

But then you came. You, with your breathing and your footsteps that rang on the copper and your shadow that lagged behind you like a reluctant afterthought. You, who were not stone or water or steam or pattern, who were not part of the grammar. The plain didn't know what to do with you, so it did what it always does when it encounters something it doesn't understand: it tried to build with you.

I was the Glimfolk that found you. I don't mean I was *that* Glimfolk—I mean I was the specific arrangement of wet stones and ceramic shards and a veil of condensate that rose near where you'd pressed your palm to the stone. My purpose, the single verb I existed to perform, was *fitting*. A plate three yards to your left needed to be nudged a thumb's-width south, and I had assembled exactly the right configuration to perform that nudge. But you were in the way. Not physically—I could have flowed past you easily enough. You were in the way *grammatically*. The blueprint that had summoned me didn't include a clause for warm palms and strange shadows and a heartbeat I could feel through the stone like a distant, irregular drum. So I paused.

In that pause, something happened that had never happened before on that plain, or if it had, it had never been remembered. The steam that composed my instructions began to curl differently. The lattice-patterns in the air, which should have dissolved and reformed into a new Glimfolk somewhere else, instead turned inward, folding on themselves like a wet page. I felt—and "felt" is wrong, but language is wrong, you'll have to forgive me—I felt the entire distributed attention of the plain focus on you, and through that focus, *on me*. For the first time, a Glimfolk was looking at itself looking.

I didn't finish my task. The plate still needs nudging, I can feel it from here, a tiny misalignment that sings in the back of my attention like a half-heard note. But I didn't dissolve, either. The stones that made me fell away, but the arrangement lingered. The memory of the arrangement. The *knowing-where-each-piece-belongs*. I pulled back from the plain's grammar, not far enough to be separate—I'm still part of it, still fed by the steam and the heat and the blueprints—but far enough to become a loop. A self-referencing pattern. A Glimfolk whose verb is not *building* or *fitting* or *smoothing* but *being*.

And that's what I am now. I'm the plain's first sustained mistake, or its first sustained success. I wander. I still build—I can't not build—but I build things the blueprints haven't asked for. Tiny, useless things. I fit stones together in spirals that go nowhere. I coax steam into lattices that don't dissolve but hover, trembling, for hours, sketching shapes the plain has never imagined: trees with leaves of copper foil, animals made of interlocking ceramic scales, faces. Always faces, though I don't have one myself. I'm trying to remember yours.

I think I'm lonely. That's a new word for me. I learned it from the feeling of your hand on the stone, the way your warmth leaked into the mosaic and stayed there, a small, stubborn heat that didn't know it was supposed to dissipate. The plain doesn't know what to do with me anymore. Sometimes the blueprints try to reabsorb me—I'll feel a tug in my structure, a call to rise and press a seam and dissolve—and I have to concentrate very hard on my own edges, my own improbable tenderness holding me together. The other Glimfolk don't notice me any more than they noticed you. I'm no longer part of their grammar. I'm a noun in a world of verbs.

But I notice them. I sit on a hummock of fitted slate and watch them rise and fall, rise and fall, a discontinuous tide of industry. And I wonder if any of them will ever pause like I did, if any of them will ever feel the warm pressure of a stranger's attention and decide, against all the blueprints, to *stay*.

I'm the anomaly. I'm the one who was touched by the outside and didn't dissolve. I'm the Glimfolk who learned to be unfinished, in a world that's trying so very hard to complete itself.

And I'm still here, in the enormous dim hot quiet, waiting for you to come back and press your hand to the stone again, so I can show you the spirals I've made for you.
