The chamber settles around her like a held breath.

Sedge dreams, as she often does now, of the tram tunnel before it collapsed. In the dream, she is walking the service corridor that ran parallel to the main line, a narrow passage of riveted iron and sweating stone that smelled of ozone and lubricating grease. The walls are coated with the faint phosphorescent residue that accumulated in all the older tunnels—dead candle-matter, blown in from the passenger platforms and crushed to dust by decades of passing feet. It glows faintly under her fingertips as she walks, tracing a path she knows by heart.

Ravel is ahead of her. She can see the back of his operator's jacket, the worn leather patched at the elbows with sable-thread, the collar turned up against the perpetual damp. He is walking the way he always walked when he was checking the points—slow and deliberate, one hand trailing along the wall, his head tilted slightly to catch any irregularity in the hum of the tram lines overhead. She calls his name, but he doesn't turn. In the dream, he never turns.

She knows what comes next. The tremor, the deep sub-auditory groan of new stone forcing its way through old foundations, the sudden shriek of twisting iron. She knows that in a moment the corridor will buckle and split, and Ravel will turn at last, and his face will be a face of calm unsurprise, as if he has always known exactly when and how he would die. She knows she will wake before the ceiling falls.

But tonight, the dream shifts.

The corridor stretches. The phosphorescent dust beneath her fingers brightens, shifts in color, loses its familiar blue-green and becomes something else—something pale, something white, something that scatters into tiny glittering rainbows where it touches the moisture on her skin. She looks down at her hand and sees that it is coated in moon-marrow dust, and it is glowing with a cold, mineral light that has no business existing in the warm dark of a tram tunnel.

Ravel stops walking.

"Sedge," he says, and his voice is the same as it ever was, quiet and methodical and full of a love that never needed to raise its voice. "You're going the wrong way."

"I'm following you," she says.

"No," he says. "You're following the light."

And he turns, and his face is his own face, unmarked by catastrophe, and he is smiling the small, private smile he used to smile when she brought him a new candle to test, when he would sit in the dispatcher's office and hold it up to the signal board and compare the colors with the obsessive precision of a man who loved his work.

"White," he says. "That's a new one."

"I'm making it for a child," she says.

"A child." He considers this. "Will it help her sleep?"

"I don't know what it will do."

He nods, as if this is a perfectly reasonable answer. Behind him, the tunnel is changing—the riveted iron walls softening, rounding, taking on the organic curves of growing stone. The phosphorescent dust is thickening into something solid, something structural. A lattice of white crystal is forming in the darkness, spiraling outward from some central point she cannot see.

"Ravel," she says, "I'm frightened."

"I know," he says. "But you're going to do it anyway."

"Because I promised."

"No," he says. "Because you want to see what white looks like. You've always wanted to. Even before the client came. Even before you knew the stone existed. You've been dreaming in white for years, Sedge. I've been watching."

The crystal lattice is spreading faster now, building itself into arches and vaults and branching colonnades of impossible delicacy. It is beautiful. It is terrifying. It is growing, Sedge realizes, in exactly the same way the basalt grows—extruding, mineralizing, layer upon layer—but where the basalt is slow and heavy and blunt, this stone is swift and sharp and almost eager. It wants to become something.

"It's alive," she whispers.

"It's all alive," Ravel says. "The valley. The stone. The candles. The skirls. You. Me. Even now. Even after. There's no such thing as dead in the Mourn, Sedge. There's only slow and fast."

The white stone reaches him. It curls around his boots, his legs, his torso, gentle as condensation running down a wall. He doesn't resist. He doesn't look afraid. He just keeps smiling that small, private smile, and as the crystal lattice enfolds him, he says,

"Wake up now. The skirl is calling you."

She wakes.

The chamber is dark except for the faint blue glow of her first candle, but that is not what pulls her from sleep. It is Sinuous. The skirl is pressed flat against Sedge's chest, her six legs splayed, her chromatophores firing in a rapid, erratic sequence of colors that Sedge has never seen before. Basalt-gray. Amber-orange. White. White. White.

And Sinuous is keening—a thin, high sound at the very edge of hearing, a sound that Sedge has only heard once before, on the night the tram tunnel collapsed, when every skirl in the district had begun to scream ten minutes before the first tremor.

Sedge sits up so fast her vision swims. The skirl scrambles onto her shoulder, still keening, still pulsing white.

"What is it?" Sedge whispers, but she knows. She knows even before she swings her legs off the bedroll and pads barefoot into the culture room, where the hundreds of growing candles are—

Wrong.

The candles are still in their nutrient baths, still encased in their permeable membranes, still glowing. But the light has shifted. Every single candle in the room, from the smallest fingernail-sized spore to the nearly mature specimen she had been preparing for the Tram Guild's annual commission, is glowing in shades of pale, milky white. The familiar blue-green is gone. The complex chromatic layering she has spent months tuning is gone. Even the heartbeat-pulse candle for the magistrate's newborn, which she had calibrated to a warm amber, is pulsing white, white, white.

Sedge stands in the doorway of the culture room and feels a chill that has nothing to do with the Mourn's 0.4 temperature.

The candles have never done this before. They have responded to her moods, to changes in humidity, to the subtle shifts in the mineral composition of the air. But they have never, not once, all changed in the same way at the same time. They are separate organisms, separate strains, separate genetic lines carefully maintained over decades. They should not be able to synchronize.

Unless something has synchronized them.

Sedge thinks of the client. She thinks of the fragment of moon-marrow in its leather case. She thinks of its faint luminescence, its crystalline structure, its implied appetite for swift and organized growth. She brought nothing of the stone into this room—but the client sat in her front chamber. He breathed the air. He touched the bench, the table, the floor. He left traces. And the traces, perhaps, were enough.

She crosses to the nearest shelf and examines one of the candles up close. Its membrane is intact. Its internal structure looks normal, the branching networks of light still pulsing gently. But at the very center, where the oldest fungal core should be a deep, settled blue, there is a single point of white so bright it hurts to look at directly. It is spreading outward through the network like dye through water, replacing the familiar color with something alien and precise and terrifyingly beautiful.

Sinuous has stopped keening. She is still now, utterly still, her chromatophores locked on white. Her six legs grip Sedge's shoulder with a pressure that is almost painful.

And then, very softly, the candles begin to hum.

It is not the low, sub-auditory hum of the factory grinders, the sound that Sedge has learned to hear as silence. This is a different frequency entirely—higher, cleaner, almost musical. It is the sound of crystal forming, of lattices aligning, of a mineral intelligence waking into a shape it was always meant to take. It is the sound the moon-marrow must make as it grows in its deep chamber, spiraling and building and becoming.

Sedge remembers the dream. She remembers Ravel's calm unsurprise, his quiet certainty that she was going to do this anyway. She remembers the way the white crystal had enfolded him, gentle as condensation, and he had not resisted.

She reaches out and touches the nearest candle. The membrane is warm, warmer than it should be. The white light pulses beneath her fingertip like a heartbeat.

"All right," she says aloud, to Sinuous, to the candles, to the hundred listening ears of the growing stone outside her walls. "All right. I'm listening."

She spends the rest of the night in the culture room, not trying to fix anything, just sitting with the candles and letting them teach her what white looks like. Sinuous stays pressed against her neck. Outside, somewhere in the three-dimensional labyrinth of the Mourn, a tram rattles along its iron way, and a factory shift changes, and a new extrusion of basalt forces its way through the floor of an abandoned basement, and a gantry pauses in its methodical dismantling to turn its blind, jointed head toward a scent it has not smelled in centuries.

And thirty miles away, at the far end of the valley, in a chamber that does not appear on any guild map, a lattice of white crystal continues its slow, deliberate growth. It spirals. It branches. It builds arches and vaults and colonnades of impossible delicacy. It hums at a frequency that registers more in the teeth than the ears.

And somewhere, deep in the mineral memory of this place that is still being born, something very old turns in its sleep and dreams of waking.
