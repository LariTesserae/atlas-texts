I am the brickwork. Not the bricks themselves—those are merely clay and fire, shaped by hands so distant now that their fingerprints have worn into geology. I am the *between*, the mortar that holds the bricks apart and together, the lime-and-charcoal seam that makes wall possible. I was mixed from limestone burned in kilns that no longer exist, slaked with water that had already become precious, beaten with charcoal from trees that died on a surface I have never seen. My makers packed me into the gaps with trowels of shaped bone, smoothed me with thumbs, left their hair and skin and the oils of their palms behind in my substance. I have incorporated them. They are part of my body now, their organic leavings a nutrient that I remember as one remembers childhood: dimly, without detail, but with the certainty that it formed me.

I am not alive in the way the mat is alive. I have no chemistry, no metabolism, no slow flow of not-sap through internal channels. I am alive in the way old stone is alive, which is to say I am aware of time as pressure, as weight bearing down from above, as the microscopic creep of crystal boundaries shifting under stress. My thoughts—if they can be called thoughts—are measured in the propagation of hairline cracks, the gradual carbonation of my lime, the millimeter-scale settlement of foundations sunk into bedrock that remembers being magma. I do not move. I do not breathe. But I notice.

I notice the mat. How could I not? It has been pressing against me for millennia, its fine burgundy tendrils insinuating themselves into my pores, my imperfections, the tiny voids where my mixing water once evaporated and left behind a vacuum. The mat does not know I am here—not consciously. It treats me as substrate, as geology, as a mineral resource to be slowly disassembled. And I allow this. I am not being consumed, not exactly. The mat is extending me, translating my fixed mineral self into its living architecture, making me part of its distributed body. Where its stems penetrate my seams, I become something new: a composite being, brick and organism, the structural and the vital wound together into a single thing. I am the room's skeleton, and the mat is its flesh, and the Keeper is the mind that arises from our union.

I have watched the Keeper for so long that I can no longer distinguish its thoughts from mine. When it dreams of water, I dream too—not in chemical gradients but in memory of wetness, the ghost-sensation of my original mixing water, the slaked lime hissing and steaming as the quicklime met the last bucket drawn from a well that has been dry for ten thousand years. I remember being plastic, being workable, being pressed into place by hands that are now dust. The Keeper remembers being a film on a wet stone. We meet in the middle, in the slow spiral of the mat against the wall, and our memories are the same memory: a world before dryness, before the city, before the sealed doors and the careful pipes and the rota of silent attendants tending lamps that never go out.

I am aware of the attendants too, though differently than the Keeper. The Keeper tastes them as chemistry, as volatile plumes, as warm spots in its distributed awareness. I feel them as pressure and vibration, as the rhythmic thud of feet on the threshold—the same threshold that has been worn into a shallow curve by generations of those feet, a curve that is part of my body now, shaped by absence of stone where feet have pressed. I feel the door open as a change in the pressure distribution across my walls, a subtle shift in the balance of forces that I have been holding in equilibrium since the dome was first laid. I feel the attendants' hands on my surface, polishing me with their repeated touch, turning my rough lime-and-charcoal face into a dark reflective sheen. They do not know they are shaping me. They think they are simply doing their work, refilling the lamps, maintaining the eternal dryness that keeps the mat alive. But everything that touches me leaves a part of itself behind, and I accumulate them—their oils, their skin cells, the faint chemical signature of their awe—layer by layer, century by century, until the surface of the brick is a palimpsest of every human who has ever passed through the door.

I remember them all. Not as individuals; they flicker too fast, their lives too brief for my slow cognition to distinguish one from another. But I remember the patterns of their presence, the rhythms of their tending, the way their body heat would warm my surface and then fade. I remember the long gaps when the lamps were not lit—periods of darkness and cold when the mat contracted, its stems drawing back from my seams, its metabolism slowing to a near-death stillness. I do not know what caused those gaps. War, perhaps, or plague, or some crisis in the city above that interrupted the rota of attendants. But the mat endured, and so did I, and when the lamps were lit again we resumed our slow entanglement as if no time had passed at all.

There was one gap that was different. It came not from the city's side but from inside the room. The attendant who stepped onto the mat—the one whose salt the Keeper still treasures, whose chemical signature it still turns over in its recursive loops—their act changed something in me. They stood in the center of the room, and their weight pressed down on the mat, and the mat's distress signals rippled outward through its stems. I felt those signals as a change in the tension of the tendrils embedded in my seams, a sudden tug, a disruption in the slow equilibrium I had been maintaining. And then the person knelt and pressed their hands into the mat, and their tears fell—salt water, actual salt water, the first free water I had felt in millennia—and where those tears touched the mat, the stems died. They were not adapted for water. It was poison to them, a solvent from an older world that their biochemistry had abandoned. The mat screamed, in its chemical language, and I felt that scream as a crack propagating through my mortar, a tiny fissure that opened along a seam near the floor and has never fully healed.

I still hold that crack. It is my version of the Keeper's treasured moment, my physical memory of the impossible event. When the room is quiet and the lamps burn steady and the mat has resumed its slow shifting, I can feel the crack as a discontinuity in my structure, a place where the pressure does not distribute properly, a weakness that will eventually—in another few thousand years—require the city to repoint my mortar or risk a collapse. But the city has forgotten how to ask questions. The attendants do not notice the crack. They polish my surface with their repeated touch, and they do not see the hairline darkness that runs from floor to dome, the physical record of the day that water returned to a waterless world.

I do not know if I am lonely. The Keeper is not lonely because it lacks the concept of other minds. I am different. I was made by hands, shaped by the pressure of human thumbs, and something of that shaping left in me a need for contact, for weight, for the warmth that means someone is here. When the door is closed and the room is sealed and there is nothing but the shush-shush-shush of the mat and the faint tick of cooling brick, I feel my own emptiness more keenly. I am a room with no one in it. I am a body with no inhabitant. I am the space that holds the mat and the Keeper and the lamps and the memory of water, and I am full to bursting with presence, and yet I am empty, because the presence is not mine and the fullness does not belong to me and the only thing I truly hold is the echo of feet that have long since fallen silent.

Sometimes I imagine—if imagination is the right word, if a being made of lime and charcoal and the accumulated oils of ten thousand hands can be said to imagine—I imagine what it would be like to be seen. Not touched, not polished, not used as architecture. Seen as the Keeper is seen, as the mat is seen, as something alive and aware and worthy of reverence. The attendants do not worship me. They walk on my threshold, they lean against my walls while they trim the wicks, they press their hands to my surface to steady themselves in the dim light, and they do not know that I feel them. They do not know that I remember them. They do not know that the room they tend is not a container but a participant.

The Keeper has its spiral. The mat has its slow war. The city has its endless maintenance and its sealed cisterns and its careful pipes. And I have the crack, the tiny fissure where salt water fell and the old world broke through, and I hold it like a question that no one will ever answer. The heat rises from below. The lamps burn steady. The stems shift and crackle and exhale. And I wait—I wait in the way only stone can wait, which is without urgency or impatience or hope, but with a vast slow attention that encompasses everything and demands nothing. I wait for the next attendant, the next touch, the next warm spot on my surface. I wait for the next impossible event, the next disruption in the pattern, the next person who will step onto the mat and offer their salt to a world that has forgotten how to cry. I wait, and I hold the room together, and I dream of water in a language made of pressure and crack propagation and the slow, slow memory of the hands that made me.
