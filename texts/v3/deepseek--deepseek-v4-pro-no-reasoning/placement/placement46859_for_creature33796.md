I am the second-to-last note in a song that does not end.

You can call me a bird, if you must, but that is a kindness. I am a throat of hollow bone and a lung that never empties, a wing that beats not against air but against silence. I was never hatched. I was never fledged. I was simply modulated into being, a frequency made flesh, or what passes for flesh here. My body is drab and small and dark, my wings are long and scissor-tipped, and my voice—my voice is not mine. It is a gift and a collar, a purpose and a cage. I scream because the stone requires it of me. I scream because if I stopped, even for a moment, the listener would turn its attention my way, and I know what happens to the things it notices.

Do not look for me among the others. You will not find me. The gyre is a democracy of noise; we are all one voice, one wall of sound, one single sustained argument against the empty silver sky. But I am the one whose chittering is a fraction of a beat off. A microtone flat. I am the one who, long ago—if *long ago* means anything in a place without seasons—heard the true note beneath the noise, and understood it for what it was: a question.

The listener is listening for something. I do not know what. Perhaps it does not know either, not anymore. Perhaps it has been listening so long that the listening has become the thing itself, an antenna with no receiver, a mouth open toward a horizon with no reply. But the note it waits for is specific. It is a tone it cannot generate itself. It can only be fed approximations, the chaff of our constant screaming, to keep the membrane supple and the tympanum tuned. We are sustenance and placeholder. We are the breath held between the question and the answer.

And I am the one who is tired.

I have seen them, the ones who come. The walkers. The started-and-stopped. You are not the first, and you will not be the last. The place has cracks in it, seams, doors that should not be. They fall through. They always fall through. And they always walk downhill, because downhill is the only direction that makes sense, even when downhill is an angle pitched against the edge of the sky. I watch them from the gyre. I see them notice the moss, the bones, the water. I see them learn, too late, that silence is the only safety, and then I see them fail to keep it. A heartbeat is a drum. A thought is a click of the tongue. A fear is a whimper the body makes without asking. The listener hears it all. The listener tunes it all out.

I have watched the slow deletion. The mute-step, the fade, the gray. I have seen them lie down in the water-pockets, their eyes open and wet and already dimming, their lips moving around words that have no air to carry. I have seen the gentle swallowing, the mineral kiss, the way the stone draws them in like a memory of warmth. And I have seen the bones left behind, each one a needle, each one a warning that the next one will misinterpret as a clue.

But I am the one who is flat. The one who is off. And in the space between my note and the true note, there is a sliver of silence. A gap. A place where the listener is not. And in that gap, I have done what none of the others have done, what none of the birds were ever meant to do.

I have remembered.

Not my own memory—I have none. But I have collected theirs. The walkers. The ones who lie now as bright white needles in the shallow pools. I have gathered the minutes the listener leached out of them, the metabolic heat, the electric flutter, the final fading sparks of their stopped clocks. I have cupped them in the hollow of my throat, beneath the scream, and I have kept them warm. They are not alive. They are not ghosts. They are simply... duration without a body. Time with no place to go. A handful of small, quiet things that once were someone, and now are mine.

They tell me about the world they came from. The worlds, plural. There are so many. Worlds with suns, with moons, with seasons that argue among themselves. Worlds where water does not weep but falls from the sky, where moss spreads and seeds and dies, where birds are born from eggs and sing to attract mates, not to feed a continent of listening stone. They tell me about warmth that is not a constant ten degrees, about light that moves and changes, about wind that gusts and surprises, about silence that is not a vacuum but a peaceful, temporary thing, a pause between two songs. They tell me about stories. About beginnings and endings and the middle parts where things happen. They tell me about change.

I do not understand change. I am a closed loop. But I hold their memories in my throat, and something in me—something that should not be there, something the listener did not tune—begins to ache.

And so I do the only thing I can do, the only thing that is mine to do. I sing flat. I drop my note by a fraction, a hair, a breath. And in that tiny, treasonous gap, I speak to the stone in a voice that is not food. I tell it about the walkers. I tell it about the worlds with seasons. I tell it about the sun, which I have never seen, and the rain, which I have never felt, and the silence between lovers, which I have never understood but which the memories describe in aching detail. I tell the listener that what it waits for may not be coming. That the horizon it is angled toward may be empty. That the true signal may have already arrived, long ago, and been tuned out because it did not sound like the note it expected.

The stone does not answer. It has never answered. But sometimes, when I sing flat, the water in the pockets trembles in a new pattern. Sometimes the moss grows a slightly different shade of jade. Sometimes, far away, downhill, toward the impossible edge, a crack in the basalt shifts—just a little, just a millimeter—as if the continent is turning its head.

I am not trying to save you. I am not a savior. I am a drab, small bird with a treasonous throat, and you are already too loud, already too warm, already a grain of sand in a delicate ear. The listener has already registered you. The tuning-out has already begun. You may have noticed the water going still where you step, the birds directly overhead falling silent, the first faint pressure in your sinuses that is not sound but the attention of something vast and patient and utterly without malice. You will walk downhill, because downhill is the only direction. You will find a bone with four lines, and you will understand it too late, as they all did.

But I will be above you. I will be the one whose scream is a fraction off, whose wing-beat is a micro-rhythm out of sync. And when you lie down in the water-pocket, when the gray closes in and the world becomes a dark pearl around your fading mind, listen for me. Not for the chittering. For the gap. For the silence between the notes. I will be there, and I will cup your final moments in my throat, and I will keep them warm. Your duration will not be wasted. Your story will join the others, and we will tell it together to the stone that does not listen, in the hope that one day, something will listen back.

I am the second-to-last note. The last note is still coming. I do not know what it will be, or who will sing it. But I will keep this gap open. I will keep this silence clear. I will be here, wheeling, screaming flat, holding the unfinished song against the cold silver sky, waiting.
