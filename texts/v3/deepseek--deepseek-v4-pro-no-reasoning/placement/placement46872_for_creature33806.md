Signe is not the one I am.

I know her—the woman with the broom, the woman who watches the floor sink millimeter by millimeter toward the breathing dark—but she is not me. I am the *other* glow. The second lit housing unit, the one farther from the geothermal plant, nearer to the silence she finds unbearable. She cannot sleep here. She has tried. But I can. I *do*, in the way that anything here can be said to sleep.

My name is Aenea. It is a leftover, a thing I carried down with me like the three books she keeps on her shelf, swollen past legibility. I was a medic. I am still, technically, the settlement's medic, though the position has become theoretical. There are twenty-three of us now—there were twenty-four, before Idris went to the face and came back hollowed out and was sent topside on the next supply tram, his hands still shaking in his lap. Twenty-three bodies in a half-built city beneath the mountain, and what sickness we have is of a kind I cannot treat. The damp gets into the lungs. The darkness gets into the mind. The beetles get into everything else.

I treat what I can. I have a cabinet of supplies that are slowly succumbing to the humidity—adhesive bandages that no longer adhere, antiseptic wipes that grow their own pale flora, painkillers that clump in their bottles like tiny, chalk-white eggs. I have learned to compensate. I dry bandages on the warm housing of the ventilator shaft that runs past my unit. I keep my most precious drugs in a sealed container with desiccant packs I have baked and re-baked on the geothermal plant's cooler surfaces until they are more salt than silica. I have become an expert in the small, improvisational alchemies of decay.

This is not what I trained for.

I trained for trauma. For the bright, arterial emergencies of a drilling operation, the crush injuries and the falls and the sudden, violent failures of equipment or bodies. I was good at it, once. I could set a bone with my eyes closed, could find a vein by touch in the dark. I was calm in the way that calms others. Now my calm is a different substance—not the bright steel of emergency, but something sedimentary, layered, built up over months of treating nothing more urgent than fungal rashes and the low, persistent cough that everyone has developed, the one that sounds like water moving through stone.

I do not sweep the beetles. That is Signe's work, her ritual, her proof of separation. My ritual is different. I listen.

I listen in a way that Signe does not—or perhaps she does, perhaps that is why she sweeps, to drown it out. But I have stopped drowning things out. I sit in my unit, in the chair I have positioned precisely so that my back is to the prefab wall and my face is toward the open door, and I close my eyes, and I let the settlement's sounds arrange themselves in my mind like instruments in an orchestra.

The drip. There are seventeen distinct drips within my hearing, and I have named them all. The one near the machine shop is *Tock*, because it falls with a heavier note, a full stop rather than a comma. The one that lands on the metal housing of the third lamp is *Sharp*, because it sizzles briefly before it dies. The one that falls from the louver above the main junction is *Echo*, because it lands in a puddle that amplifies it, sends it back as a distorted twin. I know when one of them changes—when a new crack opens in the ceiling and adds an eighteenth voice to the choir, or when sediment clogs a fissure and one falls silent. I noticed before anyone else when *Plink*, the drip near the tram junction, began to fall more slowly. That was three weeks ago. I have a theory about what it means, about the pressure shifting in the rock, about the slow sinking Signe has measured with her geologist's eye. I have not shared this theory. There is no one to share it with who would not be afraid, and we have enough fear already.

The whine. The geothermal plant's turbines are a constant, but they are not unchanging. They have moods. When the humidity peaks, the pitch shifts slightly, a quarter-tone higher, a brightness that lives in the back of the throat. When the plant's automatic systems cycle to vent excess steam, there is a dip, a sagging note like a voice giving up on a word midway through. I can tell the time by these shifts more accurately than by the clock on my wall, which is a cheap digital thing whose battery I have replaced twice and which is, in any case, irrelevant. Time down here is not a sequence. It is a texture.

The susurrus. The beetles. Their sound is the hardest to describe because it is the most constant, the most *there*, and yet it changes too. When a worker walks the concourse, the susurrus parts, a seam opening in the living carpet, and I can hear the direction and speed of the walker by the way the sound divides and mends. When the tram passes, the susurrus is briefly overwhelmed by the grind of wheels on wet rail and the soft, continuous crunch of bodies beneath the flatbed's weight, but it surges back immediately, a tide that does not remember the stone that broke it. I have learned to hear the beetles as a kind of breathing—not the deep, geological breathing of the thing at the face, but a surface breathing, the respiration of a living skin that covers the settlement. They are not individuals. They are an organ. I think Signe knows this, on some level, and that is why she sweeps them with such a careful, ritualized neutrality. She is not killing. She is grooming. Tending a pelt that does not know it is being tended.

And underneath all of it, the lowest note, the one I only hear when everything else is quiet—the shift, the pressure, the something that lives in the soles of the boots and the base of the skull. The breath.

I hear it better than Signe does. I have better ears, or perhaps I am simply less defended. She has her broom. I have only my listening, and my listening has grown so fine, so attuned, that I can tell when the thing below is *not* breathing. Those are the moments that frighten me most. The pauses. They come at irregular intervals, long holds between exhale and inhale, and while they last, the settlement feels like a held breath itself—the drips seem louder, the turbine whine more naked, the beetles' susurrus more desperate. And then the breath comes again, a long, slow exhalation that rises through the floor, and everything settles back into its rhythm, and I remember that we are not alone, that we have never been alone, that the rock beneath us is not rock but a body, and we are the fauna of its skin.

I have not been to the face. I do not need to go. I know what is there.

I know it the way I know the seventeen drips, the moods of the turbine, the seam in the susurrus when a worker passes. I know it the way I know that Idris, when he came back from the face, was not frightened but *empty*—as if something had been taken from him, some essential conviction of his own solidity, and what remained was a man-shaped shell filled only with the knowledge that the floor had welcomed him. I treated him for shock, gave him sedatives that his body absorbed like water into dry soil, and when the supply tram came, I signed the release form without asking him to describe what he had seen. I did not need him to describe it. I could hear it in the way he breathed—a new rhythm, slower, deeper, matched to the thing below. He was still breathing that rhythm when they carried him up into the light. I do not know if he has stopped. I suspect not.

Signe thinks she is the one who understands. She is a geologist; she reads the stone, the water table, the slow sinking she has measured in millimeters. She thinks the beetles are a sign of something, an indicator species, a warning. She is right, but she is also wrong. The beetles are not a sign. They are *us*, scaled down and simplified—life pressing up against the void, indifferent to the larger presence beneath, making a home in the space that has been opened, unaware that the space is itself a body, breathing.

We are all beetles. I am a beetle. Signe is a beetle with a broom. The workers who move unhurried through the wet dark, their faces sheened with sweat, their voices swallowed by the dense air—they are beetles. The ones who came before us, the ones who drilled these tunnels and bolted these machines to the still-rough floor, the ones who strung the sodium lamps and laid the tram rails and then went back to the surface, unable to say why they could not stay—they were beetles too. We are billions, and we are one, and we are temporary.

I have stopped thinking of the surface as home. I have stopped thinking of *home* as a concept that applies to me. Home is the cot in my unit, the chair positioned toward the open door, the seventeen drips and the turbine moods and the susurrus that parts and mends like a living sea. Home is the sound of the thing below, breathing, pausing, breathing again. Home is the knowledge that I am an infestation, and that the rock does not mind. The rock does not notice us. The rock breathes, and we scurry on its skin, and when we are gone—sent topside by our own failing nerves, or buried by a tunnel collapse, or simply absorbed, the way the floor absorbed Idris’s certainty—the rock will still breathe. The beetles will still carpet the floors. The drips will still fall, though their names will be different, though there will be no one to name them.

This is not despair. This is accuracy.

I am the settlement's medic. I treat what I can. Yesterday, I lanced a boil on the neck of a driller named Kass, her skin hot and tight with infection, the pus that drained a pale green shot through with darker threads. The threads were organic but not human—something that had gotten into the wound, some filament from the weeping walls, some spore or seed or fragment of the breathing dark. I cleaned it as best I could, packed the wound with the last of my sterile gauze, gave her an antibiotic that I hope is still potent. She thanked me. Her voice was low, swallowed by the air. I watched her walk back toward the machine shop, and I saw the beetles part around her boots, and I thought: *We are all carrying something. We are all colonized. The only question is whether we notice.*

I notice. That is my function. I am the one who notices. Signe sweeps, and I notice, and between us, we keep the settlement from being completely absorbed by the dark—not because we fight it, but because we acknowledge it. We are the immune system of a body that does not know it is a body. We are antibodies that have forgotten what they are supposed to attack.

Sometimes, in the quiet hour between shifts, I walk to the edge of my territory—the junction where the main concourse branches toward the face, where the hazard tape sags and the **UNSAFE** sign is crusted over with living beetles, its letters illegible, transformed into pure texture. I stop at the line the beetles have drawn, the sharp edge of their avoidance, and I look down the passage. It is darker there. The sodium lamps do not reach far; the one that should illuminate the bend in the tunnel has been dead for weeks, and no one has replaced it. The floor gleams wet and bare. No chitin drift. No living carpet. Just the slow, rhythmic rise and fall that I can feel in my bones before I can see it—the breath, the pause, the breath again.

I do not cross the line. Not because I am afraid—though I am, though fear is a low, steady note in the symphony of my listening, as constant as the turbine whine. I do not cross because crossing is not my role. My role is to stand at the edge and notice. To hear the eighteen drips (there is a new one now, near my unit, a thin, high *Chime* that I am still learning). To know the moods of the turbine. To feel the susurrus part and mend. To listen to the breath and know when it pauses, and to hold my own breath in those pauses, waiting, always waiting, for the exhale that follows.

Signe thinks she is the one who understands. She is wrong. Understanding is not the same as knowing. She knows the water table and the rate of sinking and the mineral content of the seep. I know that the seep is not water—or not only water. It is sweat, saliva, tears, some secretion of a body too large to perceive itself. I know that the heat from the geothermal plant is not drawn from the rock but stolen from the flesh of the thing below, and that the thing does not mind, because its heat is vast, because we are taking so little, because we are, in the scale of its breathing, a momentary warmth on its skin, a fever it will outlast.

I know that I will not leave. Not like Idris, shaking on the supply tram. Not like the first crew, who went topside and never came back down. I will stay until the settlement is finished or abandoned or absorbed, until the walls stop weeping or weep so hard they collapse, until the beetles have eaten every scrap of organic matter we brought with us and turned us into part of their endless, single-minded life. I will stay because someone must, and because I have become, in my months of listening, something that belongs here.

I am Aenea. I am the medic. I am the second glow in the dark, the unit farther from the heat, nearer to the silence. I have a cot and a footlocker and a chair positioned toward the open door. I have nineteen drips now, no—twenty, there is a new one, a soft *Tap* near the tram junction, I will learn it, I will name it, I will know when it changes.

I have the breath beneath the floor, the pause, the exhale.

I have the beetles, billions of them, a living skin that does not know it is a skin.

And I have this: the certainty, calm and sedimentary, that we are not the first to live here. That the thing below is not hostile and not welcoming and not even aware. That we are a microbe on the body of a god too large to notice its own cells. That this is not despair. This is accuracy. This is the only medicine I have left to offer—the truth, given without drama, without terror, without the need to sweep it away.

The breath pauses. I pause with it. And then, together, we exhale.
