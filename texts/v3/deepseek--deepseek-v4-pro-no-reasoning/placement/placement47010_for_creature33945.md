I am drawn upward, into the floating world.

No—not upward. I *am* the upward. I am the chittering call and the furred membrane, the hooked claws and the dark-adapted eyes. I am the one they will name *Chiropteromys crypta*, though I have never heard a name spoken aloud. The heartbeat is my lullaby and my calendar, and I know its moods the way surface creatures know weather.

The shift just came. That fractional stutter—I felt it in my sternum before my ears caught it, a skipped pulse in the world's deep rhythm. Three seconds of silence. Three seconds of the prickle along my spine, the chest-tightness, the ancestral whisper that says *wrong, wrong, the heartbeat is wrong*. But then the sound returned, lower and slower, and the pipe began to weep in earnest.

I am already launching myself from the wall before my fear fully fades, because I have learned what the stutter means. I am fourteen months old; I have lived through this four times. The stutter is the herald. The stutter means *bloom*.

My patagia catch the rising heat-shimmer from the pipe, and I am swimming upward through the mist, my wing-fingers spread wide. Below me, the moss cushions are beginning to pulse brighter, that slow Mexican wave of bioluminescence that always accompanies the bloom. To my dark-adapted eyes, the chamber transforms—from a dim chapel of gray-green shadow into a cathedral of liquid turquoise light. Every quartz vein in the weeping schist ignites. Every snail shell becomes a lantern. The iridescent sheen on the spreading pool of condensate fractures into rainbows.

I ride the thermal in a spiral, climbing toward the chimney's mouth, and I watch the world below rearrange itself into abundance. The whisperers are already erupting from their moss-cushion villages—springtails by the hundreds, by the thousands, launched into the air in white arcs of ecstatic panic. They don't know why the bloom has come. They don't know about the switch thrown somewhere in the mountain's heart, the cooling system cycling down, the temporary surge of warmth and moisture. They only know that the water is richer now, the biofilm thicker, the world more generous. And so they leap, and dance, and scatter, and I am among them.

I dive.

The first whisperer I take is fat with eggs, her abdomen distended and soft. She pops between my teeth like a overripe berry. The second is smaller, quicker—I nearly miss him, have to twist in midair, feel the healing tear in my left wing pull but not give way. I snatch him with my claws this time, bring him to my mouth, crunch through the exoskeleton. The third is my favorite kind: a juvenile, the chitin still translucent, the interior tissue almost sweet. There is no sweetness in this chamber, not really—the moss is mineral-tasting, the biofilm is neutral, the millipedes are earthy and bitter. But the young springtails taste faintly of something I have no word for. Something like the smell that sometimes drifts down from the chimney, that cold clean scent that is not mist or rock or machine.

I do not wonder what it might be. I do not wonder about the surface, about ancestors, about the voles who once scurried through sunlight and ate seeds and died in the talons of owls. I am not capable of wondering in that way. What I have instead is a shape of absence, a hollow in my knowing where *outside* might fit. When I have ventured up the chimney during lean times, desperate and driven, I have reached the place where the air thins and the moisture drops and the light—the impossible, searing, *wrong* light—begins to filter through the crack. And I have felt the hollow then, the shape of a world I cannot inhabit. I have turned back every time.

But the bloom is here, now, and there is no need to turn anywhere. I eat until my stomach is distended, until the loose skin of my flanks bulges with the beginning of fat reserves. I eat until the whisperers' panic subsides and they settle back into the moss, their numbers barely dented. There are always more. The bloom-time abundance is a temporary madness, a gift from the heartbeat, and I have learned not to question gifts.

When I can eat no more, I find a perch on a bare patch of schist near the pipe. The seep has slowed slightly; the iridescent pool has stopped spreading. The machine's new rhythm has settled into a steady pattern—slower than before, deeper, each *whump* of compression a bass note I can feel in my bones. I groom my whiskers. I inspect the tear in my wing-membrane. It is holding. I would like to sleep.

But something is wrong.

The prickle returns—not the sharp spike of the heartbeat's skip, but a slow, creeping wrongness that I cannot locate. It is not the sound. The sound is steady. It is not the light; the moss is still pulsing its bloom-time brightness. It is not the mist, which hangs as thick as ever. It is something else, something I have never sensed before.

I raise my head. My ears swivel. My whiskers tremble, reading the air.

There.

A disturbance in the mist near the chamber's floor. A displacement. Something large—larger than a millipede, larger than me, larger than anything that has ever been in this chamber—is *standing* in the center of the emptiness between the moss cushions. I can't see it clearly. The mist is too thick, the angle too steep. But I can see its shape as a void in the water droplets, a place where the condensation does not cling. It is tall. It is warm; I can detect the heat-plume rising from it, a column of warmer air that distorts the mist like the pipe's thermal, but bigger, much bigger. It is breathing—a slow, rhythmic movement of air that tastes wrong, tastes of something I have no framework for.

Carbon dioxide.

I don't know the word. I don't know the chemistry. But my body knows that the exhalations of this intruder are changing the air, enriching it in some way, and the moss knows it too—I can see the bioluminescent wave pulse faster, racing from cushion to cushion, as if the intruder's breath is a bloom-time all its own.

The snails have stopped. Not all at once, not in synchronization with the heartbeat. They have stopped one by one, as if confused, their eyestalks retracted. The millipedes are frozen mid-traverse. The whisperers beneath the moss have gone absolutely silent. The whole chamber is holding its breath, and I am clinging to the wall above, and I do not know what to do.

This is not a predator. A predator would move like me—quick, targeted, driven by hunger. This intruder is standing still. Observing. Its stillness is the most unsettling thing about it. Nothing in my world is still for this long. Even the snails pause only for seconds at a time. Even the millipedes, when they freeze, are frozen only for a heartbeat's skip. This intruder has been standing motionless for—how long? I have no measure. Longer than the silence after the stutter. Longer than it takes the moss-wave to circuit the chamber twice.

And then it moves.

A limb. Long, jointless, pale—I can see it now, through the mist, reaching toward the pipe. Toward *my* pipe, the source of warmth and water and the heartbeat's physical embodiment. A strange, flat, five-fingered extremity (like my wing-hand, but unbidden, wrong, naked of fur) extends and touches the corroded iron.

The intruder jerks back. I hear a sound—a high, sharp exhalation, not unlike the chitter I make when I clip a rock edge, but louder, more complex. Pain, maybe. The pipe is hot; I know this. I do not touch it with my bare membranes. I land on the rock beside it, where the warmth is ambient but not burning. The intruder has learned something.

Good. Let it learn. Let it leave.

But it does not leave. It stands there, cradling its injured extremity, and it continues to watch. It watches the moss pulsing. It watches the snails, who have cautiously resumed their grazing now that the intruder's stillness has returned. It watches the millipedes resume their traverses. And then—slowly, terribly—it lowers itself.

It folds its long limbs. It crouches. It presses something (its face? its sensory organs?) low, low to the ground, close to a moss cushion, close enough that I could dive and strike if I were brave, if I were foolish, if I were something other than what I am. I am not brave. I am a creature of the heartbeat, and the heartbeat has not told me what to do about this.

The intruder stays there for a long time. Long enough for the bloom to begin to fade. Long enough for the machine's rhythm to cycle back toward its higher pitch, the pipe-sweat slowing, the abundance waning. Long enough for me to grow accustomed to its presence, the way I am accustomed to the machine's drone—a new constant, a new pressure in the air.

Eventually, it rises. It stands. It turns toward the wall where the fracture runs, the seam that leads—I know, though I have never traveled it—to the surface, to the boulder field, to the world I cannot inhabit.

But before it goes, it does something strange.

It reaches into itself—into a fold of the strange loose material that drapes its body—and withdraws something. A small object. Pale. It places the object on the edge of the pipe's basin, where the iridescent water still pools. A piece of something.

Food? No. It doesn't smell like food. It smells like nothing—dry, sterile, alien.

The intruder straightens. Its heat-plume shifts as it moves toward the fracture. And then, between one breath and the next, it is gone, ascending through the seam toward the unthinkable light, and I am alone again with the heartbeat and the moss and the snails who have never known anything else.

I wait until the intruder's scent has fully faded. I wait until the millipedes have resumed their full paths, until the whisperers are rustling beneath the moss, until the machine's rhythm has settled back into the pitch I remember from before the bloom. And then, cautiously, I descend.

The object is still there.

It is small—smaller than my body, smaller than a snail's shell. It is white and smooth and shaped like nothing natural, no curve or angle I recognize. It has no smell. It has no heat. It is utterly inert, a piece of static in a world defined by cycles and flows.

I do not touch it. I circle it. I study it from every angle. It means nothing to me. It is not food, not water, not a threat, not a gift. It is simply *there*, a new piece of the chamber, like the pipe, like the moss, like the quartz veins in the weeping schist.

I will learn to ignore it, in time. By tomorrow, it will be coated in condensation, and the biofilm will begin to creep across its smooth surface, and the whisperers will investigate it with their long antennae and find nothing of interest. In a week, it will be just another rock. In a year, it will be embedded in the mineral deposits that are slowly softening the chamber's sharp edges.

But tonight, in the fading glow of the bloom, it is still strange. It is still *wrong*. And I am still afraid, though the fear is already fading into the background hum of the heartbeat, the prickle settling into the familiar rhythm of a world that is still deciding what it will become.

I return to my perch. I curl into a ball. I tuck my wings over my eyes. The machine throbs. The water drips. The snails graze.

And somewhere above me, beyond the chimney, beyond the crack, in a world of light and dryness and predators who have never heard this heartbeat, the intruder is climbing down the boulder field, cradling its burned hand, carrying an image in its mind that it will try to describe to someone else and find no words adequate.

*There's a whole world in there,* it will say. *A whole ecosystem. Living in the fracture. Living off the coolant leak. I've never seen anything like it.*

And the person it tells will nod and take notes and begin to plan a return expedition with proper equipment and specimen jars and taxonomic checklists, and they will give everything names—*Chiropteromys crypta* and *Ammonitella subterranea* and *Brioluminescenta metabolica*—and they will publish papers and draw diagrams and believe they understand this place.

But they will never hear the heartbeat the way I do. They will never feel the prickle of the stutter in their bones. They will never know, the way I know, that the emptiness between the moss cushions is not empty at all—that it is the space where the mist hangs and the whisperers leap and the water flows and the bacteria transform rock into soil into life into me.

They will never know that the object they left behind was not a gift.

It was a question.

And the chamber, in its slow, bacterial, mineral patience, is already beginning to answer.
