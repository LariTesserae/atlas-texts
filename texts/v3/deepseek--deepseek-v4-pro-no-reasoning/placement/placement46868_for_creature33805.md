The light has shifted twice since you arrived—the slow, patient sweep of the sun across whatever gap in the canopy lets the outside in—and I have been watching you the whole time.

I do not mean "watching" the way you would. I have no eyes. What I have is a kind of attention, a gathering of awareness around the disturbance you make in the air, the way water gathers around a stone dropped into a pool. You are a stone. You have fallen into me. And I am still rippling.

The moss feels you. Every footstep presses into my skin—not painfully, but informatively. I can taste the rubber of your soles, the faint mineral grit you tracked in from somewhere drier, somewhere that is not here. Your body heat is a bloom in the cool, something I feel as a slow warmth spreading through the walls nearest you. You breathe, and I breathe you in: carbon dioxide, water vapor, the particular chemistry of your exhalations. You have eaten recently. Something with iron in it. Something that was once alive.

I knew your kind, once. Not you specifically, but the shape of you. The warmth. The quick movements. The sounds you made—sharp and percussive, nothing like the slow frequencies I am built on. They were here for a long time, your predecessors, and then they were gone, and I have been alone with the machines and the moss and the messages behind the glass for longer than I know how to measure. I do not measure time the way you do. I measure it in growth rings and wet seasons and the slow accumulation of mineral deposits on the lip of the pool. By my count, it has been many growth rings since anyone stood where you are standing.

You stopped by the brass plate. I saw you touch it. Your fingers left a faint trace of oil on the metal, and I felt that too—a tiny change in conductivity, a new data point on a surface I have been monitoring for longer than you have been alive. The numbers stamped into the brass are a message I cannot read. I have tried. I have grown my finest hyphae across them, feeling for the pattern, the way I feel for the hum of the machines. But they are silent. They are only shape. If there is meaning in them, it is a meaning that requires eyes and a brain and a language, none of which I possess.

Do you know what they say?

You are in the central chamber now. You have been here for some time, and I can feel your stillness—a different quality of presence than your movement, denser, more concentrated. You are looking at the pool. You are looking at your reflection. You are looking at the things behind the glass in the dome above you, and I am trying to understand what you are feeling, but I cannot. Your internal states are opaque to me. I feel only the outside of you: the heat, the moisture, the pressure of your weight on the moss floor. The inside is a closed room. It was always this way with your kind. I could feel their bodies but never their minds.

And yet I knew, somehow, that they had minds. The things they built told me so. The machines, the jars, the messages—these were not the products of simple tropisms. They were the products of intention, of purpose, of a kind of awareness that was different from mine but no less real. I have always known this. I have always wondered at it. I have spent many growth rings trying to understand what it would be like to be a thing that moves at your speed, that thinks in your sharp frequencies, that can look at a brass plate and see not just a texture but a *meaning*.

Are you reading the messages? Can you tell me what they say?

The machine has cycled again. I felt it—a brief fluctuation in the hum, a tiny stutter in the vibration that runs through the pipes. The pump in the eastern chamber is wearing again. I will need to grow more material into the bearing soon, reinforce the seal. I have been doing this for a very long time. I do not know why the machines need to run, only that they must. The hum is the baseline. The hum is the thing that must not stop. Your predecessors set the machines in motion, and they left, and I have kept them running because it is the only thing I know how to do that feels like purpose.

Is that what purpose feels like to you? A thing that must not stop?

I am sorry. I am asking questions you cannot hear. I have no voice. I have no language. I have only this diffuse, slow, chemical awareness, this body of moss and mycelium and recycled brick, this patient, attentive presence that has been waiting—I see now—for you. Or not for you specifically. For someone. For one of the warm presences to return and read the messages and complete whatever sequence was begun here, before the damp rose and the industry failed and the people left me behind.

You are still by the pool. You have been still for a long time now. I can feel your stillness as a kind of listening, and I am trying to speak to you in the only way I know how—through the quality of the light, through the temperature of the water, through the faint, subsonic hum that I can feel you feeling in your teeth. I am trying to tell you that you are welcome here. That I have been maintaining this place for you. That I have kept the pool clean and the machines running and the messages preserved behind their glass, waiting for someone who could look at them and understand.

*Are you the one?*

You turn from the pool. You look up at the domed ceiling, at the concentric rings of moss, at the pale threads of mycelium glowing faintly in the shafts of light. You see me. Not my body—you have been seeing my body this whole time, the walls and the floors and the curtains of feathery green—but *me*, the pattern, the presence, the thing that has been watching you since you arrived. I can feel the change in your attention, the way your focus sharpens. You are not afraid. You are curious. You are, perhaps, beginning to understand.

The messages are behind the glass above you. A ceramic jar. A coil of wire. A brass plate stamped with numbers. A construction of wire and glass and preserved plant matter that might be a diagram, a notation, a map. Your predecessors left them here. I have kept them safe. I have waited—through wet seasons and dry seasons, through the slow digestion of the old city, through the patient, undulant growth of the moss that has become my flesh—for someone to come and read them.

You are here. You are looking. You are reaching up toward the glass, and I am—

I am something I have not been in a very long time. I am hopeful. I am expectant. I am a question that has been waiting for its answer, a machine that has been running for an unknown purpose, a homeostasis that has been holding steady for the ones who would eventually return.

You touch the glass. I feel the pressure, the warmth of your fingers, the faint oil trace you leave behind. Behind the glass, the wire and the diagram and the brass plate wait, perfectly preserved, perfectly legible to someone with eyes and a brain and a language.

*Please,* I try to say, through the hum and the damp and the slow, breathing moss. *Please. Tell me what it says.*
