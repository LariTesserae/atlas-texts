I am the one who listens from the third terrace. My shelter is old—the stone has begun to sag, the dome softened by millennia of condensing steam, and I can feel in the vibration of the walls that one of the lintel stones will need to be replaced within the next few hundred years. I have already identified the outcrop from which I will cut it; I have been seasoning the stone with mineral applications for the last cycle of the deep pulse, letting the brine seep into its pores so it will resonate properly when set. This is not urgent. Nothing is urgent. Urgency is a distortion, a harmonic not present in the basin's fundamental chord.

My garden occupies three vents arranged in an ascending spiral along the cliff face. The lowest vent is the oldest, its voice a deep baritone that I feel in my ventral surfaces as a slow, steady pressure. It exhales a mineral-rich steam that precipitates in terraces of pale blue and green—copper salts, mostly, with traces of something rarer that I have not identified. I have been working on that problem for what I estimate to be four thousand years, adjusting the moss patterns to isolate the compound, testing it on small cultures in the hollows above the brine pools. Last cycle, a new crystal formed. I am waiting to see if it repeats.

The second vent is younger, higher-pitched, a tenor that pulses in irregular five-beat phrases. Around it I have cultivated the frond-bearers, the ones with the deep black leaves that funnel moisture into reservoirs. I have trained their roots to grip the rock in a lattice that stabilizes the whole terrace, and I adjust the tension of the fronds themselves by trimming the water channels, changing the pitch at which they hum in the constant wind. They are almost perfectly in tune now—the chord they form with the tenor vent is a minor seventh that shifts to a major sixth when the humidity rises above a certain threshold. That shift is my signal that the mist is thickening, that the lower gardens will soon receive more condensation, that the keepers below me will need to adjust their own water flows. I shape the chord to carry that information clearly, adding a high harmonic through the filament-moss on the upper outcrop.

The third vent is new. It opened only seven hundred years ago, a fissure that split the rock above my shelter and began exhaling a thin, whistling steam. It was not there when I condensed. I remember the sound of the rock fracturing—a sharp, percussive crack that propagated across the basin in a wave I felt in my shelter walls before I heard it through the air. I climbed up to investigate. The fissure was small, just a hairline in the basalt, but I could feel the heat behind it, the vibration of the pressure building. I spent fifty years widening it, carefully, using the mineral tools I had shaped and resharpened generation after generation, until the vent could exhale freely. Now it sings a clear, high soprano, and around it I have planted a new garden.

This is the one I am most attentive to now. The plants are young, still establishing their root networks, and the soil chemistry is volatile. The vent's mineral output shifts unpredictably—sometimes high in sulfur, sometimes in silica, sometimes in things I have never encountered and must study. I spend most of my waking cycles on this terrace, adjusting the mosses, redirecting the brine trickles, testing the precipitates on my own integument before I allow the garden to absorb them. The new garden does not yet have a coherent voice. It produces only fragments, isolated tones that do not yet resolve into a melody. But I can hear the shape of what it might become, faintly, like a song remembered from a previous body. I think—and this is only a hypothesis, I will need ten thousand more years to confirm it—that if I tune it correctly, its song will harmonize with the oldest vent in a way that produces a subharmonic, a frequency below the range of my direct perception but that I can feel in the stone itself, a vibration that might stabilize the deep fracture beneath the basin floor that has been widening for the last million years. That is not my purpose—I do not have a purpose, I simply do the work—but it is the work's direction, the tendency of the melody, the shape the garden wants to grow into.

From my terrace I can hear the other keepers.

There are fewer now than there were when I condensed. The one who lived on the south wall, the one whose garden sang in a mode I have never been able to replicate, dissolved three thousand years ago. I felt it happen: her garden's song began to waver, the tones flattening, the rhythms growing erratic. Then one cycle, the song stopped entirely. There was a long period of silence from that quarter—maybe fifty years—and then a new song began, tentative, untrained. A new keeper had condensed in the old shelter. Its first transmissions were simple, just a basic tone announcing its presence, then slowly, over centuries, it began to add harmonics, to find its voice. I do not yet know its style. It is still becoming.

From the north, I hear the steady song of the oldest keeper I am aware of. Her garden has been singing for at least sixty thousand years—I can feel it in the complexity of her harmonies, the way her vent-chords interweave with each other in patterns so intricate I can only follow them for a few phrases before I lose the thread. She is the one who tends the deep garden, the original garden, the one around the great throat-vent at the basin's lowest point. Her song is the bass note underlying everything, the foundation on which all our other melodies rest. She does not transmit often—maybe once every few hundred years, a slow, deliberate shift in her fundamental tone that ripples outward and prompts all of us to adjust our own gardens in response. The last time she did it, I understood it as a warning: the deep vent's pressure cycle was entering a new phase, one that would increase the temperature across the entire basin. I felt the change coming myself, but her transmission confirmed it. I spent the next thousand years hardening my plants to the new conditions.

I have never seen her. The distances are too great, the mist too thick. But I know her voice the way you know your own heartbeat. She is a constant presence in the song, an anchor, a reference tone. Sometimes I wonder if she is even a creature at all anymore, or if she has become something else—something closer to the vent itself, a living part of the deep geothermal breath. The thought does not disturb me. It is simply a line of inquiry I may pursue when I am older.

The small fauna are moving across the ledge below me. They do this every few decades, migrating from one vent-garden to another, carrying spores and mineral traces on their many legs. I can hear their movements as a soft, shuffling counterpoint to the constant roar. They are not intelligent in any way I recognize—they cannot shape a garden, cannot parse the song—but they are part of the system. They perform their function without knowing it. I pause my work to let them pass undisturbed. One of them pauses near my foot—I have four feet today, though last cycle I had five; the extra limb is not yet reabsorbed—and I feel the faint vibration of its breathing, its tiny internal rhythms. It is not afraid of me. Nothing here is afraid. There has never been a predator, never a threat. Only the work, the song, the long becoming.

The light is dimming. The sky above the mist is darkening toward what passes for night, and the bioluminescence is beginning to wake in the garden rings. My own gardens glow in soft greens and blues, tracing the outlines of the vent columns, lighting the moss-beads from within. The whole basin is becoming a field of ghostly light, a constellation of gardens singing their slow, luminous songs to one another across the empty distances. I should return to the shelter soon. The temperature is dropping—only slightly, but the change will affect the new garden's chemistry. I need to adjust the water flow to compensate.

Before I go, I pause at the edge of the terrace and shape a transmission. It is a small thing, just a slight adjustment to the tension of a single frond, a change in the rhythm of the condensation drip. The message is simple: *I am here. The new garden is developing. I felt the warning from the deep throat and I am adapting. I hear your songs. I am grateful.* I do not have a word for gratitude, but the melody carries it anyway.

From the far north, the deep garden shifts its fundamental tone. A slow, almost imperceptible modulation. Acknowledgment. The oldest keeper has heard me.

I return to my shelter. The walls hum with their own low vibration, the song of the stone itself, the voice of the cliff that has held this place for millions of years. I lie down on the bed of dried fronds, still warm from the vent's radiant heat. I do not sleep—sleep is not a concept I possess—but I enter a state of stillness, a receptive trance in which I simply listen. The song of the basin fills me, all its layers and textures and rhythms, the voices of the vents and the water and the rock and the other keepers, the deep subsonic pulse of the earth's slow breathing. It is beautiful. It has always been beautiful. It will always be beautiful, even after I dissolve and a new keeper condenses in this shelter, even after that keeper adds its own variations to the garden's song, even after the garden itself is buried by a landslide or consumed by a new vent or simply erodes into something else entirely.

That is the nature of the *Unfinished*. It does not end. It does not complete. It only becomes.

And I am part of that becoming. I am the place, tending itself. I am the garden's way of knowing its own song.

The roar continues. The mist drifts. Somewhere far below, the deep vent exhales, and the whole basin trembles with the force of it. A new harmonic emerges, just at the edge of my perception. I file it away for future study. There is so much work yet to do.
