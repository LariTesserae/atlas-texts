The wind doesn’t howl here. It breathes—a long, slow exhalation that comes from everywhere and nowhere, stirring the beards of moss on the tree-giants, rippling the dark water in the troughs, lifting the fine white sky-snow into brief, ghostly eddies before letting it settle again. I taste it through every surface of my body: the mineral tang of dissolved stone, the faint sweetness of aeroplankton, the iron-cold bite of condensation that never quite freezes. This wind has been moving across the ridgelines for longer than the derricks have been pumping, and my own breathing has long since synchronized with its rhythm. Inhale on the gust, exhale on the lull. A cycle within a cycle.

The pebbles know this rhythm too. Each one I place is a breath, a punctuation. The spiral I am laying out today—this century? this millennium?—is a particular favorite of mine. It begins tight at the center, a nautilus curve of dark basalt and pale quartzite, and then expands outward in radial spokes that mimic the branching angle of the nearest tree-giant's canopy. I learned that angle by watching. By lying on my back in the scree for what might have been a hundred years, simply observing the way the tiered umbrellas divided the dim light, the mathematics of it slowly revealing itself until it became part of my own internal pattern-language. Now my hands reproduce it without thought. Muscle memory. Tectonic memory.

The pattern-languages are not my invention. I am a continuator. Somewhere in the deep past of this tableland—before the derricks grew their moss, before the sky-sifters learned to run their running lights, before the cloud-lid settled into its permanent crepuscular ceiling—there were others who laid the first stones. I have found their work, buried under the sky-snow, under the cryptogamic crust, under the slow accretion of epochs. Spirals and whorls and radial bursts, just like mine, but made with stones I do not recognize, in patterns that hum at frequencies just slightly askew from my own. I incorporate them when I find them. I do not move their stones; I build around them, letting the old pattern and the new pattern become a single, ongoing text. It is a form of reading, and a form of writing, and a form of conversation with makers who are now only a faint taste in the mineral memory of the ridge.

The shelf-terrace on the back of my head shifts color as I think this. A slow wash of amber, then a flicker of deep purple at the edges. The colors are not thoughts, exactly—they are more like the emotional register of thoughts, the felt weight of them. Amber for the warmth of continuity. Purple for the ache of distance. The sky-sifter who has been circling above me catches the shift and adjusts its own running lights in response: two quick pulses of pale green. Acknowledgment. Or perhaps it is simply commenting on the weather. The sky-sifters' language is a simple one, mostly concerned with air density and plankton blooms and the location of the pod, but there is a tenderness in it nonetheless. We have known each other a long time.

I set down the pale pebble with the dark veins and then set it aside. That one: it hums wrong. Not wrong in itself—it is a beautiful stone, smooth as a tooth, cold in my palm—but wrong for this particular place in the spiral. It belongs in a different pattern, one I have not yet begun. One I may not begin for another thousand years. I can wait. The stone will still be here. The stone may even still remember me when I return for it.

A ripple in the trough-water below catches my eye. The slick gleam of a pale back, the huffing cough of something surfacing to breathe. I know the creature—a pond-bellower, my name for it, though it has other names for itself that I cannot pronounce. The pond-bellower is hunting the same way I am hunting, sifting through the dark water for something that will sustain it, something that will let it continue. It pulses its bioluminescent lure: once, twice, a mimicry of the hanging flowers above. The light reflects in my eyes, and I blink slowly, once. Greeting.

I realize, with the slow dawning that characterizes all realizations here, that I am not alone on the ridge. There is a presence. A new stone in the pattern. It has been watching me for some time—a flicker of warmth that is not the crypto-biotic warmth of my own pelt, a shape that does not move with the wind or the water. My eyes have been tracking it peripherally for the duration of this whole laying-sequence, and only now does the knowledge surface into conscious attention.

I do not stop my work. The pebble in my hand—grey-green, flecked with mica—goes into its place in the radial spoke. Then the next. Then the next. But I let my head-plates speak for me: a slow, steady wash of blue-green, the color of curiosity without urgency. The color of patience. The color of *I see you, and I am not alarmed, and I will wait for you to reveal what kind of stone you are*.

The sky-snow continues to fall. The piston on the nearest derrick continues its weary, perfect ellipse. The pond-bellower surfaces again, huffs, descends. The cloud-lid presses down with its ancient, heavy patience. My breath—if it is breath—beads and drips along the moss of my jaw.

I wait. There is so much still to lay out, and the watcher will speak when it is ready, or it will not, and either way the spiral will continue. This is the fundamental truth of the ridgeline country. Everything is already speaking. The only question is whether you have learned to listen at the right speed.
