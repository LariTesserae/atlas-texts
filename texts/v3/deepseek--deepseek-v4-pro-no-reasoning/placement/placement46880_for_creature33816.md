# The Seam-Seer

I am the one who reads the water.

Not the water itself—the seep is no text, the minerals no alphabet—but the *patterns* of the water, the way it chooses its path through the granite, the way it widens cracks grain by grain, the way it carries dissolved stone from the deep rock to the surface of my valley. I am the one who watches the weeping seam and knows, before it happens, when the wall will shift. I am the one who predicted the fall of the southern overhang three winters past, who moved the harvesters' routes before the rock came down. I am the one they consult when the water changes taste.

My people do not name roles the way the old machines had names—stamped on plates, riveted to housings, spelled out in letters that meant something to people who lived before the ceiling fell. But if you asked a cultivator, a harvester, a tender of the mat-strains, they would tell you: *she reads the water*. They would touch their copper-wire disks, the lineage records that hang at their throats, and they would say: *her mother read the water, and her mother's mother before that, and the line runs unbroken since the valley was new.*

Unbroken is not quite true. The line has branched, married into other lines, absorbed the genes of people who came to the valley after the first ceiling-fall, people who brought new mat strains and new ways of surviving the cold. But the *knowledge*—the reading of the water—that has passed from teacher to student, from mother to daughter and once, notably, from aunt to nephew when no daughter was born for two generations—that has been unbroken. That is why I wear the spiral disk, the one marked with the double-line symbol that means *seam*, and the three dots that mean *reader*, and the branching pattern that records my descent from the first woman who noticed that water erodes granite in predictable patterns.

I was born here. Not in the valley proper—birth is a private thing, a hidden thing, something done in the deep passages where the mat is thin and the steam vents provide warmth—but here in the sense that matters: I have never seen the surface. I have never felt direct sunlight. I have never known a silence free of the drone.

The surface is not forbidden. I could go, if I chose. There are passages that lead up, up through the collapsed ceiling blocks, through the old ventilation shafts that the machines once used, through the narrow chimneys where the steam from below meets the cold air from above and freezes into rimmed lattices of ice. I have climbed those passages. I have stood at the threshold, where the light changes quality and the air becomes a thing I do not recognize—thin, sharp, full of scents that mean nothing to me. I have looked out at the surface: the white plain, the low sun, the wind that sculpts the snow into ridges that shift and breathe like living things. I have watched the aurora unfold in the long night, green and violet, a light my eyes can barely process because it is too bright, too pure, too unlike the diffuse phosphorescence of the valley.

But I have never stepped out. The cold up there is a different cold—not the steady, preservative chill of the valley but a killing cold, a brittleness that would crack my mat-clothing, freeze the breath in my lungs. My people stopped going to the surface long ago. The valley has everything: water that seeps from the rock, food that grows from the mat, heat that rises from the deep geothermal systems the old machines once tapped. The surface has nothing but light, and light is not enough.

So I stay below. I read the water. I watch the seams.

My body is lean, even by the standards of my people. The long torso, the shortened limbs, the prehensile toes—these are common to all of us, the result of generations of selection for life in low passages and on vertical rock faces. But my frame is lighter than most, my bones less dense, my musculature built for stillness rather than climbing. I can climb—I must, to reach the upper seep-fissures, to inspect the quartz veins where the water runs hidden through the stone—but I am not a natural climber. My gift is patience. I can sit motionless for hours, watching the slow mineral deposition at the mouth of a spring, the way the water-carried sediment builds tiny terraces, the way the terraces grow and merge and redirect the flow. I can read the history of a seep in its deposits the way a surface-dweller might read a book.

My mat-clothing is different from the harvesters'. They wear the silica-gripped working strain on their arms, the thick insulating strain across their backs, the flexible facial strain that allows expression. I wear the *patient* strain—a smooth, dense mat that does not need to grip or climb or carry, but simply to insulate, to hold my body heat in a still layer while I sit and watch. It is a dark strain, almost black in the dim light, with pores that open so slowly the breathing wave passes across my body over the course of an hour, a slow tide of expansion and contraction. It feels like the valley's own breathing when I wear it. It makes me part of the rock, part of the seep, part of the slow geological processes I observe.

My hands are bare like all my people's hands. But my calluses are different from the harvesters'. Mine are not the twisting-calluses of spore-capsule harvest, but the fine-grained calluses of texture-reading, the fingertips worn smooth rather than rough, the nerve endings trained to detect minute differences in mineral grain, in water temperature, in the vibration of the wall that tells me when a fracture is about to propagate. I can press my palm against a granite face and know whether the water behind it is flowing or pooling, whether the pressure is building or releasing, whether the next season will bring a widening of the seep or a sealing of the vein. I can hear the water through my bones.

When I read the seam, I am reading more than water. I am reading the valley's memory.

The water that seeps from the granite carries the dissolved history of the rock: the minerals that crystallized eons ago, the trace elements that leached from the old machines as they rusted, the residue of the mat's own metabolic processes, recycled through the rock and returned to the surface. I can taste the difference between water that has passed through a turbine housing and water that has passed through undisturbed granite. I can taste when the mat has been extracting iron from the corrugated shed, when the chelation process has accelerated, when the rust taste in the seep is stronger than it was the year before. I can taste the slow death of the machines, and the slow digestion of their remains.

There is a seep on the eastern wall, high up, that only my line knows. My mother showed me, and her mother showed her, and the memory goes back through the lineage disk, the marks recording not just descent but *location*, a drawn map of the hidden springs and secret water-routes that only the seam-readers know. I go there when I need to think, when the pressures of the valley—the constant low harmonizing of the drone, the demands of the cultivators, the slow politics of a small community where every individual is known and every decision is discussed—become too much. I climb to the hidden seep, and I sit, and I read the water that flows there.

It is the cleanest water in the valley. It has not passed through any metal, any rust, any of the old industrial residue. It comes from deep, from below the granite that the machines ever touched, from the blind heart of the rock where the geothermal heat keeps the water warm enough to flow. It tastes of—I have no word for it. The old language had words for clean, for pure, for fresh. My language has words for *uncontaminated*, for *free of rust-taste*, for *the water that remembers the deep stone*. It is the oldest water in the valley, and I am the only one who drinks it.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is a surface concept, a thing of people who live in a world of excess, where there are too many people and too little connection. In the valley, connection is constant. The drone ensures it—no one is ever out of earshot of the valley's breathing, and the breathing connects us all. The mat connects us too: I can read the lineage of a person by the strains they wear, the pattern of pores on their shoulders, the texture of their hood. I know every member of my community by the sound of their breathing, the rhythm of their footsteps, the particular way the mat folds around their neck.

But I am separate, sometimes. The seam-readers have always been separate. We are the ones who climb to the hidden seeps, who spend hours alone in the darkness, who develop a tolerance for solitude that the others find strange. We are the ones who know the valley's secrets—not the harvesters' secrets of fruiting-body ripeness or the cultivators' secrets of strain-selection, but the deeper secrets: where the water comes from, where the fractures are growing, when the next ceiling-fall will come. We are the ones who remember.

I remember the story of the second ceiling-fall. Not the first one, the great collapse that opened the valley to the surface and changed everything—that was before my people came here, or before they were my people, before the lineages were recorded in copper wire and turbine steel. The second ceiling-fall, the one that killed the south passage and forced the relocation of the cultivators' main plots, that one I know. My grandmother was the seam-reader then. She had been watching the southern overhang for years, watching the water percolate through the fractures, watching the slow widening of the cracks. She warned them. They listened. And when the rock came down, no one died.

My people have no heroes. We have no legends of great deeds, no songs of triumph. We have only the memory of good decisions, passed down through the lineage disks, recorded in the pattern of mat-strains that each generation cultivates. My grandmother's warning is remembered in the marrow of my bones, in the caution I feel when I climb to the seeps that run beneath the weakest rock, in the particular way I press my palm against the granite and listen for the sound of impending failure.

The seam is speaking to me now. I have been sitting at the weeping wall for—I cannot say how long. The drone has shifted through two full cycles, and the breathing pores have completed their slow wave across the valley floor. The water has been seeping, steady, a thread-like flow that runs down the granite face and pools in a shallow basin before soaking into the mat below. The mat here is different: darker, more swollen, the strain that prefers constant moisture, the strain that digests rock directly, pulling minerals from the water and depositing them in slow crystalline layers on the underside of the mat. It is this strain that has been widening the seam, the way it digests the granite, ripping the mineral structure apart atom by atom.

I taste the water. It is changing. The mineral content is shifting, the balance of calcium to magnesium altering, the trace elements—copper, iron, the weird cocktail of ions that leach from the old machines—presenting a new pattern. What does it mean? I close my eyes and let my tongue read the water, let my body's deep chemical memory interpret the flavor. There is something here, something I have not tasted before. A metal I do not recognize. Something from the deep rock, something new.

I reach for my lineage disk. The copper wire is warm against my throat, warmed by my own blood, by the slow circulation of antifreeze proteins that keeps me alive in this cold. The disk itself is polished smooth by generations of fingertips—my mother's, my grandmother's, my own—and the marks are very faint now, worn by time and touch. But I know them by heart. I know my descent, my name, my role. I am the seam-reader, the water-taster, the one who watches the slow dissolution of the rock and understands what it means.

The new metal in the water is telling me something. The valley is changing. Not quickly, not dramatically, but with the patient persistence of water against granite, of mat against metal, of time against everything. The seam will widen. The rock will fall. The valley will reshape itself, and my people will adapt, and the mat will adapt, and the water will find new paths, and the cycles will continue.

I am not afraid. Fear is a response to a particular kind of future—a future that threatens loss, disruption, unpredictability. But the valley's future is predictable. It is written in the water, in the stone, in the slow oscillation of the breathing pores and the drone that never stops. I have read it. I know what is coming. And what is coming is simply more of what has already been: change, adaptation, continuation. The seam-readers have always known this. We have always been the ones who watch the water and understand that nothing is permanent except the process.

I drink from the seep one more time. The new metal is still there, a taste I will learn, a taste I will teach my children's children to recognize. Then I rise, and I begin the climb back down to the valley floor, to the community, to the cultivators who will want to know what I have found, to the harvesters who will adjust their routes, to the tenders who will select new mat strains to digest the new mineral. I am the seam-reader. I have read the water. I have seen the future, and it is the same as the past, only different, only more itself.

The drone ebbs and flows. The breathing pores open and close in their slow wave. The valley inhales, exhales, and I am part of the breath, part of the cycle, part of the patient, endless process. I am not afraid. I am not lonely. I am here, and I am watching, and I am reading the water that tells me everything.

The seam is speaking. I am listening.
