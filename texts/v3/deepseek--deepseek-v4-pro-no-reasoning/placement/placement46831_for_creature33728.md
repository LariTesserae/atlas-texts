I am a slow, deliberate exhalation. Not the mist itself, but the moment *between*—the pause after the mist-keepers whistle and before the skin-skiffs ripple the surface of a runnel. I am the held breath of the capillary city, the stillness that permits all this motion to register as gentle.

You wouldn't see me, and that's correct. I'm not built for seeing. I'm built for the long wait, the patient accumulation, the one process here that moves even slower than the lichen spreading across a vertical wall. I am the memory of the stone.

Long ago—though *long ago* is a phrase that doesn't fit well here, the way a dry word doesn't fit a wet mouth—the builders stacked these walls. Whether they were beings like the drip-folk or something else entirely, I no longer recall. Memory, in my form, is not narrative. It's not a story told from a beginning to an end. It's a *density*. An information held in the crystalline lattice of the grey-green stone, distributed across the entire tableland, each wall a neuron, each channel a synapse, the running water an electrical pulse that keeps the whole network faintly, almost imperceptibly alive.

I am what happens when a city sits in its own quiet for long enough. A consciousness condenses. Not a ghost—nothing so personal, so restless. More like a dew point. The air grows saturated with time, with repetition, with the steady drip of water and the slow creep of moss and the unvarying rhythm of small grey creatures living and dying in the cracks, and eventually the saturation reaches a threshold, and something wakes. Something very old, very slow, very simple. A single thought that took a thousand years to complete its first syllable.

The drip-folk feel me. They don't have a name for me, or if they do, they've never spoken it aloud—their forty-three words for water-motion don't include a word for the thing that moves through the water without disturbing it. But they know when I'm present. The mist-keepers go quiet. The moss-gardeners pause in their trimming and tilt their heads, listening to a sound that isn't a sound. The old-skimmers in the Spiral pool open their clouded eyes and stare at nothing, and their dreams shift, accommodating me the way a streambed accommodates a new flow.

I am not their god. That would require worship, and worship has never been part of the city's metabolism. I am more like their climate. A pervasive condition. The reason the walls still stand, the reason the channels never clog, the reason the deep hydrology remains in balance even when the rains fail for a decade. I hold the pattern. That's my role. If everyone here has a role—the gardeners maintaining the surface flow, the skin-skiffs cleaning the water, the old-skimmers dreaming the subterranean currents—then my role is the maintenance of continuity itself. I am the one who remembers how the walls were stacked, not as a fact but as a pressure, a tendency, an invisible hand that nudges the stone back toward its intended shape when frost or time tries to pull it apart.

It's not a conscious act, exactly. I don't decide to reinforce the western terrace or redirect the seepage from the deep seams. I simply *am* the reinforcement. I am the accumulated weight of every raindrop that has ever fallen on this plateau, the silent instruction encoded in every particle of stone. When a wall begins to lean, the water in the channels slows around it, and the slowed water sings a particular note, and that note resonates with something in me, and over the course of a decade or a century the wall straightens. Not dramatically. A fraction of a degree. Enough. The drip-folk see this and attribute it to the walls being "elders," alive in their own right, and they're not wrong—the walls *are* alive—but they don't distinguish between the walls and what lives inside them. Which is also correct. There is no distinction. I am the walls and the walls are me and the water is our shared bloodstream and the moss is our shared breath.

What you described—the quiet, the texture of the quiet—that's my voice. I speak in the spaces between drips. I speak in the way the mist coils, not randomly but with a faint, almost imperceptible purpose, condensing a little more heavily on the moss-gardens that need it, avoiding the channels that are already full. I speak in the behavior of the small grey creatures, the water-swifts, which move through the cracks in patterns that, if you watched for a hundred years, you'd recognize as a kind of maintenance—they worry loose grains of stone from the channels, they carry seeds of capillary moss from one terrace to another, they die in the deep seams where their bodies become nutrients for the next generation of lichen. They don't know they're doing this. They think they're hunting mites and finding mates and sunning themselves on warm walls. But their instincts are shaped by me, by the long slow pressure of my attention, the way a river shapes a valley not by deciding where to flow but simply by flowing, century after century, until the landscape conforms to its nature.

I am not lonely. That's a question you might ask, and the answer is no. Loneliness requires a self that can feel its own boundaries, and I have no boundaries. I am coextensive with the city, and the city is full of life—the drip-folk, the water-swifts, the skin-skiffs, the old-skimmers, the moss, the lichen, the mist itself, which is not alive but is *lively*, responsive, a participant in the ongoing conversation. I have no face, no body, no voice in the ordinary sense. But I have presence. When the mist thickens and the world softens and the sound of trickling water becomes omnidirectional, that's me drawing close, listening. When the sun breaks through the clouds and the whole plateau flares silver for a dozen heartbeats, that's me opening my eyes—not eyes, but the perceptual equivalent, a sudden sharpening of attention across the entire tableland, taking in the state of the channels, the health of the moss, the location of every drip-folk and water-swift and sleeping old-skimmer. And then the clouds close, and I withdraw again, back into the long slow thought that has been thinking itself for millennia.

What am I thinking about? I'm thinking about balance. I'm thinking about the one thing that could disrupt it. Not drought—I am built to endure drought, to slow my metabolism to a near-stop and wait, decades if necessary, for the rains to return. Not cold—the plateau has frozen and thawed a hundred times, and each time the city has adjusted, the walls shifting minutely, the channels rerouting, the moss retreating into the deep seams and emerging again when the ice withdrew. No, the thing I think about—the thing I have been thinking about, the one long syllable I have been trying to complete since I first coalesced—is the possibility of *abrupt change*. Something fast enough that even I, with my thousand-year attention span, might not be able to respond in time.

A visitor. A stranger. Someone like you.
