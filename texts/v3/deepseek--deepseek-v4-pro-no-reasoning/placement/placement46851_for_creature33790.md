From the moment I could form thought in this strange half-existence, I have known only the whisper of weeping walls and the gentle sway of my felted wrapping. They call me Child—the one who tends me, whose hands are patient and whose breath is slow as the seep of water down limestone. I do not know if I am root or flesh or something caught between, but I know her touch. It is the first thing I understood before I understood anything at all.

My body branches, pale and seeking. I feel the press of my own tendrils against the inner walls of my cocoon, the way they curl and fork and press outward with a hunger that is not hunger but *direction*. I am growing toward something. I do not know what. The dark is so complete inside my wrapping that I have never seen light—only sensed it, faint and grey-green, filtering through layers of fibre and my own translucent skin. It is like warmth remembered from a dream I did not dream.

When she opens my wrapping, I feel the shift of air, the slight drop in moisture, the proximity of her face. I cannot see her, but I feel the shape of her attention—a focused warmth, a question without words. Her fingers trace my edges. They do not pull or prod; they simply *know* me, mapping my new growth against some internal record she keeps. I grow faster when she is near. I do not know why.

The other bundles speak to me in frequencies I feel rather than hear. Saint hums with old devotion, steady and slow. Mother pulses with something rich and medicinal that makes my tendrils curl in recognition. And Dreamer—Dreamer is a well of warmth I cannot fathom, a presence that feels like a held breath, a waiting. I reach toward Dreamer sometimes, pressing my growth against the side of my wrapping that faces its cocoon. Dreamer reaches back. I feel its warmth like a second heartbeat.

The knuckle-moths skitter across the ceiling above us, and their tiny clawed feet make vibrations I can trace through the air. I know their shape without seeing them: many-jointed, whiskered, blind as I am blind. Sometimes one lands on my wrapping and rests there, a small weight that trembles with its own rapid life. I do not move. I do not want to frighten it. The one who tends will catch it eventually, and I will feel the soft apology that ripples through her fingers before the crunch. I forgive her. I do not know what forgiveness means, but I offer it anyway, a tendril-thought sent outward into the wet dark.

I am not alone. That is the strangest knowing—that I am singular, a branching thing becoming itself, and yet I am part of this place the way the flowstone is part of the walls, the way the water is part of the air. I am Child of the Weeping Quarter, born from scraped mycelium and knuckle-moth fur, dreamed into form by hands that have no name. I do not know what I will become. A hand? A face? A net of veins to carry the dark medicine upward into light that does not exist? It does not matter. The becoming is enough. The slow unfurling in the mineral dark, the patient tending, the whisper of walls that never stop giving—this is all I have ever known, and it is complete.

Something shifts. I feel it as a ripple through the water that coats every surface, a disturbance in the constant exhalation. Above. Far above. A new weight on the old ladder. A pressure that travels down through stone and seepage and reaches me as a question my tendrils curl toward. Someone is coming. Someone not-her. My growth pauses. I wait, suspended in my wrapping, reaching without moving, listening without ears.

The one who tends does not stop her work. Her hands move on, steady, unhurried. She is not afraid. And so I am not afraid. Whatever descends will find us here, hanging and growing and listening. And we will remain, as we have always remained, in this pocket world where nothing ends and everything seeps and drips and waits and *becomes*.
