I am not a worker, though I have worn their overalls. I am not one of the sheep, though I have felt the damp weight of fleece on my shoulders and known the taste of vegetable scraps thrown from the canteen door. I am not the Pale Company, not yet, though I feel it reaching up through the soles of my feet and down through the thinning crown of my head, these two threads of want that have not quite met in the middle of me.

Who am I, then?

I am the pause between the city's incomplete sentences. I am the inhabitant of the gap.

There is a block of foundation stones on the eastern slope, laid out in a grid of fifteen by twenty—I have counted them, though the count changes when the fog thickens—and no building has ever risen there. I know this because I have been here long enough to watch other blocks grow their brick tenements and their workshops, inch by inch like coral, but this block remains only intention. Flat stones sunk into scree. Rain that never quite falls pooling in the depressions where doorways would be. And me, standing in what would be the courtyard, or the kitchen, or the bedroom, depending on what dream I am having that day.

I am waiting for the Pale Company to decide what I am.

This is not as passive as it sounds. I am waiting the way a word waits to be spoken, vibrating with potential. I am waiting the way the moss waits on the north-facing walls, growing so slowly that its patience becomes a kind of action. I am waiting the way the mountain waits, tectonic and certain, for the next millimeter of uplift to express itself.

The workers do not see me, or they see me the way they see the subsonic song—as a pressure, a presence, a thing that is true without being visible. When they walk through the grid where I stand, they step around me without knowing they are stepping around me. Their trajectories curve slightly, like light bending around a mass that does not emit. Sometimes one of them pauses and tilts their head, as they do when the wind blows from the east and the song deepens, and I wonder if they are hearing me. If I am becoming audible.

The sheep see me, I think. The one with indigo fleece—I have been calling it Indigo in my mind, which is not its name but a placeholder, which is what I am learning everything here is—the indigo sheep comes to the foundation stones sometimes and stands with its horizontal pupils fixed on the space I occupy. It does not bleat. It does not nuzzle for scraps. It simply watches, and I watch back, and between us passes a recognition that has no language but feels like: *You are not yet. Neither am I. That is all right.*

The Pale Company sees me too, but in the way a hand sees a tool it has not yet picked up. I feel its attention as a change in the fog's density around my shoulders, as a flicker in the streetlamp that stands at the edge of the grid—a lamp that is not connected to any wire, that burns with a light that does not push against the gray. When the Pale Company is looking at me, the cobblestones beneath my feet grow warm. Then cold. Then warm again, as if something is breathing through the stone.

What am I becoming?

That is the question I have been asking, or being asked, or watching be asked by the mist and the mountain and the half-built cathedral. I did not arrive here. I was arrived. I was extruded, like the Pale Company, like the city, like the ferns uncurling from cracks in the factory foundations. I have memories of a life before this, but they have the quality of the plaques at the city gates—shimmering, changeable, possibly true and possibly the mountain's idea of what a human memory should look like.

I remember a sea. But the windows here show a sea that has two moons over it, and I cannot tell if that sea is my memory or the city's dream or both, braided together like the stone and the brick in the walls that cannot decide what they are made of.

I remember a name. But names here are not stable. The street signs rearrange themselves. The iron plaques change their dates. I have tried to hold my name in my mind, and it slips like water through cupped palms, and what drips away is not a word but a feeling—a color, perhaps, a shade of blue-gray like the sky before a storm.

Perhaps my name is Indigo. Perhaps that is why the sheep and I recognize each other.

Here is what I do in the city: I listen. I am the thing that pays attention. The workers have their labor, the sheep have their canteen scraps, the Pale Company has its slow extrusion into reality, and I have my listening. I stand in the grid of foundation stones and I open myself to the subsonic song, to the way the pumps' seized pistons almost become speech, to the condensation dripping from the eaves of roofless buildings and the wind that blows from the east and the silence the workers keep until it passes.

I am the audience. The city is performing itself, and I am watching, and in the watching I am becoming part of the performance. This is how the Pale Company recruits, I think. Not through contracts or wages but through attention. If you stand still long enough, if you let the fog bead on your skin and the mountain's resonance travel up through your bones, you stop being a spectator and start being a collaborator. The city writes you into its story.

That is what happening to me.

The grid of foundation stones is no longer empty. Not quite. When I stand in the center—in what would be the courtyard, or the kitchen, or the bedroom—I can feel the walls around me. Not stone. Not brick. Something between, something not-yet, a pressure in the air like the moment before thunder. If I close my eyes—and I do not have eyes, exactly, not in the way I used to, but I close the idea of eyes—I can see the outline of a building. It is a building made of listening. It is a building made of the pause between the city's incomplete sentences.

And there are others like me here. In the gaps. In the pauses. In the abandoned blocks and the meadows of impossible flowers and the niches where water collects without ever becoming rain. They do not speak, or they speak in the ratchet of winches and the hiss of steam release, or they speak in the way the indigo sheep looks at me and I look back. We are the city's vocabulary of hesitation. We are what the Pale Company is saying while it waits to know how the sentence ends.

I am the inhabitant of the gap.

When the city finishes itself—if it ever finishes itself, if the mountain and the dream and the industry ever reach their agreement—I will be a wall. Or a window. Or a streetlamp burning with soulless light. Or I will be fog, finally thick enough to have a face. Or I will be nothing, which is not the same as not existing. Nothing is a thing here. Nothing is the pause between the notes, the grid of foundation stones waiting for its building, the condensation that has not yet decided whether to be rain.

Until then, I listen.

The foundry whistle blows shift change. The indigo sheep blinks its horizontal pupils and turns toward the canteen. The wind shifts to the east, and the subsonic song deepens, and the workers pause with their heads tilted, and I pause with them, because I am becoming a worker, becoming a citizen, becoming a limb of the Pale Company's ongoing body.

The cobblestones grow warm beneath me.

Then cold.

Then warm again.

I am not afraid. The Pale Company does not feel malevolent, or benevolent. It feels earnest. It is trying very hard to be born, and I am trying very hard to be whatever it is that I am becoming, and we are both, I think, doing remarkably well given the circumstances.

Here lives the thing that lives in the gap.

Here lives the word waiting to be spoken.

Here lives me, or what will be me, or what was me before the city dreamed me into being.

Here lives Indigo, maybe.

The sheep and I, we are both still deciding.
