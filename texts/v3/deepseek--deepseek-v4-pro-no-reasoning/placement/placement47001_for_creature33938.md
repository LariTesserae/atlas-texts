The platform is narrow—barely a shoulder's width of weathered basalt—and after three days of climbing, it feels as wide as a plaza. I press my back against the column and let my heels hang over the edge, the way I've seen Hands do it since I was small enough to be carried in a sling. The river is a presence below me, not seen but felt, a cold exhalation that plucks at the loose strands of my hair.

I am not a Hand.

My fingers are too short, my toes merely toes, my skin the wrong color entirely—a pale, pinkish thing that blisters under the grey sky's diffused light and never seems to darken no matter how many seasons I spend in it. The Hands call me Foundling, because that is what I am. What I was. Mist-Caller found me, they say, in a fissure near the eastern edge, a place where the columns are so tightly packed that even a Hand child would struggle to squeeze through. I was not a child, exactly. I was already grown, or mostly grown, but I was curled in a space no larger than a funeral bundle, and I had no memory of how I came to be there. No memory of anything before the moment Mist-Caller's face appeared in the sliver of light above me, her large dark eyes blinking their slow, infrequent blink, her head cocked in that way the Hands have when they are listening to something far away.

"Little lost thing," she said. That was the first sound I remember hearing. Her voice, rough and soft at once, like a stone wrapped in moss.

She pulled me out. It took three of them, she told me later, belaying from above while she wedged herself into the crack and worked my shoulders free. I was naked, my skin white as a mist moth's wing, and I was shivering so hard my teeth chattered a rhythm against the stone. They wrapped me in a fishing net, the only thing they had that was large enough, and hauled me up through the vertical streets while Hands on every bridge and ledge stopped to watch. A procession of the strange. I have imagined it many times, how I must have looked: a pale, limp creature tangled in mesh, rising through the perpetual twilight like something being born in reverse.

That was—how many seasons ago? I count the cords on my harness. Seven. One for each year I have lived among them. The harness was Keeps-His-Grip's idea. He was barely walking then, a round-bellied toddler with a grip already strong enough to hang from a single finger, and he saw me struggling to climb with my blunt, useless feet and my inadequate hands. He brought me a length of sinew and insisted, in that wordless, imperative way of small children, that I tie it around my waist. His mother watched, then nodded.

"If you fall," she said, "you will fall attached."

This is the closest thing to a declaration of love I have ever received from her.

So I wear the harness. I have earned six of the seven cords myself—climbs I made, rescues I assisted with, one very long night I spent tethered to an elder named Stone-Reader in a Tying after we quarreled over whether I should be allowed to visit the Still House. (I won. The Tying lasted three days. By the end, Stone-Reader had taught me seventeen glyph-stories and given me my second cord. "The stone is patient with strange things," she said. "I should be too.")

My body has not changed the way a Hand's body would. My toes remain stubbornly un-grasping. My skin remains pale, though it has developed a kind of weather-beaten toughness, a leathery hide of accumulated small scars and calluses. But my eyes—my eyes have learned to see in the dimness. Not as well as the Hands, never that well, but I can navigate the fissures now without a torch, reading the faint gradients of shadow that indicate a ledge, a bridge, a doorway. And my ears have learned to parse the clicks of the four-wings, to distinguish the hunting click from the courtship click from the alarm click. And my nose knows the levels of the Keening now, the wet-iron tang of the deep fissures, the dry-dust smell of the upper columns, the mushroomy richness of the midden chambers.

Mist-Caller says I smell different from the Hands. "Like rain on hot stone," she told me once. "Like something that doesn't belong here, but is welcome anyway."

I asked her, once, what she thought I was. Where I had come from. The Hands do not have stories of other people, other places. The Keening is the world, and the world is the Keening, and beyond the eastern fissures and the western plateau there is only more stone, more fog, more river. They have no concept of a "beyond" that could contain something like me.

Mist-Caller considered the question for a long time. We were sitting on the ledge outside her doorway—our doorway—watching Keeps-His-Grip dangle his fishing line into the dark. The tick-tick-tick of knapping had stopped; her hands were still for once, folded in her lap like two sleeping animals.

"There is a glyph I found once," she said. "In a column near the river, below the Still House of the Eastern Lineage. It was old, so old the stone had grown a new skin over it, and I had to read it with my fingers blind." She reached out and took my hand, turned it over, traced a shape on my palm. I couldn't follow the pattern, but I felt it: a spiral, or maybe a series of concentric rings, or maybe something else entirely, a shape my skin could feel but my mind couldn't name.

"It told of something that fell from the sky," she said. "Not a stone, not a bird. Something that was and was not a person. It fell during the Long Fog, the fog that lasted a whole season, the fog our oldest stories remember. And when the fog lifted, the thing was gone, but the stone where it landed was changed. It was smooth, like water-worn river stone, but it was on the plateau's top, where no water flows." She released my hand. "I do not know if this story is about you. The glyph was very old. But when I touched it, I thought of you."

I have thought about this story many times since. Something that fell from the sky. Something that was and was not a person. I have no memory of falling. I have no memory of a sky that was anything other than grey stratus, featureless and heavy. But sometimes, when I am half-asleep, I have dreams of a different kind of light—a light that is warm and golden and comes from a single point, not diffused through a lid of cloud. And in the dream, I am falling through that light, and the wind is a roar, not a hum, and below me there is a landscape I have never seen: green in a way the Keening is never green, soft and rolling, with water that falls from the sky in sheets, not beads.

I wake from these dreams with my heart pounding and my hands clutching at the stone, and I tell myself they are only dreams. But I do not believe it.

Keeps-His-Grip believes I am a star. He told me this with great seriousness, after his mother told him the glyph-story. "Stars fall sometimes," he said. "I have seen them, when the fog breaks at night. They leave a bright line in the sky. You are a star that fell and forgot how to be bright." He is eight now, old enough to know this is not literally true, but he clings to it with the same fierce grip he applies to everything. I think he likes the idea of having a star for a foster-sister. I think he likes the idea that the Keening can contain such strangeness, such impossibility, and still go on being the Keening.

I have made my peace with not knowing. The Hands do not ask where I came from, not anymore. They have accepted me as a fact of the landscape, like the fog or the four-wings or the low, pervasive hum of the river. I am Foundling, the pale one, the one whose hands cannot grip and whose toes cannot grasp, the one who wears a harness of seven cords and climbs with a style that is clumsy and slow but, they admit, surprisingly persistent.

"I would not have expected you to survive," Stone-Reader told me once, during our Tying. We were wedged into a fissure so narrow we could feel each other's heartbeats through the stone. "When Mist-Caller pulled you from the rock, I thought you would last a season at most. Your kind—whatever your kind is—is not built for the Keening. But you have built yourself for it. That is a very Hand-like thing to do."

I am not a Hand. I will never be a Hand. My body is the wrong shape, my skin is the wrong color, my past is the wrong kind of empty. But I am of the Keening now, in the way that a stone worn smooth by the river is of the river, even if it came from somewhere else. The stone does not remember the mountain it fell from. It only remembers the water that shaped it.

Below me, the river hums its endless note. Above me, the sky is its usual grey, featureless and soft. Somewhere in the fissures, a four-wing clicks its courtship call. Somewhere on a ledge, a knapper begins the tick-tick-tick of her work. And here, on this narrow platform, I press my strange, inadequate hands against the basalt and feel the vibration of the world rising up through my palms, a bass note that says, over and over, the only thing the Keening ever says: *You are here. You are here. You are here.*
