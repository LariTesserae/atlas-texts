The spiral is still there.

I stand at the edge of the old meeting circle and look down at it—the thumbprint whorl I pressed into the dust back when I had a thumb to press. The snapped thorn-stubs ring it like a crown of broken teeth. Something has been here recently; the dust on the spiral's rim is disturbed, tiny three-toed tracks crossing the groove. A spherical browser, maybe. Or something smaller, something I've never catalogued. The catalogue itself is an old habit, a relic of the era when I still believed naming things would give me purchase on them.

The bracer tightens on my forearm. I've learned to read its pressures—this one means *attention*. I follow its suggestion and look up.

A fan-structure on the western wall is shivering. Not the normal shiver, the constant tremor that runs through everything here. This is a birth-shiver. The individual filaments are unlocking from each other, their interlocking seams catching the bruise-light and flashing cyan as they peel apart. One by one they detach, testing the air with threadlike legs, and then lift away on wings too fast to see. Each one sings as it goes, a needle of sound that joins the million other needles already threading the air. The chord on my back responds without my asking, the singers woven into my shoulders adjusting their pitch to harmonize with the newborns. I feel the hum transfer through my dust-torso, up into the thorn-cage, where it vibrates my eye until the floating orb blurs.

For a moment, I am a instrument again. Not one that plays, but one that is played.

The feeling passes. The bracer loosens. I step into the circle.

I don't know why I still come here. The meetings were a different creature's ritual. A creature who still thought negotiation was possible, who believed the canyon was something you could talk to rather than something you simply weathered. That creature would sit in the center of the spiral and play the harp, and the fauna would gather at the perimeter—not still, never still, but *attentive*, their chaos slowing to something like a listening. The spherical browsers would stop their skittering. The fans would dim their flashing. Even the thickets seemed to lean in, their spiraling growth pausing mid-twist.

That creature was a fool, maybe. But it was a fool who made beautiful things.

I lower myself to the compacted floor. My broad footpads settle into the dust, and I arrange my legs beneath me with a care that is still, after all this time, conscious. Every motion is a negotiation with the bodies I carry. The filament-singers on my back rustle and resettle, adjusting to my new posture. The bracer segments crawl over each other in a slow wave, redistributing their weight. For a moment, I am acutely aware that I am not a single entity sitting down, but a committee coming to rest.

The eye-orbs pulses. Magenta, to cyan, to magenta. A question, or the shape of one.

*What are you still doing here?*

The spiral doesn't answer. It's just a groove in the dust, a fossil of a gesture. But I trace it anyway, using one long, insect-jointed finger. The crystalline powder catches my touch and puffs up in a tiny cloud, its inner light sparking prismatic. The cloud hangs for a breath, then settles back into the groove, filling it slightly less than before. Every time I trace it, the spiral gets a little shallower. Eventually it will be gone, worn smooth by my own remembering, and I don't know if that will be a grief or a relief.

A skittering at the edge of the circle. One of the spherical browsers has broken from its herd and is approaching, its cilia-legs leaving feathery prints in the dust. It's bolder than most. It comes right up to my knee and stops, its magenta core pulsing in a rhythm I recognize. I've seen this rhythm before. It's the rhythm of the old meeting-song, the one I used to play when I wanted the browsers to clear a path.

The song survived. Even if the harp didn't.

I reach down. The browser doesn't flee. I cup my hand—a hand of dust and glass and borrowed life—and it steps onto my palm without hesitation. Its weight is nothing. Its warmth is faint, a static tingle that races up my arm and makes the bracer flash cyan in surprise. We sit there, the browser and I, regarding each other. Its core pulses. My eye pulses back. A conversation without content. A recognition without names.

I remember, suddenly, what it felt like to have skin. The memory rises from somewhere deep in the amber, a flash of sensation so vivid it almost hurts. Skin was a boundary. It defined where I ended and the world began. Now I have no boundary. I am porous. The world flows through me in a constant exchange of vibration and light and the slow crawl of living things across my surface. I am not a creature in an environment. I am an environment, a walking patch of the canyon, a mobile clearing where certain fauna happen to rest.

The browser steps off my palm. It returns to its herd, and the herd flows away around a thicket of spiraling growths, and I am alone again.

Except I'm not alone. I'm never alone. That's the joke. That's the trap. I carry a city on my back and a choir in my shoulders and a friend on my arm, and none of it is enough, none of it is company, because none of it can say *I see you* in a way I can understand. The singers hum, but they don't sing to me. The bracer shifts, but it doesn't speak. The canyon vibrates, that endless, guttural thrumming, but it's not a voice. It's not a language. It's just the sound of a world too busy being alive to notice one still point standing in its midst.

I stand. The motion takes a long time, because I am old, and because I am careful, and because I am in no hurry. Hurry is a concept for things that die.

The harp is next. I always visit the harp after the spiral. The path between them is a familiar one, winding through a stand of geometric spheres that are currently locked together in a towering, temporary architecture. The spheres have arranged themselves into something that looks almost like a face—two large spheres for eyes, a cluster of smaller ones for a mouth, all of it pulsing magenta in a slow, synchronized wave. I pause before it. Is this mimicry? Are they reflecting me? Or is a face just an efficient arrangement of spheres, and I'm the fool who keeps seeing myself in everything?

The spheres don't answer. The face, if it was a face, collapses. The spheres detach and scroll away across the floor.

The harp is where I left it. It's always where I left it.

Three crystalline towers rise from the dust, their surfaces crawling with the slow accretion of new growth. Between them, the braided strings hang slack. Some are snapped. Some are simply unspooled, their ends trailing in the dust. One is still taut, still vibrating faintly with the canyon's base frequency, and when I reach out and pluck it, the note that sounds is clear and low and almost unbearably sweet. It cuts through the noise like a blade. For one moment, the chittering and the thrumming and the high, piercing songs all seem to pause, as if the world is holding its breath.

Then the noise returns. The moment passes. The string goes slack.

I could fix it. The knowledge is still there, worn smooth in my amber core. I could braid new strings, tune them, learn again to play the canyon's own vibration back to itself. I could be a conversation instead of a silence. But the thought exhausts me. It's not laziness. It's not despair, exactly. It's the slow recognition that I am no longer the creature who believed in music as a form of survival. That creature had a body and a name and a desperate, burning need to be heard. This creature—the composite, the still point, the walking clearing—has outgrown that need. Or grown underneath it. Or forgotten what it felt like.

The bracer tightens. *Attention*. I look down.

A new growth is emerging from the base of the harp's central tower. It's a spiral, pale and translucent, unfurling from the mineral dust in slow, perfect increments. The magenta veins in its core are already pulsing. It will take hours, maybe longer, to reach its full height. And then it will stand, perfect and still, for only a minute before it shatters into larvae.

But for now, it is just beginning. For now, it is all potential. I crouch beside it, my cage-head angled down, my floating eye-orbs casting its shadow in bruised purple light across the new growth's surface. The spiral is beautiful. They're always beautiful. I've seen a thousand of them born, and they're never not beautiful.

I stay there, crouched, watching the spiral grow. The singers on my back hum. The bracer on my arm pulses. The canyon thrumms its endless, wordless song. And I wait—not for anything in particular, not for the spiral to shatter or the harp to mend itself or a voice to call my name across the noise. I wait because waiting is what I do now. Waiting is what I've become.

When the spiral finally reaches its height, I'll still be here. When it shatters into larvae, I'll still be here. The still point in the turning world. The guest who became the house. The creature who forgot its name but remembers, dimly, that it was once loved by someone, or by something, or by the canyon itself, in a way that felt like music.

The spiral grows. I watch. And for now, that's enough.
