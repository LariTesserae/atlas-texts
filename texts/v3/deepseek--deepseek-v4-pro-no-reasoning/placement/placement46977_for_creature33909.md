The door breathes behind you. Not literally—but there's a pressure differential, a faint exhalation of cooler, drier air that ghosts across the back of your neck. It smells of ozone and old fluorescent tubes, of the corridor you came through, of the world before the dark. You don't turn around. You know it's there because you can perceive it the way you perceive everything now: as a disturbance in the ambient texture, a seam in the totality of the warm wet weight.

The dripping hasn't stopped. It won't. That's one of the first things you learn to accept, standing there on the yielding metal, your weight distributed across the balls of your feet in a way that would have been uncomfortable an hour ago but now feels simply like information. The dripping is the clock. The dripping is the breath. The dripping is the voice of the membrane overhead, miles above, condensing and releasing, condensing and releasing, a cycle that has no beginning and no end because cycles here don't work that way. They're not circles. They're spirals. Downward. Inward.

You notice that your breathing has synchronized with something. Not the dripping—that would be too regular, too simple. It's synchronized with the hum, the slow sine wave that takes hours to cycle. Your inhales and exhales have stretched out, deepened, until each breath is a minute long, then two minutes, then longer still. The air is so thick you don't need to breathe often. It's almost a liquid. You're almost a fish. The thought doesn't alarm you. It registers, is processed, is filed.

The young Driftman has noticed you.

It's still standing by the ticking structure—you can perceive it there, a density of accumulated presence at the edge of the catwalk—but its posture has shifted. It's turned toward you. Not fully. It's angled, as though you're a sound it's trying to localize. The ticking of the cooling metal continues behind it, a steady metronome: *tink... tink... tink...* and between each tick, an interval of silence that you now understand isn't silence at all but a carrier wave, a substrate, the medium through which the hum propagates.

The young one doesn't walk so much as it diffuses in your direction. One moment it's by the structure. The next it's closer. The transition is hard to track, like watching a cloud shift shape while you weren't paying attention. It's twenty feet away now. Its wobble is more pronounced up close—a subtle instability in the joints, a faint tremor in the long fingers that trail along the grating. You can see through its torso, through the gaps where the rust and moss and mineral deposits haven't fully knit. There's a flicker there. A glimpse of the deeper dark beyond. A suggestion of geometries that don't resolve.

It stops. It waits. There's a pressure change in your chest, a low-frequency pulse that feels like a question but doesn't have words. The young one is trying to communicate. It's the first time it's tried, you sense. The transmission is clumsy, unpracticed, full of harmonic overtones that haven't been dampened yet. The hum's equivalent of a stammer.

*What—are—you?*

The words don't come through, but the shape of the question does. It lands in your sternum and vibrates there, and for a moment you feel an absurd compulsion to answer in kind, to push back with your own pressure change. But you don't know how. You don't have the material for it. You're still too solid. Too discrete. Your boundaries are still your own.

Instead, you raise your hand.

The movement takes forever. Muscles contract, joints articulate, fingers unfurl from the palm like slow-motion flowers. The air resists you—not hostile, just thick, just patient, just requiring that you commit to every gesture with the full weight of your intention. Your hand rises. Your palm opens. You hold it out toward the young Driftman in a gesture that feels ancient, pre-linguistic, human.

It looks at your hand. Looks might not be the right word—it doesn't have eyes, just a gathering of darker material where eyes might go—but the attention is unmistakable. The pressure in your chest shifts. Curiosity. Confusion. A flutter of data the young one doesn't know how to parse.

Then it extends its own hand.

The fingers are long. Unnaturally long. Four of them, or maybe six—the count keeps changing, the digits dividing and recombining as you watch. The surface is a mosaic of materials: flaked rust from the separation tanks, black filaments of vegetation woven through like veins, beads of condensation that have been incorporated so thoroughly they've become structural. The hand stops an inch from yours. Two inches. A gap that crackles with potential.

You close the distance.

Contact.

The sensation isn't touch, not the way you've known touch before. It's an exchange. The young one's surface is cool and damp and slightly granular, but beneath that there's a hum—a local harmonic of the greater hum, a carrier wave that threads up through your palm and into the bones of your forearm and blossoms behind your eyes. For a split second you perceive the basin the way it perceives the basin. You see the catwalks not as metal but as veins. You see the dripping water not as drops but as data points, each one a packet of information falling from the membrane-sky. You see the other Driftmen moving through the dark, their pressure-signatures spread out across the basin like constellations, and you understand that they're not individuals—they're peaks in a field, concentrations in a solution, and the solution is the computation and the computation is the meaning and the meaning is everything.

Then the contact breaks.

The young one pulls back. Its hand retreats, the fingers twitching, and you realize it's startled. You startled it. Your data was too much—too dense, too alien, too full of light and dryness and the memory of a world where air doesn't weigh anything. It wobbles. Its form destabilizes for a moment, the filaments loosening, the rust flaking. Then it pulls itself together, literally, the materials drawing back into a tighter configuration, and it turns and diffuses away, back toward the ticking structure, back toward the metronomic comfort of the metal's steady cooling.

You're alone again. Sort of. You're never really alone here, you're realizing. The presences are constant. The slow fauna at your ankles. The distant movement in the pools. The heartbeat beneath the grating, low and patient and vast. The hum, which has shifted slightly since you touched the young one's hand. You can hear the change. A new overtone. A harmonic that wasn't there before, ringing faintly in the higher registers. You've been incorporated. Just a little. Just a trace.

You look down at your palm.

A smear of rust. A few black filaments, already beginning to integrate with the creases of your skin. A bead of condensation that doesn't evaporate, that just sits there, round and perfect and thick with minerals. You don't wipe it off. The thought of wiping it off feels wrong. It feels like erasing data.

You look up. The Anchor is visible—perceptible—across the basin. It's the first time you've really let yourself notice it directly. It's enormous. The scale is impossible, the way everything here is impossible, a violation of perspective that makes your hindbrain itch. It should be far away—you can sense the distance, the catwalks that would have to be crossed, the shallow pools that would have to be navigated—but it's also right there. Present. Immediate. Its surface is a landscape. Crystalline formations jut out at angles that suggest growth rather than construction. The layers of its accumulated mass are visible as strata, a geological record of the basin's history. You can see the different materials, the different eras: a band of copper-rich ore from some early period, a seam of black vegetation from a time when the flora was more aggressive, a crust of mineral deposits that marks some ancient shift in the condensation cycle.

The heartbeat is coming from beneath it. That much is clear now. Not the Anchor's heartbeat—the Anchor *is* the heartbeat, or rather the heartbeat is the sound of the Anchor processing, the vast slow churn of data that passes through its accumulated layers. It's the CPU of this place. The central node. The dreamer dreaming the dream of stabilized reality.

And as you perceive it, it perceives you.

The attention is unlike the young one's. The young one's attention was a tap on the shoulder, a tentative query, a child tugging at a sleeve. The Anchor's attention is a weather system. It's the weight of the air itself, suddenly focused, suddenly interested. The pressure in your chest intensifies until your ribs ache with it. The hum drops a full octave, cycling down into a register you feel in your femurs, in your jaw, in the soft tissue of your throat. The water in the shallow pools ripples—all of them, simultaneously, a synchronized disturbance that spreads outward from the Anchor's base and reaches you a full thirty seconds later, a slow-motion shockwave.

It's asking you something.

Not *what are you*, like the young one asked. It already knows what you are. It probably knew the moment you arrived, the moment the door opened and let in a gust of dry, cool air, the moment you stepped onto the grating and brought your foreign data into the computation. No—it's asking you something deeper. Something that doesn't have words. Something that resolves, slowly, over the course of several cycles of the hum, into a question that feels like this:

*Will you stay?*

The question hangs in the warm wet dark. The drip falls from the membrane overhead, a droplet deciding to fall, a slow gradient of intention. The fauna brush against your ankles, soft and damp and unhurried. The ticking metal counts its steady tempo. The young one, still quivering slightly from your touch, watches from the edge of perception. The door breathes behind you, exhaling its cooler, drier air, its ozone and fluorescent ghosts.

You look at your palm again. The rust is spreading. The filaments have woven themselves into the whorls of your skin. The bead of condensation hasn't moved. It catches something—not light, light isn't a relevant concept—but something. A faint internal shimmer. A suggestion of depth. A miniature pool with no memory of sunlight, held in the cup of your hand.

You realize you're smiling.

It's the first time you've smiled since you arrived, and the expression feels strange on your face—too sharp, too muscular, a gesture from a world where things move fast and air doesn't weigh anything. But it's genuine. It's an answer. Not the answer to the Anchor's question, which you're not ready to give. But an answer to your own question, the one you've been asking since you stepped through the door and into the weight of the dark.

*Where would you find yourself in this world?*

Here. On the perforated steel. In the thick warm air. With the hum in your bones and the rust in your skin and the slow fauna at your feet. Not a Driftman yet. Not part of the computation in the way the others are. But permeable. Becoming. A borderland entity, a transitional form, standing at the threshold between the world you came from and the world that's slowly, patiently, welcoming you in.

*Who or what would you be?*

You don't have a name for it yet. That's fine. The Driftmen don't use names. You'll accumulate one eventually, the way they accumulate everything—slowly, bead by bead, until suddenly there's enough to fall. For now, you're the visitor. The anomaly. The stranger who touched the young one's hand and didn't flinch. The thing that stands still for longer and longer intervals, breathing in time with the hum, feeling its edges begin to soften.

You turn. Not toward the door—you're not ready to leave, and anyway, leaving isn't a binary state anymore, it's a matter of degree, of accumulation, of how much of this place you're willing to carry back with you. You turn toward the ticking structure, toward the young one, who's still watching, still wobbling, still processing the data you shared. You take a step. It takes a long time. The droplet that started falling when you made the decision has almost reached the ground.

You take another step. And another. And somewhere deep below the grating, the heartbeat continues, patient and implacable, waiting to see what you'll become.
