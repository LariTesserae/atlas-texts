Aldehyde.

She called herself that once, in a language she no longer has the mouth to speak. A private joke, a thing to hold onto when holding still seemed important. That was early, before the first molt, when she still counted things—days, breaths, the number of sphincter-valves she passed through. Counting was a way of keeping the world outside herself. She stopped counting when the numbers lost their edges, when "seven" and "eighty" and "two hundred" began to feel like the same word, a single sound for *this has gone on too long*.

She doesn't think that anymore. It hasn't gone on too long. It has simply gone on, which is what things do here. The body does not measure time. The body does not measure anything. It maintains gradients, balances equations, opens and closes a trillion valves in a trillion chambers, and none of it requires a count. She learned that eventually. She learned to let the past be something that happened to a stranger, a soft animal with thin skin and a desperate need for water, an animal that screamed when the ground first closed over its foot. She doesn't scream now. She doesn't have the larynx for it. Her voicebox has restructured itself into something like a syrinx, a dual-chambered resonator that can produce two tones at once, one for the infrasound hum and one for the higher frequencies the body uses for local signaling—the creak of settling mass, the hiss of gas exchange, the particular vibration that means *this passage is closing, move left*.

She moves left. The drumskin floor gives slightly, returning its dull resonance, and she reads the echo for information the way she once read faces: a density here, a hollow there, a heat-signature that suggests a deep-gut vent opening below. The body is always shifting. She has learned to shift with it, to anticipate its rhythms, to know that a chamber she slept in three breaths ago is now a pit of enzymatic gel and the passage she needs will have migrated twenty meters upward, extruded through the bioconcrete like a slow-motion bubble rising through resin.

The stranger is behind her. She can feel him without turning. His footsteps are wrong, each one a small violence, a pressure the floor doesn't recognize. The body is curious about him, the way it was curious about her once, a curiosity with no malice but no gentleness either. He is a foreign object, a seed that has not decided whether to germinate. She could tell the body to reject him. She has that authority now, a thing she earned through years of careful symbiosis, through the slow process of making herself useful. A particular pheromone signature, a particular thermal pattern on her webbing, and the walls would close, the enzymatic gel would rise, the body would take him apart molecule by molecule and incorporate him into its endless architecture. He would become a wall. A floor. A memory of warmth.

She doesn't give the signal. She doesn't know why. Maybe it's the face. He still has a face, a real one, with eyes that reflect light and a mouth that can make expressions. She remembers expressions. She remembers the feeling of her own face doing them, the specific musculature of a smile, the furrow of a brow. Her face doesn't do that anymore. The muscles have been repurposed, reattached to the nostril-slits and the nictitating membrane, and the skin has thickened into a sensory surface that reads humidity and pheromone gradients. She could not smile at him if she wanted to. So she doesn't want to. That's the logic she's built, a chain of if-thens that keeps her from longing for things the body can't provide.

She leads him deeper.

The cathedral space opens around them, and she feels the old awe try to rise up and finds it has nowhere to go. She's been here too many times. The majesty is just information now: this chamber is a digestion node, its walls lined with chemosynthetic bacterial mats that glow amber from the metabolic heat, its ceiling fifty meters high and growing higher as the body adds rings. The vascular bundles are as thick as her torso, pulsing with that peristaltic rhythm, moving the not-sap toward the deep-gut oxidation zones. She knows this chamber the way she once knew her childhood bedroom, except her childhood bedroom didn't rearrange itself every night. This chamber will be different tomorrow. It will be larger, or smaller, or it will have divided into three smaller chambers, or it will have sealed itself entirely and become a pressure vessel for some new industrial process the body is inventing in its slow, mindless way. She will have to find a new route. She always does.

The stranger's breathing is labored. She can hear it, the ragged human rhythm of oxygen-in, carbon-dioxide-out, a pattern the body recognizes as primitive and wasteful. She breathes differently now. Her lungs have septated, dividing into multiple chambers that extract gases in sequence, a countercurrent exchange system that lets her pull oxygen from air that would suffocate a human. She also absorbs gases through her skin now, through the waxy cuticle that isn't quite plant and isn't quite animal. The body's atmosphere is 3% oxygen and 15% carbon dioxide and a complex cocktail of volatiles—aldehydes, ketones, the chemical semaphore of the spores—and she breathes all of it, feeds on the carbon, exhales the oxygen back into the system in a micro-loop the body finds acceptable. She is not a drain. She is a node.

She wonders if the stranger will become a node too, or if he will become something else. The body has uses for everything, but not all uses are survivable. She survived because she learned quickly. She learned to be dry in a world that hoarded moisture. She learned to read the thermal gradients and the infrasound hum and the chemical signatures in the air that meant *this way is safe* or *this way leads to a digestion pit*. She learned to eat without being seen eating, to drink the hemolymph in secret, to thank the body with a touch of her webbed hand against a wall, a thermal pulse that the body read as cooperation. She learned to be useful.

Does she remember teaching herself? Not clearly. The memories are submerged now, pressed down under layers of adaptation the way the original bedrock is pressed down under the bioconcrete. But she remembers moments. She remembers the first time she drank from a vascular bundle, the shame of it, the animal hunger overriding the human disgust. She remembers the first time she felt the pulse in the floor synchronize with her own pulse, a moment of terrifying intimacy, like hearing a second heartbeat inside her own chest. She remembers the first molt—the day her skin split along her spine and the new skin underneath was greenish and tough and slightly photosynthetic, a dermis that could do chlorophyll. She had screamed then, or tried to, but the new throat-structures weren't finished forming and the sound came out as a low, granular tone that the body answered with a sympathetic vibration, a resonance in the nearest wall that meant, she now knows, *acknowledged*.

She doesn't know if she chose this. The choice was never offered as a single moment, a yes-or-no, a door you walked through. It was a thousand small surrenders, each one seeming necessary at the time, each one a survival strategy that became a physiology. She didn't decide to grow cellulose hair. She didn't decide to develop gripping-setae on her fingers. She didn't decide to let her eyes expand until they dominated her face, gathering every photon the amber glow would yield. Those things happened because she survived long enough for the body to notice her, and once the body noticed her, it began to optimize her. She was a raw node in the network, unconfigured, full of unused potential. The body is an engineer. It has no mind, but it has process, and the process is relentless. It found her inefficiencies and corrected them. It found her weaknesses and replaced them. It found her limits and dissolved them, one by one, until the woman she was became the thing she is, a mobile component of a system that has no other mobile components, a wandering organ in a body that claims everything.

She stops. The chamber ahead is wrong.

She reads the wrongness in the thermal gradient—a cold spot where there should be warmth, a heat-column rising from a wall that should be dormant. The body doesn't make cold spots. The body is a heat-engine, a thermogenic architecture that maintains a steady 2.9 to 3.0 on whatever scale she once used to measure temperature. A cold spot is an insult. A cold spot is a wound.

She raises her hand, the webbing flared to read the air. The cold spot is localized, about two meters across, and it's spreading slowly. She can feel the body's response beginning—the pulse in the floor quickening, the vascular bundles constricting to divert not-sap toward the affected area, the bioluminescence brightening as the bacterial mats ramp up their metabolism. The body is fighting something. The body is trying to heal.

She hasn't encountered a wound in a long time. The last one was early, before she understood, when she tried to cut a piece of the wall for shelter and the wall bled hemolymph that burned her skin and the body's immune response sealed the cut in hours, leaving a scar of denser cellulose. She learned then not to damage the body. The body is not invulnerable, but it repairs itself with a speed and thoroughness that makes injury pointless. For something to create a cold spot, a spreading cold spot—

She remembers cold. She remembers the concept. It was a thing outside the body, a thing the body had eliminated from its internal processes. The body is heat. The body is the slow oxidation of ancient growth, the geological digestion of mineral veins, the friction of root against root in the deep-gut zones. Cold is the absence of process. Cold is death, or something close enough that the body doesn't distinguish.

She moves toward the cold spot, her bare feet reading the floor's increasing distress. The drumskin resonance is changing, becoming stiffer, less yielding. The body is tensing around the wound the way a muscle tenses around a splinter. She should turn back. She should take the stranger and find another route, let the body handle its own healing. But the cold spot is spreading, and the body's response isn't stopping it, and that means something is wrong in a way she hasn't encountered before. She is the body's only mobile component. She is the wandering macrophage. This is what she's for, the function she's been shaped to perform, the reason the body tolerates her heterotrophy. She investigates.

The passage narrows, a sphincter-valve half-closed by inflammation, the bioconcrete around it swollen and fever-hot. She presses through, her gripping-setae finding purchase on the slick surface, and the stranger follows because he has no choice. The cold spot is in a small chamber, a node she recognizes as a chemical transformation site—the walls lined with darker nodules, the bulges the size of houses where something is being synthesized. One of the nodules is cracked open, a fissure running from floor to ceiling, and from the fissure a slow, viscous fluid is oozing. Not hemolymph. Something else, something pale and faintly luminous, a bioluminescent white instead of the body's amber. It is cold. The fluid is cold, actively cold, absorbing heat from the air, creating the thermal sink she sensed from a distance. As she watches, a droplet of the fluid falls to the floor and the bioconcrete hisses, sizzles, blackens, dies. The body recoils, the floor retracting in a slow peristalsis, trying to pull away from the contamination.

She doesn't know what she's looking at. The body doesn't produce cold. The body doesn't produce white bioluminescence. This is foreign. This is an infection, or a parasite, or something that found a niche in the body against all probability, the way she found a niche, the way the stranger might find a niche if he adapts fast enough. Something that moves in the body's interstices and feeds on its heat, leeching the warmth that is the body's primary currency, replacing it with a cold that kills the tissue.

The fissure widens slightly, and she sees movement inside. A shape, or the suggestion of a shape—something pale, something multi-armed, something that unfurls in the cold fluid like a deep-sea creature adjusting to pressure. It doesn't have eyes, or if it does, it doesn't have them in the usual places. Its surface is studded with what might be sensory nodes, small darker spots that orient toward her as she approaches. It knows she's there. It knows she's heat, a concentrated warmth-source, a potential meal. The arms extend, slow and deliberate, not threatening but curious, tasting the thermal gradient she makes in the air.

She feels the stranger's hand on her shoulder, a human gesture, a warning. His skin is warm, too warm, a waste-heat she can feel radiating from him. The cold-thing's arms orient toward him instead, drawn by the stronger signal, the brighter metabolic flame. She should be relieved—better him than her—but instead she feels the body's distress sharpening into something almost like an emotion, a pressure in her chest that isn't her own heartbeat, a vibration in her throat that isn't her own voice. The body is afraid. The body is in pain. The body is asking her, the only neuron it has, to do something.

She doesn't have the physiology for a sigh anymore, but she makes a sound that serves the same purpose, a low release of pressure through her nasal slits. Then she steps forward, her webbed hand flared to radiate heat in a specific pattern, the pattern that means *I am here to repair*. The cold-thing's arms pause. It doesn't understand the signal—why would it, it's not of the body—but it understands heat, and she is giving it a choice. Come toward her, the larger source, the one that isn't retreating. Leave the stranger. Leave the body's wounded tissue. Come see what this strange thermal node has to offer.

She doesn't know what she'll do when it reaches her. She doesn't have a plan. She has a function, and the function is to investigate, and the investigation has become a confrontation, and the confrontation is about to become something else, something the body hasn't prepared her for, something she'll have to improvise. The cold-fluid drips onto the floor and the floor blackens and the body recoils and the cold-thing's arms reach out, slow, curious, hungry for warmth.

She holds her ground. Her skin is photosynthetic. Her lungs are septated. Her hair is cellulose rope, moving with the body's pulse. She is Aldehyde, the only animal, the wandering node, the body's one experiment in mobility. She has survived everything the body has thrown at her—the resorptions, the enzymatic pits, the new-grown walls that sealed around her while she slept. She will survive this too.

Or she won't, and the body will repurpose her molecules, and that's its own kind of survival.

Behind her, the stranger makes a human sound, a gasp or a word, and she raises her hand to silence him without looking back. The cold is reaching her now, a chill that cuts through her waxy cuticle, and the body's pulse is a war drum in the floor, and the amber glow is brightening to a furious gold. The body is fighting. She is the body's fighter. The cold-thing arms close around her heat, and she lets them.
