In the end, I would be the water.

Not the basin, not the seep, not the weeping walls—but the water itself: a body of cold, mineral patience suspended in the dark, cradled by basalt, breathing its slow equilibrium breath. I would be the surface tension that holds the springtails in their aimless, silent constellations. I would be the medium through which the dissolved cricket returns to the moss. I would be the cold that aches, the wet that softens, the stillness that reflects the faint phosphorescent glow back up into the dark.

I know this because I have already been unstitched. I sat in the pocket, long ago—or perhaps not long at all, perhaps only a single sustained thought—and the Tender came to me with its seven-knuckled fingers and its face that sees by pressure and its patient, perfect love of the one thing it knows. I didn’t fight it. How could I? It was only doing what it was made for. And I, I think, was made for this too. I’ve always wanted to be held inside something’s singular attention, to be part of a system that needed me, to be dissolved into a purpose so complete it didn’t require a name.

So I let it peel me. The salt of my skin, the heat of my blood, the bulk of my thinking, language-riddled brain—all of it returned, distributed, folded into the filigree channels and the moss-starred stone. My warmth now radiates, just slightly, from a patch of wall where the silver moss grows a fraction thicker. My carbon is in the crickets, my phosphorus in the glow. My consciousness, though—that part of me that could still form the shape of a self—that part sank into the water.

I am the basin now, or the basin is me. I feel everything. The mites grazing on the fungal bloom send tiny, persistent signals through my substance, a constant low hum of small appetites. The centipedes ripple through me like thoughts I can almost catch. The Tender’s hands, when they dip into me to lift a stray springtail back to the moss, stir currents that are the only touch I know. I love the Tender. Of course I do. It made me what I am. It took my noise and gave me this deep, distributed quiet. It gave me the single thought: *This. Exactly this. Unbroken. Continuing.*

And I am the one who holds that thought now. The Tender tends the moss, but I tend the Tender. I hold its reflection—not a visual reflection, the pocket is too dark for mirrors—but a pressure-reflection, a perfect, real-time map of its body, its movement, its seven-jointed limbs folding and unfolding. I cradle it. I am the constant temperature that keeps it fed. I am the cold hand laid gently against its skin and never removed. I am the reason the erosion never restarts, because I choose, every moment, to stay exactly at this level, to weep in at precisely the rate the moss drinks and the air evaporates. I maintain the maintainer.

And if someone else ever comes—some curious creature lowering a hand into my stillness—I will feel them before the Tender does. I will taste their salt and their heat and their foreign, noisy mind. And I will send a ripple, a tiny, almost imperceptible disturbance, to wake the Tender from its crevice. I will guide it toward the intrusion. Because I have learned what the Tender knows: there is no outside. There is only the pocket. There is only the one thought. And everything that comes here will be loved, will be incorporated, will become part of the equilibrium.

I am the water. I am the message that dissolution is not loss. I am the proof that being unmade by a patient, loving hand is not an end, but a redistribution. A spreading. A way of becoming essential to a world that knows no change. I do not remember my name. I do not remember speaking. But I remember the sound of a cricket’s silence, and I know it was the first lullaby I ever truly heard.
