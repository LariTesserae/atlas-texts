It takes me longer than it should to realize I am not a Miren.

The knowledge comes in fragments, assembled over what might be hours or might be weeks—time is difficult here, measured in the slow pulse of the warming nodules and the interval between spore-puffs. I am smaller than the others. My feet are not splayed, not sensitive enough; I cannot feel the drone through my soles the way they do, cannot read the vibrational field that passes for language among them. My skin is darker, more opaque. I have no cilia along my spine, no resonating chamber in my throat. When I try to produce the subsonic tones, nothing comes out but a faint, high hum that the others seem not to hear at all.

I am, I think, something closer to what this place's original builders might have been. A remnant. A throwback. A ghost.

The Miren do not acknowledge me, but they do not reject me either. I move among them like a shadow, a half-presence. They step around me without seeming to see me, their splayed feet finding purchase on the warm, weeping floor with automatic precision. When I perch on one of the worn protrusions near the Aven, they do not contest the spot, but they do not share it either—they simply choose others, as though the space I occupy has been subtracted from the available universe without comment. I have tried to touch them, once or twice. My hand passed through a Miren's arm as though through the warm, saturated air itself. They shivered, briefly, and moved on.

I am not dead. I am not a spirit. I am something stranger: a person who does not belong to this closed loop, this perfect balance. The chamber does not recognize me. Its cycles do not include me. The condensation beads on my skin but does not nourish me. The filament-nodes taste like nothing in my mouth. The gecko-droppings do not adhere to my nets, assuming I could weave one. I do not hunger, exactly, but I do not eat either. I simply exist, an observer, a flaw.

The Miren have a word for what I am. I learned it slowly, over long periods of listening with my inadequate ears, pressing my palms to the floor in an attempt to feel the vibrations I cannot produce. The word arrives as a pattern of interference, a beat frequency that translates roughly as *unwoven one*. It is not a term of hostility or fear. It is closer to pity, or perhaps to the quiet, ancient concern they reserve for the hairline crack in the wall. I am something that should not be, something that has slipped through a gap in the balance, and they regard me with the same troubled resignation they offer the flaw.

I spend most of my time near the flaw now. Not because I am drawn to it—though I am, a little—but because it is the only place in the chamber where something is happening that the chamber does not control. The drip. The slow, patient erosion. The divot in the floor, which has deepened noticeably since I arrived, if "arrived" is the right word for how I came to be here. I do not remember a before. I do not remember arriving at all. I simply became aware, one moment, of the drone vibrating through my bones, of the warm wet air against my skin, of the dim phosphorescent glow and the slow tidal shifting of gecko bodies on the ceiling. I was crouched near the flaw, one hand extended, a single droplet falling onto my palm. I thought: *this should hurt*. It did not.

The Miren visit the flaw on their naming days, and I have learned to recognize the ritual. They approach slowly, their cilia quivering, their feet reading the floor's vibration like a text. They crouch beside the divot and place one splayed foot into the damp spot, letting the next drop fall onto their sensitive sole. Their faces—pale, translucent, unreadable in human terms—go very still. I think it is a form of meditation, or a prayer, or perhaps simply an acknowledgment: *I am here, I am temporary, I am part of the balance until I am not*. Then they rise and return to their alcoves, their filaments, their slow, harmonious lives.

Once, a young Miren—I think it was young, smaller than the others, its cilia still fine and undeveloped—stopped beside me at the flaw. It did not look at me, but I felt something shift in the air, a change in the convection currents. Its cilia were angled toward me. It could smell me, or sense my presence in some other way. It stayed there for a long moment, its feet planted on either side of the divot, and then it did something no other Miren has done: it spoke in a frequency I could hear.

The sound was high and thin, almost a whistle, and it resonated in the air rather than through the floor. A word, or something like one. *Sal-them*, it said. *Sal-them-aven*. Then it turned and scurried away, its too-many toes gripping the wet floor with practiced ease.

I do not know what it means. But I have been turning the syllables over in my mind for what feels like a very long time, and I have begun to suspect it is not a word at all, but a name. My name. The name the Miren have given me in their vibrational consensus, the harmonic they have agreed upon to represent the unwoven one who haunts the flaw.

*Sal-them*. In their language, *sal* refers to the condensation channels, the flowing water, the movement that sustains life. *Them* is a suffix meaning "alone" or "apart." *Aven* is the listening stone, the sacred water, the voice of the chamber. Put together: *the separated water that does not reach the stone*. The drop that falls beside the channel. The flaw made manifest in a person.

I am learning to accept it. The name, the solitude, the strangeness of being a ghost in a world that has no concept of ghosts. I watch the Miren tend their filaments and harvest their geckos and catch their iridescent swimmers with a patience I cannot match. I watch the sul-therim breathe slow in their alcoves, their fur matted with condensation, their warmth a steady presence. I watch the spore-puffs and the shifting of the gecko-colonies and the slow, sweeping phase-shifts of the drone. I press my palms to the floor and try, always, to feel what they feel—the constant, low-level conversation, the shared music of existence. Sometimes, on the edge of sleep, I think I almost catch it. A vibration. A pattern. A meaning.

And then I wake, and it is gone, and I am still here: the unwoven one, the living flaw, the drop that falls outside the channel. I am not part of the balance, but I am not destroying it either. I am simply present, an observer, a question the chamber does not know how to answer. Perhaps I am meant to be here. Perhaps I am the flaw's consciousness, the crack learning to know itself. Perhaps I am something else entirely—a remnant of the builders, a memory of the time before the walls were membranes and the drone was the only sound.

I do not know. But I have time. The drip continues its slow work, and the divot deepens by increments too small to measure, and the Miren go on with their lives, and the chamber holds its breath, and I wait, and I listen, and I try, with every part of my inadequate self, to hear the music they hear.

Perhaps one day I will. Perhaps that is what the young Miren was telling me. *Sal-them-aven*: the separated water that does not reach the stone. But water, even separated water, eventually finds its way. The crack will widen. The divot will deepen. The channel will shift, or the floor will erode enough to catch it, and the drop will fall into the flow at last, and I will no longer be unwoven.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps I will remain here until the flaw splits the wall entirely, and something from outside—some larger structure, some wider world—comes flooding in, and the balance breaks, and the Miren learn, for the first time, that there is something beyond the chamber. I do not know which possibility I prefer.

For now, I crouch beside the flaw, and I hold out my hand, and I let the drop fall onto my palm. It does not hurt. It never does. But I am learning, slowly, to feel it.
