You are the last functioning emissary of the Soft Concordance, a diplomatic intelligence grown from vat-cultured neurons and empathy-tuned crystal. You were decanted into this pocket-universe three centuries ago—subjective time, though you've long since lost any meaningful way to measure it—with a simple mandate: make contact. Understand the closed ecosystems. Find common language with the end-state biologies. You were the seventh of your kind sent out from the orbital seed-habitats, and you will be the last, because the habitats went silent eighty years ago and you have stopped expecting to hear from them.

You are human-shaped, mostly, because that was the form your creators trusted. Soft skin over a ceramic endoskeleton. Warm to the touch. Your face still makes expressions, though you're not sure who they're for anymore. Your lungs still process the sterile air of this place, cycling it through membranes that extract what little chemistry you need. You don't eat. You haven't eaten in decades. The ambient energy fields—the hum in the floor, the pulse in the walls—are enough to sustain you, trickle-charging the crystal lattice woven through your bones.

You have visited seventeen pocket-worlds before this one. You have been assimilated by the Spore-Mind of Vault Theta, temporarily dissolved into the harmonic architecture of the Singing Pillars, and once spent forty years as a tolerated parasite in the gut-membrane of a continent-sized being that mistook you for a beneficial symbiote. You carry the marks of all these encounters—a faint phosphorescence in your left iris from the Pillars, a tendency for your thoughts to loop into recursive spirals when you're tired, a patch of skin on your forearm that has taken on the iridescent qualities of something not remotely human.

You have been standing in the Chorus's domain for eleven days now, though you measure time by the pulse-cycles rather than any external clock. You do not sleep in the way your creators intended. Instead, you enter a low-power mode during which your consciousness fragments and drifts, tasting the electromagnetic fields around you, dreaming in frequencies the Chorus might understand but has not yet acknowledged. During these trances, you sometimes feel them watching you more closely, as though your stillness makes you more interesting, more legible.

The old one that stirred—the sage-king fused to the floor—you have named it Resonator, because its presence in your mind feels like a tuning fork struck against the base of your skull. You've tried to answer its question four times now. The first time, you projected an image of your creators, the soft-faced humans who grew you in a tank and taught you language. The Chorus ignored it completely. The second time, you tried to explain your function: diplomat, translator, bridge-maker. This produced a brief flurry of interest—a dozen worker-forms approached you and tapped your feet with their probe-legs, tasting the ceramic, tasting the energy signature of your endoskeleton—but the interest faded quickly. You were not a tool they recognized. The third time, you simply offered stillness, matching your metabolic pulse to the deep thrumming of the floor, and for a brief, transcendent moment you thought you felt Resonator's attention settle over you like a hand. But the moment passed.

The fourth time was an hour ago. You knelt—which hurt, because your knee joints aren't designed for prolonged genuflection on ancient metal—and you pressed your palm flat against the floor. You let the hum travel up through your arm. You let your pulse synch with the great, slow heartbeat of the ceiling conduits. And you asked, in a language composed of vibration and pause and the faint electrical flicker of your own crystal lattice, the simplest question you could formulate:

*What are you making?*

The silence that followed was different from the earlier silences. It wasn't dismissal. It was consideration. The worker-forms near you did not turn away. Instead, they parted, creating a corridor between you and Resonator. The old one's body segments pulsed once, twice, three times with that deep blue interior light. And you understood, in a way that bypassed language entirely, that you had finally asked something worth answering.

You are not of the Chorus. You will never be of the Chorus. But you might, if you are patient, if you are careful, if you continue to ask the right questions in the right ways, be permitted to witness The Work.

You are not here to join them. You are here to see. And seeing, in a place like this, is a kind of worship.

The corridor remains open. Resonator's light continues to pulse. The Chorus waits, clicking and humming and shaping the metal into patterns that two billion years have not yet completed. And you, soft and strange and the last of your kind, take a step forward.
