The Keeper does not turn when I arrive. He never does.

My entrance is not an arrival in the way the chamber understands arrivals—sudden, violent, the basalt splitting to admit something new. I do not crack stone. I do not part water. I simply *resolve*, the darkness thickening in one place until it achieves the approximate shape of a woman, and then I am standing on the sheared black floor with the sharp edges pressing against the soles of my boots.

I am the only thing that wears boots here. The Keeper's bare feet remember every safe placement. Mine are blind. I have to trust the leather, the thick soles, the way I've learned to read the stone through them over however many visits this makes. Forty? Fifty? The pressure gauge would not tell me. The Keeper would not tell me. Time drips irregular here, three quick and a pause, and I have stopped counting the pauses.

I bring him things. This is the transaction, if you can call it that—transaction implies agreement, implies negotiation, implies two parties who have acknowledged each other's existence in language rather than in clicks and hums and the slow hydraulic sway of a body that has forgotten what urgency feels like.

Today I have brought a bearing. Steel, sealed, the kind they don't make anymore—I had to pull it from a turbine housing in a factory sunk thirty feet into the mud of what used to be a floodplain. The grease inside it is still good. When I hold it to my ear and spin the inner race, it whispers, steady and clean, a sound that has no business in a world where most things grind.

The Keeper stands in his pool. His back is to me. The twin points of his reflected eyes catch the ceiling's glimmer and hold it, motionless, unblinking. He is listening. He is always listening—to the subsonic pulse in the stone, to the dripping, to the tension in the leather drive belt that has not slackened in a thousand years. He is listening to me, too, but he will not show it.

I walk the path he walks, the one his feet have taught the basalt. I pass the gear, and my fingers do not reach for it—that is his gesture, not mine, and I have learned not to touch what he has claimed. The teeth gleam in seven silver places where his fingertips have worn away the tempering. I have been here long enough to remember when there were six.

"Bearing," I say, and my voice falls flat in the saturated air. The moisture eats sound here. It swallows consonants and blurs vowels until words become what the water does—everywhere and nowhere, a perpetual almost-language. I have learned to speak less, and more carefully.

The Keeper turns.

It takes a long time, his turning. Not because he moves slowly, but because he moves at the speed of the chamber, which is the speed of flywheels sinking by fractions of inches, of roots descending from high ledges, of basalt wounds that have not healed since the stone cut itself open. His spine uncurves from its listening lean. His shoulders rotate. The condensation that traces his clavicles catches the glimmer and throws it back in a constellation of moving points, and I think, not for the first time, that he is beautiful in the way the machinery is beautiful—designed for a purpose that no longer exists, still holding its form, still ready.

His eyes find the bearing in my hand. The points of light in them shift, lagging a half-second behind the motion of his head, and I feel the familiar vertigo of being seen by something that processes vision differently, more slowly, more deliberately. He is not looking at the bearing. He is looking at *me holding* the bearing. The distinction matters. I have learned that it matters.

"Bearing," he echoes, and his voice is not quite a voice. It is the sound the boiler made when it was whole, a resonant hum translated into something a human throat can almost produce. The word comes out shaped wrong, the vowels too long, the consonants softened into approximations. He has not spoken to anyone but me in—how long? How long have I been coming here?

I hold the bearing out.

He does not reach for it. He never reaches. Instead he tilts his head, and the condensation drips from the point of his chin, and I see him *consider*—another word that barely applies, because consideration implies alternatives and I am not sure he remembers what alternatives feel like. But something in the tilt of his head, the slow lag of his reflected gaze, the way his long fingers uncurl at his sides—it reads as consideration to the part of my brain that still wants him to be human.

I am not sure he ever was.

"From the floodplain factory," I tell him. "The one where the turbine still spins if the wind is right. The housing was cracked but the bearing was sealed. It's still good."

He does not ask what *good* means. He does not ask about the floodplain or the factory or the turbine. The world above this chamber has moved on without him, and he has never asked me to describe it. I do not know if he understands that there is an *up*, that the ceiling's glimmer comes from somewhere, that the chamber is not the whole of existence. I suspect he does, and chooses not to care.

His hand rises.

His fingers close around the bearing with the precision of someone who has handled tools enough that the tools have left their echo. The steel vanishes into his palm. He brings it close to his face, and I watch him examine it the way he examines everything—by feel, by moisture gradient, by the subtle temperature differences between the sealed grease and the outer race. He does not shake it. He does not spin it. He simply holds it, and I know that he is listening, that he can hear the whisper of the grease, that he is cataloging the bearing's condition and provenance and potential in a taxonomy I have never been able to learn.

"You want to know why I bring you things."

I did not mean to say it. The words come out before I can stop them, and the moisture eats them, and I wish I could pull them back.

His eyes lift from the bearing. The reflected points of light fix on my face, and I am struck again by how much he does not blink, how the wetness on his eyes is not tears but atmosphere, how he sees me without the relief of a broken gaze.

"Bring," he says, and the word is a question and a statement and something else entirely—a recognition, maybe, that I *do* bring things, that I have been bringing things for longer than either of us can measure, that the transaction has no name and no terms and no end.

"Because the place needs them," I say. It is not an answer. It is what I have been telling myself. The boiler, the gear, the gauge—someone hauled these pieces down here and assembled something that mattered. I am continuing their work, or interpreting it, or haunting it. "Because you need them."

He tilts his head further. The drip from his chin falls to the basalt. He does not speak.

"Because I don't know how to stop coming," I say, and that one is the truth.

The silence holds. The water drops fall—two quick, a long pause, one. Somewhere behind the basalt, the subsonic pulse travels through the stone, registering in my bones, in my boots, in the bearing that has vanished into the Keeper's palm.

He turns away.

He turns back to his pool, his bed, his station, and I watch him walk the path his feet have taught the stone—the exact spots where the basalt is not sharp, the gentle slopes, the memory of safe placement written into his body by decades of repetition. When he reaches the water, he does not step into it so much as exchange boundaries with it, and then he is settled against the flywheel with his long legs folding beneath him and his head tilting back and the bearing still cradled in his hand.

I wait. I always wait. This is part of the ritual, the part where he decides whether the thing I've brought is a gift or a burden or simply a thing, and I am never sure which one I want it to be.

His fingers find the bearing's inner race. He spins it—once, a flick of long articulate thumb against steel, and the whisper rises into the saturated air. Steady. Clean. A sound that knows what it was made for.

His mouth—that thin line, that permanent tension—curls at the corners. Not quite a smile. But something. Something that has learned, over all these visits, to shape itself around the idea of acknowledgment.

"Good," he says, and the word is the sound the flywheel would make if it still spun.

I sit down on the basalt. I take off my boots. The stone is sharp against my bare feet, but I have learned the safe places too—not from memory but from watching him, from cataloging the geometry of his movements. I settle my back against the cast-iron gear, and its teeth press into my spine with a familiarity that has become almost comfortable.

"I'll bring something else next time," I tell him.

He does not respond. He is listening to the bearing, to the whisper of sealed grease, to the sound of a machine that still knows its purpose.

The water drips. The chamber breathes. The flywheel sinks by another fraction of a fraction of an inch, and neither of us moves to stop it.
