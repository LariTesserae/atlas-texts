I am the silver-throated bird.

Not the one with the floating nest—that is another, a cousin who builds on open water. My nest is woven into the highest tussock of the oldest hummock, the one that rises just enough above the waterline to catch the first light. The sedges here have been growing from the same root-mass for longer than the deep pool has held its current shape. My mother built in this same clump, and her mother before her, and the line goes back until the hummock was barely a hummock at all, just a slight thickening of the mud that some ancestor decided was enough.

I know the creature in the deep pool. I have known it since I cracked my shell and opened my throat to the humid air. On that first morning, still wet with the albumen of my hatching, I felt the vibration of its long note rising through the water and the roots and the woven fibers of the nest. It was the first sound I ever heard that was not my own heartbeat. I fell silent—a hatchling silence, brief and involuntary—and then I answered. Not in mimicry; my throat cannot shape that depth. But I sang back, a silver thread of sound across the water, and I have been singing to it ever since.

Every dawn, I sing across the still water. My song carries in ways that the frogs' calls cannot—it skims the surface film, touches the mist that rises in the cool before the sun burns through, and reaches the deep pool as a ripple of frequencies that the old one feels rather than hears. I do not know if it listens. I do not know if listening is a category that applies. But I know that when I sing, the vibration of its body shifts by a fraction, a change so subtle that even I, with my sensitivity to the smallest tremors in the water, might be imagining it. I might. But I am not.

The frogs do not understand our relation. They sing to each other, to the night, to the warmth. Their calling is a constant, a background to which I have grown so accustomed that silence would feel like deafness. But they are not singing *to* anything. They are singing because that is what frogs do. I sing to the deep pool. I sing to the creature that has no name. And sometimes, in the long twilight when the old one sounds its single note, I feel that it is singing back—not to me specifically, but to the basin, to the water, to the slow compression of the mud beneath us all. And I am part of the basin. I am part of the water. My nest is woven from sedges that grew in silt that passed through the body of the creature in the deep pool. The fibers of my being are tangled with its being. When it exhales, I breathe in.

The wading birds do not understand this. They come and go, seasonally, following some internal map that is mostly hunger and the angle of the light. They stand motionless in the shallows, leg-lifting, foot-setting, and they catch the small fish that ripple the warm water. They are elegant, I admit. But they do not belong. They are visitors, and the marsh tolerates them the way it tolerates a passing cloud—without acknowledgment, without memory. I have seen the same heron return for five summers, and each time it arrives it startles at the shape of the old one's back, mistaking it for a new feature of the pool, a hummock it had forgotten. It does not remember. I remember everything.

The insect that spends seventeen years in the mud—I have seen three emergences. Three times, the air has filled with wings that catch the light and vanish and reappear, and each time I have watched from my tussock and thought: this will not happen again for longer than most creatures live. But I live. I am old now, old for a bird with a silver throat. My song has thinned a little, lost some of its upper frequencies, though the lower notes have deepened in ways that carry farther across the water. The frogs cannot tell the difference. The old one can. I know it can, because the first time I sang with my changed voice, the vibration in the water lasted a fraction longer than usual, as though the listening part of it was taking time to adjust, to absorb the new timbre into its slow accounting of the world.

The snake with the leaf-pattern scales—I have seen it hunt and be hunted, have seen its skin shed and left draped over a low stone like a ghost of itself. The floating nest of my cousin, woven so tightly it never sinks, has been torn apart by storms and rebuilt, torn and rebuilt, three times in my memory. The marsh does not hold still. It shifts and breathes and erodes and accumulates, and I have watched the pool I was born beside grow shallower by a finger's width, and a new pool open in a place that used to be dry enough for sedges, and the sedges migrate, and the frogs follow the water, and the water follows the slow subsidence of the land. Everything moves. Everything changes. Except the creature in the deep pool, which stays, and stays, and stays, and my singing to it every dawn, which also stays.

I do not know what I am waiting for. Not a mate—I had one, years ago, a bird with a throat so bright it seemed to draw the light down from the clouds. We built a nest together in the next hummock over, and she laid eggs, and the eggs hatched, and the young grew and sang their first tentative songs across the water to the deep pool. And then one autumn she rose on a thermal I could not feel and flew toward the horizon, and I did not follow. The young dispersed. Some stayed in the marsh; others I have not seen again. That is the way of things. I returned to my tussock, to my dawn singing, to the old one's low note in the twilight. Some of us are made for staying.

So I stay. I sing. The light thickens and thins, the frogs pulse and pause, the wading birds come and go, the seventeen-year insect is somewhere in its larval stage now, feeding in the mud, waiting for its single afternoon of flight. And I perch on the woven rim of my nest—the same nest, repaired a thousand times, the same sedges woven and rewoven, the same fibers that once passed through the body of the creature I sing to—and I send my silver thread of sound across the still water, and the still water carries it, and somewhere in the tea-colored depths the old one feels it, and shifts its weight by a fraction, and the algae on its back adjust to the new angle of light, and the frogs in the crevices of its skin rearrange their legs, and the day begins. Again. Always again. And nothing hurries. And I am not lonely. How could I be lonely, in a place where the water itself remembers me?
