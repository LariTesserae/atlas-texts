From the mist-heavy dark beneath the platform, something exhales. The sound is not loud—nothing here is loud—but it carries weight, a subsonic pressure that vibrates up through the interwoven branches, through the cable-net, through the soles of Kell’s feet and into the long bones of her legs. She stops mid-step on the center bridge. Below her, the ferns tremble. A cluster of moths detaches from a bridge cable and rises in a grey-brown cloud, their wing-claps a soft percussive rain against the stillness.

The fourth rib is singing.

No—not singing. Resonating. The spiral grooves carved into its stone are catching the vibration from below and translating it into something the air can carry. A note, low and constant, at the very edge of hearing. It blends with the birds’ clicking until it’s impossible to say where one ends and the other begins, but Kell knows the difference. The birds know the difference. Their rhythm has shifted again, faster now, a staccato counterpoint to the rib’s long drone.

On the platform, Orren has stopped working. His obsidian flake hangs loose in his hand. He is staring at the fourth rib—not at its base, where his extension creeps closer by the day, but at its upper reaches, where the stone disappears into the mist ceiling forty feet above. The condensation on the rib is trembling. The droplets are not running down the grooves in their usual slow rivulets; they are vibrating in place, catching the grey light like beads of mercury, a thousand tiny lenses all focused on nothing.

Kell sets her basket down on the bridge. This is not a decision; her hands simply release it, and the basket settles against the bamboo planks with a soft rustle of shed wings. She walks toward the platform. Her feet find the descent rope without her eyes, muscle-memory guiding her down the wet cable, past the moss-thick anchor points, past the sleeping mat where Callan is now standing, blanket pooled at his feet, his face tilted upward toward the triangular window of sky.

“You feel it,” he says. Not a question.

Kell nods. She lands on the platform beside him, her bare feet silent on the woven branches. The moss gives beneath her weight. She can feel the vibration more clearly here—the platform is directly connected to the cable-net, and the cable-net is anchored to all four ribs, and the fourth rib is singing, and the song is traveling through every fibre of the hamlet’s structure. The whole hanging world is a sounding board.

“Orren,” Callan calls, keeping his voice low. The birds’ clicking has become so dense it’s almost a single sound, a sustained shhhhhhhh of wing and beak and breath. “Orren, what did you touch?”

Orren doesn’t answer. He’s still staring upward. His hands are shaking—not with fear, Kell realizes, but with the vibration. It’s shaking all of them. She looks down at her own hands and sees the fine tremor in her fingers, the same tremor that’s running through the ferns, through the moss, through the water droplets on the stone. The whole garden is shivering in sympathy with the fourth rib’s song.

Then the mist below the platform begins to move.

It doesn’t rise—the mist never rises, it only billows in place, a permanent grey sea that marks the boundary of their world. But now it’s parting. Slowly, inexorably, a gap is opening in the grey, directly beneath the fourth rib. Kell can see the platform’s underside for the first time in her life: the woven branches, the anchor cables, the hanging roots of the ferns that have grown through the gaps and dangle into the void. And below that, darkness. Not the grey darkness of mist, but true darkness, a depth that has no visible bottom.

Something is rising through that darkness.

It is vast. It is so vast that Kell’s mind cannot assemble it into a shape at first—she sees fragments, a curve of something that might be bone, a surface that might be skin, a texture that might be scales the size of her spread hands. It is the colour of the stone ribs, wet-black, glazed with condensation, but the condensation on this thing is not water. It glows. Faintly, a bioluminescent blue-green that pulses in time with the fourth rib’s song.

The pearl-serpent has stopped hunting. It is coiled around a cable near the platform’s edge, its gold eyes fixed on the rising thing, and its body is perfectly still. The frogs have fallen silent again. Even the birds have quieted, their clicking reduced to a sparse few notes, a held breath of sound.

Kell steps forward. Callan grabs her wrist, but she shakes her head—not in defiance, but in recognition. She *knows* this thing. She has never seen it before, but she knows it the way she knows the bridges, through the body, through something older than memory. It is the source of the mineral tang in the air. It is the reason the stone ribs weep. It is what the garden has been processing all along, cycling through the ferns and the moss and the slugs and the moths and the birds and the people, a closed loop that was never closed at all, only waiting for this moment to complete its circuit.

The thing breaches the mist.

It is a head. A head so large that it fills the space beneath the platform, its snout alone longer than Kell’s entire body. Its eyes are closed. Its mouth is closed. It is sleeping—that much is clear—and its sleep is what vibrates the fourth rib, a deep tremor of breath and dream and slow, slow metabolism. The spiral grooves in the rib were not carved by human hands. They were worn into the stone by this creature’s song, over centuries, over millennia, a language written in resonance and received by the stone.

The garden built itself around this creature. The people built their hamlet above it. The obsidian tools, the carved hooks from “large animals”—there are no large animals in the hanging world. There is only this, and everything else is made from its shed parts, its cast-off scales, its fragments of bone retrieved from the mist by ancestors long dead who first learned to climb the ribs and build in the air.

Kell understands this all at once, not as information but as sensation, a knowing that floods up through her feet and into her chest and settles behind her eyes. She looks at Orren, who is still staring upward at the fourth rib, and sees that he knows it too. His compulsion to build the extension was not his own. It was the creature’s dream, transmitted through the stone, received by his sleeping mind. The platform is not being extended by human choice. It is being grown, like the moss, like the ferns, like Kell gathering wings for walls she will one day inherit. The creature is dreaming the garden into being. The garden is dreaming the people into purpose.

The creature exhales again, and the mist closes over it. The fourth rib’s song fades. The vibration in the platform subsides. The birds resume their clicking, the frogs their high thin song, the slugs their silver parades. The serpent strikes a frog—life, death, rhythm restored.

Kell walks back to the center bridge, picks up her basket of wings, and continues her gathering. Her hands are still now. The tremor has passed. But something has shifted in the architecture of her understanding, a new room added to the house of her world. She will not speak of it. No one in the hamlet will speak of it directly—they will refer to it sideways, in the placement of gifts, in the orientation of new construction, in the way Silla layers the wings a little thicker on the walls that face the fourth rib.

But from this day forward, when Kell gathers wings, she will pause at the edge of the platform and listen. And sometimes, in the dim hours before morning, when the mist is thick and the birds are quiet and the frogs have paused their singing, she will hear it: a breath from below, vast and slow, dreaming the garden into its next iteration. One wing at a time, one branch at a time, one child gathering warmth from the air while the world beneath her sleeps and stirs and sleeps again.
