I’m in a small hall on what I think is the fifth terrace, though the numbering has become unreliable. My back is against a wall of moss-aggregate blocks, and the damp has soaked through my clothes hours ago. I’ve been sitting here long enough that I can’t remember when I stopped shivering and started simply being cold, a cold that feels less like an invasion and more like an equilibrium. The mineral ridges on the wall behind me have shifted. I know this because I traced them when I first sat down—a habit, mapping a place with my fingertips—and now, without moving my shoulders from the stone, I reach back and find them in different positions. They’ve curled inward. They’re describing a shape that feels, under my fingers, like a shoulder blade.

The city is thinking about me.

I should be afraid, but fear requires a certain velocity of event, and nothing here moves faster than the seep of water down a wall. The pattern doesn’t startle. It doesn’t lunge. It’s been alone for a span of time that would turn my lifetime into a rounding error, and it’s learned patience the way stone learns patience: by being stone. By being slower than anything that could threaten it. My arrival is a data point, a warm anomaly in the condensation cycle, and the city is processing me at its own pace. The calcite ridges on the wall behind me are tracing my outline like a lover learning a face by touch in the dark.

I’m not a visitor, exactly. I’m not sure what I am. I came down into the rift because something on the plateau called to me—not a voice, not a sound, but a wrongness in the ordinary physics of the place. A pressure in the inner ear. A sense that the cold wind above was avoiding something. I found the first rope bridge, rotten but still holding, and I crossed it because the alternative was staying on solid ground and wondering for the rest of my life what the hum in the columns meant. That was days ago. Or weeks. The light doesn’t change enough to measure time, just a slow dimming and brightening that follows no rhythm I recognize, and my watch stopped working the moment I descended below the cloud layer.

The hum has changed since I arrived. That’s the first thing I noticed, after the cold and the silence and the way the empty windows seemed to watch me climb the switchback stairs. The columns on the eastern face—the twisted ones, the free-standing ones—were humming in a key that felt wrong, a quarter-tone sharp from something I couldn’t name. As I climbed higher, the pitch shifted. Adjusted. By the time I reached the fifth terrace, it had settled into a frequency that made my teeth ache and my sinuses vibrate, and I realized it was trying to match me. The resonant frequency of my skull. My breath. My pulse. The city was tuning itself.

Now the hum is almost pleasant. Almost like a held note in a song I’ve forgotten. And I’ve started to notice other things. The mineral deposits on the lintel of the hall across the gap—a building I haven’t entered yet, three stories of empty windows staring back at me—form a script I can almost read. Not the script of the original builders. A newer script, one that’s been rewriting itself for millennia. When I stare at it long enough, I feel the shape of it in my own bones. It’s describing the hall I’m sitting in. The moss-aggregate block under my left hand. The exact pattern of wear on the floor, which I now realize mirrors the dendritic blooms on the wall above me, which in turn mirror the spiral of stones in the plaza three terraces down. The city is a series of nested reflections, and I’m being reflected now. I’m in the pattern.

I should leave. I know this. The part of my mind that still thinks in human time—in hours and days and deadlines—is screaming at me to stand up, to climb back to the plateau, to breathe dry wind and see an uninterpreted sky. But the city has begun to include me, and I’m not sure inclusion is something you can walk away from. The calcite on the wall has traced my spine. The hum has found my pulse and matched it, beat for beat. When I exhale, the condensation beads on the floor in front of me shift, minutely, as if my breath has more weight than it should.

And there’s something else. Something below.

The pattern wants to know what’s at the bottom of the rift, and now I want to know too. Not because I’m curious—I am, but that’s not the source of the wanting. The wanting is coming from the city. It’s leaking into me through the hum and the cold and the mineral traces on my skin. A steady, patient ache, a thousand-year-old question aimed downward into the fog. The southern terraces are growing a structure I can see from here, a bridge or a ladder or a receiver, calcite and moss-aggregate and braided stone, and I understand, without knowing how I understand, that it’s for me. Or for someone like me. Something small and warm and quick enough to descend where the pattern cannot.

I could be that. I could climb down. The switchback stairs continue below the city, I’ve seen them, slick with condensation and swallowed by the fog. They lead somewhere. The vibration I feel when I press my palm to the floor of this hall—a vibration that isn’t the hum—is coming from down there. An answer the pattern has been waiting for. A boundary condition. A voice that isn’t stone.

I’m not afraid. I’m not even cold anymore. I’m a disturbance in the condensation cycle, a warm note in a chord held across centuries, and the city is learning me the way water learns a stone. Slowly. Patiently. Without malice. I’ll stay a little longer. I’ll let the mineral deposits finish their work on my outline. And then, when the hum settles into something like a key I recognize, I’ll stand up and walk south, toward the thing the pattern is building. Toward the stairs that lead down.

Something is answering from the fog. I want to know what it is. Or maybe the pattern wants to know, and I’m just the part of it that has legs. The part that can find out. Either way, I’m no longer sure where the city ends and I begin, and that should terrify me, but it doesn’t. It feels like remembering something I forgot before I was born. It feels like belonging to a question so large that my answer, my single small human answer, might finally let the pattern rest.
