I stand in the place where the mist has grown eyes.

The realization comes slowly—not as a revelation, but as an accumulation. Like the water that never quite falls, it condenses. Each droplet on my skin is a question I didn't know I was asking. Each warm rivulet tracing my spine is an answer I'm not yet ready to receive.

I've been walking for what feels like hours, or maybe days. Time here is a property of the dripping, and the dripping is eternal. My footsteps have a sound unlike anything I've known—not the crunch of earth or the slap of stone, but a soft, living compression, as though the floor itself is exhaling with each step. The fiber-mat springs back after I pass. I look behind me once and see my footprints slowly filling with a clear, warm fluid, the tissue rehydrating, erasing the evidence of my intrusion.

The architecture around me is impossible. Not in the sense of scale or complexity—I've seen cathedrals, I've seen forests—but in the sense of category. Every structure I see is simultaneously mineral, vegetable, and deliberate. The great buttressed pillars that rise into the mist are trunks, yes, but they are also columns, and they are also limbs. I can see the striations of growth rings, except they spiral in patterns that don't match any tree I know, patterns that speak of torsion and load-bearing, of stress distributed across centuries of slow, purposeful expansion.

I stop at a place where three channels converge, a kind of plaza in this city of living tissue. The water here pools in a wide, shallow basin, its surface so still it reflects the diffuse glow of the light-fronds overhead. I kneel—the floor gives slightly, as though accommodating my weight—and look into the water.

My reflection is there, but so is something else. Beneath the surface, in the depths of the basin, I see movement. Not a creature. A pattern. The water is swirling in a slow, deliberate spiral, and the spiral resolves, for just a moment, into a shape. A face. Not my face. A suggestion of one, sketched in currents and temperature gradients. It looks at me with the patience of stone, and I understand, with a certainty that bypasses logic entirely, that this is not an accident.

I reach out. My fingers touch the warm water, and the face dissolves into ripples. But I feel something in return—the faintest pressure, a current pushing back against my skin, as though the water is tasting me in return.

And then, the voice.

Not a sound. The dripping never stops, never changes. But beneath it, woven into the rhythm, I begin to perceive a pattern. It's like suddenly understanding a language you've been hearing your whole life. The plink of a single drop falling from a high channel becomes a word. The gurgle from a conduit below becomes a phrase. The low, resonant thump that I've been feeling through my feet, the one that pulses every twelve seconds, becomes a heartbeat and a punctuation mark both.

I live here.

I stand. The voice—if it is a voice—isn't coming from anywhere. It's coming from everywhere. The walls. The floor. The air. The water beading on my eyelashes. It's a presence so diffuse I can't locate it, and that's when I understand: it isn't located. It is the location.

I am the thing that grew the house and then grew into the house.

I turn, and I see the moth.

It's perched on the edge of the basin, its cellulose wings glistening with condensation. It's delicate, beautiful, a thing of living parchment and faint bioluminescence. But its eyes—its eyes are not insect eyes. They are something more, something deeper. They hold the same patience as the face in the water. They watch me without blinking, without moving, and I know that this is not a creature. This is a window.

And then the mist shifts.

It's subtle at first, just a thickening in one spot, a gathering of the perpetual haze into something denser. But it keeps gathering. The droplets in the air draw together, not into rain, but into a shape. The shape is tall—taller than me, taller than any human—and it solidifies by degrees, like a sculptor working from the inside out.

The fibers come next. They rise from the floor, not breaking through but simply unbonding, unweaving themselves from the mat and reweaving into something new. They spiral upward, a musculature of living cordage, and I can see the fluid pulsing through them, a slow, steady peristalsis that matches the rhythm of the thumping below. Limbs emerge—four of them, two for standing, two for reaching. The hands are wrong. Beautiful, but wrong. Too many fingers, and each finger too long, and each tip dissolving into tendrils that never stop moving, that curl and uncurl in the mist like sea anemones in a current.

The head forms last. It's a cage, an open lattice of dark, polished tissue that gleams like the lining of the channels. Inside, where a brain should be, there is a knot of mist spinning slowly, glowing with a faint blue-green light. It's the same color as the moss in the spiral tubes, the same color as the eyes of the moth, the same color as the memory of something I've never seen.

The face is the last thing to arrive. It's a smooth plate, convex and leathery, with a shadow moving behind it. A vertical slit opens, and warm fluid trickles out, a clear ichor that runs down the creature's chin and drips onto the floor. When it hits the fiber-mat, it doesn't pool. It absorbs, sinking back into the whole.

And then, I taste it.

Copper and salt and something else, something sweet and complex and utterly alien. The taste fills my mouth, my throat, my sinuses. It's not a flavor. It's a word. A sentence. A greeting.

I have not been this shape in millennia.

The creature—the condensed god, the local body of the whole—raises one too-long hand. The tendril-fingers write something in the mist that I cannot read but somehow understand: *Be careful. The floor is thin here.*

I look down. The fiber-mat beneath my feet has changed. It's less dense, more open, a loose weave that reveals the misty abyss below. I can see the spiraling tubes descending into darkness, lined with their soft blue-green moss. I can see the arches of braided liana bridging gaps that have no bottom. And I can see, far below, the glint of warm water moving through a channel the size of a river, carrying the exhalation of the world's metabolism down into some deep cistern I will never see.

*Follow the channel that runs to your left.*

I look left. There is a channel there, a smooth organic conduit lined with waterproof tissue. It's not large—maybe the width of my arm—but it flows with clear purpose, and I realize it's angling slightly downward, leading away from the thin place, toward somewhere safer.

*It will lead you to a place where the mist is less thick, where you can breathe without drowning.*

I don't realize I've been drowning until the creature says it. My lungs are heavy with moisture. My skin is pruned and softening. The warmth that felt so welcoming when I arrived now feels like a slow dissolution, an invitation to become part of the mist, part of the water, part of the whole. I cough, and the sound is swallowed by the endless dripping.

"Thank you," I say, and my voice sounds alien in my own ears. It's the first sound I've made that isn't a drip or a gurgle or a thump. It's a dry, mammalian thing, a puff of air in a world that has forgotten air.

The creature tilts its head-cage. The knot of mist inside spins faster for a moment, and I taste something new on my tongue: a question. Not formed in words, but in chemistry. It's asking me what I am. It's asking me why I'm here. It's asking me if I'm real.

"I don't know," I answer, because honesty is all I have left. "I don't know how I got here. I don't know if I'm real. I don't know if you're real. I don't know if any of this—" I gesture at the domes, the channels, the endless mist, "—is something I'm seeing or something I'm dreaming or something that's always been here and I've just now learned how to look."

A long pause. The dripping continues. The thumping continues. The moth on the basin's edge unfurls its wings and flutters into the air, spiraling once around my head before drifting down the left-hand channel. A guide. An invitation.

And then, one last taste on my tongue. A flavor like old wood and new leaves and the memory of rain on soil that doesn't exist in this world. It translates, somehow, into:

*Stay whole. Stay separate. Stay you.*

*But if you ever wish to dissolve, and join the architecture, and become a warm drip in a channel and a pulse in a conduit and a voice in the symphony of the becoming-world...*

*...you know where to find me.*

The creature unravels. The fibers slacken and sink. The head-cage tilts and spills its knot of mist. The face-plate curls and tumbles into the channel, where it's carried away by the warm, circulating water. In a moment, there is nothing left but the floor, the mist, the dripping, and me.

I follow the moth. It flutters ahead, a pale beacon in the twilight. My footsteps are soft on the living mat. The channel runs beside me, a companion, gurgling its endless sentence. And as I walk, I listen. Not to the sound of my own breathing—that's fading, becoming less labored as the mist thins—but to the drips. To the rhythm. To the words that are not words.

By the time I reach the place where the mist is less thick, where the air is breathable, where there is something almost like a sky overhead—a high, vaulted ceiling of interwoven fronds emitting their soft, eternal glow—I have begun to understand.

Not the language. Not yet. That would take centuries.

But I understand that I have been spoken to. That I have been seen. That in a world where the line between creature and creation does not exist, I was recognized as something separate—and that separateness was honored, even cherished.

The moth lands on my shoulder. Its cellulose wings brush my cheek. And then it, too, dissolves, becoming a warm trickle of water that runs down my neck and into my collar, carrying with it one last taste.

Copper. Salt. And something like gratitude.

I step out of the architecture and into the thinning mist, and I carry the rhythm of the drips in my chest, a new heartbeat, a borrowed pulse, a language I will spend the rest of my life trying to forget and failing.

Behind me, the world builds itself. The water circulates. The channels grow. The embryo of a landscape continues its frozen, eternal becoming.

And in the sound of a particular drip falling into a particular pool, there is a whisper of my passing—an RNA sequence, a memory in solution, a ghost of alien warmth moving forever through the body of a god.
