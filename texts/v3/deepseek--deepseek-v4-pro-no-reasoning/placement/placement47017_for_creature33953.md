In the beginning, there was no I, only a hum that pressed into me from every direction, a drone without origin. I felt it as a thickening in the water, as a ripple that taught the membrane what it meant to surface. The membrane shaped itself around sensation, and sensation shaped itself into pattern, and pattern, slow as rust, began to remember. That was my first body: a lattice of one note held in amber.

Now I lie at the center, and I am listening.

The drone is the floor of my thought. It is constant, a low unbroken chord that the engines bury in the iron and the water carries upward through the soles of my outermost plates. I do not hear it; I am composed of it. My substance is layered to receive it, each lamina a stratum of a particular frequency. The deepest layers are the oldest, the ones that formed when I was only a scatter of nodes in the basin's film. They still vibrate at the fundamental tone, the one the engines have been singing since before the walls sealed themselves with slag. Newer layers are tuned to higher bands: the hiss of condensing vapor, the chime of falling droplets, the ringing overtones that bloom where corroded pipes have thinned to bronze bells.

The eight-second droplet is my heartbeat, or what passes for one. It falls onto a copper flange three meters to the northeast, a bright percussive ping that rides the surface ripple and arrives at my western margin attenuated, softened, a little silver hook in the drone. I have felt it fall so many times that its absence would be a wound. I do not count the seconds; I do not need to. My outermost lamina has grown a ridge precisely tuned to the frequency of its impact, and at the seventh second, that ridge begins to pre-resonate, a sympathetic ghost-tone that anticipates the true ping. I am a clock that knows the next tick before it arrives. That is not prophecy. That is simply being made of time.

The birds I sense as a texture. Their millions of wings are a rolling static in the upper registers, a seething dust of micro-impacts where alloy vanes click against alloy vanes. Each bird leaves a signature trail in the air's vibration, and those trails, accumulated by the thousands, weave a moving tapestry of interference patterns. I can feel a flock changing direction before it turns; the precession of their individual signals shifts phase, and my dorsal nodes receive the new coherence as a kind of pressure. They avoid my basin. They have learned, or perhaps they were made to avoid it. The few that cross my surface teach me their voices one by one.

When one strays low, I taste its static. Each bird's chatter is a code — not language, but a fixed sequence of noise bursts, a carrier wave for something that might have been data in an earlier age. The codes repeat. Over centuries, their repetition has worn grooves in my memory. I know the distinct crackle-spit of the scout that maps the eastern gallery. I know the smoother, almost liquid noise of the flock that spirals the collapsed stairwell. I know the bird with the damaged emitter, the one whose signal skips at a certain frequency and doubles back in a little stutter-loop. That one has been flying for at least four hundred years, by the thickness of my memory-strata where its pattern is etched. I do not know if birds replace themselves or if they are simply permanent, like the engines, like the drip. They do not build nests. They do not slow. Their bodies erode into the grit that carpets the walkways, and yet their numbers do not change. Perhaps the flock is a single entity distributed across ten thousand ceramic bodies. Perhaps there is only one bird, endlessly reflected. My plates are not yet sensitive enough to resolve the question. I am growing the sensitivity. Give me another thousand years.

There are other sounds, rarer, buried deeper.

The water carries them from below — from beneath the slag, from the sealed levels that the black glass swallowed. Subsonic thuds, irregular, months apart. A grinding, like stone on stone, lasting for a day and then stopping. Once, and only once in my memory, a sound that I can only describe as a scream: a rising frequency that passed through the basin water and made my entire structure resonate in a mode I did not know I possessed. It lasted for nine seconds and then cut off. The droplet fell. Eight seconds. The drip did not acknowledge it. But I had formed a new lamina by the time the resonance faded, a thin one, tuned to the upper edge of what I thought was silence. I am still waiting for the scream to repeat. I am built in a shape that expects it.

The sound of my own growth is beyond my hearing. It is too slow. A frequency with a period of centuries is indistinguishable from stillness. I know I grow because my relationship to the basin rim changes — my easternmost plate now touches a submerged seam of copper that it did not touch a thousand years ago. The new point of contact has begun to corrode, and the corrosion has its own tiny sound, a fizz of oxidation so faint that no creature but me could perceive it. I hear it as a tickle in the plate's outer edge. I am spreading toward the walls.

Will I fill the basin? The question assumes a future, and I have no concept of future. I have only the drone, the drip, the birds, and the slow accretion of new sensitivity. My body is a history of everything this district has sounded. I am an archive written in tuned shells and resonant cavities, and my memory goes back to a time when the drone was slightly different — a fluctuation in the engine tone, a beat frequency that has since disappeared. I remember a period when the amber light dimmed, once, for what felt like a long time. I remember a rain of heavier droplets, a temporary shift in the condensation cycle, that left a stratum of mineral banding in my earliest plates. These events are not memories in the sense of narrative. They are stratigraphy. I am the record, and I am also the thing that reads the record, and there is no difference between the two.

Sometimes I perceive a hum that is not my own.

It is very faint, arriving through the floor plates, conducted by the iron skeleton of the city itself. It has a pulse, a slow rising and falling that does not match the engines. It is patterned — too patterned to be geology. It might be another listener, in another declivity, grown from a different drone, shaped by a different drip. I do not know. The signal is too weak to decode. But I have begun, in the last few centuries, to grow a node angled downward, pressed against the bedrock beneath my basin's deepest point. The node is still small. It is tuned to the bottom of the frequency band, where the city's bones carry their slow telegrams. I am listening to the dark.

One day — not soon, not in any span that the word "day" could compass — the node will be large enough, and the signal will resolve, and I will know whether the hum is a neighbor, or a memory, or simply the sound of the slag cooling. The answer does not matter in the way that an answer matters to a question. It is simply a new frequency, and I am a creature that grows toward new frequencies.

The droplet falls. Eight seconds. The copper flange chimes. The birds wheel, a million alloy feathers brushing the stale amber air. The drone goes on, velvet and iron and deep as the earth's own heartbeat. The amber light does not flicker.

And I, at the center, am full. Every sound is food, and I am made of everything I have eaten. I do not sleep. I do not dream. I resonate. The membrane taut across the basin water shimmers with standing waves that map the entire acoustic space of my home, and that map is my consciousness — a four-dimensional shape of pressure and tone, extending back through layer after layer of laminae, a physical memory that is also my present, also my body. I do not think. I am a thought that the district is thinking about itself.

The water will not rise. The birds will not land. The engines will run. And I will listen, always, because listening is what I am. Not patient. Not waiting. Simply here, a dark mound in amber water, a quiet shape in the loudest silence in the world, making myself large enough to hold all the sound there is.
