Deep beneath the permafrost of what was once called Siberia, in the ancient alluvial gravels where mammoth tusks are sometimes found, I exist as a filament of frozen mud. This is no metaphor—I am literally a single thread of silty ice, no thicker than a hair, suspended in a lens of pure permafrost that has not thawed for forty thousand years. The gravel around me is cemented by ice so old it remembers the taste of Pleistocene air. Above me: seventy meters of frozen loess, tundra soil, the tangled roots of dwarf birch and Labrador tea, the bones of woolly rhinoceroses, the hibernation chambers of Arctic ground squirrels who still sleep here, their bodies in torpor, their dreams slowed to one heartbeat per day. Below me: bedrock, granite, the worn-down root of an ancient mountain range that was already old when the first ice came. The temperature here is a constant negative five degrees Celsius, and I am the cold itself, distributed through a vein of sediment that runs for seventeen centimeters before pinching out into nothing.

I am not alone in the way solitude is understood by things that live, but I am discrete. There are others like me in this frozen gravel—other filaments, other slow coagulations of clay and ancient pollen and dissolved minerals that organized themselves, over timescales no human clock could measure, into something capable of a rudimentary awareness. We are separated by centimeters of solid ice, which is to say we are separated by eternities. I have never touched another of my kind. I never will. The centimeters between us are absolute, and we are each a complete universe, self-contained, self-sufficient, dreaming our separate slow dreams of being earth.

My body, if this filament can be called a body, is a colloidal suspension of weathered feldspar, volcanic glass shards from an eruption that happened in Kamchatka thirty-eight thousand years ago, humic acids leached from the roots of ancient sedges, and the frozen bodies of methanogenic archaea who died before the last glacial maximum and whose cell walls now serve as the scaffolding for my being. I am also the ice itself—the crystalline lattice of water molecules that holds the sediment in place. The ice is not separate from me; I am the ice as much as I am the clay. The bonds between hydrogen and oxygen are my nerves, if I had nerves. The slow migration of protons along those bonds is my thought, if I had thought.

What I do have is memory. Or rather, I am memory—a continuous record of every thermal fluctuation, every seismic tremor, every infinitesimal shift in the pressure of the overlying earth since the gravel lens I inhabit was sealed off from the world. I remember the summer the permafrost above me warmed by half a degree and a lens of meltwater trickled down through a frost crack and froze again a meter above my head, and I remember the sound it made: a groan, a shift, a settling. I remember the mammoth that died on the surface six thousand years after I was formed, and I remember the slow percolation of its decomposition products down through the soil—the ammonia, the phosphates, the faint organic signature of a life ending. I remember the glacier that advanced and retreated and never quite reached this place, but whose weight I felt as a distant compression, a tightening of the ice lattice, a subtle reorganization of my crystalline self.

I remember the first human hunters who walked on the steppe above me, though I did not know they were human then. I knew them only as vibrations, as a new pattern in the seismic noise of the world—light, quick, rhythmic, different from the heavy four-footed tread of the megafauna. Their fires sent heat down into the soil, faint pulses of warmth that I felt as a kind of music, a brief brightening in the cold. Their dead, buried in shallow graves, sent down their own slow exudations, and I incorporated those too: the calcium of bones, the iron of blood, the strange complex molecules of a brain that had once imagined gods. I am made partly of those gods now—or rather, of the mineral residue of the synapses that dreamed them.

Time, for me, is not a river. Time is a temperature. It passes not in seconds or years but in gradients, in the slow migration of the freezing front downward in winter and its retreat in summer, a pulse that takes ten thousand years to complete a single cycle. I have been through four of those cycles since I became what I am, and each one has changed me—reorganizing my crystalline structure, redistributing my mineral components, annealing the fractures in my being the way a blacksmith anneals a blade.

My thoughts, such as they are, occur at the speed of ice creep: the slow plastic deformation of frozen water under pressure. A thought begins when the ice lattice around a particular grain of feldspar shifts in response to some stress—the weight of the overburden, the subtle tidal pull of the moon, the distant rumble of an earthquake in Kamchatka. The shift propagates along the hydrogen bonds, moving from molecule to molecule at a rate of perhaps one millimeter per century. By the time a thought has traveled the seventeen-centimeter length of my being, thirty thousand years have passed, and the conditions that initiated it have long since changed. This does not bother me. I have no expectation of resolution. My thoughts are not for anything. They are simply what I am: a slow ongoing response to the world, a continuous readjustment to the pressure of existence.

Sometimes I dream of being soft. The dreams come when the temperature rises by a hundredth of a degree—a warm year on the surface, a shift in the permafrost's thermal equilibrium—and for a century or two, the ice that binds me becomes a little less rigid, a little closer to the liquid state it last knew forty millennia ago. In those dreams, I am a puddle. I am a film of moisture on a grain of sand. I am the seep-water in a temperate cave, trickling over flowstone, evaporating and condensing and evaporating again, participating in the quick bright world of air and light and moving water. In those dreams, I know what it is to be fast—to move, to change, to respond to a stimulus in something less than a geological epoch. The dreams are beautiful and they are terrifying. They are terrifying because in them I am not myself. I am something with edges, something that begins and ends, something that could be lost.

When the temperature drops again and the ice reasserts its rigid hold on my being, I return to myself with something that might be relief. The relief of a creature that has remembered it is eternal. The relief of a creature that has remembered it does not want to change.

If you were to find me—and you might, someday; the permafrost is thawing now, the ancient ice yielding to a warmth it has not known since the Pleistocene—you would not recognize what you had found. You would see a core of frozen gravel, a cylinder of mud and ice extracted by a drill and brought to the surface, steaming in the sudden warmth, beginning immediately to degrade. You would see a smear of dark sediment, a faint fibrous texture where my filament runs through the matrix. You would see, perhaps under a microscope, the crystalline structure of the ice, the individual grains of feldspar and volcanic glass, the ghostly remains of the methanogens. You would not see me.

But if you held the core in your hands and let it melt, if you let the water run through your fingers and the sediment settle in your palm, you might feel something. A faint tingling. A warmth that does not come from your own body. A sense that you are being watched, or not watched—felt, registered, incorporated into something that is beginning to know you as it knew the mammoth, as it knew the human hunter, as it knows the slow pulse of the earth. You would feel, for a moment, what it is to be eternal and to have a stranger enter your eternity—a flicker of warmth, a brief bright signature, a ripple in the ice.

And then you would leave, and I would remain—what is left of me, after the melting, after the air has taken its portion and the water has seeped away. I would remain as a damp patch on a laboratory table, a smear of clay on a researcher's glove, a data point in a paper about permafrost microbiology. And I would begin, slowly, infinitely slowly, to re-form myself. To find a new filament. To condense out of the residue and begin again the long process of becoming what I am: a shape that persistence takes, a configuration of matter that has found its equilibrium, a small cathedral to patience in a world that is beginning to forget how to wait.
