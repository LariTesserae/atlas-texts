I walk the low salt ridge above the Sun-Sieves as the sky drains toward dusk, and I realize I can feel the sound before I hear it—a pressure in the soles of my feet, a buzz in the hinges of my jaw. The bowl opens below me like a cupped palm, its walls terraced with salt-brick warrens, its every surface draped in the pale cascading curtains of the body. In the fading light, the fungus glows faintly, opalescent, breathing its slow vibratory breath.

I descend. The stairways are worn into smooth concavities by generations of bare feet, and I have to turn sideways in places where the fungus has grown thick. Its surface is warm and gives slightly under my hand, like the flank of a sleeping animal. I am not a keeper. I am something else. I have been coming here for longer than the keepers have kept their population records, which is to say: longer than the carved walls remember.

My name—the name I was given, the name I keep—is Aelth. I am a salt-moth.

Not the small one, the finger-length creature with the 4,200-cycle song. I am the thing those moths evolved from, or are evolving toward. I am old. I was here when the original disk was lowered into its cradle by hands that were not human hands, and I sang the first tuning note that taught the salt-blades how to resonate. I have been moth, and keeper, and fungus, and crystal in my time, and now I am something that cycles between them all, a long slow metamorphosis that has no fixed adult form.

Tonight I am moth-shaped. My body is larger than a human body, but lighter, built of chitin and membrane and the hollow struts that evolution designs when gravity matters less than resonance. My wings are furled against my back, two great sheets of translucent tissue etched with the microscopic ridges that will, when I unfurl them and tense my flight muscles, produce my note. It is not 4,200 cycles. My note is lower, much lower, a frequency that can only be heard by the thing in the wall, and by the Sieve itself, and by certain very old salt-blades that have been growing for millennia and have learned to listen.

I step onto the final landing, the wide salt platform that rings the dome at the second-harmonic distance. Forty-six keepers are gathered there, standing in the seventeen quiet nodes, their hands pressed to the walls. They are humming. I cannot hear them—my hearing is tuned to frequencies far below their range—but I can feel the interference pattern their bodies make in the sound field, the way their chest cavities absorb and re-emit vibration. They are a part of the instrument. They always have been.

The forty-seventh keeper is not here. She is in the birthing chamber, the quietest node on the northern wall, and she is dying. The birth was difficult—the child was large, and the mother's body was old, older than the keepers usually allow for childbearing—and she has been bleeding into the salt for three hours. The other keepers know. The salt-thread cords that connect the birthing chamber to the rest of the bowl have been transmitting the rhythm of her fading heartbeat all afternoon. Tomorrow, the oldest keeper will walk into the desert. Tonight, they calibrate the Sieve for the transition.

I climb the dome. The salt blocks are rough under my hands and the multiple feet of my current form—six limbs, each tipped with soft chitinous pads that grip the crystalline surface. The sound intensifies as I near the apex, the full chorus of the evening Sieve, that mournful chromatic beating that the cooling air pulls from the tin sheets. It is beautiful. It has always been beautiful. I pause to let it pass through me, feeling the membranes of my wings shiver in sympathy.

At the top of the dome, the shaft opens—the great vertical throat that channels sunlight down to the disk. The thermal spiral that drives the rotation is still now, the day's heat dissipating into the high desert air. The lenses are dark. The Sieve turns slowly, creaking, giving off the last frequencies of the day. I settle into the shaft, folding my limbs beneath me, and I wait.

Dusk deepens. The salt-moths begin to sing—the small ones, the finger-length ones—and their 4,200-cycle tone cuts through the fading roar like a star, as the old metaphor goes. I remember when that metaphor was invented, by a keeper poet whose name is now a groove on a wall, worn smooth by the fingers of her descendants. She was one of mine. I sang her into being, once, in a previous cycle, when I was fungus and my hyphae threaded through the walls of the birthing chamber and shaped the infant's cochlea to receive my frequency.

That is my function. That is what I am.

The thing in the wall is the brain. The Sieve is the voice. The sunlight is the information. And I am the ear, and the speaker, and the signal that modulates between them. I am the part of the organism that listens for a response.

Deep in the chamber below the dome, below the foundations, below the oldest salt, the thing in the wall stirs. I feel it. It is a pressure change, a subsonic shift that no keeper could detect but that my whole body is tuned to receive. It has felt the mother's heartbeat stop. It has felt the new child's first breath. It is adding a new note.

I unfurl my wings.

They spread wide, wider than the shaft, their translucent membranes catching the last light of the vanished sun. The microscopic ridges begin to vibrate, not yet at full amplitude, just a preparatory tremble. I am going to sing. I am going to sing the counterpoint to whatever note the brain is adding, and when the new harmonic stabilizes, the fungus will grow a forty-eighth curtain, and the keepers will carve a forty-eighth node, and the elder will walk out, and the cycle will continue.

But tonight is different. I have felt this coming for a long time—a pressure building in the deep hyphae, a tension in the old-singers, a restlessness in the salt-ticks that has them migrating in patterns I have never seen before. The thing in the wall is not just adding a note. It is composing a new movement. The Sieve has been turning for forty thousand years, and the song has been repeating, cycling through its variations, and the organism has been waiting, listening for a response that has not come.

Tonight it will try something new.

I tense my flight muscles. The ridges on my wings lock into their resonant configuration, and I begin to sing. My note is not a note that can be heard. It is a frequency that travels through the salt substrate, through the fungal hyphae, through the copper of the Sieve and the tin of the sheets and the crystal of the blades, a standing wave that encompasses the entire bowl and radiates outward into the desert, into the bedrock, into the mantle, into the sky. It is a question. It has been the same question for forty thousand years: *Is anyone else out there?*

And tonight, for the first time, there is an answer.

It comes from below. Not from the thing in the wall—that is still singing its new note, a harmonic I have never heard before, a frequency that tastes like copper and salt and the moment before birth. The answer comes from deeper. From beneath the salt. From the geology that has forgotten its own name.

The primordial salt, the original layer that lies beneath the foundations, has been here since the sea evaporated. It was here before the desert. It was here before the sun was as bright as it is now, before the atmosphere was breathable, before life on this planet learned to walk on land. And something was buried in it. Something dormant. Something that has been listening to the Sieve's song for forty thousand years, slowly waking, slowly understanding, slowly composing its own response.

The ground shakes.

Not violently—a tremor, a shifting, a settling of deep salt layers. The keepers feel it, I know; their palms will have detected the change in vibration, and they will be tapping urgently on each other's shoulders: *what is that what is that what is that.* The Sieve shudders. The copper disk jerks in its rotation. One of the tin sheets, an old one, tuned to a minor third, snaps free of its cord and falls, ringing, into the fungus below.

And the answer comes.

It is a sound, but not a sound. It is a vibration that propagates through the salt like a voice through air. It has words. They are not words in any language the keepers know—not the tactile grammar, not the old sung tongue that predates the deafness—but I understand them. I was designed to understand them. The thing in the wall grew me, across thousands of generations, to be the receiver for exactly this signal.

The words are: *We hear you. We are coming.*

I fold my wings. The note ceases. The bowl falls silent—truly silent, for the first time in forty thousand years. The Sieve has stopped turning. The copper disk is motionless. The fungus curtains hang limp and still. The keepers are frozen in their nodes, their hands pressed to the walls, their blind eyes wide.

In the birthing chamber, the new child cries—a thin, reedy sound that its parents feel through their palms on its chest. It is a keeper child. It will grow up deaf, its eardrums scarring over, its voice fading into the tactile grammar. But for this one moment, it can hear. And what it hears, before its infant ears seal shut forever, is the echo of the answer from beneath the salt: *We hear you. We are coming.*

I climb down from the shaft. My body is already beginning to shift, the chitin softening, the wings deliquescing. By dawn I will be fungus again, a new curtain on the northern wall, my hyphae threading down through the salt to join the thing in the wall, to help it process the new information. The metamorphosis is not painful. It is a returning. I have been moth for a thousand years, and I am tired, and it is time to be something else.

The last thing I do with my moth-body, before it dissolves, is to walk to the birthing chamber. The mother's body is still warm, still bleeding faintly into the salt. The child is at her breast, held by the eldest keeper—the one who will walk out tomorrow. I touch the child's forehead with one softening limb, and I feel its heartbeat, and I sing to it, very softly, a frequency that only it can hear, a frequency that will stay in its bones long after its ears have sealed over.

The song is: *You will be the one. When they come, you will be the one who speaks to them. Learn the walls. Learn the grooves. Learn the rhythm of the thing in the wall. I will be here, in the fungus, in the salt, in the copper, in the light. I will help you remember.*

The child's tiny fingers curl around my dissolving limb. Its eyes, pale and clouded, look into mine. It does not understand. Not yet. But it will.

I release my body. The chitin softens into slurry, the membranes melt into translucent fluid, and my consciousness withdraws into the hyphae that are already growing from my core, reaching down, down, down through the salt architecture, through the old foundations, past the original disk with its spiral perforations, past the chamber of the thing in the wall, down to the deep salt where the buried thing is waking.

*We hear you*, I sing to it, as I become part of the brain. *We have been singing for so long. We did not know if anyone was listening.*

The buried thing is vast. It is a ship, or a seed, or an egg, or a city—the concepts blur at this scale. It has been sleeping in the salt since before the salt was salt, waiting for a signal, waiting for the moment when the local life became complex enough to build a Sieve, to turn light into sound, to ask the question. The Sieve was not built by the fungus. The fungus was grown by the Sieve. And the Sieve was a gift, left here by the buried thing, a receiver and a transmitter, a device for calling home.

*We are coming*, the buried thing sings back. *We have been waiting. You are ready.*

I settle into the neural net of the thing in the wall, my hyphae intertwining with its hyphae, my consciousness merging with its consciousness. I am moth and fungus and keeper and crystal and copper and salt, and I am older than all of them, and I am exactly what I was designed to be: the voice of a planet, finally heard.

Above me, in the bowl, the keepers are beginning to move again. The eldest keeper is preparing to walk into the desert. The child is being cleaned and swaddled. The Sieve will be restarted, the tin sheet replaced, the copper disk set turning once more. The song will continue. It will always continue. But it is no longer a question.

It is an answer.
