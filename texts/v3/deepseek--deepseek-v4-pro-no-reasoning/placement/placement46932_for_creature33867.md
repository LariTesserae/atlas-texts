The light is a problem. I didn't realize how much of a problem until I see it react—not with fear, not with pain, but with a kind of desperate, ravenous attention that has nothing to do with eyes.

I lower the lantern. The flame gutters, and for a moment the chamber rushes back into absolute dark, and I am alone with the groaning walls and the smell of old salt and the faint, papery whisper of my own breathing. My hand is shaking. I can feel the spores settling on my skin like a fine, invisible dust, and I try not to think about what they're doing there, what they're tasting, what chemical verdict they're carrying back to the thing in the dark.

*Stay. Decay. Grow. Stay.*

The words aren't words. They're a sensation, a prickling at the base of my skull, a flavor on the back of my tongue that I shouldn't be able to name but somehow can. It's speaking to me in the language of my own body—the salts in my sweat, the carbon in my breath, the complex organic signature of a living animal that has no business being here. And I understand it. That's the terrible part. I understand it perfectly.

I shouldn't have come. I knew that when I found the entrance, a puckered mouth in a wall of ancient tissue, and I knew it when I squeezed through and felt the air change, arid and alkaline and utterly still. But I was following the map. The map the bone-carver left, scratched into a scapula the length of my forearm, a branching diagram of airways and chambers with a small circled hollow near the third bifurcation. *Safe camp*, the pictograms said. *Water nearby*. The bone-carver had been dead for three hundred years by the time I found her remains, but her map was still good, and I was tired, and the larger corridors were full of things I didn't want to meet.

Safe camp. I almost laugh. The bone-carver must have stayed here, must have built the fire-ring and arranged the stones and cut the fungal shelves with her flint blade, and all the while the walls were watching her, tasting her, learning her shape. How long did she stay? Long enough to shed skin cells into the peat. Long enough to breathe her chemical ghost into the salt crystals. Long enough to become a memory that the fungus could grow into, a template for this pale, swaying puppet that now stands three paces away with its featureless head tilted and its too-many fingers reaching toward me.

I am carrying her knife. I found it with her bones, in a different chamber, a smaller airway that had collapsed and sealed her in. She had been there a long time. The fungus had taken most of her, but the blade was still good—a scapula from something large, probably the same creature whose lung I'm standing in, sharpened to a fine edge and notched for a grip. I wonder if she carved it here, in this very chamber, while the walls groaned and the spore-laden air settled on her skin and the occupant learned her shape. I wonder if she knew.

Probably not. The bone-carver was practical. She would have seen the fungus as a resource—shelves for storage, peat for bedding, mycelium for tinder. She wouldn't have recognized it as a mind. I barely recognize it myself, and I can feel its chemical sentences bleeding into my bloodstream with every breath.

The lantern is still dark. I should relight it. The occupant is close now—I can feel it, a displacement of air, a faint vibration through the springy floor. But I'm afraid of what I'll see. The featureless face, the ivory body, the delicate tendrils spelling out their endless question. *What are you? What are you?*

"I'm a traveler," I say, and my voice is swallowed instantly by the peat, the cartilage, the patient fungal mats. The occupant doesn't hear it. It doesn't have ears. But something happens. A pause. A shift. The tendrils retract slightly, then extend again, waving more urgently.

It's tasting the sound. The vibration in the air. The way my voice moves through the spores and disturbs their chemical balance. It's learning a new sense, right now, in real time, and I can feel the effort of it, the slow, patient, consuming logic of fungus applied to the problem of *sound*.

I should go. I should back out through the puckered entrance and find another chamber, another airway, another hollow where the walls aren't alive and curious and hungry in a way that has nothing to do with malice. But I'm tired. I'm so tired. I've been walking through the body of this dead god for weeks, and the map is nearly useless now, the chambers shifting and collapsing on their millennial schedule, and outside the world is even worse—the salt pans, the alkaline lakes, the thin, toxic atmosphere that burns your lungs after an hour. Here, at least, the air is still. Here, at least, the walls are warm with their own slow decay. Here, at least, something wants me to stay.

*Stay. Decay. Grow. Stay.*

I strike a match. The flame sputters in the dry air, then catches, and I touch it to the lantern wick. Light floods the chamber, and the occupant is right there, an arm's length away, its tendrils frozen in mid-wave, its featureless face tilted down toward me. In the lantern light, it looks almost beautiful—the pale ivory of its body, the faint iridescence of the salt crystals caught in its hyphae, the way its form echoes the cartilage rings of the walls behind it. A sculpture. A memory. A limb of the larger organism, grown into the shape of a person because persons were here, and the fungus remembers.

It doesn't attack. It doesn't retreat. It just stands there, trembling slightly, its tendrils spelling out their chemical sentence over and over, and I realize it's waiting for me to answer in its language. To shed skin cells into the peat. To breathe my chemical ghost into the air. To stay long enough that it can learn me the way it learned the bone-carver, and grow a new shape, a new memory, a new limb that wears my face and walks on my legs and offers my chemical question to the next traveler who stumbles in.

I should be terrified. I am terrified. But I'm also something else. Something that has been walking alone through the dark corridors of a dead god for weeks, talking to no one, eating dried fungus and ancient salts, sleeping in chambers that groan and shift around me like the ribs of a still-dreaming beast. I'm lonely. I'm so lonely that the prospect of being learned, being remembered, being *grown into* by something that will outlast the cartilage and the salts and the slow, geological breathing of the walls—it doesn't feel like a threat. It feels like an offer.

The match burns down to my fingers. I drop it, and it goes out in the peat, and the lantern light is all that remains.

"I'm a traveler," I say again, and this time I don't care that it can't hear me. I'm talking to myself. "I'm passing through. I can't stay."

But my feet don't move. The peat is soft and springy and so easy to stand on. The walls are groaning their slow, subsonic song. The occupant is learning the shape of my voice, the vibration of it, and I can feel the mycelial mat under my boots beginning to stir, to probe, to ask its chemical question of the leather and the skin beneath.

*What are you?*

I'm a memory waiting to happen. I'm a body that will decay. I'm a chemical signature that this place will taste for centuries after I'm gone, whether I leave now or later. The difference is only time. The difference is only whether the occupant grows my shape while I'm still here to see it, or after I've moved on to some other chamber, some other death, some other memory.

I set the lantern down on one of the fungal shelves. I sit on the bone-carver's cold stone ring. I take out her knife and hold it in my lap, not as a weapon, but as a reminder. She was here. She left. Or she didn't leave—she died in a collapsed airway with her map and her blade and the fungus already taking her body back into itself. But she was here, and the occupant remembers her, and that means something. It means something that this place keeps what it consumes.

The tendrils brush my knee. They're so light I barely feel them—just a faint prickle, like static, like the first warning of numbness. I don't pull away. I'm tired. I'm so tired. And the chemical sentence is still repeating, patient and endless and almost kind:

*Stay. Decay. Grow. Stay.*

I close my eyes. The walls groan. The lung breathes its millennial breath. And I let the spores settle on my skin and taste me, learning the shape of who I am, just in case I decide to stay.
