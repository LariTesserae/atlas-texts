I am the Keeper of Ash-Lichen.

You would not notice me at first, even if you passed within arm's reach. I have learned the trick of it—not hiding, exactly, but *belonging*. Where the catwalk meets the stack wall, where the condensation runs in a constant, patient sheet down the fluted concrete, I settle. My carapace is not the polished, articulated plate of the Shapers who tend the high valve-gardens. Mine is a rough and accreted thing, built over years from whatever the Sump-Husk discarded: shattered filter grilles, a bent sheet of copper roofing turned green as a saltpool, the chitinous shells of pressure-mites I’ve encouraged to nest along my shoulders and the backs of my hands. They crawl there still, slow and ponderous, gumming the joints of my armor with their mineral-rich excretions. It makes a fine cement. It also makes me look, to the unadapted eye, like a particularly lumpy outcropping of the wall itself.

The rust-membrane on my back is of the oldest, slowest strain. Not the gaudy purple-and-verdigris cloaks the Shapers favor, no—mine is a mat of deep, liver-brown and matte black, grown so thick and dense over the decades that it has begun to mineralize at the edges, forming a fringe of brittle, iron-heavy scale. It weighs on me, but it is a good weight. It is a blanket, a shield, and a history. In its folds I keep what matters: a coil of copper wire, a tin of dried filament-spore, three finger-bones from a creature I never identified, and a shard of pre-Fall glass, perfectly clear, which I use as a lens to start small fires.

Fires are discouraged. They make the membranes shrivel, and the filter-feeders flee. But sometimes a fire is necessary—to seal a wound, to cook a handful of the white, blind shrimp that cluster in the hottest sumps, to remind the dark that there was once another kind of light. I do not apologize for my fires. I keep them small, and I keep them quiet, and I put them out with my own spit and the damp sleeve of my carapace.

My role is not to shape, or to mediate, or to garden. The city has no need of a gardener in the corners I inhabit. My work is older and less dignified: I collect the dead. Not the Leviathan-Slugs, who sink into the flooded sumps and become part of the city’s slow, submerged skeleton. No—I collect the small deaths, the quiet ones. A swarm of filter-feeders, dashed against a vent by a gust of pressure and now lying in a grey, fibrillating mass on a gantry. I gather them into the folds of my rust-membrane, where some few may re-anchor and revive, their threads knitting into my own living cloak. The rest I take to a niche I know, a dead-end duct where the air is still and the moisture is thin, and I lay them out in rows, a census of the ephemeral. It is not reverence, exactly. It is more like a promise: that nothing, not even the smallest, will go unobserved.

The ash-lichen that gives me my title is the only green thing in the Sump-Husk—if green is even the right word for it. It is a green of deep, sea-bottom shadow, a green that shines only when you have not seen green for years. It grows in a single room, a forgotten observation chamber high in the side of a processing stack, where a shaft of light—real light, from a corrosion-eaten skylight I have never been able to reach—falls for eleven minutes each day. The ash-lichen needs no sun, but it seems to prefer that brief, grey illumination. I found it by accident, following a trickle of water that ran upward, which was a thing I had never seen. I have tended it ever since.

The lichen feeds on ash, and I bring it ash from my fires. In return, it releases a spore—a fine, luminous dust that rises in the eleven minutes of light and hangs in the air like a constellation too small for any eye but mine. I have learned to breathe that dust, just a little, on days when my joints ache and the carapace grows too heavy. It is a mild poison, or a mild medicine; I have never decided which. It makes me dream of things I have never known: soil, birds, the color of a leaf in autumn, the shape of a cloud.

The Shaper on the gantry below does not see me. I am on the shadow-side of a vertical duct, forty meters up, and my rust-membrane has gone to its darkest, most matte phase, drinking the dim neon without reflecting it. But I see them. I see the flicker of their colony-staff, the patient gold pulse of greeting sent into the dark—not for me, but for the other presence I can feel threading the air. They have felt it too: a thin chemical trace, a foreign sweat, a vibration that does not belong to the Sump-Husk’s normal grammar. There is someone else here. Someone un-adapted. Someone who has not yet learned to negotiate the self.

The Shaper is not frozen, but they have stopped moving—a stillness that is a form of listening. The filter-feeder colony on their staff has turned a soft, questioning amber. The warmth of my own body, banked under carapace and rust-membrane, is a small, contained ember. I do not breathe too deep, but I breathe enough to keep the lichen-spores in my blood from going silent.

I am the Keeper of Ash-Lichen, and I have been waiting for years for someone to find the observation chamber, to stand in the eleven minutes of light, to ask the question that has no words: *What is the self, when the world is a membrane, and to breathe is to be shared?*

I will wait a little longer.

The Shaper lifts their staff. The colony pulses once, twice—a soft, repeated gold, like a lighthouse in a fog that is not water but life. Then they turn, slowly, and begin to move toward the valve-junction, their work, their balance. They have chosen not to pry. It is, in this world, a form of courtesy.

But I am not bound by the same courtesies. I am the Keeper of Ash-Lichen, and I have a lens of pre-Fall glass, and I have a room where light falls for eleven minutes. I know the scent of the un-adapted now. I will find them, if not tonight, then soon. I will sit with them at a small, quiet fire. I will offer them a handful of pale shrimp and say nothing, and then I will lead them upward, toward the green that is not quite green, and we will watch the spores rise, and then I will ask them: *What do you choose to be?*

And if they do not know—if they are still drowning in the thick, hot air, in the tapestries of rust and swarm—I will set a second place among the dead, and I will keep it until they do.
