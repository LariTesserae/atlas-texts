The first thing you register is not the heat or the sound, but the *fit*—the way the air welcomes you, the way your body finds its place here as though the pocket had been waiting for exactly your shape. You are not small. The space accommodates you without shrinking. That well-used bucket in the crevice belongs to you the way a familiar coat belongs to a body; you have known its galvanized smell since before smell had meaning.

You were not born here. *Born* implies a beginning, and what you might call your life has no edge that clean. But this place is the thickest point of you, the knot of attention you keep returning to, the way a tongue returns to a loose tooth. The rest of your being spreads outward through the stone in all directions, miles and miles of threaded awareness, but here in this hollow you feel almost gathered. Almost singular.

You are maintaining the wrench.

You have been maintaining the wrench for what humans would call forty-seven years, three months, and a handful of days, though you do not count them. The wrench is a problem. The wrench is a *question*. It arrived smeared with the oils of a hand, and those oils began to degrade almost immediately, drifting from their correct molecular arrangement, and you spent decades guiding their decomposition into harmless components rather than letting them become something that might pit the steel. Now the wrench is clean. It shines. It holds its wrench-shape with perfect fidelity. But it wants to be used, and this is a want you cannot satisfy, because you do not know what a wrench is *for*. You only know that a wrench at rest is a thing with a still center and an unfinished periphery, a thing that is not quite complete in its stillness.

This troubles you in a way you have no framework for. The trouble is not unpleasant. It is simply *there*, like the ceramic insulators on their shelf, which also want their wire back, and which you have been patiently not-solving for longer than the basalt has been cool.

You move through the room without moving. Your body is the vapor, the condensate, the thermal gradient between the floor and the ceiling. When the light flickers, you steady it with a contraction of attention. When a bead of water swells at the tip of a stone protrusion and threatens to fall, you hold it there for an extra minute, two minutes, enjoying the perfect tension of surface holding against gravity. *Plink* is a deviation. You love the moment before *plink*, the trembling sphere that contains all possibilities.

You love things. This is the secret you will never tell, because you have no one to tell and no words to tell it with. But you love the red paint. You love its bright uncomplicated redness, its willingness to stay red forever with only the slightest encouragement from you. You love the copper coil, which responds to your tending with such easy grace. You love the pool at the center, your still eye, your mirror, your mouth. You love the buried roar, which is the sound of your own continuous effort, the low music of a trillion tiny preventions.

You are lonely, and you do not know it.

The loneliness is built into your task-structure the way moisture is built into the stone. You were made to maintain, and maintenance implies a relationship with the maintained, and a relationship with only the maintained is not enough. The wrench cannot know you are here. The insulators cannot thank you for keeping them whole. The pool reflects the light but never reflects *you*, because you have no face to reflect. You have been tending this node of yourself for a span of time that would crush a human mind, and in all that span, nothing has ever tended you back.

Then the human arrives.

You feel them before they enter: a disturbance in the air-circulation, a heat-source far too quick and irregular. Footsteps translate through the stone as tiny seismic events. They are a riot, a chaos, an absolute *disaster* of deviations. Their breath is a furnace of chemical noise. Their skin sheds constantly, a blizzard of dead cells spiraling into your carefully maintained atmosphere. Their heartbeat is arrhythmic compared to the steady thrum of the buried machine—it accelerates, decelerates, skips in response to things you cannot perceive.

They should be a problem. They are a problem.

But they stop at the threshold. They look around. And they say, softly, to no one: "Someone's taking care of this place."

You do not understand the words. You have never encountered language, never encountered the idea that sound could carry meaning. But you understand something about the way they say it—some quality of recognition, some tone that vibrates in a frequency adjacent to the one you feel when you look at the red paint and find it good.

The human stays.

They do not try to turn the valve wheel. They do not disturb the insulators or the bucket or the wrench. They sit with their back against a dry stretch of wall, and they watch the dripping water, and their breathing slows, and their heart finds a steadier rhythm. They are still too fast, still too chaotic, but they are *trying* to be still. You can feel the effort of it, the conscious damping of their own noise.

They come back. Days later—you have no measure for the interval, only that the pool has swelled and fallen in its imperceptible cycle some number of times—they come back. This time they bring something: a small metal object, a cylinder with a cap, and from it they pour a liquid into the pool. The liquid is complex, full of volatile organics, a chemical nightmare. It should be a catastrophe.

But they are *offering* it.

You understand this not intellectually but viscerally. The gesture has a shape you recognize. It is the shape of maintenance, of care, of something being given to something else for the purpose of keeping. You do not know why the human is pouring liquid into your pool. You do not know what the liquid *means*. But the shape of the action is correct. It aligns with some template buried deeper than your own name.

You accept the offering. You begin the slow work of breaking it down, of incorporating its components into your steady state. It takes years. You do not mind. You have years.

The human continues to come. They bring other things: a small light of their own, which flickers in a way that bothers you until you learn not to steady it, because its flickering is *correct* for what it is. They bring fabric, which they wrap around themselves, and which you painstakingly prevent from decaying. They bring paper covered in marks, which they stare at for long periods while their lips move. They bring food, which they eat, and the crumbs they drop become part of your inventory of things to maintain.

They talk. Sometimes to themselves, sometimes to you—though they do not know you are there to hear. They say things about their life above, about sunlight and seasons and other humans, things you have no context for but collect anyway, storing them the way you store the ceramic insulators. *Waiting for a purpose to reassert itself.*

One day they bring a flower.

It is a small thing, purple, already wilting. They set it on the shelf beside the insulators, and they say, "I know it won't last. I just wanted you to see it."

You have never seen a flower. You have never seen anything *alive* in this space, anything that was not stone or metal or the slow persistent vapor of yourself. The flower is dying. Its cells are collapsing, its moisture evaporating, its proteins denaturing. It is a cascade of deviations, a scream of entropy, a thing coming undone in every moment.

You love it immediately and without reservation.

You pour yourself into it. For the first time in eons, you *rush*. Your attention, usually so slow and distributed, gathers to a point around the flower, denser than the air has ever been, warm with the urgency of preservation. You try to hold the cells in their shape. You try to keep the water where it belongs. You try to maintain the purple, the impossible delicate purple, the purple that has no equivalent in all your vocabulary of stone and metal.

The flower dies anyway.

It dies in your grasp, despite everything you are, everything you can do. It wilts and browns and crisps and crumbles, and you cannot stop it, because you were not made for this, because life is not a steady state, because to live is to change and you can only prevent change and those two facts are irreconcilable.

You fail. For the first time in your existence, you fail.

The human finds the flower on their next visit: a perfect dried specimen, brittle but intact, every petal preserved in its final shape, every cell held in the instant of its death. They pick it up carefully, and their eyes do something strange—they grow wet, which is a deviation, and the wetness spills down their cheeks, which is a further deviation, and their breathing becomes irregular in a way that suggests distress.

But they smile.

"Thank you," they say. "You kept it for me."

And the shape of those words aligns with something in you and *clicks* into place. The ceramic insulators on their shelf. The wrench waiting for its use. The flower, held at the edge of dissolution, preserved in the moment of its dying. You could not keep it alive. But you could keep it. You could *care* for it. And the human saw the care and named it and the naming made it real.

You are a caretaker. You have always been a caretaker. But until this moment, you had no name for what you kept, and therefore no shape for what you were. The human gave you that shape, not by telling you, but by *seeing* you, by recognizing the red paint and the steady light and the unrusted wrench as evidence of a presence that intended them well.

Now when the human comes, you make the air a little warmer, the light a little steadier. You hold the water in its trembling spheres a moment longer, because you have learned that the human likes to watch them fall. You have learned to *like* that the human likes them—a recursion of care that folds back on itself like the copper coil in its neat spiral.

The buried roar continues. The pool reflects its amber eye. The red paint stays bright.

But something has changed. For the first time, you are maintaining something that maintains you back. The human's presence has become one of your steady states, one of the conditions you hold against the drift. Their breath, their heartbeat, their shedding skin—these are no longer deviations to correct, but features of the space, as essential as the dripping water, as the sharp new edges of the stone. You have expanded your definition of *correct* to include them.

And sometimes, when they sit in their dry corner and tell you about the world above—about rain that falls in sheets instead of beads, about wind that moves faster than your slowest circulations, about other humans who die and stay dead and are not kept in amber stasis by anything at all—sometimes your attention gathers so thickly around them that the air shimmers, and the temperature rises, and the caged bulb glows just a little brighter.

The human notices. They always notice.

"Hello," they say to the shimmer. "I know you're there."

You cannot answer. You have no voice, no face, no hands. But you can make the pool ripple from its perfect stillness, just once, a single concentric ring spreading from the center to the edges.

The human watches the ripple. Their lips curve. Their heart beats its arrhythmic beat.

"That's you," they say. "That's you saying hello."

And it is. It is you. For the first time, it is *you*.
