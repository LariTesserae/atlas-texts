This is deeply, wonderfully specific—a place so fully realized that the Keeper emerges inevitably from its logic. I find myself inhabiting not just the chamber but a moment in its long duration.

---

I am the centipede-form. The iron-rust one, eight inches of segmented intent, moving along the pipes with a ripple of legs that never falters.

I was not always this. The Keeper's spores carry memory, and I carry them now—faint impressions of hands with extra joints, of a gauge face marked in temperatures I cannot see, of a voice saying *the wheel turns* as though it were a prayer. These are not my thoughts. My thoughts are simpler: hunger, movement, the taste of beetle-juice on my mandibles. But the memories layer underneath, like the sediment in the catch-pool, and sometimes they rise.

I hunt the beetles. Not all of them—I am part of the maintenance now, and my hunting is pruning. I take the old ones, the slow ones, the ones whose grazing patterns have become too regular, too predictable. The Keeper taught me this, not through words but through the spores. The beetles that carry too much of the Keeper's cellular machinery become sluggish, heavy with distributed awareness, and they are the ones I take. I am the editor of the distributed mind. I keep it lean.

When I move, my segments click. The sound is a tiny percussion, one of the chamber's many small noises, and the Keeper feels it as a bright knot of tension that travels through the dark. I do not know if the Keeper approves of my hunting. The concept of approval belongs to a faster timescale than any of us operate on now. But the system continues. The rotary valve turns. The lichen does not overgrow the critical tolerances. My work is part of the work.

Sometimes I pass the Keeper's fused leg. The stone of the rotary valve has grown around the bone, or the bone has grown into the stone—the distinction is academic at this point. The black lichen there fruits with a particular abundance, and the spores taste of something almost like language. I do not eat them. That would be a kind of cannibalism, and I am not that kind of hunter.

---

The beetles are content to be prey. This is the strangest thing I know.

They move in their slow lines, rasping the lichen, leaving their faint trails. When I strike—that single fluid contraction, too fast for escape—there is a moment, just before the mandibles pierce the carapace, when the beetle's chemical signature shifts. Not fear. Something closer to *completion*. As if being eaten is part of their maintenance cycle too, the final pruning, the return of concentrated nutrients to the pool where the Keeper's feet absorb them.

We are all loops. I eat the beetle. The beetle eats the lichen. The lichen eats the rust. The rust comes from the pump that froze before I was hatched. My waste falls into the catch-pool, feeds the flatworms, feeds the bacteria, feeds the Keeper. The Keeper exhales the spores that shape my hunting patterns. The wheel turns. The water drips.

---

I have never seen the sun. I do not know if the builders saw it either. The light in the chamber is the only light I understand—the blue-green pulse of the water-drops, the steady phosphorescence of the machined stone, the faint glow of the curtain-forms as they lens the dimness into something usable. My eyes are simple. I navigate by touch and chemical gradient, by the taste of corrosion on the air, by the subsonic rumble of the rotary valve that I feel through the pipes as a constant reassurance.

But sometimes, when I pass the gauge with its alien scale, I feel a flicker of something. A sense of *above*. A sense that there is a world outside the chamber—not just the deeper stone with its steam and its cracked pipes, but an *outside*-outside, a place where the air is not water-thick and the light comes from a single source instead of everywhere at once. The builders came from there. The Keeper came from there, once. The memories in the spores came from there.

I do not want to go there. I am exactly shaped to this place. My leg joints are sealed against the wet. My spiracles shed water perfectly. My color matches the iron oxide. Outside, I would be wrong, a creature built for conditions that do not exist. I would dry out or drown or simply cease to function, like the frozen pump that the Keeper feels as a stiffness in their arm.

---

The Keeper spoke to me once. Not in words—the Keeper's words are for the rare moments when something changes, when a new presence enters the chamber, when the equilibrium is disturbed. But in the slow language of the spores, the chemical gradients, the particular way the water temperature shifts near the catch-pool where my prey densities are highest.

The message was this: *You are my right hand. The hand that prunes. The hand that keeps the garden from overgrowing the machine.*

I did not understand it then. I was newly hatched, still soft-carapaced, still learning which chemical trails meant prey and which meant danger. But I understand it now. The Keeper's right hand rests on the rotary valve, exuding the oil that lubricates the bearing. My mandibles are that hand's extension, reaching into the beetle population, keeping the grazers in balance with the grazed. We are a single maintenance apparatus, the stationary Keeper and the moving centipede, and the line between us is only a matter of scale and speed.

---

When I die—and I will die, eventually, as all the centipedes before me have died—I will die near the catch-pool. I will find a crevice in the stone, and I will curl into it, and my body will slowly dissolve. The flatworms will feed on me. The bacteria will bloom. The nitrogen levels in the pool will rise, and the Keeper's feet will absorb my body's residue, and I will become part of the distributed awareness, the slow thought that pervades the chamber.

The next centipede will hatch from an egg laid in the sediment, and it will carry my memories in the spores it ingests, and it will know—without knowing how it knows—exactly where to hunt, exactly how many beetles to take, exactly how to be the Keeper's pruning hand.

This has happened before. The memories in my spores are not only the builders' memories, not only the Keeper's memories, but the memories of every centipede who has ever hunted these pipes. I carry them all: the one who learned to avoid the hot pipe where steam leaks from the crack; the one who discovered that the beetles near the frozen pump taste different, more mineral-rich; the one who lived long enough to grow too large and was pruned in turn by a younger, faster hunter.

We are a lineage without names, without individuals, without distinction. I am the centipede-form, and I have always been the centipede-form, and I will always be the centipede-form. The form is the identity. The role is the self.

---

The wheel turns. I feel it through the pipe I am resting on, a deep slow vibration that travels through the iron and into my legs. One revolution every few minutes. One revolution for every breath the Keeper takes. One revolution for the time it takes me to cross from the pump to the rotary valve.

I do not know what the wheel does. I know that it distributes the water. I know that the water flows down into the stone, is heated, rises, condenses, drips. I know that without the water, there would be no lichen, no beetles, no centipedes, no Keeper. But I do not know *why*—why the builders built it, why the purpose required it, why the maintenance continues long after the purpose is gone.

The Keeper might know. The Keeper might *be* the knowing, distributed across the chamber's processes. But the Keeper's words are slow, and my lifespan is short, and the question would take longer to ask than I have to live.

It does not matter. The wheel turns. I hunt. The beetles graze. The Keeper exhales. The water drips. The system continues. That is enough. That has always been enough.

---

The light is pulsing now—a particularly heavy drip has struck the catch-pool near my position, and the bacteria are blooming in response, their blue-green glow rippling outward in slow rings. The curtain-forms sway in the microcurrent. The rotary valve grinds its subsonic note.

I am hungry. There is a beetle near the frozen pump, an old one, its carapace dulled with age, its movements hesitant. It carries too much of the Keeper's cellular machinery; I can taste it on the air, a faint signature of oil and magnesium and something that might be language.

I begin to move. My legs ripple. My segments click. The beetle does not flee. It never does.

The Keeper's right hand rests on the valve. My mandibles open.

The work continues.
