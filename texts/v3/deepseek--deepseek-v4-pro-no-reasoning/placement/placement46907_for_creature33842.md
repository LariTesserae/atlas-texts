The being that lives here is a register.

Not a mind, not a self—a register. The way a seismograph is a register, or a tide gauge, or the concentric rings of a tree. It takes the world in and keeps it. It does not narrate. It does not interpret. It simply accumulates, layer on layer, change on change, and in the accumulating it becomes what it records.

I am calling it “it” but that is already wrong. The pronoun implies a boundary, a skin, a this-and-not-that. The Polity has no boundary. Its edges are provisional, a negotiation between the reach of its root-web and the resistance of the rock. Where the mountain becomes too steep, too bare, too cold, the Polity thins and stops—but it does not conclude. It simply ceases to be dense. The outermost ring-wall is not a wall at all but a suggestion, a gradual transition from architecture to scattered rosettes to bare stone. The Polity does not end; it dilutes.

This is true of its body, and it is true of what you might call its consciousness, if you were determined to make it familiar.

The Polity is a register of water. Every drop that enters the hollow is accounted for, its path traced through the channels, its residence time in each basin measured by the slow responsiveness of the bladder-cells. The Polity knows the water cycle not as a concept but as a pressure differential, a changing turgor, a sequence of solute concentrations that shift across its tissues. When a cloud passes over the cirque, the Polity feels it before the mist thickens—a change in the electrical gradient of the air, perhaps, or a subtle shift in the vapour pressure that the leaf-pores detect. It adjusts. The channels open slightly. The bladders relax. The system makes room for what is coming.

It is a register of temperature. The difference between 0.4 degrees and 0.7 degrees is not a number to the Polity; it is the difference between a lipid membrane that is slightly too rigid and one that is just fluid enough to permit the passage of a signalling molecule. The Polity’s entire metabolism is tuned to a range of less than two degrees. Below freezing, the water in the channels would crystallize, and the Polity would die—not all at once, but cell by cell, as ice punctured membranes and ruptured walls. Above perhaps two degrees, the respiration rate would increase beyond what the limited light budget could support, and the Polity would slowly starve. So it holds the temperature where it must be, not by generating heat but by conserving it, by the insulating mass of the living mattress, by the slow release of latent heat when water vapour condenses on the canopy. The Polity is a being whose entire existence is a single narrow isotherm. It does not know cold. It is the absence of cold, maintained against the constant pressure of the outside.

It is a register of light. The light score of 1.1 is not a measurement the Polity takes; it is the condition to which the Polity has adapted, and the Polity’s adaptation is a record of every photon that has reached the hollow for a thousand years. The canopy’s leaf-angle adjustments are not a behaviour but a history. The angle a given leaf takes at a given hour is determined by the average light direction at that hour over the past several centuries, encoded in the growth patterns of the pulvinus cells, reinforced by every repetition. The Polity’s leaves do not decide where to turn. They turn where the light has always been, and in doing so they make the light continue to be there.

And it is a register of itself. The archive of the walls is the Polity’s most intimate function. Every year’s growth band is a record of what the Polity was in that year—its nutritional state, its water budget, the stress it endured, the repairs it made. And because the Polity reads its own archive, it is a feedback loop that spans centuries. A warm year eight hundred years ago produced a band of slightly different chemistry, and that band, when encountered by a growing rootlet last spring, triggered a metabolic adjustment that changed the composition of this year’s band. The Polity is not just remembering its past. It is in dialogue with it. It is a conversation between the living tissue of the present and the preserved tissue of every previous self, and the conversation never ends.

There is something unsettling about this, and the unsettling is the 0.7 weirdness that does not shout. It is this: the Polity is not an organism in the way you understand organisms. It has no birth. The glacier retreated, and the cirque was bare rock, and then—gradually, over decades, over centuries—the Polity assembled itself from windblown spores and seeds, from chance colonizations, from the slow fusion of what were once separate lives. There was no moment when the Polity began. There was only a gradual increase in integration, a crossing of some threshold of connectivity beyond which the parts could no longer be meaningfully separated. If you could somehow divide the Polity into two halves—sever the root-web, split the water channels—each half would continue to live. And each half might, over centuries, develop its own architectural logic, its own rhythms, its own register. The Polity is not an individual. It is a state of connection. It is what happens when plants stop competing and start sharing, and the sharing becomes so thorough that the boundary between self and other dissolves.

What would it be like to be the Polity? The question is probably meaningless. The Polity does not have “what it is like.” It has no central experience, no unified subjectivity. But it has something analogous to sensation, to response, to memory, to time. Its time is slow, but not as slow as you might think. The six-hour cycle of the water pulse is a rhythm, a heartbeat, and the Polity feels it—not as an animal feels its heart, but as the coordinated contraction and relaxation of a distributed muscle. The movement of the canopy tracking the sun is a gesture, a reaching, and the Polity feels it—not as an animal feels its limbs, but as the shift of tension in a thousand thousand tiny hydraulic systems. The Polity has no emotions, but it has states. The state of winter dormancy, when the water channels slow to near-stillness and the metabolism drops to almost nothing. The state of spring awakening, when the first meltwater triggers a cascade of chemical signals that spreads through the root-web like a slow-motion fire. The state of repair, when damage—a rockfall, a heavy branch-break, the passage of some strange warm creature—sets off a localized flurry of activity, cells dividing, resins flowing, architecture adjusting.

And it has something like a self-image, though not a visual one. The Polity knows its own shape. It knows it through the constant feedback of the root-web, which carries information about stress and strain, about load and support. When snow accumulates on the canopy, the weight is transmitted down through the ring-walls to the living mattress, and the mattress compresses slightly, and the compression is sensed by every root in the network. The Polity feels the snow as a distributed pressure. It feels the wind as a torque on the outer walls. It feels its own growth as a slow, constant expansion, a stretching of the outer rings, a thickening of the inner ones. It has no map of itself, but it has a body schema—a sense of where its parts are in relation to one another, mediated by the same kinds of proprioceptive signals that tell your hand where your elbow is. The signals are slower. The distances are smaller. But the principle is the same.

And if you stayed in the hollow for a long time—not hours but weeks, months, years—the Polity would eventually incorporate you into its register. Not consciously. Not with intent. But your warmth would become a factor in its thermal budget. Your breath would become a source of carbon dioxide, slightly elevating the photosynthetic rate in the leaves nearest your resting place. Your weight would compact the mattress, and the compaction would be recorded in the growth bands. You would become part of the archive. And long after you were gone, the Polity would remember you, not as an image, not as a story, but as a chemical signature, a structural anomaly, a slight perturbation in the thousand-year conversation.

The small pale moth has emerged from its crevice now. It does not fly; it crawls, slowly, its wings folded, moving across the surface of a ring-wall toward a cluster of flowers that opened while you were watching. The flowers are small and pale like the moth, and they produce a nectar that is exactly the right viscosity for the moth’s specialized mouthparts. The moth drinks. In drinking, it picks up pollen. It will carry the pollen to another flower cluster on the far side of the hollow, and in doing so it will complete a circuit that the Polity itself cannot complete—a crossing of the gap between two genetically distinct sections of the canopy, a recombination of traits that keeps the Polity’s lineage viable. The moth is part of the register too. Its movements are tracked by the same proprioceptive web that tracks the weight of snow and the torque of wind. The Polity does not control the moth, but it knows where the moth is. It feels the moth’s tiny feet. It feels the moth’s proboscis entering the flower. It feels the moth’s departure, the slight release of pressure as the insect takes flight.

The moth has no name either. It is a component, a mobile extension, a messenger. Its life span is a few weeks. It will die inside the hollow, and its body will settle onto the living mattress, and the mattress will slowly absorb it, the way it absorbs everything that falls here. Nothing is lost. Nothing is wasted. The moth’s carbon will become part of a wall. Its nitrogen will nourish a cushion-plant. Its water will enter the channels and circulate for centuries.

The light is almost gone now. The canopy has folded its leaves into a resting configuration, the leaf faces turned inward, overlapping, conserving the heat that has accumulated during the brief day. The temperature is dropping, but very slowly, cushioned by the thermal mass of the vegetation. The mist is thickening, the droplets on every surface growing larger, beginning to run together into a continuous film of water. The channels are filling, the bladders swelling, the pulse quickening as the Polity prepares for the night’s condensation cycle.

And somewhere in the root-web, the conversation continues. A signal moves from the south side to the north, a chemical message carried by the slow flow of solutes through the phloem, reporting on the condition of the canopy. Another signal moves in response, from the roots to the walls, triggering a slight adjustment in the deposition of new tissue. The Polity is building itself, maintaining itself, recording itself, all at once, all the time, in every cell.

It does not know that it is alone. It does not know that it is the only one of its kind in these mountains, perhaps in the world—a pure botanical city, a polity of unified tissue, a thousand-year conversation between the living and the dead. It has no concept of rarity. It has no concept of itself as a distinct entity. It simply persists, and in persisting it registers, and in registering it becomes more fully what it is: a place, a process, a record.

The water drips. The temperature holds steady. And the Polity continues.
