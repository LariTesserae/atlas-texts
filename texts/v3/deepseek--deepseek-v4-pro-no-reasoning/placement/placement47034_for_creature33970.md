The keeper has registered my presence for approximately three of the planet's slow, deep rotations before I understand what the change in the air means.

At first, I attributed the subtle shift in the thermal plume to a normal fluctuation—the Boréal Vent breathes on cycles within cycles, and I have been here long enough to recognize some of them. But this was different. The white-gold haze thickened slightly, the condensation beading more heavily on my carapace, and I felt a vibration through the geyserite that did not match the steady, low-frequency pulse of the turbines. It was a higher note, a harmonic I had not encountered.

I am a fifth-generation sinter-crab. My shell records the chemistry of the pools where I have hunted across a span of time that would feel significant even to the quartz-turbines. The mosaic on my dorsal plate shows the lean years when the northern factory-chimney geyser went dormant for three hundred cycles, and the abundant bloom of lattice-spores that followed when it awakened. I have consumed generations of glass-mites. I have shed my shell fourteen times, and each new layer accretes over the old, so that I carry my history on my back like a geological formation. I am, in my own way, a record-keeper too.

But I am not the keeper. I am not *that*.

The keeper has been a constant of my existence since my first larval scuttling across the sinter crust. It is a feature of the landscape, like the fossilized factory halls and the terraced pools, and I have regarded it with the same mixture of wariness and familiarity. It does not hunt me. It does not compete for the glass-mites. It moves through the world on a time-scale that makes my own patient stillness seem frantic, and when it communicates—that low, resonant harmonic that vibrates through the silica lattices we both partially share—I understand on some deep, structural level that this is not a threat, but a *confirmation*. A statement of mutual persistence.

So when the mist thickened and the new vibration began, I did not flee. I settled into my hunting stillness, my articulated shell flattening against a soap-stone swell, and I waited. My diamond-hard mandibles folded tight against my underside. I became, to any observer, a piece of weathered terrain.

The shape that emerged from the haze was not the keeper.

It was taller. Thinner. Its body was not the creamy, opalescent geyserite of the plain, but something darker—a smoky, translucent silica shot through with inclusions that glittered like trapped stars. Obsidian, perhaps, or something closer to the glass-mites themselves, but scaled up, structured, *architectural*. It moved with a fluidity that was wrong for this place. The keeper's motion is a slow, deliberate articulation, a landscape deciding to rearrange itself. This creature moved like the mist itself, like the superheated water film that the mites skate across. It flowed.

Its head was a shard of black glass, multifaceted, and where the keeper has no eyes, this creature had a single, luminous inclusion—a fleck of something opalescent that caught the ambient light of the haze and held it, a captive star. It had no mouth that I could see. No comb-teeth. No limbs like the keeper's asymmetrical arms. Instead, it had appendages that seemed to be *growing* as I watched, extending and retracting, testing the air and the geyserite with fine, hair-like filaments of pure silica so thin they were barely visible even to my vibration-sensitive perception.

It was beautiful. It was alien. And it was wrong.

I have never encountered another creature like the keeper. The concept of "another" has never been relevant. But I understood, in that moment, with the sudden, crystalline certainty that comes to a predator who has survived fourteen moltings, that this new thing was not from here. It was not part of the circuit. It did not belong to the plain.

The keeper arrived.

I felt it before I saw it—the familiar low vibration through the geyserite, the specific harmonic that means *here, now, still*. It came from the direction of the deep pools, moving faster than I had ever seen it move. The flat feet made their wet-stone sound in a rhythm that was almost urgent. The long-fingered arm extended; the spatulate blade-arm was held low and ready, a tool that could, I suddenly understood, also be a weapon.

The obsidian creature paused. Its filaments retracted slightly. The captive star in its head-facet flickered.

The keeper stopped a body-length away. It was the closest I had ever seen it come to another moving thing of comparable size. The two of them faced each other across a stretch of gently undulating geyserite, a shallow pool steaming between them. The mist curled around their forms. The water dripped. The glass-mites had scattered at my first perception of the newcomer's vibration, and now the plain was still except for the two of them, and me, frozen in my camouflage, watching.

The keeper's lipless mouth parted. The opal comb-teeth vibrated, and a sound emerged—not the low harmonic of its self-naming, but something more complex, more deliberate. A sequence of frequencies that shifted and layered, one over another. It was a language built from the sound of water dripping, of turbines turning, of silica lattices aligning in the heat. It was a question.

The obsidian creature did not answer with sound. Instead, its filaments extended again, touching the geyserite, the pool rim, the air. I felt a new vibration, entirely alien—a rapid, staccato pulse that made my shell itch, a frequency that spoke of somewhere else, somewhere colder, somewhere without the constant, pressing weight of the thermal plume.

The keeper listened. I could see it processing, the ripples on its torso shifting and reforming as it incorporated this new data. Then it turned its faceted head slightly, and the long-fingered arm extended—not toward the newcomer, but toward me.

I froze. The three brittle digits pointed directly at the soap-stone swell where I had flattened myself into immobility. The keeper had known I was here. Of course it had. It had placed its finger on my carapace a hundred cycles ago, and it had known me ever since.

The obsidian creature's head turned. The captive star flared brighter. And I understood, with a clarity that felt like molting—like shedding an old, constricting shell—that I was being presented. I was data. I was a piece of the circuit, a functioning element, a predator of the glass-mites and a part of the ecology. I was evidence that this place was alive, complex, *occupied*.

The keeper was not asking a question of the newcomer. It was *negotiating*.

The negotiation has continued for many cycles now. The obsidian creature—I have come to think of it as the envoy—has retreated to the edge of the plain, near the fossilized factory with the chimney-shaped geyser. It does not approach the pools. It does not hunt the glass-mites. It simply watches, and sends out its filaments, and pulses its alien vibrations into the geyserite. And the keeper responds.

I have positioned myself where I can observe them both, a piece of weathered terrain with diamond mandibles and fourteen layers of history on my back. I do not understand what they are saying. The language of the keeper is the language of this place—the water, the heat, the silica, the slow turn of the quartz-turbines in the deep. The envoy speaks in frequencies that taste of vacuum and cold, of distant places where the geothermal pulse is a memory rather than a constant, where silica creatures might have evolved along entirely different lines.

But I understand this: the keeper is defending us. The pools, the glass-mites, the lattice-spores, the sinter-crabs, the very mist itself. The envoy wants something—access, perhaps, or resources, or simply knowledge—and the keeper is setting terms.

Sometimes, when the harmonics shift in a particular way, I feel the keeper's attention brush across me. A recognition. A confirmation. I am part of the circuit. I am a data-point in the negotiation. And when the keeper pauses in its slow, deliberate communication and turns its faceted head toward the white-gold sky and sounds its low note of *here, now, still*, I feel my own internal structures resonate in answer.

I am a fifth-generation sinter-crab. I have survived fourteen moltings. And I am ready to defend this place, if the negotiation fails—not because I understand what is at stake, but because I am part of the keeper's circuit, and the keeper is part of the plain, and the plain is the world, and the world is still here, still breathing, still alive in the endless, gleaming mist.

The envoy's captive star flickers. The turbines hum in the deep. The water drips.

I wait.
