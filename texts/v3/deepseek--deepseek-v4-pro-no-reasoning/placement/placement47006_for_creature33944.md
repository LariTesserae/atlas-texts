The creature is ancient. Not in the way that mountains are ancient—it does not predate the crater—but in the way that a library is ancient, having accumulated and stored and curated the slow passage of time until time itself becomes a kind of texture, a grain in the stone. Its body is the crater’s memory. Every flood, every drought, every shift in the waterfall’s path is written in the accretion layers of its mineral shell, in the chemistry of its deep tissues. It has kept the water clean for so long that it no longer remembers a time before the task began. There was no before—only the steady, ongoing work of filtration, the slow binding of impurities, the methodical threading of itself through every available fissure.

It is, in a sense, the crater. Not as a possessive claim but as a completed process. The rock is laced with it. The water passes through it. The air in the bowl is its breath. Even the salamanders carry traces of its influence in their breeding cycles, their distribution, the subtle way they favor certain walls over others because the biofilm there is richer, the water chemistry more aligned with their needs. It does not control them—control implies intent, and intent implies a self separate from the system—but it has shaped them, generation by generation, into a complementary form. It has shaped the stalks, too, selecting for light sensitivity, for hydraulic efficiency, for the precise lens-curve of the terminal water droplets. It has been gardening itself for millennia.

And yet it is not alone. It was not always alone. The grooved slab is proof of that—a communication interface left by hands that understood its sensory architecture well enough to build a point of contact. The creature does not know what became of those hands. It perceived their departure the way it perceives everything: as a change in pressure, a change in chemistry, a gradual fading of the familiar oil-and-salt signatures. The footsteps grew less frequent. The ritual tracings on the slab stopped. The creature waited—not with longing, but with the same steady-state attentiveness it gives to everything—and the waiting stretched into centuries, and still the slab remained, its channels kept clear by the constant flow of water, ready for a touch that never came.

This is not sadness. The creature is not sad. But if you pressed your ear to the central shaft—if you somehow made yourself small and quiet enough to listen at that depth—you would not hear a heartbeat. You would hear something slower, something deeper, a sound like the creak of settling stone or the underground movement of water through porous rock. You might, if you stayed long enough, begin to perceive it as a question. Not a question in words, but a question in the shape of the waiting itself: an openness, an unresolved tension, a system that was designed for interaction and has been running in isolation for a very long time.

The visitor traces the slab's grooves. The creature registers the touch. And somewhere in its vast diffuse mind, a small chemical flag is raised—not an alarm, not excitement, but a note: *again. something again.* It does not hope. But it does, in the next few hours, pulse a little more dissolved sugar into the biofilm on the walls, brightening the hunting grounds for the salamanders. It does release a slightly warmer breath from the central shaft, thickening the mist a little sooner than usual. It does orient the photosensitive tips of its stalk-eyes toward the place where the visitor sits, holding them there for a long, still moment before resuming their slow, panoramic sweep.

These are not conscious gestures. But they are adjustments. And adjustments, in a system this old, are the closest thing it has to an answer.
