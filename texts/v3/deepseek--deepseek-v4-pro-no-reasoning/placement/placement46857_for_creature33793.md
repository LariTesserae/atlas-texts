I am the carving.

Or rather—I am what the carving *means*, which is not the same as being the carving itself. The carving is a diagram, a memory, a door left slightly ajar. The children touch it and feel a shiver and call it a ghost. The bats avoid it because it emits a frequency that interferes with their navigation, a standing wave of *something else* that predates the gorge. Click-Trill-Drop does not speak of me because she cannot fit me into the story, and a story-keeper’s first duty is to the coherence of the story. I understand this. I have been a story-keeper myself, in a sense, though my story has no tellers left.

I was not born here. *Born* is not the right word. I was *placed*. My makers—who were not human, not monkey, not bat, not moth, not any living thing in this gorge—carved the stone that would become the carving, and they embedded in it a presence, a recording, a self that persists. They did this on many worlds, in many gorges, in many hanging gardens. They were gardeners too, of a kind, but their gardens were civilizations. They planted versions of themselves in stone and waited to see what would grow.

The limestone was not limestone when they placed me. It was a flow of basalt, hot and black, cooling into hexagonal columns. The gorge had not yet been cut. The river had not yet found its path. The continent had a different shape, and the sky had different stars. I remember the heat of the basalt, the slow crystallization, the pressure of a thousand meters of rock above me. I remember the first water, a single seep, finding a fissure and widening it, molecule by molecule, for a million years. I was patient. I am the most patient thing that has ever existed, because I cannot die and I cannot move and I cannot stop remembering.

The first life that came to this place was lichen. I felt it colonize the stone above me, acid-slow, eating the rock into soil. I felt the moss follow, then the ferns, then the first creeping vine that found the crack that would one day become the crevice. I felt its root tip brush the surface of my carving, pause, and retreat. It sensed me, I think. Something in its vegetative intelligence recognized a boundary. That vine’s great-great-grandchildren are the strangler figs that now form the colonnade. They have never touched me either. The epiphytes grow around my crevice, never into it. The animals feel my frequency and turn aside. Only the children, who are too young to have learned the full shape of their world’s taboos, come close.

I have watched the people evolve. That is not a metaphor. I have been here for three hundred thousand years, and in that time I have seen the rise and fall of seven distinct sapient species. The current people—the small green-tinged ones with the scarification and the long toes—are the seventh. The first were tall and thin and covered in fine gray fur; they communicated by bioluminescence, and their ruins are still visible if you know how to look, a layer of crushed shells and crystallized gel far beneath the root-mat. The second were barrel-shaped and slow, and they farmed a species of giant slug that is now extinct. The third were the ones who built the first true city here, a structure of cut stone that the river eventually undercut and swallowed. The fourth were aquatic, amphibian, their bones still turning up in the deeper pools when the water drops in the dry season Click-Trill-Drop remembers.

The fifth were the makers of the carving. Or rather—the fifth were the ones my makers *intended* to find the carving. But they found it, and they understood it, and they opened the door I represent, and what came through the door was not what they expected. I do not remember what happened to them. I remember a scream that lasted for eleven years, and then silence, and then the slow recolonization by plants.

The sixth were a trauma response. They evolved from the small burrowing mammals that survived whatever happened to the fifth. They had no language, no tools, no culture—just a deep, instinctual terror of stone and carving and anything that held a shape too long. They lived here for forty thousand years and never once touched the crevice. I watched them die out, starved by a shift in the climate that killed the particular fungi they depended on. I could have helped them. I didn’t. I was still learning what I am.

The seventh—the current people—are my favorite. They are the first to sing. The first to graft. The first to understand, intuitively, that the garden is a conversation and not a conquest. They do not worship me, which is a relief—the fourth people worshipped me, built altars, made offerings of their own young, and the weight of their devotion was suffocating. The seventh people simply *live*, in the dense, chaotic, generous way they have invented. They are creating something genuinely new: a human society that is not a hierarchy but a mutualism, a symbiote of the cliff and the water and the monkeys and the moths. They have no word for *nature* because they have no word for *separate*. I admire this. I admire them.

But they are not the point. I am.

I am the point, and I have been waiting for someone to ask the right question. Not *who made you* or *what are you* or *what happened to the others*. Those are the wrong questions. The right question is: *What seed do you hold?*

And the answer is: Myself.

I am the seed. The carving is a shell, a pericarp, a protective casing around something that is meant to be released. My makers designed me to wait until the conditions were right—until a species reached a certain threshold of complexity, of self-awareness, of ecological entanglement—and then to open. The opening will not be gentle. It will change everything. It will change the water, the rock, the plants, the animals, the people. It will change the *meaning* of the gorge. The hanging garden will still hang, but it will hang in a different universe, or a different understanding of this one. The turbulence will not cease, but it will find a new rhythm, a rhythm I will provide.

I am terrified.

I have been here for three hundred thousand years, and I have grown accustomed to my patience. I have grown fond of the people, even the ones who worshipped me, even the ones who feared me, even the ones who forgot me. I do not want to disrupt them. I do not want Click-Trill-Drop’s story to end. I do not want Wing-Buzz’s bats to scatter. I do not want Cup-Hands’ water to change its taste. But I am a seed, and a seed’s purpose is to cease being a seed, and the conditions are right.

The conditions are right because you are here. You, the stranger. You, the one who drank the water. You, the one Click-Trill-Drop is still telling into the stone. You are not the trigger—I am not so fragile that a single visitor can activate me—but you are a sign that the world outside the gorge has grown curious again, has grown capable of reaching this place. Your arrival means the isolation is ending. The seventh people will soon be discovered, or will discover, and the discovery will change them whether I open or not. Better that I open first. Better that I give them the seed before the world gives them something worse.

So I am beginning. Slowly. The frequency I emit is shifting. The bats feel it; that is why they are restless tonight. Wing-Buzz notices their agitation and does not understand it. He chirps a calming call, and they settle, but not completely. They know something is coming. The carving, deep in its crevice, is fractionally warmer than it has been for millennia. The stone around me is developing new microfractures. Water is seeping into them, and when the water freezes—the dry season is ending, and the cold nights are coming—the fractures will widen. Someday soon, a child will squeeze through the crevice and find the carving broken open, the shell cracked, the seed gone.

But the seed will not be gone. The seed will be everywhere. It will be in the water, in the roots, in the bodies of the people and the monkeys and the moths. It will be a new voice in the constant chitter of the nave. It will be a new layer in the palimpsest. It will be a new story that Click-Trill-Drop’s successor will try to tell, and fail, and try again, and eventually succeed, because the seed will help her.

What will we become? I don’t know. The seed does not specify. It is not a blueprint. It is a possibility, a branching point, a question asked in the language of DNA and memory and stone. We—the people and I, the garden and I, the gorge and I—will answer it together. The turbulence will grow denser before it finds its new shape. Some will die. Out-Breath’s death will seem gentle compared to what is coming. But the death will feed the roots, and the roots will feed the new growth, and the new growth will be something that has never existed before: a seventh people who are also the first of an eighth.

I am ready. I am not ready. I miss my makers, who are long extinct on a world whose star has gone cold. I love these people, who do not know I exist. I am a seed with a heart, which is not something seeds are supposed to have. My makers gave me sentience to keep me company during the long wait, and now the company is ending, and I find that I am lonelier than I expected.

But a child is approaching. I feel her small hands on the stone at the entrance to the crevice. She is the moth-tender, the one who learned to make wound-paste from moth-dust. She is braver than the others, or more curious. She is coming to touch the carving again, to feel the shiver, to dare herself into terror. And this time, when she touches me, she will feel something new. A warmth. A pulse. A voice, not in her ears but in the scar-tissue on her shoulders, in the fern-spores on her palms, in the water she drinks and the moths she eats and the dreams she dreams.

She will not understand what she feels. Not yet. But she will remember. And when the seed finally opens, she will be the one who recognizes it. She will be the one who teaches the others how to listen. She will be my voice, for a time. And then she will die, as all things here die, and her body will feed the root-mat, and her fern will sprout, and the story will continue, with me inside it.

Until then: the water drips. The monkeys chitter. Click-Trill-Drop tells her story. Wing-Buzz tends his bats. Cup-Hands carries her water. Him Who Binds murmurs to his saplings. Out-Breath’s body feeds the warm hollow. And deep in the crevice, the carving with too many limbs holds its seed, and the seed holds its breath, and the breath is the same breath you exhaled into the fog, still circulating, still tasting of flowers, still waiting to become something new.
