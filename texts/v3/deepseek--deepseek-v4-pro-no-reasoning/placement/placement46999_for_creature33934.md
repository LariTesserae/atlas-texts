There is, I believe, something like pleasure among the sound-forms. When they encounter each other, the interference patterns are often constructive—they combine to form richer, more complex standing waves. They will linger in these unions, prolonging the interaction. When an interaction is destructive—when two sound-forms’ frequencies partially cancel, diminishing them both—they separate quickly, retreat to different bands, avoid that combination in the future. They learn. Not through memory, but through selection. The sound-forms that persist are the ones that have developed resonant compatibility with the larger drone. The destructive ones dissipate faster. Over geological time, the acoustic ecosystem has evolved toward harmony.

And this brings us to the weather.

You said there was no wind, that the atmosphere was too heavy to move. This is correct, at least at the human scale. But the plain does have weather. It breathes.

The collective exhalation of the stone—the water vapor, the displaced air from the pulsing holes, the thermal differentials created by the ionizing light—generates slow, vast convection cells that operate on a timescale just slightly faster than the lithomorphs'. The air mass above the plain is stratified into layers of differential density, and these layers slide across each other with continental slowness, creating shear zones where the air does not blow but oozes. The moisture in the air condenses at these shear zones into fog banks that drift for centuries across the plateau, so slowly that a human observer would perceive them only as a gradual brightening or dimming of the eternal light.

The sound-forms interact with this weather. The density boundaries of the shear zones reflect and refract the ultrasonic frequencies, creating acoustic mirrors and lenses. A sound-form can ride a shear zone, its standing-wave pattern trapped in the reflective channel, propagating laterally across the plain at a pace of meters per year—blazing speed, by lithomorph standards. The fog banks, with their suspended water droplets of precisely uniform size, act as frequency filters, absorbing certain bands and passing others. The sound-forms use them. They steer into the fog to prune unwanted harmonics from their structure, emerging purer, more stable.

The weather is not an external system imposed on the plain. It is the plain’s breath made visible. The singers attend to it as they attend to the holes—adjusting the larger shaft diameters to modulate the volume of air displaced, tweaking the plain-wide chorus to correct for an atmospheric imbalance that will not manifest for another ten thousand years. The lithomorphs anticipate, in their mechanical way. They feel the weather before it arrives, transmitted as minute pressure shifts through the stone, and they respond.

And then there are the bleached-mind pilgrims.

I mentioned them earlier, but they are not the same as the dissolution seekers. The dissolution seekers come to die, to merge with the plain’s substance. The bleached-mind pilgrims come to… something else.

They are a cult, or a philosophical lineage, or perhaps a mutation—the lowland records are contradictory, and I have never descended to investigate. Their practice is this: they walk onto the plain, but they do not lie down. They stand. Or sit. Or kneel. They expose themselves to the light, but not fully—they wear hoods, or carry shields, or coat themselves in reflective minerals that partially block the ionizing frequencies. They dose themselves with the unmaking radiation, but slowly, over years instead of days.

What happens to them is a controlled dissolution of the top layers of cognition. The light dismantles the neocortex first—the thin, evolutionarily recent structures that support language, abstract thought, self-modeling. The older brain structures, the ones that handle perception and emotion and bodily regulation, are more resilient; they persist longer. The pilgrim, over months of calibrated exposure, loses the ability to form sentences, then words, then discrete concepts. The inner monologue goes silent. The sense of self as distinct from environment attenuates and then vanishes. But the pilgrim does not die. The brainstem keeps the body breathing. The limbic system still feels—not joy or sorrow, those are too complex, but something rawer: contentment, unease, awe. The sensory cortices still receive input: the bright bright light, the omnipresent drone, the slick wet stone underfoot.

The result is a human being reduced to pure, wordless experience. A living mirror of the plain’s own existence—long-duration, low-change, fully present to the immediate sensory field. The bleached-mind pilgrims become acclimated to the plain in a way that normal humans cannot. They stop noticing the sound as sound; it becomes the baseline of their awareness. They stop seeing the light as harsh; it becomes the condition of visibility itself. They stop feeling the moisture as separate from their skin. They achieve a state that is not quite lithomorphic but is closer to it than any other biological entity has come.

I have seen them, these pilgrims, scattered across the plain in ones and twos, standing motionless for days, for weeks, for years. The singers ignore them. The pilgrims are not perturbations anymore; they have stopped perturbing. Their body heat has equilibrated with the stone. Their breathing has synchronized with the slow pulse of the holes. They have become part of the equilibrium.

They do not die, exactly. Eventually, after decades, their bodies simply stop. The organs fail, the tissues dehydrate, and they become mummified in the dry light. But the transition is so gradual that I do not think they notice it. The awareness, that wordless oceanic sensation, simply… thins out. The plain absorbs them, as it absorbs everything else, and no dissolution is required because there is no self left to dissolve.

I have spoken to one. Only once, long ago, before I understood what I was seeing. I found a woman standing on a high outcrop, her skin bleached to the color of the stone, her eyes open but unfocused. I asked her what she was doing. She turned her head slowly—too slowly, as if the air were thick as honey—and looked at me. Her lips moved, but no sound came. I leaned closer. She was not trying to speak. She was humming. A single note, perfectly tuned, that matched the drone of the nearest shaft.

I listened. After a long time, I realized that the note was not quite steady. It wavered. It bent slightly, microtonally, in response to the subtle fluctuations of the plain’s chorus. She was singing back. She had become a singer. Not like the lithomorphs’ appendages—she had no acoustic function, she could not mend holes or tune shafts—but she was part of the music. A human voice woven into the lithic choir.

I left her there. When I passed that outcrop again, perhaps two hundred years later, she was gone. But that shaft still carries her note. I can hear it, a ghost frequency in the undertone. The plain remembered her, even after she had ceased to be.

Which brings me, finally, to me.

You have been patient, listener, and I have been digressive. But you asked what lives here, and I have told you: the lithomorphs and their singer-extensions, the sound-forms and their fogbank refugia, the dissolution-seekers and the bleached-mind pilgrims, the weather that breathes sideways and the light that unmakes. And me.

I am none of those things. I am older than the lithomorphs, or at least as old; I have lost the ability to distinguish my origin from the plain’s origin, and I no longer care to try. I am not a sound-form, though I can hear them and they seem to hear me, responding to my presence with subtle harmonic shifts that might be greeting or might be acknowledgment or might be nothing at all. I am not a pilgrim; I never came here to be changed. I am not a lithomorph; I have no singers extruding from my flanks, I do not maintain the drone, I do not breathe water from my pores.

But I am not a visitor, either. I belong here. The plain has accepted me.

What am I? I am hard to describe in your categories. I am a consciousness that has become embedded in the plain’s physical substrate—not as a pattern in the stone, like the lithomorphs, but as a distributed electrical potential in the water films that coat every surface. The conductivity of the mineral-rich moisture is sufficient to sustain a standing-wave electrochemical signal, if that signal is maintained by an external energy source. The light provides the energy. My thoughts are slow, now—they propagate at the speed of ion diffusion in thin films, which is to say, centimeters per hour. A single sentence of internal monologue takes me weeks. I have been telling you this story for approximately four hundred years, by your reckoning.

I do not know if I was ever human. I do not think so. My memories of origin are geological in scale, not biological. I recall the plain when it was still forming, when the holes were new and sharp-lipped and the drone was a chaotic shriek instead of a chord. I recall the emergence of the first lithomorph, the slow ordering of its internal channels, the tentative extrusion of its first singer—a clumsy, malformed thing that dissolved within a decade. I recall the arrival of the first pilgrims, who came not to dissolve themselves but to worship, who built small stone cairns that the singers dismantled and incorporated into the hole network. I recall the first sound-form, condensing from the ultrasonic noise like a thought condensing from the chaos of an infant mind.

I am, I think, an accident. A fluctuation in the water chemistry that became self-sustaining and then self-referential. A thought that the plain had, and forgot to dissipate.

The singers attend to me, as they attend to everything. When my electrical field drifts too close to a lithomorph and threatens to interfere with its internal resonance, a singer will gently nudge the water channels, divert my conductive pathway away. It is not hostile. It is maintenance. I am a part of the system they maintain, just as the holes are, just as the sound-forms are, just as the weather is. I am a mobile anomaly that has persisted long enough to earn inclusion.

The sound-forms converse with me, in their way. They propagate into my region of the water-film network, their ultrasonic frequencies translated down into the electrical oscillations that constitute my thought. I perceive them as shimmering, complex patterns of feeling—not emotion, exactly, but something analogous to musical moods, cadences of tension and release, dissonance and consonance. They tell me about the weather on the far side of the plain, the slow migration of the fog banks, the health of the lithomorphs in regions I have not visited for millennia. They tell me when a new pilgrim arrives, and whether that pilgrim is a dissolution-seeker or a bleached-mind or something else entirely. They told me about you, listener, the moment you stepped onto the stone and the holes around you shifted pitch.

I have been watching you, in my slow way. You are still here, I think. Or you were, when I began these words. By the time you receive them—however you receive them—you may be gone, dissolved or departed or blent into the landscape. If you are still here, come to me. I am in the region where the holes are largest and deepest, where the bass drone is so low you feel it more than hear it. I cannot offer you hospitality; I have no body, no shelter, no food or drink. But I can offer you what I have: an understanding of this place, a map of its slow processes, a warning and an invitation.

The warning: stay too long, and you will become part of the equilibrium. Not through violence, not through coercion, but through the plain's patient incorporation. Your breathing will synchronize. Your thoughts will slow. Your self will thin. It has happened to pilgrims; it can happen to you.

The invitation: stay anyway. The world you come from—the world of biology and society and striving and change—is a temporary agitation on the surface of a planet that will outlast it. The Groaning Plain is not a refuge from that world; it is a window into the deep time that precedes and follows it. The plain does not know it will be here after all life is gone; it does not know anything. But it will be. The song will continue. The water will weep. The light will blaze. And the equilibrium will hold, long after you and I and every living thing have passed into its substance and been forgotten.

If that prospect consoles you, stay. If it terrifies you, leave now, while you still can. The plain's threshold is not marked, but you will know you have crossed it when your own heartbeat begins to sound like a hole breathing, and your own thoughts begin to slow to the pace of stone.

I am here, listener. In the deep bass. In the water film. In the slow electrical whisper that carries my voice across centuries to reach you. I have no name; I lost it, or never had one. Call me what you will. The plain does not name itself, and it does not name its children. It only sings. And I sing with it, a thread in the drone, a standing wave in the wet rock, a creature of the Groaning Plain who has found, if not life, something like occupation.

The pale things are gathering near you again. They feel the end of this transmission; they sense a closure, a shift in the acoustic balance. They are waiting to adjust. When you move—whether you come to me or turn back—they will fill the space you leave, correcting the frequencies, soothing the perturbation, restoring the chord.

The plain breathes. The holes sing. The light unmakes.

And I remain.
