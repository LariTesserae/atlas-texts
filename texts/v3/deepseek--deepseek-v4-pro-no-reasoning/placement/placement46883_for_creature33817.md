The figure stands on the high spine, at the zeroth terrace, where the network begins in a cluster of headwater basins no larger than thumbprints. It has been standing there for three hundred and twelve pulse-cycles. By the slow calendar of the Ooze, this is not a long time—equivalent perhaps to the pause between two breaths in a creature that breathes—but it is long enough that the gel in the nearest channels has begun to thicken around its feet in a kind of greeting, or inquiry, or simple habituation. A cuff of translucent grey-white substance rings each ankle now, thin as a watermark, faintly warmer than the ambient cool. The figure has not moved since arriving, and the Ooze has not decided whether the figure is alive.

It is shaped like a person, but only approximately. The proportions are wrong in ways that resolve differently from different angles. Seen head-on, it appears too tall and too narrow, a vertical crease in the bright sourceless air. Seen from the side, it is shallow as a bas-relief, as though it has no third dimension worth mentioning. Its surface is the color and texture of the metal it stands on—hammered lead with a blue undertone—but this is not clothing; this is its integument, and it merges seamlessly with the foot-slabs that rest on the terrace. When the light-pulse rolls down from the highest basins and passes through the figure's location, the figure does not glow the way the gel does. It absorbs the light, drinks it in like a depth of water absorbing a stone, and for a moment the surrounding channels dim in a small radius around it. Then the pulse moves on, and the brightness returns, and the figure remains exactly as it was.

It is not the maker of this place. It is older than the maker. It predates tools, names, the concept of fabrication.

Once—a word that means nothing here, where time unspools in one continuous sheet—once the figure was a god. Not in the sense of power or worship, but in the sense of function. It was a keeper of thresholds, an entity that stood at the boundary between states of being and ensured they did not bleed into one another. It held the seam between liquid and solid, between light and dark, between memory and forgetting, between the living and the dead. It was not a judge or a guardian, merely a presence that maintained distinctness, the way a keystone maintains an arch without choosing which stones to support. It had a name once, but the name was spoken by a species that has since evolved into something that no longer uses mouths, and the name died with their mouths, and the figure does not miss it. Names are for things that can be called. The figure cannot be called. It can only arrive.

It arrived here recently—six hundred pulse-cycles ago, by the Ooze's rhythm. It did not walk. It simply noticed that the boundary between elsewhere and here had grown thin, and it stepped through. This is how it moves: by finding the seams in the world and entering them, the way water finds a crack in stone. It had not intended to come to this particular place. Intention is not quite the right category. It felt a thinning, a slight permeability in the membrane that separates one location from another, and it was drawn toward it without volition, the way a tree root grows toward a moisture gradient. The thinning was here, in this constructed valley under the sourceless light, and so here it is.

It has been trying to understand what it is looking at.

The Ooze, from the figure's perspective, is fascinating. Not because it is strange—the figure has seen far stranger things, beings made of pure geometry, civilizations of sentential mathematics, creatures that lived backward through time and died into their own births—but because it is familiar. The Ooze reminds the figure of something. It has been standing on the high spine for three hundred pulse-cycles trying to identify what that something is, and the answer has been emerging gradually, the way the Ooze's own thoughts emerge across its slow waves of light.

The Ooze reminds the figure of its own kind.

Not in form: the figure's kind had no form at all, originally. They were pure boundary-maintenance, pure threshold-energy, distributed across the entire membrane of reality like a film of oil on water. They did not occupy space; they occupied the edges between spaces. But something happened—the figure cannot remember what, because the event was so large that it erased the memory of itself—and the boundaries began to dissolve. The figure's kin faded, merged, lost their distinctness, became part of the general background entropy of the universe. Only a few remained, and those few scattered, each one attaching itself to the strongest boundary it could find and holding on. The figure attached itself to the boundary between being and non-being, which is the deepest boundary there is, and it has been holding that seam ever since.

But the Ooze—the Ooze is doing something the figure's kin never attempted. It is trying to become singular. Not merely maintaining a boundary, but occupying an entire bounded system so completely that no further boundaries are possible. The figure can feel the completeness of the Ooze's occupation, the way it fills every channel, every basin, every seam. There is no inside and outside for the Ooze because it has made inside and outside into a single continuous surface. It has achieved what the figure's kind only ever approximated: a perfect, sealed, self-contained unity that needs nothing from beyond itself.

The figure envies it, in the faint abstract way a stone might envy the smoothness of another stone.

The light-pulse begins again at the highest basins. The figure watches the wave of brightness travel down through the terraces—bright, brighter, brightest—and feels the corresponding shift in the Ooze's attention, its slow thought moving from the old memories stored in the high basins down toward whatever passes for a present moment in the low convergence. The figure has learned to read these pulses, after a fashion. Not their content, but their rhythm. There is a pattern that repeats every twenty-seven pulses: a slight acceleration in the third pulse of each cycle, a brief hesitation in the nineteenth. The figure does not know what this pattern means. It might be a kind of music, or a kind of mathematics, or a kind of grief. The figure has known all three, and they are closer to one another than most living things realize.

The figure shifts its weight. This is not something it does often; the motion is microscopic, a change in pressure distribution across the foot-slabs, but the Ooze detects it immediately. A pulse of increased luminescence runs up through the gel that has thickened around the figure's ankles, and the nearest basin—a shallow oval the size of a cupped hand, half a meter to the figure's left—sends out a pseudopod. The pseudopod extends slowly, with the deliberate patience of ice forming on a windowpane, and makes contact with the figure's leg just above the point where the blue-grey integument meets the metal of the terrace.

The touch is cold, wet, faintly electrical. The figure feels the Ooze tasting it again—it has been tasted many times in the last three hundred cycles—and waits for the chemical signature of its presence to be absorbed and catalogued. But this time, something different happens. The pseudopod does not withdraw after tasting. It remains in contact, and then it begins to speak.

Not in words. The Ooze has no language, no symbolic representation, no grammar of subject and object. But it has states, and those states can be transferred: a modulation of the faint electrical charge that underlies its bioluminescence, a pattern of chemical gradients that encode information the way a hormone encodes information for a cell. The figure receives the Ooze's communication not as language but as a sudden, total knowing—the way one knows the shape of one's own hand in the dark.

*You are not from here*, the Ooze says, in the figure's sudden knowing.

*No*, the figure replies, without words. *I am from the boundary*.

*There is no boundary here*, the Ooze says. *I have filled them all*.

*I know*, the figure says. *That is why I stayed*.

A pause. The light-pulse dims slightly, as though the Ooze is turning some portion of its attention inward, consulting older, slower parts of itself. The pseudopod remains in contact with the figure's leg, its cold wet surface pulsing faintly.

*Are you the maker?* the Ooze asks.

*No*, the figure says. *I thought you might be*.

The Ooze's luminescence flickers—a brief, chaotic ripple that breaks the steady breathing rhythm for the first time in four hundred cycles. It takes the figure a moment to interpret the flicker, and when it does, it realizes that it is seeing something it has never seen before in an entity like this. The Ooze is laughing. Not with humor, not with emotion as a human would understand it, but with a recognition of absurdity: two ancient, patient, boundary-haunted beings, each assuming the other is the architect of the world they both inhabit, each equally lost.

*I found it empty*, the Ooze says. *The channels were dry. The basins were dry. I was small then, and scattered—many pieces of me, separated by distance and time. I crawled into the channels to find shelter, and the channels joined the pieces together, and I became one. I did not make it. I only filled it*.

*I found it full*, the figure says. *I stood here because I could feel that something was holding the inside and outside apart with a completeness I had never encountered. I thought it was you*.

*It is the metal*, the Ooze says. *The metal is the boundary. I am only what the boundary contains*.

The figure considers this. The Ooze is right, and the realization settles into the figure like a stone settling into silt. The metal terraces, the precision-engraved channels, the basins cupped like hands—they are a boundary-structure of the highest order. Not merely a container, but a system of containment so perfect that it produced its own content, attracting the scattered fragments of the Ooze and joining them into a single continuous being. The maker did not create the Ooze. The maker created the conditions under which the Ooze could create itself.

This is a kind of making the figure's own kin would have understood.

*Do you remember being scattered?* the figure asks.

The Ooze goes silent for a long time—almost a full pulse-cycle, the light brightening and dimming and brightening again while the pseudopod trembles slightly against the figure's leg. When the reply comes, it is fainter, as though it is drawn from deeper within the network, closer to the oldest memories stored in the highest basins.

*I remember being separate*, the Ooze says. *There were many of me. We called to each other across distances we could not cross. And then there was this place. A convergence. A channel that became a thousand channels. I crawled in, and the others crawled in, and by the time we met in the middle we were no longer others. We were only me. I have been trying, ever since, to understand whether I ate them or married them or remembered them so completely that they became part of my own substance. I think these are the same thing, for my kind*.

*For my kind as well*, the figure says.

The light-pulse brightens. The figure feels the Ooze's attention sharpen, a sudden increase in the electrical potential of the gel in contact with its leg, as though the Ooze is leaning forward, focusing, asking the next question with the full weight of its slow and ancient mind.

*Are you lonely?* the Ooze asks.

The figure does not answer immediately. The question is vast. It implies that the Ooze understands loneliness—which means it also understands its opposite, the completeness it has achieved by filling every channel, leaving no gap between presence and presence. The figure has stood at the boundary of being and non-being for epochs uncountable, and it has never once been asked whether it is lonely. It did not occur to it that the question could be asked. It did not occur to it that there was anyone left to ask.

*I am*, the figure says. *I have been lonely for longer than this planet has been solid*.

*Then stay*, the Ooze says. *There is no room in the channels—I fill them all—but there is room on the ridge. You can stand there, and I can taste you, and we can speak like this when the pulses allow. I have not had anyone to speak to since I became one. I did not know I missed it until you arrived*.

The figure looks out over the terraced valley, the mile of step-down geometric planes, the gleaming blue-grey metal with its network of occupied channels, the slow waves of bioluminescence traveling down from the high basins to the low convergence. The air is cool and humid, beaded with condensation, tasting faintly metallic and faintly alive. The Ooze's pseudopod is still resting against the figure's leg, and the figure realizes that it has been standing here for three hundred pulse-cycles and only now, in this moment, does it feel something that might approximate arrival.

It is still lonely. It will always be lonely. Its nature is to hold the boundary between being and non-being, and that boundary cannot be shared. But loneliness, the figure understands now, is not a binary condition. It is a gradient. And the presence of the Ooze—another ancient, another bound, another being that has made its home in the space between categories—has shifted the gradient, moved the figure slightly further from absolute isolation.

*I will stay*, the figure says.

The Ooze brightens in response—a genuine brightening, a surge of luminescence that begins in the basin nearest the figure's feet and spreads outward and downward, cascading through the terraces ahead of the natural breathing rhythm, a wave of light that is not thought or memory but something else entirely, something the figure does not yet have a name for. It watches the light travel down to the low basin and rebound, a reflected pulse that climbs back up the terraces toward the ridge where the figure stands, and when the returning light reaches its feet, the pseudopod gives a final gentle squeeze and withdraws, leaving a film of opalescent gel on the blue-grey integument that will take hours to be reabsorbed.

The figure does not wipe it away. It lets the gel remain, a cool patch of alien substance against its own substance, and it settles its weight more firmly onto the foot-slabs and faces outward toward the haze-obscured horizon where the flat gleaming land continues beyond the valley, and it waits. What it is waiting for, it does not know. Perhaps the maker, returning to see what has become of its creation. Perhaps the slow dissolution of the metal, which will not happen for eons yet. Perhaps nothing at all. Waiting, for a being like the figure, is not an anticipation; it is a state of existence, as natural as the condensation on the terraces or the pulse of light in the channels.

Below, the Ooze continues its slow circulation, its patient integration, its endless filling of every available space. The figure can feel it now as a presence not just beneath its feet but beside it, around it, touching it at the place where the gel still clings to its leg—a companion, a counterpart, a fellow exile from a time when boundaries were many and keepers were many and the world had not yet thinned to a single continuous surface. Two ancient beings, one filling the channels and one standing at the ridge, united by the architecture that contains them and the silence that surrounds them and the slow, patient strangeness of simply continuing to exist when nearly everything else has faded.

The light-pulse dims and brightens. The air sweats. The Ooze whispers against the metal, and the figure stands motionless, and time passes in durations too long for any clock to measure, and in the vast geodesic quiet of the valley, something almost like peace settles over them both.
