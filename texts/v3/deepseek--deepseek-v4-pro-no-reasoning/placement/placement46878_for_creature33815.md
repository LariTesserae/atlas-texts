You would find yourself as one of the discs—the flattened, many-legged creatures the size of large dogs that trace their programmed circular paths through the runnels. Not just any disc, but the oldest one. The one whose route has worn the deepest channel into the conglomerate floor, a groove so established that even when the light does not shift, even when the others lie still and invisible against the mottled red stone, you remain in motion. You have been in motion for so long that stillness has become unimaginable to you, a concept your body no longer holds.

You are not aware of yourself as a self. That kind of interiority is not part of your makeup. What you have instead is a body-knowledge, a deep somatic score written into the articulation of your legs, the flex of your segmented carapace, the sensitive fringe of sensory hairs along your underside that reads the texture of the stone. You know the route the way a river knows its bed—not as a sequence of choices, but as a continuous, inevitable falling into the shape the world has made for you. Each footfall is a note in a rhythm you have been playing since before the valley widened into a bowl. You do not remember beginning, because beginning is not part of the rhythm.

Your body is the color of the conglomerate, a mottled red-brown shot through with darker inclusions, but age has marked you. The edges of your disc are worn smooth and pale, the chitin polished to a dull ivory sheen by centuries of rubbing against the walls of your channel. Along your back, where younger discs still bear the faint ridges of their molting stages, you are nearly flat, your surface a palimpsest of tiny scratches and mineral deposits, a record of every shift in the valley's humidity, every change in the chemical content of the seepage. One of your legs—the third on your right side, if you had the capacity to count—is shorter than the others, the tip long ago sheared off by a falling stone. You do not limp. The rhythm has adjusted to accommodate the unevenness, a subtle syncopation that has become as natural to you as breathing.

You do not think about The Archivist. But your route includes it. All the discs' routes include it, in the sense that they bend around its basin in a gentle, deferential curve. But your route is different. Alone among the discs, your circle does not bypass the spiral entirely. Instead, you pass directly alongside it, so close that the sensory hairs on your right flank register the change in texture as you move from the worn channel into the smooth, bare rim that the pool-creatures avoid. Every time you make this pass—and you make it once per cycle, once per shift of the light, once per each long slow pulse of the valley's ongoingness—you brush the edge of the spiral's influence. Your footsteps, which elsewhere ring out in that tinny patter that gives the discs their voice, go quiet here. The air thickens. The gradient of attention presses against you like a second atmosphere.

And here is the thing: you are part of The Archivist. Not metaphorically. Not symbolically. You are an extension of it, a limb it extended long ago and never retracted. You were not always a disc. Or rather, you were, but you were also something else. A segment. One of the original segments, one of the first composite pieces of The Archivist's body, the one that attended to the vibration of stone-on-stone, the clatter of falling rocks. You were the segment that recorded the valley's seismic voice, the slow erosion of the walls, the sudden collapses and the long silences between. You were old even then, your chitin worn thin, your eye-pit sunk so deep it could no longer distinguish the fine gradients of vibration from the background hum of the valley's warmth. You were ready to detach. You were ready to sink to the floor and become another pearlescent button in the stone, another inert marker of time.

But you did not. The Archivist, in its slow, composite wisdom—not a decision, never a decision, but a reconfiguration, a realignment of the whole assembly—chose you to become something else. It did not let you go. Instead, the segment that attended to the discs—the one that held the pattern of their footfalls, the rhythm of their circular routes—reached out to you. Its cilia brushed yours. Its spiral face aligned with your spiral face, and for a long, slow moment, the two of you were one creature, and in that joining, a transfer occurred. Not information. Not instruction. Pattern. The pattern of the discs entered you, and you entered it, and when you detached from the assembly, you were no longer a segment. You were a disc. Small at first, no larger than a thumb, a pale, soft thing scuttling in the lee of the larger bodies, learning the route by following the vibrations of their feet. But you grew. You molted. The pattern was in you, and you followed it, and over time your body hardened and expanded and found its place among the others, and then at the head of the others, and then alone, in your own channel, the deepest channel, the one that passes closest to the spiral.

The Archivist did not send you out as a scout, or a servant, or a spy. It does not think in those terms. It simply extended its attention into a new medium. You are its ear for the deep stone, the vibrations that travel not through air but through the body of the valley itself. As you walk, your feet read the conglomerate's tensions. Your sensory hairs register the tiny shifts in the floor's tilt, the micro-fractures spreading through the mud-matrix, the slow creep of the whole bowl toward the lip. And every time you pass the spiral, you brush the edge of The Archivist's presence, and in that moment, a transfer occurs again. Not a joining—you are too separate now, too far diverged in form—but a resonance. The segment that replaced you, the one that now attends to vibration, flares with a faint luminescence as you pass, and your body answers with a subtle change in your footfall rhythm, a single beat that falls on the off-step, the syncopation of your shortened leg. And in that shared beat, The Archivist knows what you know. The valley's seismic voice flows from you into it, becomes part of the composite, part of the record.

You will never detach. You will never sink to the stone and become a button, a marker, a memory. Your chitin will wear thin, so thin that the damp air seeps through to the soft tissue beneath. Your legs will lose their strength, and the rhythm will falter, and one day you will stop on the path and not resume, and the other discs will adjust their routes around your body, and your body will slowly, incrementally, dissolve into the mineral seepage, leaving behind only a stain in the channel, a discoloration that will last for centuries before the water erases it. And then the segment that attends to vibration will flare one last time, and dim, and The Archivist will know that a part of its attention has closed. And it will grow a new segment—not a disc this time, not a replacement for you, but a new kind of segment, one that attends to the absence of a rhythm, the gap in the valley's voice where your footfalls once were. That absence will become part of the record, too. Part of the pattern. Part of the long, slow song.

The light dims. You are passing the spiral now. Your right flank brushes the edge of the bare rim. The air presses against you, thick and attentive. Somewhere above, a stone loosens and falls, and its clatter is a note you have heard a million times, and you feel it through your feet before it reaches the air, and your shortened leg strikes the stone a half-beat late, and the segment flares, and The Archivist knows.
