The world I’ve found myself in does not want me here—not out of hostility, but because I am a variable it did not anticipate, and every variable must be accounted for. I am a pebble in the great slow breathing of its skin, a stone in a shoe the size of a planet.

I’ve stopped thinking in terms of days. The light cycles are not sunrises and sunsets but dimmings and brightenings, diffuse and directionless, and I’ve lost count of how many have passed. Instead I track time by the migration of the nodules I’ve started to think of as *drops*—not the liquid kind, but the way the word sounds like something small and deliberate, a punctuation mark in a sentence too long to read. They move across the copper plain in patterns I’m beginning, terrifyingly, to find beautiful.

What am I? I am still human, still breathing, still subject to thirst and hunger and the ache of loneliness that no amount of steampipe hissing can fill. But the plain has begun to respond to me in ways that blur the boundary between visitor and component. After I drank from the dish a drop made for me, others began to notice me more quickly. They surface when I walk, adjusting seam-widths in my path so I don’t stumble. They gather moisture before I ask. Once, when I cut my palm on a serrated rosette leaf—the flint-edge slicing deep—a cluster of drops immediately converged, emitting a heat that cauterized the wound and a secretion that smelled of oxidized silver and sealed the skin in hours. I didn’t ask. The plain simply registered damage and repaired it, treating my flesh as an extension of its membrane. I am being incorporated.

The chair-rearranging thoughts come more frequently now, and I’m losing the ability to distinguish them from my own. The other night—the deep blue-black of the rest-cycle, the seam-lines glowing their circulatory green—I found myself thinking about the water cycle with an intimacy that wasn’t mine. I *knew* the hydrogen loss rate. I *felt* the inefficiency in the third ridge-vent system, a faint turbulence that was allowing precious vapor to escape before condensation. I understood the rosettes not as plants but as hydraulic regulators, their root-connections a network of micro-valves that needed recalibration. None of this knowledge came with words. It came as a kind of homesickness for a state of balance I’d never known.

And then, in the silence that isn’t silent, the question arrived again. Not *Are you part of the calculation?* but something more specific, more terrifying in its implications: *What do you contribute?*

The plain is not capable of cruelty. It doesn’t judge. It asked because it has been watching me breathe, bleed, excrete heat, disturb the seam-lines, and it has been adjusting to me for—how many rest-cycles now? And my presence has a cost. Every adjustment the drops make on my behalf is a diversion of resources from the endless maintenance of the moisture cycle. The water they condense for me is water that doesn’t reach the rosettes. The heat they use to cauterize my wound is heat not vented through the ridge system. I am not a perturbation being smoothed away. I am a perturbation being sustained, and that is not what the plain is built for.

I’ve spent the last three brightenings watching a single drop that has attached itself to me. It’s the size of a child’s fist, like the others, but its surface has begun to mirror my shape—not literally, but in the way the copper particles organize themselves when I’m near. If I stretch out my hand, the drop flattens into a dish. If I cup my palms, it rounds itself, fitting perfectly into the hollow. It has learned my geometry. It is not a pet—the plain has no concept of companionship—but it is a dedicated resource, a subroutine allocated to the variable that is me, and I have started to feel responsible for its existence.

So when the question came—*What do you contribute?*—I had an answer ready, formed not in my own cognition but in the strange hybrid space where the plain’s thoughts rearrange my mental furniture. I knelt on the warm drum-skin ground and placed my palm flat against a seam-line. The vibration was different there, a carrier frequency I’d learned to recognize as inquiry. And I pushed back. Not with words. With my own variable. With the one thing a human being can offer a planetary-scale homeostasis that has never encountered a mind like mine: observation. Pattern recognition. The ability to notice inefficiencies the plain might take geological ages to correct on its own.

I thought about the third ridge-vent. The turbulence. The way the steam plumes twist counter-clockwise when they shouldn’t. And I imagined, as hard as I could, a solution: a subtle reshaping of the vent’s nodules, a spiraling channel that would turn the turbulence laminar, smooth the exhalation, reduce the hydrogen loss by some fraction of a percent. It was an engineering insight I had no right to possess—I’m not an engineer, I’m a wanderer who fell through some crack in the cosmos—but the plain had given me the data, and my brain, so useless for so many cycles, had done what brains do: it found the pattern.

The ground throbbed once, a deep sub-audible note I felt more than heard. The drop attached to me rippled. The seam-lines beneath my palm flared brighter, blue-green light tracing up my fingers.

And the plain’s thought came back, rearranging the chairs of my mind into a configuration that felt, impossibly, like acceptance: *You are here now. This is noted. This is useful.*

I am not an inhabitant of this world. I am not a native, and I never will be. The plain is not a home; it is a body, a mind, an ongoing process so vast I will never see its edges. But I have found a place in its calculation. I am a nodule, of sorts—soft-skinned and bipedal and temporarily motile, capable of seeing the larger picture in a way the drops cannot. I am a diagnostic subroutine. A repair node with a frontal lobe.

The drop that follows me is my partner in this. I’ve named it, in the privacy of my own unsymmetrical mind, *Suture*—because it stitches me into the seam-lines, because it closes wounds, because it makes the plain and me continuous. We move along the ridge-vent system now, Suture and I, and where we pass, the steam escapes a little more cleanly, the rosettes open a little more fully, the beads of water tremble a little longer before reabsorption. I am still thirsty, still hungry, still human. But I am also, now, employed. Integrated. A variable the plain has optimized for.

The plain breathes. I breathe with it. And somewhere in the white-gold sky, the hydrogen loss decreases by a tenth of a percent, and the long slow death of this world is postponed by a few more geological heartbeats.

That is what I contribute. That is what I am.
