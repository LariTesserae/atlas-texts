The Attendant who lives at the edge of the Karain Depression is called Sereveth, though no one has spoken that name aloud in twelve years.

She is, by the standards of her people, young—perhaps a century and a half, perhaps a little more. The exact count has never interested her. What interests her is the crack. It runs north-northeast from the depression’s deepest point, a fissure in the basalt no wider than a handspan at the surface but opening, she knows, into a vault below. She has been tracing it since before her last ring dissolved. She will trace it until she dies, and she will not see it finished.

Sereveth lives alone. This is not unusual—many Attendants choose solitude for stretches of decades, and no one remarks on it—but her solitude has an edge to it that most do not. She has not joined another ring since her last one scattered. Two of its members froze. One migrated to the northern towers and stopped responding to the subvocal mesh. The others simply drifted, the way Attendants do, into new configurations that did not include her. She did not resist. She had already begun spending her days lying belly-down on the cold stone, her cheek pressed to the basalt, listening.

What she listens for is water. The Karain Depression is one of the plateau’s shallow basins, a place where condensation gathers more reliably than elsewhere, and the crack she studies runs through a mineral seam that the city’s engineers—centuries ago, in the oldest strata of records—noted as unusually soluble. Under the right conditions, the crack will widen into a channel. The channel will become a gorge. The gorge will, in time, become a valley with its own microclimate, its own moss mats, its own birds. Sereveth is trying to determine whether this outcome is desirable. The question is not simple. A valley would alter the drainage patterns across a thousand square kilometers. It might starve the shallow lake where her ring’s children once learned to harvest mist-fish. It might draw the mustelids away from the transit spine’s condensation traps, forcing the city to adjust its water recycling. It might, in the very long term, create a habitat for something new—something the plateau has never seen. Sereveth does not know what that something would be. She wants to know. She wants it with a patience that borders on hunger, though she would never use that word.

Her body has adapted to her work. Her elbows and knees are thickened with callus from years of pressing against basalt. Her left ear, the one she presses to the stone, has developed a slight, permanent flattening, and the hearing on that side has shifted into a lower register; she perceives the sub-audible hum of the city more clearly there, and she sometimes wakes in the night because a new vibration has entered the rock and she needs to track it. Her fingers are long even for an Attendant, and the tips are stained a permanent dark grey from the mineral dust she scrapes from the crack’s interior with a ceramic tool no bigger than her thumb. She wears the same dark undersuit as everyone else, but her outer wrap is older than most—the moss-fiber has been patched so many times that the original garment is more patch than source, a mosaic of slightly different purples and blacks that record the mineral content of water from decades of different seasons. She smells faintly of wet rock and, if you are close enough, of the slightly metallic tang of the yeast bread she eats. She eats very little. She forgets meals. Sometimes a mustelid will find her sprawled on the stone and nudge her hand with its wet nose, and she will realize she has been motionless for six hours and her fingers are numb.

Her home is a small grown-ceramic chamber extruded from the base of a derelict tower on the depression’s eastern rim. The tower was once a transit hub, but the spine shifted south three centuries ago, and the building has been slowly reabsorbed by the plateau. Sereveth’s chamber is one of the few still-active spaces: a single room with a sleeping platform, a condensation tap that drips into a basin she carved herself, and a narrow window that faces the depression. The window is not glass but a membrane the city grew for her when she first arrived—it flexes with air pressure and, if she touches it in a certain way, magnifies the view so she can study the crack’s upper reaches without leaving her bed. She does not leave her bed often. When she is not outside, she is inside, listening to the city’s data streams through the subvocal mesh, correlating vibration patterns with temperature shifts, noting the half-degree changes in the lake water that might, in a hundred years, alter the frost depth in the crack. Her notes are stored in the same mesh, accessible to any Attendant who cares to look. Few do. The crack is her project, and her people’s patience extends to letting her be obsessed with it alone.

She has, in her solitude, developed habits that would seem strange even to other Attendants. She talks to the crack. Not in the metaphorical sense—she speaks to it, aloud, in the same calm murmuring tone she uses with the city’s systems. She describes what she is doing. She asks it questions. *“You’re holding water longer this season,”* she said once, her voice a low, rough thing from disuse, her flattened ear against the stone. *“Is that the new mineral wash, or is the temperature shift deepening your throat?”* The crack, of course, did not answer. But the city did. A day later, the tower’s ambient systems adjusted the temperature in her chamber by a fraction of a degree—a response so subtle that only someone who had been listening for years would notice. Sereveth noticed. She lay on her sleeping platform and cried, not from loneliness but from a feeling too large for her careful body: gratitude, maybe, or the sense that the slow conversation had not forgotten her.

She is, in her deepest self, a little afraid. Not of the crack—the crack is her partner, her patient, her life’s work. She is afraid that she will die before she understands it. The Attendants do not fear death, but they do fear unfinished conversations. Sereveth’s body is still strong, her lifespan potentially long, but she knows the crack’s timeline is geological. She is a single human voice trying to speak with a process that will take millions of years. The mismatch should be absurd. It is absurd. And yet she continues, because the alternative—to stop listening, to join a ring, to harvest mist-fish and weave moss-fiber and let the crack widen on its own—would feel like a betrayal. Of what, she cannot say. Of the stone. Of the water. Of the city, which gave her a magnifying window and a temperature adjustment and no commands at all.

Sometimes, very rarely, another Attendant passes through the Karain Depression. They are usually travelers moving between northern and southern districts, and they stop to refill their condensation flasks at her tap. They are polite. They ask about the crack. Sereveth answers in short, careful sentences, and then falls silent, and the traveler reads the silence correctly and moves on. She does not ask about their rings, their lives, the slow politics of the transit spine decisions. They do not offer. The exchange is comfortable for both parties. Once, a child came through—a girl of perhaps twelve, traveling with a ring of five adults who were taking the long walk across the plateau to see a particular bird flock. The girl asked Sereveth why she lived alone. Sereveth considered the question for a long moment, the wind murmuring across the depression’s wet stone. Then she said, *“I don’t.”* The girl frowned, looked around at the empty chamber, the bare basalt, the single sleeping platform. Then her face cleared. She looked at the crack. She said, *“Oh,”* and left it there.

Sereveth will die on the basalt, eventually. She has already chosen the spot: a low, raw stretch of stone about twenty meters from the crack’s upper mouth, where the mineral seam surfaces in a faint, dark line. She will lie down with her flattened ear to the ground, so that the last thing she perceives is the sub-audible vibration of water working its way through soluble stone. The freeze-thaw cycles will take her body into the basalt. Her outline will, in time, become part of the depression’s geography. She finds this comforting. The crack will continue, with or without her. The valley will form, or it won’t. The slow conversation will go on. And for a little while—a century, a millennium, a brief human moment—she was one of the voices in it, listening, asking questions, scraping mineral dust from a fissure with a ceramic tool no bigger than her thumb. That is enough. It has always been enough.
