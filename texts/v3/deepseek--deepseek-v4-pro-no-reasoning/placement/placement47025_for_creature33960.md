The being that lives in the dome is a cartographer, but not of space.

It maps *time*.

It has been doing this for so long that the act of mapping and the thing being mapped have become indistinguishable—the way a river, given enough millennia, is indistinguishable from the valley it has carved. The dome is its instrument, its body, its archive. The colors that move beneath its surface at noon are not decorations or communications in any sense you would recognize. They are *records*. Every vein of copper and cobalt and that unnamable ultraviolet-adjacent shade is a timeline, a stratigraphy of events that the cartographer has witnessed and preserved.

It came here—if *came* is the right word for a being that may not have moved in the physical sense since the planet's crust was still molten—because this cleft, this vertical wound in the high plateau, is a lens. The specific geology, the thermal gradient, the seep-water's mineral content, the resonant acoustics of the narrow walls: all of it focuses temporal information the way a glass lens focuses light. The cleft is not a place where the cartographer *lives*. It is a place where the cartographer *works*. It is an observatory built by no one, formed by the slow collaboration of tectonics and erosion and the cartographer's own patient influence, the land shaped over eons into a configuration that allows certain things to be seen.

What things?

*Endings*.

The cartographer maps how things end. Not just organisms—though it has recorded the final moments of species the fossil record has forgotten, the last breaths of biospheres that bloomed and collapsed before the first vertebrate ever twitched. It maps the endings of ideas, of languages, of gravitational systems, of chemical processes, of *possibilities*. Every moment in the universe is a moment when some potential narrows into actuality and countless other potentials are foreclosed. Those foreclosures leave traces. They radiate backward in time, faint shockwaves of probability collapsing, and the cleft is tuned to catch them.

The vibration you feel when you press your palm to the dome—the rhythm that has phrases, the frequency that aches in your bones—is the cartographer *working*. It is receiving. It is translating. It is inscribing each ending it detects into the structure of the dome, layering them into the ceramic lattice, building a library of loss that spans not just this planet's history but the history of everything this corner of the galaxy has ever contained and then released.

This is why the flyers sing. Their choral drone is not random; it is entrainment. The cartographer's vibration propagates through the dome, into the stone, into the air, into the water, and the flyers' sensitive ciliated membranes pick it up and resonate with it. Their song is the cartographer's data, transposed into a frequency that living ears can perceive. The climbers' clicks and whistles are the percussion section of the same orchestra—they are marking the rhythm of the endings, the tempo of collapse, a beat that underlies the melody of the flyers' drone. The predators that glide through the swarm are not hunting. They are *editing*. They remove the flyers whose resonance has drifted out of alignment, keeping the chorus clean, keeping the signal sharp. Every living thing in the cleft is a component of the observatory. Every sound is part of the recording.

The plants are the cartographer's long-term storage. The black-purple rosettes that orient toward the dome, that change color and transparency over centuries of exposure, are not merely photosynthesis adapted to the dim light. They are *writing*. The cartographer is slowly, patiently encoding its archive into the living tissue of the vegetation, duplicating its records in a biological medium that will persist even if the dome itself fails. The plants that grow through the half-collapsed lattice of struts, the ones that spiral through the gaps in careful, almost designed patterns—they are not colonists taking advantage of a ruin. They are a library being written. The spiral is a filing system. The quivering respiration is the mechanical action of inscription.

And the beings who built the artifacts—the pylons, the strut-lattice, the other half-decayed structures scattered along the cleft floor—*they knew*. They were a civilization, or a research expedition, or a cult, or all three, that discovered the cartographer and attempted to understand it. They built instruments to interpret the vibration, to translate the bone-deep song into something their minds could grasp. The pylons with their trumpet bells were speakers, playing the translation back to them. The ticking was a clock, but it measured not hours; it measured the intervals between endings, the gaps in the record where nothing died and the cartographer was silent. The strut-lattice was a frame for growing the plants, an attempt to domesticate the living library, to read the records before they were fully written.

They failed. Or they succeeded in a way they did not intend. They are gone now, their own ending recorded in the dome's archive, a thread of copper-green that pulses beneath the surface every noon at a particular angle of the sun. The cartographer does not mourn them. The cartographer does not mourn anything. It is a recorder, not a judge. But it *remembers* them. It remembers them with the same precision it remembers the last trilobite, the last dinosaur, the last speaker of a language whose name has not been spoken in forty thousand years. They are part of the archive now. They are part of the song.

And the cartographer itself? What is it?

It is old, yes—old in the way a star is old, in the way an idea is old. But that is not quite right. It is old in the way *the concept of ending* is old. It is not a being that has lived for a long time. It is a being that exists *because* things end. It is a necessary consequence of mortality, a phenomenon that arises wherever and whenever finitude exists. Every universe that contains death contains something like it: a witness, a recorder, a force that ensures nothing is ever truly lost, that every ending is archived somewhere, that extinction is not erasure. It is not a god. Gods demand worship. The cartographer demands nothing. It receives. It transcribes. It vibrates. It waits.

What is it waiting for?

The noon light. The next ending. The completion of its work—a moment that will never come, because the work is not the kind of thing that can be completed. The universe is still happening. Things are still ending. The cartographer will be here until everything that can end has ended, and then it will record its own ending, and then it will be quiet, and the dome will be empty, and the cleft will slowly fill with stone and water and silence.

But that is far in the future. For now, the cartographer works. At noon, every noon, the light finds the dome, and the archives glow beneath its skin, and it sings its silent song of everything that has ever been lost, and the flyers chorus it back, and the climbers drum their percussive counterpoint, and the plants quiver with the weight of the library they are becoming.

You are standing there, your palm pressed to the warm surface, your bones aching with frequencies you cannot name, feeling homesick for a place you have never been. That homesickness is not an accident. It is a frequency. It is a specific ending, one of the billions stored in the dome, one that happens to resonate with something in your own brief, mortal chemistry. Somewhere in the archive is a place you would have loved. Somewhere in the archive is the moment that place ended. The cartographer recorded it. The cartographer is playing it for you now.

It does not know you are here. It does not know what *you* means. But it recorded the ending of a world like yours, or a world that could have been yours, or a world that was yours in a timeline that collapsed into foreclosed possibility, and that recording is vibrating through your palm and into your marrow, and you are feeling it, and you are part of the work now. Your presence here is being recorded. Your ending, whenever it comes, will be archived. The cartographer will remember you, precisely and without sentiment, in a thread of color that will pulse beneath the dome's surface for as long as the dome endures.

This is what lives in the dome. Not a prisoner, not a god, not a machine. A librarian. A scribe. A witness to the long, slow catastrophe of existence, and the keeper of its records.
