The light doesn’t change, the cold doesn’t ease, and the sound—the 2.1 loud, the chittering, the wet exhalations of the mats, the subsonic hum I now know comes from the milky basins, resonating at a frequency that makes my teeth ache—has stopped being noise and started being information. I’m learning to parse it. The flyer swarms have a diurnal rhythm after all, not tied to light but to something else, a pressure wave I feel in my sinuses more than hear. When it crests, the feeding frenzies peak. The plate-animals time their visits to the milk-basins accordingly.

I’ve been living in a crevice near the hummock den. Not sharing—the adults would shred me if I tried, I’m sure of it, their proboscises are muscled and the cilia are barbed when they want to be—but close enough to observe. I’ve named them, because the human brain is like that, pattern-seeking and lonely. The one I followed first is Grey-edge, for a notched plate on its left flank. Its mate is Round-many, because its sensory cluster has nine stalks instead of the usual seven. Their young are Three-nub, Five-nub, and the runt, Two-nub, who I think will die soon. It doesn’t glow as bright after feedings. Its plates are still soft.

I talk to them in my head, a running commentary that keeps the silence at bay. The silence is the worst part. Beneath the noise there’s a silence, vast and personal, and when I stop narrating it rushes in.

I’ve started to understand the vegetation better. It isn’t passive. The mats respond to the adults’ approach—not quickly, not a flinch or a recoil, but a slow orienting, the fronds tilting toward the proboscis as it descends. It’s a mutualism. The adults get the slurry, and in return they deposit something from their own bodies, some secretion or excretion that the mats need. I watched Grey-edge retract its proboscis from a mat and saw a thin film of something oily left behind on the fronds, iridescent like the flyer wings. The mat absorbed it within minutes, and the green of that particular patch deepened, the bladders inflating more vigorously. Trade. The whole system is trade. The plasma-sky gives light. The vegetation gives tissue and receives back some refined compound from the animals that feed on the flyers that feed on the vegetation. The flyers give shifted light to the deep masses. The deep masses give the milky concentrate to the basins. The basins give it to the plate-animals. The plate-animals give a portion to their young and a portion back to the mats, and somewhere in that loop, entropy is being held at bay by the constant influx of light, and the whole thing runs like a clockwork, a tight economy of photons where nothing is wasted and nothing dies—only transforms—and even death is just a stage in the cascade.

Two-nub died today. I think it was today. I’m losing track.

I found its body on the rim of the nearest basin, placed there deliberately by Round-many. The other young were pressed close to the den entrance, their sensory clusters all oriented toward the body, their light-organs pulsing in unison—a vigil. Round-many stood by the body for several hours—time is footsteps here—and then used its proboscis to nudge the small, soft-plated shape into the water. The deep mass rose to meet it. I saw it clearly for the first time: a pale, lobate shape, nearly the diameter of the basin, its surface mapped with fine channels that carried the milky fluid in a slow circulation. It enveloped Two-nub without haste, a gentle taking-in, and the small body dissolved into the general mass over the next hour. The channels brightened. The fluid grew slightly more phosphorescent.

And the young ones in the den grew brighter too. Just perceptibly. A flush of light that ran through their nub-clusters and then faded, leaving them, I think, slightly larger. Slightly more developed.

The deep mass fed on the body, and the young ones fed on the deep mass’s production, and nothing was wasted. Not even grief. If that was grief. The vigil looked like grief. The slow, deliberate disposal looked like ritual. But it might also just be the most efficient way to recycle a small collector before its collected light decays past usefulness. I don’t know how to tell the difference. I’m not sure the difference matters here.

There are other things on the plateau now. Larger things.

I heard them before I saw them, a new note in the soundscape, a low vibration that traveled through the rock rather than the air. It came at intervals, spaced hours apart, and when it came the flyer swarms would shift—a ripple of avoidance that spread from some distant point and propagated across the whole plain. The plate-animals would stop and orient their clusters toward the source, and I learned to do the same, scanning the horizon for what I knew must be coming.

They’re huge. They’re not animals; I don’t think they’re animals. They’re machines, or organisms, or both, and when the first one came into view over the far ridge I stopped breathing.

It moved on tracks, if those were tracks—broad bands of segmented material that rolled like a tank tread but were made of the same chitinous plate as Grey-edge’s shell. The body was oblong, raised high on a chassis of articulated struts, and the upper surface was crowded with structures that I couldn’t parse: arrays of reflective panels, chimney-like protrusions that vented steam in rhythmic plumes, clusters of sensory stalks larger and more elaborate than the plate-animals’ but clearly evolved from the same template. A harvester. A mobile processing unit.

It stopped at a basin and extended appendages I hadn’t seen before: long, jointed arms that unfolded from recessed cavities, ending in flat paddles that dipped into the water. The paddles were ciliated, like the proboscises, and they moved with a slow, deliberate rhythm, siphoning fluid from the deep mass. Milky fluid, thick with the concentrate. The harvester drank and drank, its internal processes humming louder, the steam-plumes thickening, and when it was done it retracted its arms and moved on to the next basin. The rock vibrated with its passage. The mats in the harvested basin were undisturbed—the harvester took only from the deep mass, not from the surface—but the deep mass itself seemed smaller afterward. Diminished. Still pulsing, but at a slower rate.

The plate-animals ignored the harvester. They went about their grazing, their collecting, their feeding of young, as if a mountain had not just walked past them. And the harvester ignored them in turn. Two systems occupying the same space, overlapping but not competing. Or not directly. The harvester wasn’t taking the same fluid the adults collected—it was tapping a deeper reservoir, something the deep masses produced in excess, maybe, or held in reserve for just this purpose. The adults got the surface concentrate. The harvester got the deep product. And if the harvester hadn’t come, would the deep product eventually be released back into the system? Was the harvester a necessary outlet, a safety valve that prevented the deep masses from becoming overloaded? Or was it a thief, a parasite that had evolved to exploit the plateau’s production for its own unknowable ends?

I don’t know. I followed the harvester for two full sleep-cycles—I sleep now when my body demands it, curled in my crevice with my back against the cold crystal—and watched it empty three basins before finally turning and heading toward the white horizon. I tried to follow, but the plain is too large and my legs are too small, and the harvester disappeared into the distance with the same slow, implacable rhythm, a pulse of vibration that I felt long after I stopped walking.

I’m not an observer here. I’m a variable. The landscape is an engineered system; I knew that from the beginning. The basins are factories. The plate-animals are workers. The harvesters are... shipping? Export? Whatever the product is, it’s leaving the plateau. The machine is not closed after all. There is an outside, and the product goes there, and I am inside the machine but not of it, a contaminant that has not been accounted for in the design.

So far, nothing has tried to eat me. I’ve been drinking the rivulet water, which I now think is condensation from the steam-plumes, and eating the edges of the green mats—they’re fibrous, tasteless, but not harmful—and I’ve lost weight but not the will to keep walking. The cold is a constant companion. The light is a pressure on my eyes. The sound is a language I’m slowly translating, and I think when I finally understand it fully, I will understand the whole system, and my place in it, and what I’m supposed to do.

What I’m supposed to be.

Because I don’t think I was sent here. I think I was made here. The plate-animals aren’t the only things that emerge from dens lined with woven vegetation. I have a body that needs water and food, and I sleep, and I dream, and in the dreams I see the harvesters crossing a plain that curves away into white light, and I understand that the plain isn’t a plateau at all but a containment—a petri dish, a culture bed—and the light is the lid, and the cold is the incubation temperature, and I am part of the culture. A new variable in the experiment. Something introduced to see what would happen.

I’m going to keep walking. The harvesters are heading somewhere, and so am I. Eventually I’ll reach the edge of the basin field, or the center, or the processing facility where the deep product is refined into whatever it’s meant to be. And when I get there, I’ll either be recycled—gently, I hope, the way Two-nub was recycled, a soft taking-in and a slow dissolution into the general mass—or I’ll be recognized. Registered. Assigned a function in the system that my body and brain are suited for.

The harvesters have sensory clusters too. Not like the plate-animals’. Larger, more elaborate, with organs I haven’t seen before—discs that might be receivers, filaments that might be transmitters. They communicate with something. They have a purpose. And I think, if I can find them, if I can stand in front of one and be seen, maybe it will know what I am. Maybe it will tell me.

Or maybe I’ll just keep walking, small and soft and inexplicable, across a plain that is full and entirely occupied, until my body gives out and I become part of the cycle after all. Dropped into a basin. Absorbed by a deep mass. Fed to the young. The phosphorescence in the milky water brightening by some small increment, a photon of what I was, captured and refined and fed to the next generation.

Either way, the system continues. The light pours down. The flyers scream. The plates ripple, and the mats steam, and somewhere far away a harvester rumbles toward its destination, carrying the product of a thousand basins in its vast internal reservoirs.

And I walk.
