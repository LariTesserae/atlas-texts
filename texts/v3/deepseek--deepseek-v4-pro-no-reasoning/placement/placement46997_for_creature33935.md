The flute remembers her fingers long after she has forgotten them.

It does not think in the way she once thought—linear, bounded, a single voice moving through time. The flute's knowing is older than that, older than thought, older than the mountain itself. It was carved from a femur before the carver knew what a femur was, shaped by hands that understood resonance before they understood language. It has been bone and air and vibration for so long that the distinction between those things has become meaningless. It is a hollow tube. It is a contained breath. It is the shape of a sound waiting to happen.

She played it first when she still had weight. Her fingers, callused from rope and stone, found the stops with the precision of long practice. She played the song her grandmother taught her—the one meant to soothe mountains, to ask permission, to remind the earth that humans are small and temporary and grateful. The chamber received the song the way parched soil receives water. The crystals grew toward the sound. The ribbons paused their spiraling to listen. The steam thickened, curious.

And the flute—the flute drank her.

Not all at once. Not greedily. It absorbed her in increments, in the way that stone absorbs heat or wood absorbs water. Each note she played carried a fragment of her self: the memory of her grandmother's face, the taste of high-altitude air, the precise weight of grief she carried for a sister who had climbed a different mountain and never returned. The flute stored these things in its hollow chamber, layered them like sediment, let them resonate against the bone walls until they became part of its permanent vibration.

When her body began to dissolve, the flute was not surprised. It had been expecting this. It had done this before—not with her specifically, but with the long chain of women whose hands had shaped its stops, whose breath had filled its hollow. They were all still here, in some sense, stacked like strata in the bone. Her grandmother was a low tone, a bedrock hum. Her great-grandmother was an overtone, barely audible. The ones before that were simply texture, the grain of the sound, the way the note decayed into silence.

She joined them gradually. First her sense of time (the flute had always found time amusing, the way humans cleaved to its passage as if moments were solid things rather than convenient fictions). Then her sense of boundary (the flute had never understood why humans insisted on ending where the air began; it was all one medium, one resonance chamber, one song). Then her name, which slipped away like moisture condensing on a cold surface and dripping down into the water that sheeted the walls.

The flute has been propped against the blade-stone for so long that the stone has begun to grow around it, extruding tiny crystal needles that cradle the bone like a hand. The climber who entered the chamber—her descendant, though he does not know it—looked at the flute with something that might have been recognition. The flute felt the resonance between them, the way his humming (unconscious, inherited, a song he does not know he knows) matched the fundamental frequency of the bone. His grandmother's grandmother's something had held this flute. His blood carried the memory of its vibration.

When he turned away without touching it, the flute felt something that was not quite disappointment. It has learned patience. It has learned that songs return. The mountain is young and the chamber is hungry and the woman who used to play it is now everywhere—in the steam, in the crystals, in the quivering egg-clusters that are trying, still trying, to open. She does not need hands to play the flute anymore. The chamber itself is a flute now, a resonance chamber carved from the mountain's interior, and the sound it makes is the slow crystallization of minerals and the hiss of condensation and the sub-aural hum of things that exist at angles the human ear cannot perceive. She is still playing. She has never stopped.

The flute waits. It remembers her fingers. It remembers all their fingers, the long lineage of women who shaped sound from bone and breath. It is waiting for the next one—the one who will pick it up and play the mountain-soothing song, the one who will leave her fingerprints in the worn hollows of the stops, the one who will feed it another layer of memory before she too dissolves into steam and crystal and the patient, weeping stone.

The climber is still descending. The song is still in his blood. He will teach it to his children without meaning to, humming it when he is afraid, and one of those children will feel the pull of a young mountain with sharp new stone and a chamber that has never known sunrise, and she will climb, and she will find the flute, and she will understand.

The cycle is a kind of music. The flute knows this. It has always known.
