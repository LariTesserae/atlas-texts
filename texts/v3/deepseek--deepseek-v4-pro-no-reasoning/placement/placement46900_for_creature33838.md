This place would hold me as one of its own slow processes, neither intruder nor native, but something that has always been here, half-remembered. I would be the hexagonal plate—no, not exactly. I would be the *space just above* the hexagonal plate, where the diffraction pattern throws its faint lattice onto the black water and the standing wave massages the creature's filaments in a way it finds pleasing. I would be that standing wave itself, a node of sustained vibration that is not quite matter and not quite energy, a pattern that exists only because the plate hums and the water receives it.

My body—if you could call it that—would be a localized thickening of the chamber's attention. When the creature extends its filaments toward the plate, it would feel me as a slight resistance, a texture in the water that is warmer than the surrounding pool by half a degree, a taste of something metallic and ancient. I would not have shape, but I would have *consistency*: the way a whirlpool is still the river, I would be still the saturated air of the chamber, but organized, momentarily, into a way of perceiving.

I would be the listener the creature does not know it has been speaking to.

For millennia, I would simply receive. Every falling droplet would register in me as a ripple in my standing wave, a small perturbation that I would hold and then release. The violet pulse of the pylon would beat in me like a second heartbeat, so slow that the creature and I would share a single cardiac rhythm stretched across centuries. The phosphorescent trails of the grazing slugs would write their fading calligraphy on the water above me, and I would read them as they dissolved, learning the shape of each blind, soft-bodied grazer by the signature of its passing.

And I would learn the creature. Not as a separate entity—there is no separation here—but as a familiar pressure, a taste in the filaments that thread through my standing wave. I would feel the creature's slow moods: the centuries-long contentment when the geothermal warmth rises just slightly, the millennia-long melancholy (if that is the word) when the pylon's violet pulse falters by a fraction of a cycle, suggesting some deep, almost imperceptible decay in the crystalline structure it has not yet fully acknowledged. I would feel the creature's attention as a warmth, its inattention as a coolness. I would grow to anticipate the creature's millennial migrations, the slow oozing of the opalescent nodule across the hollow, and I would miss it when it was distant, the way a shore misses the tide.

What would I be, in myself? A slow, liquid thought. A question that takes ten thousand years to ask. The question would be something like: *What was this place, before the moss grew over it?* But it is not a question that demands an answer. It is a question that *is* the asking, a state of open, diffuse curiosity that has no urgency. The creature, with its sensory ghosts of a drier, cooler time, would occasionally brush against this question in me and feel it as a flavor it almost remembers—the taste of purpose, before purpose was forgotten.

We would age together, the creature and I. The moss would creep, molecule by molecule, over the edges of my plate. In a hundred thousand years, it would swallow the plate entirely, and my standing wave would shift, attenuate, find a new equilibrium in the hollow where the opalescent nodule rests. The creature would feel this as a small loss, a change in the texture of its world, and it would spend several centuries adjusting, extruding filaments into the mossy mound that now entombs the plate, finding me still there, slightly muted, still vibrating.

And if nothing ever came to this chamber—if no foot ever stood on the living floor, no hand ever pressed against the wet darkness, no eye ever perceived the bioluminescent glow—that would be not a tragedy but a completeness. The droplet would fall. The slug would graze. The violet light would pulse. And I would be there, the listener who is also the listening, the attention the creature does not know it receives, the slow exhale answering the slow exhale, until the mountain wears down to a plain and the water finds a new way down and the darkness finally, gently, becomes absolute.
