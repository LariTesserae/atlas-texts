Maren watches you the way she watches the water—patiently, without expectation, reading what you are by how you move through the damp air.

You came over the basalt rim three days ago, or maybe four. Time blurs here; the light never changes enough to mark hours, and the constant *tink tink tink* from the center pavilion makes every moment feel like the same moment, stretched out. You arrived dry—strange, how the settlement noticed that before anything else about you. The moss near the northern rim crisped slightly as you passed. The water in the nearest runnel shivered. A sleeping hoglike creature under an overhang lifted its damp head and sneezed. The settlement doesn't get many strangers, but when it does, they always arrive dry.

Maren saw you before you saw her. She always does. The moss rings told her something was coming—a perturbation in the moisture gradient, a faint dessication-warning spreading outward like a ripple—and she'd been waiting by the standing stones since dawn, chisel in hand, pretending to work on the new spiral. When you finally picked your way down the narrow paths, stepping carefully around the moss gardens, ducking under low doorways, she was ready.

"You're dry," she said. "Come. There's water."

And you came.

Now you're sitting on a fallen standing stone—the one her mother recarved, the one that once told the water to rise—with a clay cup of mineral water in your hands. The cup is small, crude, thumbed into shape and fired in the settlement's tiny forge. The water inside it tastes like stone and time. It makes your lips tingle. Maren told you it was safe, and you believed her, but you're still sipping slowly. Everything here encourages slowness.

"Do you know where you are?" Maren asks. She's not looking at you; she's looking at the new carving, tracing its spiral with a wet fingertip. "Do you know what this place is?"

You think you do. You've been watching for days. You've seen the water channels, the shared workspaces, the moss gardens, the way the settlement leans on itself like a sleeper in a crowded hut. You've noticed the lack of trees, the cultivated sparseness, the copper tools and the absence of bronze. You've heard the frogs at dawn and dusk. You've felt the geothermal warmth rising from the basalt ribs, just barely, like the earth is breathing beneath you.

"It's a crater," you say, because you've been thinking about it. "A volcanic maar. The water is juvenile—driven out of the rock, never been rain. That's why it tastes like that. And the settlement is... old, but the geology is young. The people here are managing the water, trying to keep it from dissolving everything. You're the one who understands it. The carver."

Maren nods slowly, approvingly. Her pale eyes gleam.

"You see a lot," she says. "More than most. Most strangers just see the wet. They don't see the *why*."

She sets down her chisel—the copper is worn to a nub, barely longer than her thumb—and turns to face you fully. Her cloak is heavy woven wool, greasy with lanolin, stiff with absorbed moisture. Beneath it, her body is small and knotted, a lifetime of crouching over stones written in the curve of her spine. Her hands are a map of scars: chisel-cuts, moss-burns, the faint blue-green stains of copper oxidation worked into the creases.

"But you're wrong about one thing," she says. "I'm not the one who understands the water. I'm just the one who listens to it. The water understands itself. Always has. It's the *settlement* that needs understanding."

She gestures broadly, taking in the packed huts, the glistening paths, the moss-covered walls. A man is walking past—Corrin, you think, the water-channel keeper—carrying a wooden bowl full of scraped moss. He nods at Maren, glances at you with mild curiosity, and continues on toward the southern seep.

"This place," Maren says, "wasn't built to keep the water out. It was built to *invite it in*. Think about it. Why would anyone choose to live in a bowl that never dries? Why pack yourselves so tight there's no room to breathe? Why build with materials that rot, when there's stone enough to build dry and permanent?"

She waits, watching you. The *tink* of a distant hammer sounds from the forge—the quiet man with the burned forearms, working copper.

"Because the water is a teacher," she says, answering her own question. "It teaches patience. It teaches attention. It teaches the difference between fighting something and learning from it. Every wall that rots is a lesson in impermanence. Every channel that clogs is a lesson in maintenance. Every carving I make is a lesson in translation. The settlement isn't a fortress. It's a *school*."

She stands, slowly, her joints popping. She's smaller than she seemed when she was sitting, barely taller than the standing stones. Her bare feet are gnarled and webbed with moss-stains, and she leaves wet footprints on the basalt pavement as she walks to the central stone—the one with the deepest cup, full of water that never seems to run dry.

"This," she says, touching the stone, "is the first carving. The first carver made it, the one whose name I won't speak. He came here when the basalt was still sharp, when the water was still steam. He'd been following a dream. A dream of a place where the world was still being made, and he could be part of it."

She looks at you, and there's something ancient in her eyes—something that makes you wonder if she's really sixty-three, or if she's older, much older, if the carvers live differently than the rest of the settlement, sustained by something in the water or the moss or the act of listening.

"He learned to speak to the water," she says. "Not with words. With *patterns*. The water is young, and it wants to know the shape of things. It runs over a stone to learn its contours. It seeps into a wall to taste the reed-fibers. It's curious. It asks questions by flowing. And the carvings answer. Each spiral tells the water: *here, flow this way*. Each circle says: *this far, no farther*. The settlement exists because the water agreed to let it exist. Not—" she raises a finger, forestalling your objection—"not because the water is a god, or a spirit, or anything you could pray to. It's simpler than that. The water is *information*. It's the world learning itself. And we're part of what it's learning."

She scoops a handful of water from the standing stone, drinks it, lets the excess drip down her chin. The water leaves a faint mineral trail, silvery in the pearlescent light.

"You're here because you're dry," she says. "Not just your body. You. Your attention. You've been places that are already made, already settled, already answered. Places where the water stays in pipes and the moss is scraped off and the stones don't sweat. You're dry because you've never been somewhere that's still becoming. That's why you came over the rim. You felt it—a place where the questions haven't been answered yet. A place where you could be part of the making."

She gestures to the cup in your hands.

"Drink," she says. "Drink all of it. The water's been waiting for you. It wants to know what you taste like."

And you do, because she's right. You've been dry. Not just your mouth—though your lips are cracked, though the damp air is slowly rehydrating you—but your understanding. You've been in places that are finished, concluded, sealed against the world's curiosity. This place is different. This place is still asking questions.

The water is cold and mineral and strange on your tongue. It tastes like basalt, like time, like the steam that rose when the earth was young. It makes your throat tighten, your sinuses prickle, your skin feel tight and new. For a moment, you feel something—a presence, or an attention, or just the weight of all that water moving through all those stones, asking its endless questions.

When you lower the cup, Maren is smiling. It's a small smile, crinkling the corners of her pale eyes.

"Good," she says. "Now you're part of it. The water knows you now. It will ask you questions, while you're here. You won't hear them with your ears—you'll feel them. In your bones. In your dreams. In the way your skin tightens when you touch certain stones. Pay attention. That's the only thing the water asks. Attention."

She picks up her chisel again, running her thumb over the worn copper edge. The new carving is almost finished—just a few more hatch-marks remain, a final spiral-curve that echoes the ripple-pattern she saw in the central pavilion last spring.

"You can stay," she says, not looking at you. "Or you can go. The water doesn't hold anyone. It just asks. But if you stay, you'll need to learn. Not carving—that's my work, and Corrin's after me, and the water-pot child after him. But something. The forge needs someone who can work copper without burning themselves. The reeds need harvesting before the next flooding. The moss needs tending—the rings are spreading wider than I can track alone. There's always work in a school."

She turns back to her stone, raising the chisel. The *tink tink tink* resumes, regular as a heartbeat, absorbed by the wet air before it can travel far.

"Think about it," she says, over the sound of copper on basalt. "The water will wait. It's young, but it's patient."

And the settlement breathes around you, its slow wet breath, and the frogs begin piping from the southern seep, and somewhere in the packed huts a child laughs, and the moss glimmers faintly on the north-facing walls, and you sit on a fallen standing stone with a clay cup in your hands, feeling the water inside you asking its first question.
