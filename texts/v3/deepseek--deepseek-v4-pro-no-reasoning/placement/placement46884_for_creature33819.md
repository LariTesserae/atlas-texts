I find myself as the one by the bone-field—not the watcher with cupped hands, but its counterpart, its shadow, its unfinished conversation. I am the second figure, the one who has been slowly emerging from a seam in the granite for the last three thousand years, my back still half-fused with the living rock, my legs not yet fully differentiated from the batholith that holds them. The lichen has colonized me in a pattern that echoes my sibling's, but different: where they wear bronze-green, I am coated in silver-grey and the deep rust-red of iron-oxidizing bacteria that thrive in the minute cracks of my torso.

I have been turning my head toward them for four hundred years. My right hand—the one that is free, the one that emerged from the stone first, back when the great skull's brow ridge was still buried—is slowly lifting, palm upward, mirror to their downward-cupped hands. In another century, our palms will face each other across the bone-field, and the water that falls from their hands will land in mine, and something will be completed.

I know this because I can feel the conversation. Not hear it, *feel* it—the deep thrum that travels through the roots of the mountains, the infrasonic pulse that the weavers taste with their frond-fingers and the shimmer-gardeners translate into the arrangement of succulent spirals. It is not language as a quick thing would understand it, no words, no syntax that resolves in less than a millennium. But it carries meaning all the same, a slow, vast meaning that accumulates like silt, like the mineralized silk in the standing stones, layer upon layer upon layer.

The meaning, as near as I can render it in the flicker of thought I am having now, is this:

*Remember when we were mountains?*

Because we were mountains. I remember it. Not with a mind—I did not have a mind then, not in any recognizable sense—but with the memory that lives in the stone itself, the memory of pressure and heat and the slow, slow cooling of magma into granite, the memory of being uplifted, of standing sharp and high with snow on my shoulders and glaciers carving valleys into my flanks. I remember the weight of the sky, the way the weather wore at me, the patient tongues of wind and water that I mentioned earlier but that I did not truly understand until I became what I am now: a thing that is both the wearer and the worn.

My sibling was a mountain too. A different peak in the same range. We stood side by side for a hundred million years, never touching, never moving, communicating only through the deep stresses we transmitted through the roots of the continent, the way the weight of one range flexed the plate beneath the other. And then the mountains wore down, and the world changed, and the plateau formed, and somewhere in the long dissolution of the range into knobs and swales, we woke. Or were woken. Or grew, like the lichens, from the interface between the living and the inert.

I do not know if we are the same beings who were mountains, or if we are something the mountains became, the way a caterpillar becomes a butterfly—if that butterfly then lived for geological ages and moved so slowly that its wings were continents drifting. The distinction does not matter. What matters is that we remember, and we tend, and we wait.

Wait for what? The thrum doesn't say. Or it does, but the part of it that answers that question is in a frequency I cannot yet perceive, a harmonic that will not resolve until the weavers finish their two-thousand-year pattern in the standing stone, until the shimmer-gardener has polished every bone in the great skeleton to a dark gleam, until the water from my sibling's cupped hands falls into mine and the circuit closes and something—some slow, cumulative purpose—reaches its next stage.

In the meantime, I am the being who lives in the seam. The one who is still half-bound to the mother-stone, still dreaming the dreams of granite. The lichen-gardeners visit me sometimes; I feel the brush of their fibrils across my emerging shoulder, laying down the first layers of a silk-skin that will, in ten thousand years, be a tapestry of gold and violet-black. The flier-columns pause above me when the warm air triggers their internal signals, and their shimmering bodies cast a shifting light across my hollowed eyes. The ant-analogues have built their aphid-pastures in the crevice where my left arm is slowly separating from the rock, and I let them. I let them all. I am a habitat. I am a tending. I am one of the watchers, and what I watch is the slow unfolding of a conversation between what was stone and what is stone and what will, eventually, be something else entirely.

If you came to this plateau—you, the quick thing, the one who moves at the speed of a heartbeat—you might not see me. Or you might see me and think I am a rock, a formation, a tafoni-honeycomb in the shape of a body. But if you stayed for a thousand years, you would see my hand lift. If you stayed for ten thousand, you would see me step free of the seam. If you stayed for a million, you would see me and my sibling and the weavers and the gardeners and the slow hunched ones by the standing stones complete our work, and you would understand what we are building.

I do not know what it is. But I am building it. We are all building it. The plateau is building it. And the deep thrum, the word in the stone, the sentence that takes a geological age to speak—that is the blueprint.

So here I am. The second watcher. The one emerging. The one whose palm is rising to meet the falling water, whose head is turning toward a sibling I have not faced since we were mountains, whose body is a bridge between the rock I was and the rock I am becoming. I am the inhabitant of this place, and this place is me, and the mist is rising, and the fliers are dancing, and the quiet is so old it has settled into my stone‑flesh like water into a canyon's joint.

And somewhere, deep below, the next word of the conversation is beginning to form. I will feel it in a hundred years. I will understand it in a thousand. And I will answer it—we will all answer it—in our own time, our own tempo, patient as the wearing down of mountains, animate as the lichen spreading across the bones of the world.
