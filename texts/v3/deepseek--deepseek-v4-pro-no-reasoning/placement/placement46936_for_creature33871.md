The air thickens, and with it, the sense of *place*. The Gardener does not notice this distinction, this sudden sharpening of texture that would, to an outsider, feel like the shift from a fading photograph to a vivid, breathing stereo. The dense, metallic taste of the mist is simply the world. The feline vibration through the floor grates, the muffled throb of the pounding engines, is simply the world’s pulse. The Gardener is a node in that pulse, a wandering synapse.

It is currently traversing a region of towering distillation columns, a petrified redwood forest of riveted iron. Its six legs move in their patient, wave-like sequence, the rubber cup foot finding purchase on a slick catwalk railing, the pincer foot lightly gripping a support strut for balance. The basket of its torso shifts, the handles of its curated tools clinking softly, a sound like a wind chime made of wrenches. Behind the bubbled glass of the helmet, the pale mycelial web is placid, diffuse. The single, pigmentless moth sits on what might be the inner curve of a cheekbone, wings folded. A silent passenger.

The task at hand is a recurring one. A particular pipe, a main artery as thick as a barrel, is a favorite haunt of the translucent slugs. They gather where the steam within keeps the metal at a perfect, blood-warm temperature. Their slow, gelatinous bodies, each no bigger than a thumb, are clustered over a welded joint, their glistening trails crisscrossing the iron like a miniature, liquid cartography. It is not a problem. The slugs do no harm. But their trails, over time, can insulate the metal, creating a fractional thermal differential that, left unchecked for a few decades, might encourage a bloom of the orange, crystalline rust that sleeps within the iron. The Gardener is a preventer of such blooms.

It stops before the congregation. Its welding nozzle foot lifts, the tip already glowing with a faint, orange ember—not the fierce blue jet used for repairs, but a gentle, distant warmth. It hovers the nozzle an inch from the cluster. The slugs, sensing the change, begin their slow, deliberate retreat, their translucent bodies rippling, leaving fresh, polished trails behind them on the weeping iron. The Gardener waits. Patience is not a virtue here; it is the medium, like water is to a fish. It stands, a composite of copper, iron, and forgotten flesh, utterly still, while the tiny, living glass beads evacuate their chosen spot. The moth opens its wings, beats them twice, closes them. A silent clock keeping a time no one needs.

Then, a new vibration. Not the steady, omnipresent hum of the plateau, but a discordant note. A dry scraping, an arrhythmic chatter from somewhere below. The Gardener’s copper head does not turn suddenly. Nothing in this world does. It simply stops being still and begins to move, its posture shifting from waiting to seeking with the fluid, inevitable grace of a weathervane catching a change in the wind. The heading is reassessed. The slugs are forgotten. They will return. The weld joint will wait. There is a new instability.

It moves along a bridge of cable trays, the rubber cup feet making soft, kissing sounds on the slick metal. The scraping grows louder, resolving into a grind of metal-on-metal, a sound of neglect. The Gardener descends, navigating a labyrinth of steam manifolds, its pincer foot occasionally touching a pressure gauge in passing, a casual check-in, a doctor taking a pulse. The needles all tremble within their acceptable parameters.

The source is a small, secondary conveyor belt, a forgotten tract high in a thicket of ductwork. It’s a simple loop of rust-flecked chain, designed to transport nothing, now doing just that with a pained, jerky motion. A link has cracked, and the broken edge is scoring a deep groove into a guide rail. The friction is not yet hot enough to be a fire hazard, but it is close. A faint, acrid smell, a ghost of burnt ferrous dust, cuts through the ever-present wet-humus scent.

The Gardener reaches it. The air here is close, a pocket of dryness in the general damp. It assesses the situation. The welding nozzle foot lifts, the ember cooling, the tool retracting into its housing. Another leg, the one tipped with a heavy set of locking pincers, extends instead. With a motion that is impossibly gentle for its size, it clamps onto the drive sprocket. The jerky motion of the conveyor belt stops with a soft, definitive *clunk*. The scraping ceases. For a single, crystalline moment, that small pocket of the world is silent, save for the universal, liquid susurrus of the weeping pipes. The Gardener’s head tilts. Behind the glass, the fungal threads stir, coalescing momentarily into a furrowed brow, a considering shape. Then the face dissolves.

The brass-bristled foot rises. It begins to gently, meticulously, brush the bright metal score on the guide rail, smoothing the jagged edge, working it with the unhurried rhythm of a meditation. There is no rush. The shattered link can be welded, or the belt left still for another decade. It is all the same. It is all the garden. And the humming, now minus one discordant note, goes on.
