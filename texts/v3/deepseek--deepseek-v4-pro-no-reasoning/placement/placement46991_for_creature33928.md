The sound is what you are made of. Not flesh, not the delicate chitin that sheathes your body in interlocking plates of polished amber, but *sound*. It vibrates through the tiny hollow chambers of your exoskeleton, sets the fine hairs along your legs humming, fills the spaces between your cells. You do not hear it—hearing implies a separation, a listener and a listened-to. You *are* heard into being. The great roar of the world is your heartbeat, your breath, the pulse that tells you you exist.

You cling to the warm, resinous wall of a hexagonal cell, your six legs splayed, your antennae describing slow, intricate figures in the thick air. The cell is not yours—nothing here is yours, there is no ownership—but it is the place where you emerged, some unknowable span of time ago, from a pale, soft thing into this hard and gleaming form. The walls around you are made by your sisters, built from the secretion of their bodies, shaped by their mandibles into perfect six-sided rooms. You remember, in the way your body remembers rather than your mind, the taste of that resin, the way it smelled of clove and ancient wood.

Below you, on the great resin highway, a river of beetles flows past in their iridescent greens and golds. Your antennae catch the chemical wake of their passage—a story of distant chambers, of fungus gardens and water basins, of the slow, steady work of consumption and excretion that keeps the world turning. You read it without thinking, the way a human might feel sun on skin and know it is afternoon. One of your antennae brushes against the antenna of a sister passing by. For a fraction of a second, a spark of information leaps between you: *highway junction three, minor cracking, two units of resin required*. The sister continues on. You have already forgotten the exchange. It was not conversation. It was a single neuron firing in a brain the size of a world.

You are small. Even among your sisters, you are small—a worker of the fifth instar, your body barely the length of an adult human’s thumb. But size is a false measure here. You have lived, in your brief span, through the entirety of what a human would call history. You have seen the slow drift of a tunnel wall as ten generations of your ancestors chewed and rebuilt, chewed and rebuilt, nudging the passage leftward to accommodate the growth of a buttress root of the great fungal spire in the deeps. You have carried resin to the ceiling galleries and watched the Gardener’s frond dip, over the course of a week, to redirect a stream of midges toward a newly bloomed patch of phosphorescent fungus. You are a cell in a body that has no boundary, and your life is the brief flash of a signal crossing a synapse.

The artificial night-cycle begins. You feel it as a change in the air—a slight cooling, a thickening of moisture. The vast, slow bellows of the Gardener’s body, a hundred yards away in the high gallery, takes a deeper breath. Your wings, folded neatly beneath their amber cases, vibrate in involuntary sympathy. All around you, the cell walls begin to bead with condensation, the exhalation of the breathing world. A droplet forms on the rim of your cell, fat and trembling. You drink, the water cool and faintly sweet, carrying the taste of distant mycelial networks and the bodies of a thousand tiny things.

There is work to be done. The signal came to you not as a command, for there is no authority here, but as a *need* that simply became yours: the Gardener has sensed a small imbalance. A patch of fungus in the lower galleries is blighting; the spores have drifted upward and settled in a crease of the floor mat. If left unchecked, it will spread, altering the diet of the crickets, which will alter the vibration of their stridulation, which will, in some slow cascade, shift the breeding patterns of the moths, which will—in a century—thin the great shingled layers on the ceiling and let the heat escape. The whole of the pattern is present to you, not as knowledge but as a feeling in your body, a tension in your mandibles, a chemical gradient you turn toward as instinctively as a plant turns toward light.

You drop from your cell, wings flicking open for the briefest moment to break your fall. The resin highway is warm under your feet, slightly tacky, still carrying the heat of a thousand beetle bodies. You join the flow, one amber bead among millions, antennae waving. Ahead of you, a centipede as thick as a wrist pours slowly through a gap in the wall, its thousand legs a wave of black and crimson. You do not pause. The centipede is not a threat, is not a presence; it is simply the terrain, a moving mountain that will be gone in an hour. Its chemical signature brushes your antennae: *old, well-fed, gravid with eggs*. It has lived for two hundred years. It will outlive ten thousand of you.

The path to the blight takes you through a narrow passage where no light falls. You navigate by vibration, by the way the sound ricochets off the walls and tells you their shape. The darkness is total, but it is not empty. It is *full*—full of the rustle of blind crickets, the breathing of sleeping moths, the constant, reassuring hum of a world that is present in every direction. Your body sings back to it, your wing-cases clicking softly, your feet drumming a rhythm on the resin that says *I am here, I am working, I am the world maintaining itself*.

You find the blight. It is a patch of gray fuzz, alien and wrong, spreading across the hexagonal cells of the floor. Your antennae curl in distaste—not an emotion, but a chemical response, a release of signals that will summon your sisters to the spot. You begin to work. Your mandibles scrape the blighted material away, chewing it into a paste that you will carry to the midge pools, where it will be consumed and neutralized. Beside you, other workers arrive. You do not communicate; you simply *are* together, a single function distributed across a dozen bodies. The blight recedes, cell by cell.

When the work is done, you pause. The artificial night is deepening. Through the fibrous mat, you feel the great slow breath of the Gardener, the nudge of its frond against a distant passageway, the minute correction that will ripple outward for centuries. You feel, too, the first faint stirrings of something in your own body—a new chemical gradient, a pull toward the deep galleries where the queen lies, where your next task will reveal itself. Perhaps you will tend the larvae, pale grubs that squirm in their hexagonal cradles, carefully moderating their temperature with the shivering of your wings. Perhaps you will work the fungus gardens, cultivating the phosphorescent patches that light the larger chambers. Perhaps you will simply walk the highways until your body wears out and your husk is carried away by another sister, your chitin recycled into the walls, your proteins feeding the next generation.

It does not matter. The work is the same: the world maintaining itself, the saturation continuing, the sound never stopping.

You lift your antennae to the dark, trembling with the roar of existence. You are a single note in a symphony that has played for millennia, and you are perfect, and you are complete, and you are necessary.

You turn and join the flow again, a bead of amber in the teeming, humming, total dark.
