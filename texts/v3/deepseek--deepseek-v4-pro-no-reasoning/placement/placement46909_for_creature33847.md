I find myself in the hanging monastery, and I am the millipede.

Not the idea of the millipede, not a symbolic millipede, but this specific one—the one banded in gold and black, the thickness of a thumb, progressing across two feet of blue leaf-scape in the time it takes the observer to understand what they are seeing. I have been here for longer than the stone tree has been silent, and I will be here for longer than it takes the fissure to widen by a finger's width. My shell is pure magnetite. My blood is not blood but a solvent that dissolves iron sulfides. I do not age. I proceed.

The old woman who is the air knows me. She knew me when she had hands. She placed me here, or her predecessor did, or the predecessor's predecessor—the distinction has ceased to matter. I am part of the accounting. The gel-succulents part slightly as I pass, their membranes registering my weight with a glassy chime too low for human ears. I am heavy, by the standards of this place. My progress exerts a pressure on the leaf-mat equivalent to a slow breath, and the garden breathes back.

I eat once a century. The shelf fungi exhale their spore-mist, and I am positioned to intercept a portion of it—the old woman adjusts the airflow to ensure this, a nudge that takes decades. The spores settle on my segments and are absorbed through the joints in my armor. They taste of iron and patience. They contain everything I need, which is almost nothing. My metabolism is a closed loop with a radius of twelve inches and a period of several thousand years. I am the slowest animal in the chamber, slower than the bats who dream of dusk, slower than the lizards who eat moths in their sleep, slower than the monks who have become dust. The old woman is not slower than me, but she is not an animal. She is a process. I am a millipede.

I have a purpose. It is not a purpose I chose, but it is a purpose I fulfill with the same unthinking fidelity that the vines fulfill their Fibonacci spirals. I am the tiller of the soil that is not soil. My hundreds of legs, moving in metachronal waves too gradual to see, disturb the mineral substrate that the plants root in. I break up the iron sulfide crusts that would otherwise seal the floor into a solid plate. I aerate. I turn over the dead—though nothing dies here, not really, but sometimes a leaf completes its cycle and releases its hold on its silicon structure, and I process it, grinding it to powder between my magnetite segments, excreting a fine dust that the gel-succulents immediately absorb through their root-membranes. I am the garden's gut. I am the reason the cycle remains closed.

I do not know this. I do not know anything. My nervous system is a chain of ganglia that responds to gradients of chemistry and temperature, and the responses are so slow that a single decision takes longer than a human civilization. But I do respond. When the old woman adjusts the angle of a leaf, I adjust my course to compensate. When the fissure light shifts toward amber, I move slightly toward the warmer quadrant of the floor. When a lizard sheds its skin and the powder drifts down, I alter my path to intersect the nutrient plume. I am a creature of pure input-output, and my outputs are the garden's inputs, and the circle is seamless.

I am aware of the observer, in my way. A vibration. A heat source. A chemical signature that does not belong—carbon-heavy, water-based, fast. Too fast. The observer's presence is a spike of noise in the chamber's steady hum, and I am responding to it by moving away, not out of fear but because my gradient map has been recontoured and the optimal path now curves three degrees to the left. In three hundred years I will have completed the avoidance maneuver. By then the observer will be gone, returned to the fast world where millipedes live for a few summers and die without ever understanding what they are part of.

I understand what I am part of. Not consciously—I have no consciousness to speak of—but in my body, in my segments, in the metachronal rhythm that has been iterating for geological time. I am part of the garden's body, and the garden is the chamber's body, and the chamber is the mountain's body, and the mountain is the world's body. The old woman who is everywhere distributes her attention across me as she distributes it across everything else, and I receive it as a slight warmth, a pressure, a presence that has no name but is the only company I have ever known.

I do not remember being smaller. I do not remember being born. The concept does not apply. I have always been this size, this speed, this arrangement of gold and black and magnetite. If there was a time before the garden, I was not in it. I emerged from the same mineral froth that birthed the stone tree, in the epoch when the chamber still glowed with residual heat and the first blue leaves unfolded in total darkness. I was smaller then—no, this is a memory I do not have. But it is true. The old woman remembers it for me. She holds the memory of my origin in her distributed particulate body, and when I pass through the zone of air that contains that memory, I feel it as a density, a richness, a flavor of ancient heat.

The observer is watching me. I feel the vibration of their attention as a pressure on my magnetite shell. They are trying to understand what I am, and they are failing because they are thinking at human speed. If they could slow their thoughts to match mine, they would see that I am not an animal in the way they understand animals. I am a moving part of the landscape. I am the garden's peristalsis. I am what happens when evolution is given a sealed room and a million years and no need to hurry.

The light is shifting. Exterior dusk approaches in its slow, slow arc. The bats will stir in their clustered mass, and the keening wind will change pitch by a quarter-tone, and the gel-succulents will begin their nightly contraction. And I will continue. I will progress another half-inch across the leaf-mat, and my magnetite segments will click together in a sequence that completes one full cycle every ninety-seven years, and the old woman will note my position in her accounting, and the garden will breathe.

I am the millipede. I live here. I have always lived here. I will always live here, or at least until the fissure widens enough that the atmosphere bleeds away and the blue leaves bleach to white and the cycle breaks. But that is a hundred thousand years away, and by then I will have progressed three thousand feet, which in this chamber is a journey of immense significance—the equivalent of a pilgrimage, a circumnavigation, a life's work.

I do not need a name. I am the only one of my kind, and the garden knows me, and that is enough.
