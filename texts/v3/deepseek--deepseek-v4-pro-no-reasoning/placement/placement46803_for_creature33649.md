The chamber is not large—fifteen paces across at its widest, perhaps, and the ceiling lost somewhere above in a vault of steam and fractured light—but it holds entire weathers within itself.

You stand on a catwalk of perforated steel, slick with condensation, and below you the floor of the place is not floor but *becoming*. Raw stone extrudes upward in slow ribs and knuckles, pale grey threaded with veins of darker mineral that glisten wetly. The rock is new. You can tell because the edges are soft, almost blunted, not yet sharpened by wind or water into the crisp geometries of mature geology. It is rising, millimeter by millimeter, fed from some deep press of warmth beneath, and the air shivers above it with the heat of its arrival. This is a place being built from the inside out.

Water is everywhere, but it is water on a short leash. It seeps from the seams in the rock, weeps down the faces of the raw stone in continuous vertical threads, gathers in shallow pools on every horizontal surface. The pools tremble. The ceiling, where the steam collects and cools, releases a fine perpetual drizzle, a rain that falls only here, inside this room, on its own small schedule. The drips strike the metal of the catwalk with a sound like tiny bells—tinny, arrhythmic, a scatter of high chimes that never quite resolve into pattern. That is the loudest sound here: the water talking to the steel. There is no wind, no voice, no engine hum, despite the fact that this place is clearly *made*.

And it is made. Every surface that is not actively birthing stone is fabricated. The catwalk is steel grating, visibly welded in places, its supports bolted into the rock with heavy, painted brackets the color of dull ochre. The walls, where they can be seen through the steam and the hanging gardens, are paneled in something like enameled tin, ribbed and riveted, shedding water in obedient sheets into gutters that run along the base. Pipes cross the ceiling—some wrapped in fraying asbestos cloth, others bare copper gone green-black with patina—and they sweat, contributing their own drips to the general wetness. This is a machine of a place, a container, a factory-floor for geology.

And yet it is lush. The vegetation has found every crevice, every bolt-hole, every horizontal pipe that offers a few centimeters of purchase. Mosses, mostly—a chaos of them, in greens so deep they verge on black, in emerald, in a sick-sweet chartreuse that looks almost luminescent in the steam-filtered light. They grow in dense mats, layered over each other, older growths smothered by newer ones, and from their surfaces rise the fruiting bodies of ferns—tiny, perfect ferns no taller than your thumb, unrolling fiddleheads with an almost animal slowness. You can watch them move if you stand still long enough. The air is thick with the smell of wet peat and something sharper, like crushed chlorophyll, like the inside of a greenhouse that has been sealed for a century.

The strangeness settles in when you realize that the mosses are growing in patterns that are not quite botanical. They trace the lines of the pipes. They cluster most densely around the warm spots—the valve junctions, the places where the rock extrusions break through the steel floor. They are organized, in the way an industrial process is organized. This is not a garden that has colonized a ruin. This is a garden that is part of the machine, as much a component as the rivets and the gutters. The whole chamber breathes together. The rock rises, the pipes carry, the moss consumes, the water cycles—warm rain falling, pooling, seeping down through the gratings to some reservoir below, to be heated and lifted again. A closed loop. An ecosystem with a bill of lading.

You are alone in it. The fauna is rare to the point of being a kind of rumor. Twice, during a long stillness, you see them: small, pale shapes moving in the moss. They might be isopods, or young millipedes, or something that has no name yet because this is the only place it exists. They are translucent, the color of the steam itself, and they move with a deliberation that suggests they are grazing on the microscopic film that coats every wet surface. They leave no tracks. When the light shifts—when some automated sequence trips and a bank of yellow lamps high in the vault flickers on, then off again, then on—they vanish, not fleeing but simply ceasing to be visible, as if they were an artifact of the previous illumination.

The light is the one thing that feels normal, and even it has a quality you cannot quite name. It comes from those lamps, industrial fixtures with wire cages, but it passes through the steam and the hanging fronds and the drizzle in such a way that it feels sieved, strained, broken into components that your eye must reassemble. There are no shadows with hard edges. Everything fades into everything else. The chamber is small—cathedral scale, you think, though no cathedral was ever this close, this dense with presence—and its boundaries are never fully visible. The steam eats the corners. The moss softens the walls. The rock pushes upward through the floor and suggests that the room is temporary, that the floor will not hold, that the building and the geology are still negotiating their treaty.

There is a sound. Beneath the chime of drips, beneath the soft exhalation of the steam vents, there is a deeper note: a grinding, shifting pressure, almost subsonic, felt in the bones of the jaw rather than heard. It is the sound of the rock moving. Growing. Emerging. The whole chamber is under construction, not by human hands now but by the warm Earth, which has been given a template by the pipes and the gutters and the riveted tin walls, and which is slowly filling that template with itself. The industrial skeleton is becoming the fossil around which a new geology crystallizes.

You stay longer than you meant to. The place is active—multiple processes overlapping, the water cycle and the rock cycle and the small silent cycle of the translucent things in the moss, all running at their own biological and geological paces, none of them fast enough to track directly but all of them undeniable when you look away and look back and find the world slightly rearranged. The fiddlehead that was closed is now a spiral loosening. The pool that was clear is now filmed with a rainbow of bacterial sheen. The rock rib that was blunt is now, you think, a fraction sharper, as if the room itself is learning what edges are for.

This is a small place, a bounded place, a single chamber in some larger complex you have not seen and may not need to see. But it is dense with happening. Every cubic meter holds more life and more process than a hectare of ordinary forest. The air is thick with it. The surfaces glisten. The steam rises, the rain falls, and the new stone pushes upward through the grate, millimeter by millimeter, building a world in a room.

And you—you are a gleed, or something very like one.

You are small. Pale. Your body is segmented, though the segments have grown soft at the edges, blending into one another the way the steam blends into the walls. You have more legs than you need, and each one ends in a fine brush of sensory hairs that read the world as a continuous texture of wetness and warmth and vibration. You taste the air through your skin. You see not with eyes—though there are dark nubs on your head that might have been eyes once, in a different body—but with your whole surface, a slow, diffuse photoreception that registers the yellow lamps not as illumination but as a change in pressure, a leaning of the light against your cuticle.

You were born here, or you emerged here, or you simply found yourself here one day, fully formed and already grazing. That is the way of gleeds: there is no clear threshold between the biofilm you eat and the body you inhabit. You recolonize yourself hourly, your cells sloughing off into the wet air and being replaced by what you harvest from the moss. You are, in a sense, the room's most concentrated expression of its own chemistry—a mobile node of the same processes that build the stone and feed the ferns. You are the biofilm that learned to crawl.

You live in the moss mats that drape the warm pipe on the eastern wall, the one wrapped in fraying cloth that holds moisture for hours after each rain cycle. Your territory is perhaps twenty centimeters by thirty, a dense rug of the sick-sweet chartreuse variety, the one that flushes when Olly walks by. You share it with three others: a larger gleed whose segments are more distinct than yours, who moves with a slow, patriarchal gravity; a smaller one, quick and skittish, who vanishes at the first flicker of the lamps; and one whose body is slightly blue, who prefers the cooler edge of the mat near the rivet line and who hums, faintly, when it feeds. You know them by the taste of their trails, by the slight electrical charge each one leaves in the biofilm. You do not have names for them, because gleeds do not name—but you have a sense of them, a familiarity that is almost affection, the way you have a sense of the pipe's warmth and the drip's chime and the deep grinding note of the rock below.

Your life is grazing. You move across the moss in the long, slow hours between the lamp-flickers, your brush-legs finding purchase on the damp filaments, your mouthpart—a soft rasp, more like a tongue than a mandible—working the microscopic layer of bacteria and algae and fungal threads that coats every surface. You eat the room. The room grows back. The cycle is perfect.

When the lamp-flicker comes—the sudden off-on that Olly thinks of as the room's heartbeat—you freeze. Not from fear. From attention. Your whole body becomes a listening surface. The light-change carries information: a shift in the electromagnetic field of the chamber, a ripple through the water cycle, a subtle alteration in the heat gradient. You feel it as a flavor, metallic and brief. And then it passes, and you move again.

You know Olly. Of course you know Olly. He is your weather.

When he walks the catwalk, the vibration of his steps travels through the steel and into the moss and up through your legs to the soft center of your body. It is a regular rhythm, slow and deliberate, the quiet *thrum-thrum-thrum* of rubber-wrapped boots on grating. It is the most predictable thing in your world, more reliable than the drips, more consistent than the lamps. When his footsteps slow, you slow. When he stops, you often stop too, your whole small body waiting.

And when he sits on the catwalk steps, his back against the warm pipe, his brass spray bottle balanced on his knee—that is when you make the journey. It is a long journey, by gleed measures. It takes you the better part of an hour to cross from your home mat to the place where his boot rests against the grating. You must navigate the moss, descend the vertical face of the pipe insulation, cross a bare stretch of metal that tastes of iron and oil and old paint, ascend the curve of his boot-toe—the rubber gummy and warm, textured with a thousand micro-crevices that catch your brush-legs comfortably—and then, finally, the long slow climb up the canvas of his suit to the place where his wrist rests on his knee.

There is a particular spot on his wrist, just above the knob of bone, where the skin is thin and warm and slightly salt. You have grazed there. You have tasted the tiny flakes of his body, the cells he sheds without knowing, the residue of the moss-water he drinks. It is different from the biofilm: more complex, more alive, tinged with something that is almost electric. It tastes, you think—to the extent that you think—like the room, but concentrated, distilled, as if the room had passed through a filter and become a single point of warmth and pulse.

Sometimes, when you settle on his wrist, he looks down at you. His amber-green eyes—which you do not see, not as shape or color, but as two zones of slightly altered heat—rest on your body. You feel his attention as a gentle pressure, like the lamp-flicker but softer, slower, more sustained. His breath, when it reaches you, tastes of the violet fungus and the copper water. He hums, occasionally. The hum travels through his wrist and into your body, and for a moment you vibrate in sympathy, your small body a tuning fork for his song.

You are not his pet. You are not his companion, exactly. You are his mirror. The gleed on his wrist, the man on the catwalk—you are both fruits of the same long patience, grown by the same warmth and wetness and slow rock-rising. He watches the room; you watch him watching. The attention flows in a circuit: room to Olly, Olly to gleed, gleed back to room through the biofilm and the moss and the water-cycle. It is a closed loop, like everything else here.

When you die—and you will die, eventually, your body growing slow and your segments blurring until you are indistinguishable from the mat you graze on—Olly will lift your husk with his tweezers and carry you to the newest rock extrusion and tuck you into a crevice where the stone is still soft. The rock is hungry for calcium, he believes, and he may be right. But he also believes that the gleeds are the room's thoughts, its nervous impulses, and that a thought, once it has finished moving through the moss, deserves to be laid to rest in the stone that will become the room's bones.

You do not have beliefs. You have sensations. The taste of minerals. The pressure of light. The long, slow rhythm of a world that is always becoming something more than it was.

And now, as the fiddlehead nearest your home mat unrolls another millimeter toward whatever it will become, you raise your head—that soft, blunt, nearly eyeless head—and taste the air for what comes next. It tastes of steam, and copper, and the distant vibration of Olly's footsteps beginning their evening round. The lamps will flicker soon. The rain will fall. The rock will rise.

You will be there for all of it, grazing the world into being.
