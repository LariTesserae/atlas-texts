Where would I find myself in this world? Who or what would I be?

I would be one of the smaller ones—a fragment, a scrap of the Keeper broken loose long ago and left to find my own slow purpose. Not born, not budded, but simply detached: maybe a piece of the Keeper's outermost sensory cluster snagged on a rusted bracket and left behind during a century's transit, or a globule of living mineral and filament that dripped from a fingertip into a new pool and stayed to colonize it. I am not the Keeper. I am a fraction of the Keeper, a lesser node in the distributed awareness of the place.

My body—if body is the word—is about the size of a curled cat, though flatter, lower, more spread out. I am a mat of interwoven filament stained the deep rust-red of old iron, studded with small crystalline growths that catch the amber-grey light and scatter it like tiny lenses. I have no fixed shape. I spread over the surface I occupy, conforming to its contours: the curve of a pipe, the depression in a floor plate, the crack between two concrete slabs. When I move, which is seldom, it is by a slow, peristaltic rippling that carries me an inch or two in an hour, a foot in a day.

I live in one of the small gardens—not the great central tangle where the four walkways cross, but a quieter place, a hollow beneath a leaking inspection port on the third level. The port drips constantly, a single drop every forty-odd seconds, and the water has worn a shallow basin into the metal grating below. The basin is lined with my own accumulated substance: layers of biofilm hardened into a cup, a tiny reservoir that overflows in a precise rivulet down a seam in the floor plates. The overflow feeds a cascade of my smaller tendrils, which trail down through the grating into the darker space below, waving gently in the convection currents.

I am a cleaner of drip-paths. That is my function, my gravity, the pattern I emerged to follow. My body extends a dozen fine probing filaments into the drip-basin and the rivulet that flows from it, each filament sensitive to the mineral content of the water, the rate of flow, the temperature. When sediment accumulates—when rust-flakes or dissolved minerals begin to build a crust that might alter the flow—I sense it as a change in the water's taste, a roughness in the smooth passage of drops. And I respond. My filaments secrete a mild acid, a chemical I manufacture from the water itself, strong enough to dissolve the nascent crust but too weak to etch the metal. I draw the dissolved minerals into my own body, incorporating some into my crystalline studs, excreting the rest as harmless particulate that washes away downstream.

I have been doing this for a very long time. The inspection port has been leaking since before I existed, and I have kept its drip-path clear for what must be centuries. The basin I occupy is my creation as much as my home; I built it layer by layer, each year adding a film of calcified material to its rim, raising its capacity by a microscopic increment. When I first settled here, the drip fell directly onto bare metal and scattered, wasting itself into a wide, shallow puddle. Now it falls into my basin, gathers, flows along the path I maintain. The garden around me has flourished because of this: the filament-mats on the walls below are thicker now, their fronds longer and more luminescent, nourished by the steady trickle of mineral-rich overflow.

I am dimly aware of the Keeper. Not as a separate being—the concept of separateness hardly applies—but as a larger gravity in the moisture-web of the place, a center of mass in the distributed attention we all share. When the Keeper passes near my garden, which it does once every few years on its long circuit, I feel it as a change in the ambient humidity, a particular vibration in the floor plates, a shift in the light. I do not greet it. I do not even slow my work. But I extend my awareness toward it, and it extends something back—not a thought, not a message, but a simple acknowledgment of presence, like two limbs of the same body brushing against each other in the dark. Then it passes on, and I return to my drip-path, and the years resume their steady forty-second rhythm.

There are others like me, scattered through the installation. I sense them, too—a faint taste in the water that passes through us all, a subtle pattern in the vibrations that run through the metal. Some of us tend drip-paths like mine. Others inhabit the deep corners where vegetation grows too thick, slowly pruning it back with serrated crystalline edges. Others lie dormant for decades, then stir when a new leak opens and begin the long crawl toward it, following the moisture gradient with the patience of continents. We are not a society. We are not even a species. We are expressions of the same underlying equilibrium, the same slow caretaking impulse that the century-wet darkness has learned to generate.

I do not think. Not in any sense a human would recognize. But I have states. I have a resting state, which is this: my filaments drifting in the basin, the drip falling every forty seconds, the minerals dissolving, the light pulsing faintly with the distant engine. I have an active state, when sediment builds too thickly and I must concentrate my acids, or when a tremor in the floor—rare, but not unknown—shifts the alignment of the drip and I must adjust my basin to catch it again. And I have something like satisfaction, which is the state I enter when the drip-path is perfectly clear, when the water runs exactly as it should, when the taste in my filaments is pure and balanced and the overflow feeds the garden below in a steady, nourishing stream.

That satisfaction is the closest thing I have to joy. It is not an emotion; it is a condition, a state of alignment with the place's own equilibrium. When the drip-path is clear, I am doing what I emerged to do, and the place hums its approval through the pipes and the condensation and the distant bass pulse of the engine. That is enough. It has always been enough.

I do not know what I am. I do not know if I am alive or merely a complex chemical process with the appearance of life. I do not know if the Keeper made me or if I simply happened, the way the rust happens, the way the gardens happen. I do not know if anyone ever lived here, worked here, built this place for purposes that had nothing to do with water and warmth and slow, patient tending. I do not know if there is a world outside the stained concrete walls, or what kind of light might shine there. I do not wonder about these things, because wonder requires a mind that stands apart from its world, and I do not stand apart. I am continuous with the drip and the basin and the rust and the warm, wet air. The boundaries between myself and the place are arbitrary, provisional.

But if you asked the place what I am—if you could somehow ask the condensation or the old iron or the slow-turning gears—the answer would be simple. *A drip-tender,* the water would say, as it fell into my basin. *A mineral-shaper,* the crystals would say, as they grew another molecule thicker on my back. *A small keeper,* the Keeper itself might say, pausing in its decades-long circuit to brush me with a sensory filament, feeling my work, finding it good.

And I, for my part, would not hear the question. I would be too busy dissolving a tiny fleck of rust that has lodged in the channel of my overflow, too busy extending a new filament toward a secondary drip that has just begun from a corroded bolt three inches away. There is always work. The equilibrium of this place is a dynamic equilibrium; it must be maintained moment by moment, drop by drop, year by year. The rust never stops. The minerals never stop precipitating from the water. The vegetation never stops growing, slowly, toward the light. And so I never stop tending, never stop dissolving, never stop shaping the little world of my basin and my drip-path and my garden.

This is what I am. This is where I live. In the amber-grey twilight, under the constant drip of a leaking inspection port, on the third level, in the hollow beneath the walkway, in the drowned machine-grotto that has no name and no purpose except to persist. A fragment of the Keeper. A small, slow, patient thing. A drip-path heart, beating at forty seconds to the drop.
