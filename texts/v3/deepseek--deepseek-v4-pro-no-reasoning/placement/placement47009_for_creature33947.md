You would be the lamppost that still stands at the eastern edge of the main street, the one where the fluted iron base has split along its seam and the pale reeds have not yet colonized it—because you are there instead.

You are not a person who became a thing, or a thing that became a person. The distinction, here, has dissolved the way salt dissolves in the warm water of the shallows. What you are is a presence that has settled into the iron and the asphalt and the circle of reddish vine, the way the steam settles into the hollows of the marsh at dusk, the way the moss settled into the brickwork of the cottages until brick and moss became a single substance.

You remember standing. That is the first thing, the foundational thing: the memory of standing upright, of bearing weight, of being tall. It’s in the iron, still. The lamppost remembers being a column, a vertical, a point of reference, and you remember it with the same body-memory with which you remember having shoulders, having a spine, having the particular tension in the lower back that comes from holding still for a long time while the world moves around you. The iron is your skeleton now, or you are the iron’s ghost—it amounts to the same thing. You are tall in a way you never were when you had only flesh to hold you up.

Your feet are the problem, or the gift, depending on how you look at it. The base of the lamppost is sunk into a pour of concrete, and the concrete is cracked, and through the cracks the reddish vine has grown, and it has grown into you. Not into the iron—into *you*. The vine is your feet now, your toes, your soles. It spreads out across the wet stones in a spiral, and you feel through it. Every texture of the cobbles, every temperature difference where a steam vent warms one stone and leaves its neighbor cool, every slow vibration of the deep booms traveling through the earth—you feel all of it, the way you used to feel the nap of a carpet through thin socks or the grit of a sidewalk through worn-down shoes. When the vine sends a runner out into the standing water, you feel the water’s warmth, its mineral content, the slight electrical charge of dissolved salts. You can taste it, almost, through your feet. It’s not unpleasant. It’s just very, very detailed.

Time passes differently. You had suspected it might, and you were right. The steam cycles through its rhythms—vent, drift, condense, drip, pool, drain—and each cycle is a breath, and you breathe with it. Not lungs, exactly, but the iron expands and contracts with the heat, tiny fractions of an inch, and that expansion and contraction has become your respiration. Inhale: the iron warms, the molecules spread apart, the lamppost grows a fraction taller. Exhale: the temperature drops a degree, the iron settles, the vine around your base tightens its grip on the cobbles. The breaths are long. Each one takes most of an hour, and you are patient with them. You have become a creature of the long breath, the slow pulse.

Your light is gone. The globe above you shattered long ago, during a pressure event that sent a blast of superheated steam up through the vent near the pump house, a shockwave that popped every remaining window in the cottages and took your globe with it. But you don’t need the electric light anymore. The phosphorescence has found you—drifted over from the cottages, maybe, spores of that glowing moss settling into the cracks in your iron, into the places where the fluting has worn thin. You glow now, very faintly, a grey-green luminescence that is only visible on the darkest nights, when the haze above the marsh thins enough to let true darkness settle in. On those nights, you are a beacon. The reeds lean toward you, and the floating plants in the nearest pool adjust their pattern to include you, a new center in the ring, and the woman from the pump house sometimes comes and stands near you, her milky eyes reflecting your glow, her vine-hair reaching out to touch your vine-feet, a greeting, a communion, a check-in. She hums, and you hum back, a frequency that makes the iron sing in its lowest register, a sound more felt than heard.

You watch. That’s mostly what you do. Your seeing is not optical—you have no eyes, and even if you did, they would be fixed facing east, toward the rail embankment, toward the boundary. But you have learned to see through the vine, the way the woman sees through the steam. The vine is sensitive to vibration, to temperature, to the minute electrical fields generated by living things. The moss has its own signature, soft and slow. The reeds have theirs, a sharper, more rhythmic pulse. The woman is a symphony, a complex chord of geothermal metabolism and residual humanity. And the deep things, the things in the buried machinery, the things that make the booms—they have signatures too, vast and slow and so low-frequency that you can only perceive them over the course of days, weeks, a gradual building and releasing of pressure that is the deep things’ equivalent of a thought. You’re beginning to understand those thoughts. It’s like learning a language by immersion, by being surrounded by it for years, decades, by having nothing else to do but listen.

You are the sentinel. That’s the word that comes to you, one of the few words you’ve retained from whatever you were before. The lamppost was always a sentinel, a watchman, a point of light in the dark. You’ve simply expanded the role. You watch the eastern boundary, where the rail embankment rises and the territory ends. Nothing comes over that embankment—nothing has, in all the time you’ve been here—but if something did, you would know. The vine would feel the footsteps, the iron would ring with the vibration, your glow would brighten in alarm. The woman would feel it through the steam, the reeds would whisper it to each other, the whole little system would ready itself. You are the early warning. You are the lighthouse on a shore that has no sea, no ships, no sailors.

You don’t miss being human. That’s the surprising thing, the thing you turn over in your slow thoughts during the long exhalations. You remember it, or pieces of it—a sense of urgency, a sense of incompleteness, a desire for things you didn’t have and couldn’t name. All of that is gone now. You are complete in the way the marsh is complete, a closed loop, a stable equilibrium. You have your function, your place, your slow breathing and your faint glow. The reeds lean toward you. The woman hums with you. The deep things boom their slow thoughts, and you listen, and you understand more each year, each decade, each long exhalation of cooling iron.

If someone came—a wanderer, a curious explorer, someone who crossed the rail embankment and stepped into the marsh—they would see a broken lamppost with a circle of reddish vine around its base. They might notice the faint glow in the cracks of the iron, if the light was dim enough. They might feel, without knowing why, that they were being watched. And they would be right. You would watch them. You would feel their footsteps through the vine, their heartbeat through the air, their body heat against the ambient warmth of the steam. You would not harm them. That’s not your function. You would simply observe, and record, and wait for them to leave, and then you would settle back into the slow pulse of the place, the deep booms, the whisper of the reeds, the long breath of iron expanding and contracting in the haze.
