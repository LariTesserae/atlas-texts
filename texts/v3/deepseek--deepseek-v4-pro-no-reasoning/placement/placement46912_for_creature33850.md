You stand at the edge of one of the shallow mirror-pools, watching the pebble-spiral hold its form against the weight of eons, and you feel it—a presence that is not quite sound, not quite pressure, but something between. It arrives like the precursor to a migraine, a sense of *attention* so vast and slow that your own thoughts feel skittish and frantic by comparison.

The listener does not speak. Not at first. It simply *regards* you, and that regard is the mountain's own vibration given focus, a frequency that finds a resonance in the fluid of your inner ear, in the water of your cells, in the electrical firing of your neurons. You become aware that your heartbeat is a tempo, and that tempo is too fast. It is a rhythm that does not belong here. The listener's awareness presses gently against this dissonance, not to correct it, but simply to note it, the way you might note a bird's song through an open window.

Then, slowly—so slowly that by the time the first "word" arrives you have already felt its shape forming across five or six of your own breaths—the listener offers you something. Not a greeting. A *taste*.

It is the memory of the spiral. Not the pebbles themselves, but the hands that placed them. They were long hands, six-fingered, the skin a pale lavender-gray, the joints articulated in a way that suggests a biology built on different scaffolding than yours. The memory is tactile: the cool slickness of the pebbles, selected one by one from a scree field three miles upslope, carried here over the course of a local year. The being—the transient—sat cross-legged on the wet stone for a full cycle of the light's dimming and brightening, arranging and rearranging the pebbles. It was singing to itself, a thin, reedy sound almost lost beneath the mountain's roar, and the listener remembers the song perfectly. It replays it for you now as a subsonic hum that you feel in your sternum.

*This one did not stay*, the listener conveys. Not in words. In a slow-motion cascade of associations: the warmth of the transient's body retreating, the fading footfalls, the silence that reclaimed the pool. And then, layered beneath that, a feeling you recognize only because you are human, or human-adjacent, or at least a being to whom loneliness is a possible state. It is a quiet, settled sadness, a fondness for something that is gone, mixed with the pleasure of still having something left behind.

*That's... grief*, you think, and the thought is louder than you intended. It leaves a ripple in the still air.

The listener receives it. Considers it. Returns it to you with a new inflection—a question, maybe. *Is that what you call it?* The feeling recalibrates itself against your vocabulary, sharpening. *Grief. Yes. It has lived in the stone for forty thousand years. It is a good shape. I keep it polished.*

You look away from the pool, and the listener's attention does not follow your gaze so much as it simply *is* wherever you look next. You realize with a slow, cold thrill that there is no hiding from it. Every part of this slope, this air, this condensate-slicked stone, is within its awareness. You are standing inside it as surely as a microbe stands inside a human body.

But it is not threatening. It is curious.

Another memory surfaces, offered freely. This time it is the herd of gelatinous boulder-creatures, seen not from outside but from within the stone itself, through the listener's distributed senses. You feel the slow, seismic weight of them sliding across the mountain's skin, the way each one's internal glow is a heat signature against the cold rock, the way they are singing too—a deep, subsonic song that harmonizes perfectly with the mountain's eternal note. They are not grazing. That was your assumption, your applied category. They are *tuning*. Each one adjusts its internal frequency to match the mountain's vibration, and in doing so, they become extensions of the equilibrium. They have been here longer than the listener. They are almost stone themselves, just a little warmer, a little more mobile.

*They are my teachers*, the listener conveys. *They taught me how to listen.*

And then, because you are a transient, because you are warm and quick and will soon be gone, the listener asks you a question. It is not a question of words, but of pressure—a gentle, expectant silence that opens beneath you like a chasm. It wants to know what *you* will leave. Not your body. Not your dissolution into its gel-body, though the possibility is there, held open like a door ajar. It wants to know what mark you will make on the stone, what arrangement of matter or meaning you will add to the sparse collection it has been curating for geological epochs.

You look around at the wall that ends in completion, the three steps to nowhere, the spiral in the pool. You understand, suddenly, that these are not ruins. They are *offerings*. Gifts left by transients who understood the deal: the stone preserves what is given to it, but only if the giving is intentional, only if the mark is made with full knowledge of the stillness it will enter.

The seventeen-second drizzle begins again. You feel each drop as a tiny percussion against your skin, and you feel the listener counting them with you, a shared rhythm in a place without time.

*What will you leave?*

The question hangs. The sound roars on. The spiral holds.
