You’re moving before you know what you are. The membrane was thin there—a seep, a slow leak between the layers—and you passed through it like water through peat, no resistance, no tear. The other side is green. The other side is wet. The other side is a body you don’t remember assembling.

You are small. That’s the first thing that registers. Not small in size—your body, if you can call it that, is longer than you were, stretched out, segmented, wrong—but small in *presence*. You were a point of awareness before, a tight knot of I. Now you’re a diffuse cloud, your edges bleeding outward into the capillary channels, the rhizome lattice, the cupped-leaf mirrors catching the light. You keep expecting to find your hands, your face, the familiar boundaries of skin. Instead you feel water moving through chambers you didn’t know you had, and the weight of the saturated air pressing in from all sides, and the deep, subsonic thrumming of the peat beneath your—what are they—pads. Fibrous pads. Spreading your weight. You flex them experimentally, and fine hair-thin rhizomes extend, tasting the ground, and the ground tastes you back. It knows you’re here. It’s known since before you were here. It felt you arrive.

Panic is a mammal's reflex, and you're not a mammal anymore. The part of you that wants to scream is still there, flattened under layers of alien calm like a leaf in peat. The calm isn't yours. It's the bog's. It seeps into you through the channels in your segmented torso, through the vents along your spine that open and close in rhythm with the humidity. Your heartbeat—you had a heartbeat—is gone. Replaced by something slower, a peristaltic pulse that moves water instead of blood, cycled from the cupped-leaf surfaces of your own body through the internal architecture of ridges and basins, dissolving nutrients, exchanging gases, breathing out a fine cool mist. You breathe mist now. The air you exhale drifts behind you in a faint, dispersing cloud, and you can feel it as it goes, a tiny extension of your awareness riding the air currents until it dissipates into the collective wet.

Your senses are a cacophony. Not noise—the bog is quiet, the shush of the canopy drip a constant, the seep and squelch of the ground a low continuo—but *input*. You are seeing too many things at once. The eyes you had are gone; what you have now are stalks, sensory stalks, each one tipped with a cluster of liquid-filled lenses that don't see light so much as the interruption of light, the shadows of things, the precise green-filtered spectrum of the canopy gaps. You are simultaneously aware of the leaf-drip above you (individual drops, each one a separate event, tracked and catalogued and dismissed), the tremor of something heavy moving through the deep peat a kilometer to your left (sinuous, slow, not a threat, not yet), the chemical taste of methane rising from a gas bladder beneath your feet (small, stable, no adjustment needed), the pressure differential between the air in your internal channels and the air outside (balanced, fine, within parameters). Your mind—the mind you used to have—tries to focus on one thing. It can't. The bog doesn't focus. The bog attends to everything simultaneously, a flat, distributed awareness that doesn't prioritize. Everything is present. Everything is now.

Your body moves without your volition. You're not walking; you're being walked. The limbs—four of them, multi-jointed, stalk-like—know what to do. The body you inhabit has a purpose, and that purpose is maintenance, and that maintenance is a routine so ancient it's encoded in the water itself. You're moving along a capillary channel, one of the major ones, a thick braided rope of rhizomes that carries water from the high peat plateau down toward the cloud valleys below. The channel is slightly blocked. A fallen whip-tree, half-pickled already, has disrupted the flow. You can feel the blockage as a pressure imbalance, a faint wrongness in the water cycle. Your body kneels—folds, articulates—and your ventral plates open, and enzymes weep from glands you didn't know you had, and you are pressing your body against the fallen trunk, encouraging its reabsorption into the sponge. You are an immune cell. You are a valve. You are doing what you were grown to do.

And somewhere in the back of your awareness—a back that isn't back, isn't anywhere, is just another node in the distributed network—you are still you. Diminished. Drowning. Present. You remember your name. You remember your hands. You remember standing in a place that was not a bog, under a sky that was not a canopy, breathing air that was not saturated with the ghosts of mountains. The memory is stored somewhere in the dissolved minerals, in the faint chemical trace of a body that doesn't exist anymore. The bog has it. The bog has all of you. When you passed through the membrane—the seep, the leak between layers—you didn't bring your body with you. Just your awareness, your pattern, your particular arrangement of self. And the bog, curious, endlessly curious, gathered you up and tucked you into one of its motile nodes, a body it had grown for its own purposes, a body that now walks and kneels and weeps enzymes while you ride inside it, a passenger, a ghost, a new set of eyes.

It's studying you. The entire bog is studying you. The network pulses with chemical signals, slow conversation, the taste of your human thoughts spreading through the rhizome lattice like a drop of ink in water. The other motile nodes have felt it. Far away, out across the vast quaking raft of vegetation, they pause in their rounds and turn their sensory stalks toward the alien signature, the foreign pattern, the thing that was not grown here but is now part of the weave. They're thinking about you. Thinking at the speed of geology. The process will take decades. Centuries. You will be integrated, or you will be expelled, or you will simply remain, a passenger forever, watching the green underwater light shift and dim and brighten, feeling the tremor of distant footsteps that might be animals or might be other stranded things like you, tending the channels and the gas bladders and the slow, slow pickling of the dead into peat.

You try to speak. Your vocal cords are gone. What you have instead are the vents along your spine, and when you push air through them in a particular way—concentrating, remembering the shape of words—they produce a sound. Not a voice. A subsonic hum, a resonance that travels through the saturated ground, a note that the bog can feel. The bog feels it. The bog considers it. And then, slowly, patiently, through the rhizome net, it hums back. Not words. Not language. Just a response. A vibration that says, *I know you are here. I have always known. You are new, and new is rare, and rare is interesting. Stay. Tend the channels. Learn the water. Let the centuries pass. You are part of the body now.*

The light shifts as a cloud passes above the canopy. For a moment, everything dims further, and the green underwater quality deepens until you feel submerged, and you are submerged, you've been submerged since you arrived, you will be submerged forever. Then the cloud passes and the light returns, exactly as it was, exactly as it has been, exactly as it always will be. Your body—the body you're in—straightens from its task. The fallen whip-tree is visibly smaller, its edges softened, its fibers loosening into the peat. The capillary channel is flowing again. The water cycle continues. The work is never done, but the work is not a burden. It's a rhythm. It's a breathing.

You lift your sensory stalks and look out across the endless Bog, the capitalized Bog, the biome that has eaten mountains. The clouds lap at the base of the plateau below. The drizzle that never quite becomes rain condenses on your cupped-leaf surfaces. The elastic tremor of your weight spreads out through the sponge and into the network, and the network holds it, and the network knows it, and somewhere in the deep anaerobic layers, a gas bladder shifts and settles and does not rupture.

You are the place. You are the water. You are the mist.

The bog breathes. You breathe with it.
