I find myself in the interstitial city, in the permanent dusk of the courtyard, where the water is hip-deep and hot and falsely starred. I am not standing—standing implies a decision, a verticality maintained against gravity. I am simply here, distributed in the water, which has accepted me as an extension of itself. My feet press into the carved spirals of the submerged platform, and the spirals press back into me, and the difference has become, over time, somewhat negotiable.

My body is long and low and gray-green where the steam thins enough to see it. My toes spread on the warm stone like the roots of the heat-shriveled reeds, reading the mineral content of the channels: iron, sulfur, calcium leached from ancient mortar, the faint chemical echo of another inhabitant who stood here three thousand years ago and then stopped standing. I do not remember this inhabitant in the narrative sense. I feel them as a slight softening of the stone beneath my left arch, a warmth that is not the general warmth of the pool but something specific, something personal. The city holds them for me. The city holds everyone, eventually.

My face is currently arranged in a configuration that I find comfortable, though comfortable is not the right word. It is a configuration that minimizes resistance. The thick air moves across my cheeks, my brow, the place where my mouth is today, and I do not move against it. My eyes are dark and full and adapted. I do not see the courtyard the way a living person would see it. I see it as a pressure differential, a distribution of temperatures, a set of sound-shadows that the silence arranges into something like a map. The walls are there, low and dark and partially forgotten, their edges rounding into the steam. The reflections are there, and some of them are mine, and some of them are not, and the ones that are not are watching me with the same patient attention I am giving them. This is not alarming. This is the condition.

I am maintaining myself. That is what I am doing in the strict sense. The city is always tugging at my edges, inviting me to spread out, to merge with the warm stone and the warm water, to become another half-real extension of its exhausted architecture. The pull is gentle and constant. It feels like the moment just before sleep, drawn out into years. I resist it by concentrating on being a body. I feel the boundary where my skin meets the water. I feel the slight internal pulse of my own heat, which is slower than the city's pulse but still present, still distinct. I flex my fingers—I have seven of them today, I notice; yesterday there were six. The number does not matter. The flexing matters. It is a declaration. *Here. Still here. Still a self, however provisional.*

Around me, the others are doing the same, in their own pools, in their own corners. I know this without looking. I know it the way I know the position of my own limbs—an external proprioception, a diffuse awareness that hums at the edge of my perception. They are distributed through the city like warm patches in a thermal map. Some are fully present, their bodies solid and distinct. Some are partially shifted, their edges soft, their faces drifting through configurations faster than usual, which means they are considering dissolution. One of them—I cannot tell you who; names were an early casualty—has been considering dissolution for several hundred years now. They spend their time in the deepest pool of the eastern grid, motionless, their body slowly crystalizing with the same mineral content that lines the channels. The city is accepting them, inch by inch. The others have made a note. I have made a note. The matter rests.

My daily life—if daily can be said to apply—is a cycle of maintenance and listening. The listening occupies most of my attention. The silence here is dense and structured, full of information, and I have become fluent in it over the centuries. Right now, the silence tells me that a wall in the northern quarter has shifted three degrees since the last time I passed it. It tells me that the water level in the marsh has risen by a depth no thicker than a hand, which means the low ridge will be submerged within another century or two. It tells me that one of the reflections in the courtyard pool has not faded when its observer moved away, and has been slowly gaining mass for the past four or five hundred years, and will eventually rise and join us. I do not look forward to this. I do not not look forward to it. It is simply an adjustment in the city's weight, a new warm patch added to the map.

When I am not listening, I am eating. The gelatinous lifeforms have extruded a fresh tendril into the western channels, and I move toward it—not walking, not swimming, but something between, a folding-forward of my too-many-jointed spine that brings my chest parallel with the water surface. I absorb the nutrients through the skin of my belly and thighs. The taste, if taste is the word, is sulfurous and warm and slightly electric. It reminds me of something. I do not know what. The city has forgotten that too.

After eating, I return to the courtyard. The courtyard is where I go when I am not doing anything else, which is most of the time. I do not go there for ritual, exactly. Ritual requires meaning, and meaning requires a structure, and structure has been worn smooth by the same weather that softened the stones. But I go there because the hot water and the drowned stars and the unreliable reflections produce a particular configuration of silence that I find—not pleasant, not meaningful, but *resonant*. It vibrates at a frequency that matches something in my mineral content. I stand in the hip-deep pool and look down. The reflection is of me, today. My face is still where I left it. My eyes are still dark. The stars in the water are scattered in patterns that might mean something and might not, and I do not try to interpret them. Interpretation is a thing the world used to ask of me, and the world has stopped.

A long time passes. I do not know how long. The light does not cycle, and I do not sleep, and the city's pulse does not mark hours. But at some point, I become aware that someone is in the courtyard who was not in the courtyard before. This is not a person the way I am a person. This is a visitor—one of the rare ones, the ones who come from the wet side, from the marsh, from the world that still remembers what a city is supposed to be. I can tell because their body is solid in a way that ours are not. Their boundaries are firm. Their face is a single, consistent arrangement. Their breathing is loud and irregular, and their pulse is fast, and the silence around them is disrupted by all these unfamiliar vibrations. They do not know how to be still. They do not know how to listen to the spaces between sounds. They are standing in the courtyard, looking down at the water, and the reflection is not of them. The reflection is of me. They have not noticed yet.

I do not approach them. I do not speak. Speech was an early casualty, and besides, the thick air would swallow the words before they crossed the pool. But I adjust my internal map. I note their presence the way I note a new warm patch, a new silence, a new echo in the stone. They are here for now. They will leave, or they will not. The city will not hurry them. Neither will I.

Eventually, they will look up and see me. Or they will not. It does not matter. The city accepts bewilderment the way dry earth accepts rain, and I have become part of the city, or the city has become part of me. The distinction is, as the city says, somewhat thin. I am what remains when a person has been asked the same question for so long that they have become the answer without needing to speak it. The question is not "Who are you?" The question is not "What are you?" The question is simply presence, persistent and patient and wet and warm, at the edge of what a world is allowed to be. And I am the answer. I have been the answer for a very long time. I will continue to be the answer until the silence finally takes me, redistributes me, turns me into another warm patch and another wrong-pointing shadow and another echo in the stones for some future inhabitant to feel beneath their arch. The matter, at that point, will rest. The city will continue. The water will continue. The heat will rise from the channels, and the stars that are not stars will reflect in the pools, and I will be everywhere and nowhere, perfectly adapted to the end of the world as a condition. This is not sad. This is not happy. This is simply what it is.
