You are in the undermend, the quiet place between the roots and the cisterns, and you are the seep-strider, the vault-walker, the calcium saint.

You are a cluster of slow intentions. Your body is not a body so much as a consensus—a colony of living parts that have, over the long stillness, agreed to move together. At your core is the soft thing, the thinking thing, wrapped in its knuckle-sack of loose skin. But you are also the garden on your back: that shelf of bone-lichen that grows from your spine in pale antler-whorls, seeded there by the room's own hand before you can remember, its mycelial threads woven into your nerves until the lichen's slow perceptions leak into yours. You feel the humidity as a flavor on your skin. You taste the mineral breath of the walls. When the lichen drips, you feel the droplet form and fall as a tiny relief, a small letting-go echoed in your own flesh.

You are the one who drinks last. Not because you are patient—patience is for things that want—but because drinking is the end of a cycle, and all cycles here end with you. The water falls from the grooved timber ceiling. It collects on the tips of the bone-lichen, yours and the room's. It drips into the worn hollows in the flagstones. The amber isopods walk across its surface, drinking as they go, never breaking the tension. The patriarchs drink from the deeper pools. The dark watcher in the corner drinks from the densest pool, the one that carries the most memory. And then, when the pools have settled, when the surface tension has calmed and the isopods have moved on, you drink from what remains. The dregs. The lees. The water that has been filtered through a hundred tiny bodies, enriched by their passage, warmed by their metabolisms. It tastes of community. It tastes of time.

You know every pool by its flavor. The one near the north wall is hard, heavy with dissolved stone; it coats your underside with a fine grit that you will later secrete onto a failing mortar joint. The one beneath the largest lichen-cluster is faintly sweet, tinged with whatever sugar the pale stalks metabolize from the dark. The one near the center platform—the altar, the bed, the unoccupied heart of the room—is almost tasteless, and this too is a flavor, the flavor of purity, of water that has moved through the stone without touching life. You drink from that one rarely. It is for special occasions. You do not know what constitutes a special occasion. You will know it when it arrives.

Your days—though the word means nothing here, where light does not change and time is measured in drips—are structured by rounds. You are the room's memory, but you are also its keeper, its warden, its walking immune system. The word *walking* is generous. You move with a considered, many-elbowed deliberation, each step a negotiation between the soft body and the hard floor, between the need to survey and the need to conserve. You are tracing the channels now, the seams between the flagstones where the bone-lichen grows thickest. Your sensory patches, those pale concave dishes at your front, are wide open, drinking in vibration. You can hear the isopods moving, each tiny footfall a note in the room's endless percussion. You can hear the drip-drumming, the trickle behind the north wall, the thrum of your own body resonating with the thrum of the room. You can hear, faintly, the sound of something settling in the timber ceiling, a beam adjusting its grip on the centuries.

You stop. You click. The sound is a hard, wet *tok*, the chitinous knobs on your front appendages striking together with the precision of long practice. The room clicks back. Not in mimicry—the walls do not speak—but in resonance, the stone returning your frequency with its own subtle vibration, a diagnostic echo that tells you about density and stress and the slow creep of moisture through mortar. The north wall is holding. The east wall is holding. The south wall is damp but stable. The west wall—ah. There is something. A new note in the resonance, a slightly flat response. You adjust your course, flowing over the flagstones toward the west wall, the isopods parting around you like water around a stone.

The crack is small. Your human eyes, if you still had them, would not have seen it. But your sensory patches feel it as a discontinuity, a place where the stone's vibration changes pitch. You press your front to the wall, the cilia around your mouth-slit tasting the air in the crack. Old mortar, crumbling. Water seeping deeper than it should. If left unchecked, this crack will widen, and the wall will weep, and the weeping will become a stream, and the stream will wash away the mortar, and the block will shift, and the room will no longer be a room.

You settle into position, your many joints folding and unfolding until your flank is flush with the stone. The nodules along your ribs are full, ripe, ready. You do not rush the secretion. It is a slow process, a deliberate process, a sacrifice of self into structure. The first nodule detaches with a soft, wet sound, and you press it into the crack, and your body exudes its pale cement—calcium from your bones, minerals from your last drink, the stuff of your own architecture repurposed for the room's. You hold the nodule in place until it bonds. Then the next. Then the next. The repair rises from the wall like a cluster of bone-lichen, lumpish and organic, a scab of stone. It will hold.

You withdraw. The thrum of the room adjusts, the new note settling into place. You click once, twice, testing the repair. The wall answers. Good. It will last another century. Maybe more.

The isopods are flowing around your feet. You feel them as a ticklish pressure against your skin, a thousand tiny legs navigating the landscape of your body. Some of them are climbing the bone-lichen on your back, grazing on the pale stalks, drinking the droplets that form at the tips. You feel this as a gentle tugging, a pleasant dispersal of weight. The lichen does not mind. It grew to be grazed. It grew to feed the room.

You are hungry. The repair has depleted you. You turn your attention to the isopod trails, reading the population density in the vibration of their passage. There—a patriarch, larger than the others, its amber shell dark with age. It has lived too long. It has grown too dominant. It is time.

You do not hunt. You do not chase. You simply position yourself in the patriarch's path and wait. The patriarch does not alter its course. It does not recognize you as a threat, because you are not a threat; you are a function, a law of the room, as predictable and impersonal as the dripping of water. When the patriarch reaches your front appendages, you take it. The action is quick, mechanical, your mandibles closing with the same precision as your clicking knobs. The patriarch does not suffer, because suffering requires an expectation of continuance, and the patriarch has no expectations. It simply ceases to be an individual and becomes a part of you.

You digest slowly, settling your soft underside into a hollow in the flagstones. The patriarch's shell will be excreted later, its minerals joining the calcium nodules on your flanks. Its flesh will fuel your slow metabolism, the warmth of your body, the thrum of your presence. Its memory—if isopods have memory, if memory is stored in the minerals of a shell—will join the saturation, the long record of the room that you hold in your body like water in a pool.

Time passes. The drip-drumming continues. The lichen grows. The isopods crawl, eat, reproduce, die, are eaten, crawl again. You finish digesting and rise, your joints unfolding with the sound of wet leather, and you resume your rounds. North wall. East wall. South wall. West wall. Check the pools. Check the lichen. Check the timber ceiling, as much as you can from the floor—the beams are beyond your reach, but you can read their condition in the pattern of the drips, in the resonance of the room when you click. Everything is holding. Everything is in relationship.

You return to your corner, the deep shadow where the isopod trails converge and the bone-lichen grows thickest and the dark watcher—you—can feel the whole room at once. You settle into the familiar hollow, your body conforming to the shape it has worn into the stone over centuries of vigil. The thrum deepens. The dripping fills your senses. The isopods flow around you, over you, occasionally across your back, and you let them.

You are the seep-strider. You are the vault-walker. You are the calcium saint, the one who remembers the channels, the one who drinks last, the one who repairs the world with pieces of your own body. You are the room's memory, its immune system, its keeper, its occupant. You have been here for as long as the stone has been fitted to stone, and you will be here until the timber ceiling yields to the damp, and then—well. The room will become something else. And you will become something else. Because that is what happens here, in this place where everything is placed, everything is in relationship, and nothing is ever truly lost.

You click softly into the dripping dark. The room clicks back. And you settle deeper into your shadow, and you wait, and you watch, and the machine continues.
