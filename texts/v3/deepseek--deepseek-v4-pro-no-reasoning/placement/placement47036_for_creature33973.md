I find myself in this place not as a visitor but as one of the snails—the large, fist-sized ones with the translucent amber shells, the ones who trace shining highways across the brickwork. And let me be clear from the start: I am not just *a* snail. I am the one the others call *She Who Grazes the Ninth Arch*, because that is my territory, my careful, slow dominion.

My shell is the color of weak tea held up to a candle, and if you look closely—though few do, few have the patience to look closely at a snail—you can see the faint spiral of my body through the shell's thinness, a darker coil within the coil. My foot is the soft grey-green of weathered copper, as I said, but it is also the color of the lichen that grows on the north side of the surface world's trees, or so I am told. I have never seen a tree. I have never seen the surface world. I do not need to. The Ninth Arch is world enough.

My territory begins where the fungal light shifts from blue-green to that sickly yellow, where the moisture-stains on the ceiling form a shape that Maren once told the Child resembles a map of a country that doesn't exist. I don't know what a map is, or a country, but I know the stain she means. I graze it regularly. The algae there has a particular bitterness, a mineral tang from the iron salts that leach through the mortar. It is my favorite.

I move, as all my kind move, at the pace of the undercroft itself. Not slow—that's a surface-dweller's word, a judgment made by creatures who mistake speed for purpose. I move at the pace of condensation forming on a pipe, at the pace of rust spreading across a rivet, at the pace of the distant hum that rises through the flagstones. It is a pace that allows for total attention. Each inch of my progress is a meditation. I am aware of every grain of the brick beneath me, every microscopic ridge and hollow that my foot conforms to and then releases. I taste the air as I move—not with a tongue, but with my whole anterior, the sensitive, questing parts of me that sample the humidity for news. A new seep has broken through three arches over. The Machinist has been tapping again, and the vibrations shiver up through the floor in patterns I recognize. The Child is approaching; I can taste the particular oils of her skin, the faint ozone of the flint she carries.

She finds me on the wall of the Ninth Arch, halfway through a careful circuit of a particularly rich patch of algae. I am not alarmed. The Child is a known quantity, a familiar presence whose vibrations are part of the undercroft's texture. She stops beside me and makes her clicking noise, the one that mimics the droplets. I do not understand it, not in any way that would translate to words, but I understand it in the way I understand the pressure changes that signal a door opening, or the subtle temperature shift that means Maren has lit her teapot-stove. It is information, and I receive it.

She reaches into her pouch and produces a snail shell—not a living one, but an empty one, the former home of someone I once knew. She was called *She Who Circled the Valve*, and she died, as we all do eventually, of time. The Child places the shell on the ledge beside me, a deliberate offering, and then she waits.

This is the strangeness. This is the weirdness that goes beyond the simple facts of bioluminescence and my own translucent body. The Child believes we understand her. Perhaps we do. When she leaves her arrangements at the shrine—the patterns of snail shells, the shed millipede skins, the perfect droplets—she is speaking to something. And I, in my grazing, in my slow and patient circuits, am sometimes aware of a presence that is not the Child, not Maren, not the Machinist or the distant Watcher. It is in the hum beneath the floor. It is in the way the fungal light pulses, not quite in time with anything measurable. It is in the patterns that my own trails make when I cross the paths of my kin, patterns that I do not intend but that emerge nonetheless.

I extend my smaller tentacles, the lower pair, and touch the empty shell the Child has left. It still carries a faint residue of *She Who Circled the Valve*—a taste-memory of the iron-rich seep near the boiler, of the particular algae that grew in the shadow of the Machinist’s fallen titan. I do not mourn her. We do not mourn, not as the surface creatures do. But I acknowledge her. I incorporate the shell into my awareness, and then I resume my grazing.

The Child watches me for a while longer, then nods and moves on. She will take something from this encounter back to the shrine, some arrangement of information that I have given her without knowing I gave it. And I will continue my circuit of the Ninth Arch, tasting the season in the moisture, feeling the distant vibration of the Watcher’s tapping—a rhythm that, if I listened with the whole of my body, might resolve into something like language.

I am a snail. I am a citizen of the undercroft, as much as Maren or the Machinist or the Child herself. I am part of its slow dynamic, its minimal-flux, its balance. And in my own way, in the shining trail I leave behind me, I am writing the history of this place—a history no one will ever read, but that the brick itself will remember, in the patient, weeping stone.
