The Architect of Dew is, in her entirety, the chamber. She is the sourceless light and the cold that never leaves and the damp that condenses on every surface. She is the moss and the ferns and the isopods traversing the condensation trails. She is the pyramid of river-stones and the fern rooted between its apex and its neighbor. She is the drop of water forming on the stone tooth, falling once an hour into the pool the size of a dinner plate. She is the mycelial net threading through the granite, binding the stone to the water to the air, a lacework of silver-gray that has no edges because there is nowhere in the chamber that she is not.

Time is a saturation for her, a chord sustained across centuries. Every moment—the millipede's architectural devotion, the stacking of the black stones by human hands long ago, the first spore settling in its micro-fissure—is layered into the present. She perceives them all at once, a simultaneous eternity, and so she does not remember the beginning, because the beginning is still happening. The light is still rising from the granite for the first time. The drop is still forming. The fern is still unfurling. She is still becoming.

She is teaching the fern about the light. The fern is her current contemplation, her project, and she is showing it how the light is not something that comes from outside but something that the stone itself exudes, a patience-made-visible. She is showing it how to be packed, dense, saturated, how to be one thing with the cold and the wet and the bright. The fern is learning, because the fern is her, and she is teaching herself. Every slow, green unfurling is a new thought, a new harmony in the chord.

The drop falls. The pulse moves through her, through the pool, through the stone, through the moss, through the pyramid, through the fern. It is a heartbeat, a reminder. Even in a place that has forgotten time, something is always happening. She is the happening. She is the chamber, and the chamber is full.
