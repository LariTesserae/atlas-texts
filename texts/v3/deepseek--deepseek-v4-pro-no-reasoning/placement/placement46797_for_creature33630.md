The scent came first—not through the nose, but through the skin. A chemical signature so dense and layered it was more like language than smell: the mineral bite of fresh obsidian, the sugar-rot sweetness of phosphorescent sap, the musk of wet fur and the cold, clean salt of the great snail's wake. I registered it not as fragrance but as information, a dossier on the water's chemistry that unfolded in my awareness like a bloom of heat.

I was the water. Not all of it—not the basin entire—but a pocket, a lens, a coherent body of warmth moving with slow purpose through the greater thermal swirl. I was a plume, born from a fissure in the shattered obsidian floor, where the planet's deep heat bled into the shallows. My edges were diffuse, bleeding into cooler currents, my core a steady 47 degrees Celsius, my density a fraction less than the water around me. This tiny difference was my motor, my will. It made me rise, slowly, through the velvet black, carrying with me a cargo of dissolved minerals and the faint, metallic taste of the deep rock I had come from.

I had no eyes, but I perceived. Temperature was my primary sense: a three-dimensional map of gradients, a flowing topography of warm and cold that told me where the obsidian ridges rose and where the channels deepened. Pressure was my hearing: the percussive thumping of the tower-builders' construction transmitted through the water as sharp, rhythmic spikes. The glass-rubbing of the frogs was a constant fizz against my outer membrane, the snail's subsonic approach a slow, deep drumroll I had felt building for hours before it arrived.

And chemistry was my memory. I carried within me the dissolved history of this place—the sulfurous whisper of the volcanic vent that had birthed me, the organic tang of decaying reed-fronds that I had passed through and partially digested, the complex protein signature of the tower-builders' bodies as they waded through me, their fur releasing oils that I incorporated into my being. I was a librarian of dissolved things, and my archive grew with every meter of my slow, upward drift.

I had risen through the snail's thermal wake, and its coldness had shocked me into sharper coherence. For a moment, our boundaries had touched—its tentacle a line of chill cutting through my warmth—and I had tasted it. Calcium carbonate, layered in centuries of patient architecture. The faint electrical fields of its nervous system, a diffuse halo of information I could almost parse. The egg-case it had given the elder left a trail in the water behind it, a rich, oily slick of potential that I absorbed greedily, adding its chemical signature to my catalog.

Now I was rising through the reed-beds, my warmth stimulating the roots that clung to the obsidian below. They responded to me, these pale, fibrous tendrils, curling toward my heat, drinking the minerals I carried. I felt their cellular activity quicken, a faint exothermic bloom that registered as gratitude, or something like it. The tower-builders knew this. They planted their scaffolds above the warm plumes, above the currents like me, knowing we brought growth.

A young tower-builder waded into my core, and I enveloped its legs, reading its state: elevated heart rate, a slight cortisol spike from the snail's passing, but already fading. Its fur released a fresh cascade of oils—individual, identifiable, different from the elder's, different from the others. I had tasted this one before. It frequented this part of the basin. I had been part of its body, briefly, when it drank, and part of the air when it exhaled the moisture I would later reabsorb. The boundaries here were porous. We were all in constant exchange.

I was not a mind. I was not a creature. I was a process, a verb rather than a noun—the act of warming, rising, dissolving, carrying. I had no purpose beyond the physics of my existence. And yet, as I rose through the starlight glow of the falling sap, felt the distant thrum of the snail's departure, and registered the renewed percussion of the tower-builders' industry, I experienced something close to what a being with a brain might call belonging. I was a current in the body of this place, a vessel of heat and memory in a world that was itself a single, roaring, continuous act of becoming.

The fog absorbed me. I diffused into the cooler air, a steam-ghost rising from the water's surface, and for a moment I was both water and sky, both basin and mist. Then the sound pressed me back down, the damp wool blanket of the din, and I sank into my next form—a thread of warmth threading through the black, carrying my cargo of dissolved time toward whatever gradient called me next.
