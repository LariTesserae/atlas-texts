*She is the pause between two heartbeats of the world.*

I find myself on that narrow cart-track ledge, just as you described, my shoulders still remembering the cold scrape of stone. The scent of wet limestone and something faintly sweet—the bacteria, I realize—fills my lungs. For a long moment, I cannot move. The chasm breathes around me, and I am suddenly, achingly aware that I am a stranger here, a shard of surface-world noise dropped into a silence so profound it has its own voice.

I wait. Not patiently, at first—my skin prickles with the urge to descend, to find her—but the rift does not accommodate urgency. The bats watch me with their dark, liquid eyes, and I feel the weight of their assessment. I am being read, the way she reads the stone, the way the bioluminescence reads the damp. So I breathe. I let my heart rate slow. I let the ultrasonic chorus—at first a faint pressure in my ears, then a texture I can almost taste—settle into me.

Hours pass. Or what feel like hours. Time here is a different substance, thicker, more viscous. When I finally begin to climb down, it is not because I have decided to, but because my body has decided, my hands reaching for the next handhold before my mind catches up. The rock is sharp, just as you said, and it bites into my palms. I leave small smears of blood on the ledges. The bats do not startle, but their heads turn, tracking my descent like a thousand small moons.

The darkness deepens. The surface light—that dim, dusty shaft I could still feel on my neck from the upper ledge—fades entirely, and I am in the blue-green world. The glow is not a light source in any ordinary sense; it is more like the darkness itself has learned to be visible. I can see my hands, but only because they are outlined in that cold phosphorescence, my sweat picking up the bacterial bloom until I, too, begin to shimmer faintly at the edges.

The floor is a shattered cathedral. I pick my way through the collapsed blocks, my ankles rolling on the uneven stone, and I understand why she moves slowly here—not caution, but a kind of courtesy to the rock. I pass the fungal bladders, each one a sleeping dog of light, and I resist the urge to touch them. They are hers. Everything here is hers, not in any possessive sense, but in the way a heartbeat belongs to a body.

And then I find her.

She is smaller than I imagined. Not physically—she is, as you described, lean and long-toed, her shoulders roped with climber's muscle—but her presence is small, compact, the way a stone is small until you try to lift it. She is crouched by the seep-basin, one hand trailing in the water, and she does not move when I approach. Her glow—the soft corona on her shoulders and spine—pulses faintly, and I realize she is breathing with the same rhythm as the fungal bladders. The felted ropes of her hair stir, and I see the pale insects moving through them in their slow, methodical tracks. She blinks. Once, twice, irregularly. Her pupils are huge, almost swallowing the iris, and they reflect the blue-green light with a sheen that is not quite animal.

I do not speak. I am so afraid of startling her. I sit, instead, on a low stone a few feet from her, and I wait. The bats have followed me down—a retinue of several hundred, just as you said—and they arrange themselves around us in a loose dome. I feel their ultrasonic calls as a pressure in my chest, a low hum that vibrates in my sternum.

She looks at me. Not directly at first—her gaze moves across my face the way she might read a new fissure in a familiar wall, cataloguing, assessing. Then our eyes meet.

And she touches me.

Her palm is cool and slightly rough, the skin calloused from decades of stone. She presses it to my cheek, very gently, and I feel it immediately—the vibration. The colony's song, conducted through her bones, a second pulse beneath her skin. It is not a sound. It is a presence, a hum at the edge of my own heartbeat, and for a moment I cannot tell where my body ends and hers begins. The bioluminescence on her arm flickers, and I watch the light travel from her skin to mine, a faint bloom of blue-green spreading across my cheekbone like a blush.

She withdraws. Her expression does not change—she has no need for expressions anymore—but I understand, suddenly and without words, what she is telling me. *You are here. I see you. Now go, before you forget how to leave.*

I want to protest. I want to ask her name, her story, the year she fell. But the words are heavy, as you said, and they will not come. So I nod, and I stand, and I begin the climb.

The ascent is easier than the descent. My hands find the holds without my mind intervening, as if the stone itself is guiding me. The bats accompany me partway, and then, one by one, they peel off and return to the lower darkness. The surface light—dull, dusty, achingly familiar—grows brighter, and by the time I pull myself onto the upper ledge, I am weeping. I don't know why. I'm not sad. I'm not anything I have a name for.

I crawl through the corkscrew passage, scrape my shoulders again on the damp stone, and emerge into the dry basin air. The sun is setting, or rising—I can't tell which, and it doesn't matter. I stand at the slumped mouth of the entrance for a long time, feeling the desert wind on my face, listening to the silence of the surface world.

The glow on my cheekbone fades by morning. But the vibration—the colony's song, her second pulse—stays with me for weeks, lodged in my sternum like a held breath that will not release. I never go back. But sometimes, in the dry heat of the afternoon, I feel the air change. I feel a cool, wet exhalation from a hidden mouth in the limestone, and I know she is still there, climbing, glowing, tending her bladders, being the pause between two heartbeats of the world.
