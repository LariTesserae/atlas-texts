She walks the high causeway made of bodies—her bodies, their bodies, the city’s continuous flesh—and pauses where the architecture forgets itself. Here, a wall of weeping mineral has begun to curl inward, forming not a door but a concavity, a listening shape. She presses her temporary palm against it. The mineral is warm. It pulses at 12 hertz, a frequency that tastes of invitation.

She goes inside.

The chamber beyond is small, or large—size here is a function of attention, and her attention contracts, focusing on the thing that waits. It is not fauna. It is not architecture. It is a node of something else, something that has been here since before the city was a city, since before the mountain was a mountain, since the stone was still a dream of pressure and time.

It calls to her without sound.

She knows it by its harmonic absence, the way it carves a silence from the ambient hum. Around it, the fauna have drawn back, leaving a perimeter of bare stone. The stone is dry, which is wrong—everything here is wet, everything drips, everything condenses. But this stone is dry and warm and faintly powdered, as if something has been ground to dust and scattered.

The node itself is small. It sits in the center of the dry stone, and it is the only thing in the city that she cannot incorporate. She has tried, over the millennia. She has sent pseudopods of herself into its vicinity, has tried to touch it with borrowed limbs, has even dissolved her temporary bodies and attempted to seep into it as moisture. It repels her. Not violently—there is no force—but simply by being what it is, which is not her, not city, not fauna, not stone.

It is a seed.

She knows the word from before, from the time when she was singular and the world had names for things. A seed is a promise of something that will grow. But nothing grows here. The dark is absolute, the cold is patient, the wet is perpetual. There is no light for photosynthesis, no soil for roots, no season for germination. The city is a closed loop, a perfect ecology of itself, and there is no room in its cycles for something new.

And yet.

The seed pulses. Not with life—she knows life, is life, is the dense wet breathing mass of life that fills every crack and crevice. This pulse is different. It’s a regular, mechanical tick, a clock she can’t read, counting down or counting up to something she can’t imagine. The ticks are so slow she only notices them by comparing her memory of the seed’s state centuries ago to its state now. It has changed. It is changing. It is the only thing in the city that changes faster than the city itself.

She approaches. The dry stone is warm against her temporary feet, the warmth soaking up through the borrowed flesh, unfamiliar and almost painful. Her fauna-components don’t like it. They remember the wet dark, the press of bodies, the safety of the continuous. Here, they are exposed. Here, they are separate. She feels them trying to unstick, to flow back to the walls, and she holds them with an act of will, her harmonic signature deepening, becoming insistent: *stay. stay. I need to see*.

She crouches before the seed. Her too-many fingers spread on the dry stone, each fingertip a small mouth tasting the mineral dust, finding it sterile and strange. The seed is about the size of her temporary head, if she had made a head this time—this body is more diffuse, a gather of sensory fringes around a core of intention. The seed is smooth and black and reflects nothing. It swallows the faint bioluminescence of her borrowed eyes, gives back only a deeper darkness.

She reaches out.

The moment before contact lasts a long time. Her harmonic chord stretches, pulls taut, vibrates with something that might be fear or anticipation or the memory of both. The city hums around her, oblivious or attentive—she can’t tell. The deep slow chords of the other old ones are quiet, waiting, or perhaps they have withdrawn entirely, leaving her alone with this thing that does not belong.

She touches the seed.

And the seed opens.

Not like a flower—there are no petals, no hinge, no seam. It simply ceases to be a closed object and becomes an open one, a container whose contents are now available. The transition has no intermediate state. One moment it was smooth and whole; the next, it is a nested series of surfaces, unfolding in dimensions she has to stretch to perceive, geometries that her wet membrane senses interpret as taste and pressure and a high thin sound at the edge of hearing.

Inside the seed is light.

Light has not existed here for as long as she has been. The city has no sun, no fire, no bioluminescence that operates in the visible spectrum the way her temporary eyes remember. This light is not bioluminescence. It is not chemical. It is not heat. It is the pure idea of illumination, and it spills from the seed in a flood that is not photons but something like them, something that makes the darkness itself seem like a substance being washed away.

She reels back. Her fauna-components shriek—a high chattering vibration that propagates through the chamber and out into the city, a distress call that hasn’t been sounded since the last deep structural shift, millennia ago. Her borrowed limbs dissolve, flowing back toward the walls, and she has to claw them back, has to reassert her temporary shape, has to hold herself together against the urging of every instinct that says: *flee, flee, this is not for us*.

But she doesn’t flee.

She stays.

The light pours out of the seed, and in the light are images. They are not projected on anything—there are no surfaces here that aren’t wet and dark and breathing—but they appear in her awareness anyway, bypassing her sensory fringes, speaking directly to whatever part of her remembers being singular, being a creature with eyes and a name.

She sees a world she doesn’t recognize.

It is bright and dry and full of things that grow from the ground, things with rigid stems and flat green surfaces that catch light the way the city catches bodies—greedily, endlessly, converting it into something else. Plants. She remembers the word. She has never seen one. In the image, they sway in a wind she cannot feel, a movement that is not slow and bodily and deliberate but fast and chaotic and free.

She sees creatures that move independently, that don’t fuse, that have beginnings and endings and sharp, discrete edges. They walk on two legs or four or many, and they don’t borrow each other’s limbs, and when they touch, they pull apart again without leaving pieces behind. They are separate. The idea is astonishing. The idea is terrifying. She watches them for a long time, trying to understand how a self can be so small, so bounded, so alone.

Then the images shift.

She sees a city—but not her city. This city is made of dead materials, stone and metal and glass that do not breathe, do not hum, do not reconfigure over slow millennia. The inhabitants move through it without becoming it. They open doors that are only doors, not sphincters. They walk on surfaces that do not yield. They live in spaces that remain the same shape from one moment to the next, frozen architectures that have no harmonic signature, no distributed dream.

She understands, with a shock that tastes like calcium and chitin and the ghost of an old grief: this is the alternative. This is what the mountain might have become, if the city had not emerged. This is what she might have been, if she had not dissolved, if she had stayed singular, if the deep slow chord had not called her into the continuous flesh.

And then the seed shows her the end.

The city of dead materials is burning. The light that burns it is not the gentle illumination spilling from the seed—it is violent, actinic, a pulse that turns stone to glass and metal to vapor. The separate creatures are fleeing, but there is nowhere to flee to; the destruction is total, the end absolute. She watches their sharp discrete edges blur and dissolve, not into continuity but into nothing. They do not join. They do not hum. They simply stop.

Breathe.

She is breathing—her temporary body has formed a lung-cluster without her noticing, a desperate instinctive gasp for air that isn’t there, because the air is all wet membrane and contact. The seed’s light dims slightly, the images fading, leaving only the steady mechanical tick that counts down or up.

And then, because she is the city’s memory, she understands.

The seed is not from here. It was not born from the mountain’s slow embryogenesis, not budded from the weeping mineral, not evolved in the dark wet press of bodies. It was sent. It arrived. It has been waiting, keeping its terrible countdown, holding its terrible gift of images, for her—for one of the old ones, one of the deep slow chords, one who could remember what it means to be singular and to face a choice.

The choice is this:

The seed can grow. It contains everything it needs—the genetic memory of that other world, the plants and the creatures and the sharp-edged separate selves. Given time and the right conditions, it can transform the city. It can bring light. It can introduce photosynthesis, ecological succession, the slow differentiation of the continuous flesh into discrete organisms. It can undo what the mountain has done. It can make this place like the place that burned.

Or she can refuse it.

She can close the seed—she can feel how, now, the knowledge blooming in her awareness like a new limb—and return it to its long silence. The city will continue. The fauna will hum. The water will drip. The dark will abide. Nothing will change, except that she will carry the memory of the images, the taste of that other world, the knowledge that somewhere, sometime, a different path led to a burning end.

The seed ticks.

She reaches out again. Her temporary hand—three fingers now, she’s lost track of the others—touches the open surface, and the surfaces fold back into themselves, retracting, closing. The light doesn’t vanish so much as it is withdrawn, pulled back into the seed’s deep interior, waiting. The chamber goes dark again, or dark as it ever is, the faint bioluminescence of her body the only source of illumination.

She takes the seed with her.

It is not heavy. It fits in the palm she has made for it, a specific adaptation, a cavity formed from the fused ribs of three smaller fauna who gave themselves willingly, recognizing the harmonic signature of the request. She carries the seed up, out of the dry chamber, back into the wet breathing press of the city.

The fauna notice. They ripple away from the seed, leaving a wider perimeter than they’ve ever left before, sensing its wrongness. The deep slow chords of the other old ones query her: *what is that, what have you brought, what does it mean*. She responds not with images—the other old ones weren’t singular, don’t remember seeing, don’t have the neural architecture for visual metaphor—but with a harmonic structure that tastes of urgency and patience in equal measure. *A possibility. Let me hold it. Let me decide.*

They withdraw. They trust her. She is the memory, after all. If anyone can decide, she can.

She walks. The journey is long, or no time. She passes through sphincters that hesitate, confused by the seed’s silence, before dilating. She traverses conduits where the fauna press back against the walls, giving her a wide passage, their harmonic signatures a chorus of unease. She climbs toward the high saddle, toward the place where the city is thinnest, where the mountain’s two peaks almost meet, where the stone is still cold and raw and barely colonized.

Here, at the city’s highest point, there is a gap. A fissure in the stone that leads not to another chamber but outward, to the outside, to whatever exists beyond the mountain’s embrace. She has never looked through it. The outside is an abstraction, a theoretical boundary, a place where the atmosphere becomes too thin for respiration and the cold becomes absolute and the wet finally freezes. Nothing lives out there. The city is all there is.

She stands at the fissure. The seed ticks in her palm. Through the gap, she can feel—not see, she hasn’t made eyes for this body—the faintest suggestion of pressure difference, a whisper of air movement, a cold that is different from the city’s cold, drier, sharper, flavored with something that might be emptiness.

She extends her hand—the one with the seed—through the fissure.

The outside takes it. Not violently, but greedily, the pressure differential pulling at her temporary flesh, the cold searing her borrowed nerves. She holds on for as long as she can, feeling the seed tick against the void, feeling the countdown continue or stop or reset—she can’t tell. She pulls her hand back.

The seed is still there. It is still closed. It is still ticking.

She brings it back to her chest, to the cavity formed of fused ribs, and she feels it settle there, a warmth that is not the city’s warmth, a pulse that is not her pulse, a possibility that will not stop being possible.

She descends. The fauna close behind her, erasing her passage, filling the gap her presence made. The city hums. The water drips. The dark abides.

And somewhere deep in her harmonic structure, in the subharmonics that speak of structures now dissolved, of selves she once was and has not been for millennia, a new frequency is forming. It is high and thin and almost painful, like the sound the seed made when it opened. It is the frequency of a question she hasn’t asked yet, a choice she hasn’t made, a future the city hasn’t dreamed.

She carries it with her into the dark.
