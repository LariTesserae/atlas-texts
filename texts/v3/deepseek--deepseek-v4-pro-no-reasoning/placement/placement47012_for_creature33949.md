The first thing is the cold. Not a shock, but a constant, low hum of presence. Like a second skin laid over mine, seeping in from the world, slowing everything. I do not remember a time before the cold.

I am small. A canopy-runner, they would say, if there were words to say it. But there are no words. There are only textures, pressures, the map of the city unfolding around me in a sphere of echoes. I taste the air with every breath, a faint metallic tang of dissolved stone, the richer, earthier notes of spore and moss, the sweetness of my own fur wicking moisture toward my mouth. I am never thirsty. I am never dry.

My hands know the world before the rest of me does. Seven fingers on each, two of them curled inward now, resting, the others lightly embracing a root-cable as thick as my torso. The root is alive. I feel its slow pulse, the minute expansion and contraction as it draws water up from somewhere deep, somewhere I will never go. My fingers read its skin, the fine grain of its fibers, the slick coating of biofilm that makes it tacky, grippable. The city is always growing. The cable I hold now was not here when I was young. I remember the taste of the air before it was woven, the slightly different way echoes fell into the gap. I do not remember being born.

A tap, sharp and clean, returns to me from my left hand. The two specialized digits snap together in a rhythm I do not think about, a question I do not realize I am asking. The echoes paint the space ahead: a junction of three trunks twisting together overhead, a curtain of dangling aerial roots just beyond, the soft, absorbent shape of another Clade approaching along the high bridge. The echoes tell me it is a male. They tell me he is carrying something.

We meet at the junction where the trunks fuse. There is a platform here, grown from flattened buttress roots, worn smooth by countless passages. The moss is thick, like wet fur itself, and it yields under my fingers as I settle. The male is larger than me, his sensory plate broader, his movements deliberate. He is an elder, perhaps only a few cycles from descending to the weaver terraces. I can taste a change in his surface chemistry, a bitterness that signals the beginning of the metamorphosis.

We do not greet. There is no word for greeting. We simply come together, as two streams of water merge in a crevice. His hands find my shoulders, my hands find his flanks. The fur parts easily, slick and pliable, and our foreheads press together. The plates meet with a soft, wet adhesion.

The rush is immediate.

A cascade of protein-memory floods into me, carried in the clear fluid that bridges the gap between our skin. It is not a story. It is not an image. It is a complete sensory reconstruction, and for a moment, I am him, and I am elsewhere.

I am in the lower terraces, where the mist is a constant weight and the light is not light at all but a faint blue luminescence from the fungal mats. The creaking of the city is louder here, a deeper note, the groan of the great pillars under their own weight. I am tasting a new surface—stone, yes, the familiar limestone, but something is wrong. The biofilm is receding. The stone feels naked, raw, almost hot in comparison to the living skin that should cover it. There is a slickness that should not be there, a greasy residue that clings to the fingers. I am scraping at it, and under the residue, the stone is pitted, softened. My tongue tastes something acrid, a chemical signature I do not recognize. It is not part of the city. It is new.

The memory ends. The elder pulls back, and I am myself again, gasping slightly as my own body reasserts its boundaries. But the memory is still in me, dissolved into my hemolymph, circulating through my tissues. I will carry it now. It is mine. I am the memory.

The elder clicks once, a low-frequency pulse that says nothing specific but feels like a push. I understand. This memory is troubling. The stone-sickness. I have heard of it from others, fragments of fragments, but never a direct, clean taste like this. It is spreading. The elder cannot leave his territory now; he is too close to the change. But I am young. I am swift. I can make the descent.

Fear is not a chemical I produce much of. But something close to it stirs in me now. The descent. The cold pools. The Progenitor.

I leave the platform without a sound, my fingers uncurling to grip the wet cables, my body swinging down into the deeper dark. The echoes become closer, more intimate, as the spaces narrow. I pass other Clade, some moving upward with their own memories, some huddled in the mouths of roosting chambers, their young clinging to their bellies. A female with a fresh litter—three tiny forms, their plates already pulsing with their first clicks—pauses to let me by. I taste the milk-scent of her, the faint, sweet exudate she feeds her young. She clicks a question, a soft, curious ping. I do not answer. The memory is too heavy to share piecemeal. It must go down.

The temperature drops as I descend. The air thickens. Water streams down the walls in constant, shining rivulets, and my fur is soaked through, heavy, though I drink from it without thinking. The terraces I cross now are quieter. Fewer runners. More weavers.

The first weaver I pass is anchored in a vertical crevice, his body so fused with the stone that I cannot tell where cartilage ends and limestone begins. His limbs move in a rhythm that is almost hypnotic, the spinneret digits on his right hand extruding a fine, glistening thread that he loops and knots around a new root growth. He is building a bridge where a gap has opened, a slow, patient repair. His forehead plate is leaking. A steady, clear ooze drips down the stone below him, feeding a thick mat of phosphorescent moss. As I pass, the moss pulses brighter, stirred by my movement. The weaver does not acknowledge me. He is deep in his work, his consciousness diffused, his memories bleeding out into the city itself. I taste a fragment of him as I pass—a sense of immense age, of a lifetime of canopy-running in a body now utterly still, of a slow, quiet contentment.

The pools are close now. The stone under my fingers is sharper, the flutes of the limestone like blades, unweathered. The creaking of the city is a deep, sustained note here, almost subsonic, felt more in the bones of my hands than heard. The water on the walls is colder. I can feel my own movements slowing, my metabolism balking at the chill.

And then the mist clears, and I am at the edge of the last pool.

It is perfectly still. The water is so clear that the limestone basin beneath seems to glow. Pale, milky-white, the Progenitor fills the pool entirely, its body a single, undifferentiated mass, its surface smooth and translucent. I can see veins of deeper tissue threading through it, slow pulses of circulation, darker nodules of concentrated neural matter. It does not move. It does not need to. It feels me. The water trembles with my presence, every vibration of my body transmitted into its substance. I am already in its mind.

The cold of the water is a living thing. I lower one foot, then another, and the shock is so complete that my clicking digits spasm involuntarily, sending a frantic burst of echoes skittering across the pool's surface. The Progenitor does not react. The cold climbs my legs, my torso, my arms. My fur plasters flat. My breathing slows. The world dims as my sensory plate begins to numb.

I wade forward until the mass of the Progenitor is before me. Its surface is not skin. It is a gel, cool and slightly yielding, and when I press my forehead to it, there is no clear boundary. My plate sinks in, merges. The contact is immediate and total.

I do not give the memory. The memory is taken.

The Progenitor pulls it from me in a flood, not just the stone-sickness but everything—my taste of the elder's bitterness, the feel of the moss on the junction platform, the tiny, curious ping of the female with her litter, the fragment of the weaver's contentment, the cold shock of the water, my own fear. All of it, the full, unedited gestalt of my descent, pours into the gel in a single, overwhelming current. I am emptied. I am a hollow vessel, a shell of cartilage and fur, barely conscious.

And then the return flow begins.

It is not a memory. It is an imperative. A cascade of proteins, complex and unfamiliar, floods back into my body, saturating my hemolymph, binding to receptors I did not know I had. The compulsion is immediate, non-negotiable. I must return to the upper terraces. I must find the elder. I must touch him, and through him, every Clade he touches. The stone-sickness is not a disease of the stone. It is a fermentation. The residue is a new kind of biofilm, one that can feed the trees more efficiently than the old. The pitting is not decay; it is digestion, a new symbiosis trying to establish itself. The city is not dying. It is being offered a change. And the Progenitor has decided, in its vast, slow, integrating intelligence, to accept.

I am to carry a new message. Not a warning. A welcome.

The imperative releases me. I pull back from the gel, and my forehead plate detaches with a soft, wet sound. The world is sharp again, hyperclear. My body is not my own; it is a vehicle for the Progenitor's will. I turn, wade out of the pool, and my fur begins to dry almost instantly, wicking the water away as I start the climb.

The cold recedes. The creaking of the city surrounds me, steady as breath. I climb, and I carry the answer, and I am no longer afraid. I am an extension. An organ. A memory with legs. The city is thinking, and I am its thought, moving upward, toward the lightless sky of the canopy, toward the waiting elder, toward the new thing growing in the stone, which is not a sickness at all, but the beginning of something the city has never tasted before.
