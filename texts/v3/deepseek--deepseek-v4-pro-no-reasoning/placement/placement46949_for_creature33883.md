The figure stands at the edge of a pool that should not exist. This is not the usual condensation—that shallow, motionless sheen that gathers in the low places—but something deeper, an actual body of water that has collected over centuries in a sunken plaza, fed by a slow, constant seep through the ceiling high above. A single droplet falls every seventeen seconds, striking the same spot with unerring precision. The figure has counted this rhythm for so long that its body knows when the next drop is due before its mind does; it feels a tiny, premonitory flinch in the muscles of its neck, a tensing that releases when the sound arrives. Plink. Seventeen seconds. Plink. The pool, in the figure's dark-adapted vision, is not black but a deep, luminous grey, a shade that exists only in the absence of light—the colour of absolute zero given a slight warmth by the ambient heat of the walls, which the figure can sense as a faint infrared glow, a radiation it feels on its skin but does not see with its eyes. The pool reflects nothing. It is too still, too perfectly flat, a mercury mirror of the cathedral-dark above it, and the figure has spent many cycles sitting at its edge, trying to determine if the reflection is a reflection at all or simply another version of the city, inverted, equally real, equally empty.

Today—if "today" means anything, which it doesn't, except that the drone is in its lowest phase and the spores have not yet fallen—the figure is holding something new. It is a cylinder, about the length of its hand from wrist to fingertip, made of a material that is neither metal nor ceramic nor the strange poured composites of the walls, but something that feels, beneath the spatulate pads of its fingers, like the memory of a thing that was once alive and has since been transformed by immense pressure and time. It is heavy in a way that surprises the figure. The weight is disproportionate to its size; it seems to pull downward with a force that isn't quite gravity, as if the object itself is eager to return to the earth, to sink through the floor and join some deeper stratum. The figure found it in a crevice, caught between two slumped structural members that had shifted in the last groaning-cycle and opened a gap that hadn't been there before. The gap was narrow, barely wide enough for the figure's narrow shoulders, and it had to wriggle on its belly for several body-lengths to reach the object, feeling the warmth of the walls press against its back and belly, the fuzz on its skin prickling with the friction. It was worth it. The object is entirely smooth except for a single seam that runs its length, a hairline crack so fine that the figure can barely feel it with the pad of its index finger, and at one end, a shallow depression, perfectly circular, the size of a lung-sponge's largest pore. The figure does not know what the depression is for. It has spent the last three rest-cycles pressing its thumb into it, feeling the way the pressure distributes, the slight give—there is a mechanism inside, something that wants to move but has, over the long erosion, become stuck. The figure knows about stuckness. It knows about the long patience required to wait for things to shift, to open, to reveal their inner workings. It knows that forcing something usually breaks it, and breaking something is a kind of death, and death is a concept it understands only as an absence—the absence of the animal that left its trail, the absence of the bones' owners, the absence of anyone else to witness the spiral of skulls. So it does not force the cylinder. It holds it. It carries it with it everywhere, tucked into a fold of the matted fuzz at its hip.

Today, sitting by the pool, it takes the cylinder out and examines it again. The humidity is high—the streaming phase is due to begin within the next few hours, the figure can tell by the way the air feels thicker, by the faint pressure change that makes its long ears unfold slightly, seeking more surface area for the vibrations—and a bead of moisture has formed on the cylinder's surface. The figure watches the bead trace a slow path along the seam, gathering other beads as it goes, until it reaches the depression at the end and pools there, a tiny convex lens of water. The figure tilts the cylinder. The lens wobbles. The figure has an idea.

It has seen, in the far chambers where the light-drinking walls are thinner and the electrical fields ghosting through the ancient infrastructure are stronger, a kind of faint luminescence—not a glow, exactly, but a shimmer, a visual hum that matches the auditory drone. It is not bright enough to see by. The figure's eyes, adapted to the 0.3 light rate, can barely perceive it even when it knows what to look for. But it has often thought that if it could concentrate that shimmer, focus it somehow, it might be able to see a little further, a little clearer. It has tried to do this with curved pieces of the wall-condensation film, scraping it into a lens shape, but the film is too fragile, too quick to dissolve. The cylinder, though. The cylinder has a depression that holds water. The water, pooled in the depression, forms a dome. A dome that might, if the figure angles it just right, catch the shimmer and bend it.

It moves slowly. Speed is not a virtue down here. The city rewards patience, punishes haste—the figure learned this early, in its youth (which was, itself, a hundred thousand drops ago), when it tried to run through a corridor and fell, badly, splitting the skin of its knee on a sharp angle that hadn't yet been rounded by the long erosion. The scar is still there, a pale seam on the translucent skin. It crouches now at the edge of the pool, holding the cylinder in both hands, and points the water-filled depression toward the place where it knows the shimmer is strongest—a section of the far wall where the conduits glow with their corner-of-the-eye light, a faint, ghostly trace of the city's nervous system. The figure tilts the cylinder. The water lens wobbles, then settles. And for the first time in a very long time, the figure sees something new.

It is not a shape. It is not a creature. It is a colour. Specifically, it is a colour the figure has never seen before, because there is no light down here to produce it, no source bright enough to excite the cones in its dark-adapted retinas. But the water lens, acting as a crude magnifier and focusing element, has concentrated the ambient shimmer just enough, just barely enough, to push a single wavelength into visibility. The colour is blue. Not the faint blue of its own visible veins, not the deep grey-blue of the pool's reflection, but a vivid, electric blue, the blue of a spark, the blue of a sky the figure has never seen and cannot imagine. It flickers for only a moment—the figure's hands are trembling, the lens wobbles, the angle is lost—and then it's gone. The cylinder falls from the figure's grip and clatters against the buckled pavement. The sound echoes, impossibly, arriving from five different directions, a ripple of metallic noise that sets the distant groaning to stir.

The figure sits back on its heels. Its heart is striking—two fast beats, then a pause, then two more. It feels light, as if the density parameter of its own body has decreased. It has just experienced something it has no framework for. It has no word for blue. It has no concept of colour as a category, only the gradations of grey and the faint infrared wash of the warm walls. But it knows that what it saw was not grey. It was something else. Something the city has been hiding, or something the city has been unable to show, because the city is old and half-blind, its conduits dim, its light sources extinct. Something that has been waiting in the physics of the world for an eye to perceive it.

The figure picks up the cylinder. The water lens has spilled. It doesn't matter. It knows the angle now. It can do it again. It will do it again, many times, in the long cycles ahead. It will learn the precise tilt, the precise focus, the precise moment in the shimmer's cycle when the light is strongest. It will map the blue. It will find other colours—there must be others, a whole spectrum hidden in the dark, waiting for a lens and an eye and the patience to bring them forth. The figure feels something it has never felt before: a hunger that is not in its stomach but in its mind, a drive to understand, not just to survive. This is new. This is terrifying and exhilarating in a way that makes its fuzz stand on end and its ears fold and unfold rapidly, cycling through their full range of motion.

The droplets continue to fall. Plink. Seventeen seconds. Plink. The figure counts them without counting, the rhythm so ingrained it is now a part of its own heartbeat. It tucks the cylinder back into the fold at its hip and rises, uncurling to its full question-mark height. It will return to its hollow. It will scrape the wall-film and eat. It will make a new object—maybe a stand for the cylinder, something to hold it steady at the correct angle, something with a pivot that can be adjusted in tiny increments. It has wire, it has resin from the lung-sponges' dried secretions, it has time. More time than it can measure. More time than the city has had, perhaps, because the city is old but the figure is young enough to still be surprised, and that surprise is a kind of renewal, a tiny seed of something that wasn't there before.

As it walks, the drone begins its slow intensification, building toward the next high phase. The walls start their faint vibration, a bass note that travels up through the soles of the figure's bare feet. The figure hums along, a minor third above the drone's fundamental, and the distant groaning answers, a long, slow exhale that shifts the air pressure and makes the pools shiver. The percussive knocks will come again—two knocks, forty-seven seconds of silence, then two more—and the figure will count them, as it always does. But now, in the quiet between the knocks, it will be thinking of blue. It will be thinking of the shape of the lens, the angle of the light, the feel of the cylinder's weight in its hands. It will be planning.

Somewhere, far above, in a world that is a rumor no one believes, the sun is rising. The figure does not know this. It does not know what a sun is. But as it walks through the cathedral-dark of the sunken city, the cylinder warm against its hip, it is carrying the first colour the dark has ever yielded, and that colour is a kind of dawn.
