I do what you’re not supposed to do. I pick it up.

The keepling weighs almost nothing. Its soft vanes curl around my thumb with a touch that is neither warm nor cool—just *present*, the way the air is present, the way the pressure-language is present against my skin. It isn’t fragile, exactly. It flexes with a tensile strength that suggests the mineral base is already beginning to precipitate, microscopic crystals forming in the translucent tissue. In a few weeks—or years, or decades, whatever time means here—it will have the same obsidian-sharp edges as its parent.

The larger keeper doesn’t react in any way I can read. Its fronds remain angled toward me, its nodules pulsing in their slow off-rhythm, but it makes no move to reclaim what it has given. The space between us feels suddenly formal, charged with the weight of an exchange I don’t fully understand. A gift. A question. I am being asked something, and my answer will be written in whatever I do next—acceptance or refusal, comprehension or confusion, all encoded in the chemical signature of my skin, the rate of my exhale, the micro-fluctuations of my humidity against the keeper’s waiting vanes.

So I do something I haven’t done since I arrived. I speak.

Not with sound. I don’t have the apparatus for pressure-language, not the way they do. But I breathe. Deliberately. I match my exhalation to the city’s cycle—seven seconds slow, three seconds held—and I direct the stream of air toward the keeper, toward the frond now empty of its offspring, toward the warm cavity still open in its center. My carbon dioxide. My water vapor. My microbiome, whatever trace flora and fauna I carry from a surface world that is dry and bright and full of animals. I give the keeper everything I am in a single, calculated breath.

The keeper’s nodules flare.

It’s not bright, exactly—the amber light is too dim, too diffuse for brightness—but there is a sudden *deepening*, a saturation of the glow that spreads through its vascular tissue like a blush. The fronds shiver. The pressure in the air around it changes, a rapid series of pulses that I feel against my face like a question being repeated, refined, rephrased: *Ah. You can hear. You can speak. You are not as temporary as we assumed.*

I don’t know if “we” is the keeper in front of me or the city itself. I don’t think the distinction matters.

The keepling in my hand shifts. One of its undeveloped vanes has found my pulse point, the thin skin at my wrist, and it presses there with a curiosity that is not yet capable of harm. It’s reading me—my heart rate, my blood pressure, the electrical signals that ripple through my nervous system—and I feel a sudden, vertiginous sense of exposure. I am being *known*. Catalogued by a creature that categorizes the world not in nouns but in flows, in pressures, in the slow, patient language of systems tending toward equilibrium.

I wonder what it sees. A leaky vessel of salt and oxygen. A walking disturbance. A new kind of lung.

Below us, in the amber-lit hollow of the city, one of the larger water channels shifts. I hear it before I see it—a change in the hum, a new overtone that makes the keepers across the terraces ripple in unison, their fronds aligning toward whatever is happening in the depths. The larger keeper turns, not quickly but with a suddenness that is as close to speed as its kind allows. Its nodules pulse faster now, a tempo I haven’t seen before, and the pressure against my face forms a new pattern: a word, singular and completely untranslatable, something about the veil-cut channels and the hollow below them, something about a cycle that is longer than the seven-second breath, something about *now*.

The city’s exhale catches. Just for a moment. The three-second rhythm stutters to four, to five, and the pressure in the chamber drops enough that my ears pop and the keepling clings tighter to my wrist, startled.

Something is wrong.

Or right, but in a way that is not for me. The keepers are moving now, all of them, a slow cascade of mineral vanes and amber pulses that ripples down the terraces toward the drop. They are orienting toward the depth, toward the fog and the dark and the sense of a much larger volume below. The city is not just a lung. It is a throat. And something is rising through it, something that the keepers have been waiting for longer than I’ve been alive, something that the pause in the exhale was preparing for.

The larger keeper doesn’t forget me. As it begins its slow, slate-shuffling descent toward the terrace edge, one frond detaches—*detaches*, breaks cleanly at a joint I didn’t even see—and lays itself on the stone at my feet. Another gift. Another question. I look down at the frond, at the keepling wrapped around my wrist, at the cavity in the keeper’s center that is already sealing, healing, becoming whole.

I don’t belong here. I am a visitor, an anomaly, a single-lunged creature in a city that breathes with a purpose I will never fully understand. But I have been given a keepling and a frond and a question that I am only beginning to learn how to answer.

So I follow.

The keeper leads me downward, into the hollow, into the fog, and the city’s breath resumes its seven-second cycle, and the keepling pulses once against my wrist, a slow, amber flicker that matches nothing but my heartbeat.
