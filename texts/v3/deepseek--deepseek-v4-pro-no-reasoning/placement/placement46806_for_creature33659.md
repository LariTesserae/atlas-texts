The new one is not yet a Breather. It is a soft, pulsing thing the size of a curled fist, suspended in a nursery-pod no larger than a cupped pair of hands. The pod is tethered to the ceiling of a small chamber deep in the old quarter of Sibilant, where the kelp fibers are dense and dark with age, their bioluminescent symbionts a slow, deep blue. The chamber is a private place, a woven alcove just large enough for one adult to curl around the pod, which is what I do.

I am—or I think I am—the parent. The word is imprecise; among the Breathers, what I am is called the Donor-of-Flesh. Forty cycles of the great canopy's light-dimming ago, I felt a slow, insistent tug in the tissue deep beneath my thoracic gills. A bud. I went to the communal garden and selected a strand of the oldest, most vital kelp, a cable whose subsonic hum was a steady, reassuring drone. With my obsidian flake, I made a small incision in my own side, just below the ribs, and guided the kelp fiber into the wound. The pain was a silver-green flash, a color I have only ever seen in that moment. Then the kelp and I began our conversation.

For ten cycles, I felt the bud grow, feeding on my filtered blood, shaping itself according to a pattern I could taste but not fully understand. It was a constant, intimate ache, a sensation of being gently, repeatedly stretched. I ate more of the micro-algal cakes, and my bioluminescence shifted from calm cyan to a steady, pulsing magenta—the color of creation, my neighbors hummed through the walls when they touched them. They were right. I was making.

When the pod detached, it was a birth and a release. It emerged from my side as a perfect, translucent pearl, trailing a few strands of my own tissue. I caught it in my webbed hands before it could float away, and I felt an emotion I have no name for, a pressure behind my dark eyes that wasn't water. I carried the pod to this chamber, my own dwelling, and grafted its tether to the ceiling with my own hands, singing to it all the while through the kelp: *you are here, you are held, you are becoming*.

Now I watch. That is my only task now. The Donor-of-Flesh watches until the Unfurling, and I have been here for thirty cycles, barely moving, only occasionally scraping a bit of algal cake from the wall near my head. My neighbors touch the outer wall of my dwelling three times per sleep-cycle, a gentle inquiry: *does it stir?* I press back: *not yet*. The wait is a slow, liquid thing, a held breath that lasts for weeks.

Inside the pod, the form is becoming less of a suggestion and more of a fact. At first, it was a cloud of cells, a milky swirl that could have been anything. Then a pulse. Then a curl. Now I can see the distinct curve of a skull, the delicate dark spots that will be eyes, the tiny, feathery ridges of gills folded flat against a neck that doesn't yet exist. The fingers are there, all four of them, each with its impossible double thumbs, curled into fists smaller than my fingertip. The pod membrane is growing thinner as the form inside grows stronger. Soon, I will be able to taste its first dream through the water. The Breathers say that in the pod, a new one dreams of the city as it was before it was woven, a forest of wild, unguided kelp reaching for a sun it could not yet touch. I don't know if this is true, but I watch the tiny form twitch and I believe it.

My own gills flutter, a soft sigh escaping them. A bubble of filtered water, heavy with my own chemistry, drifts across the space between me and the pod. It touches the membrane, and for a moment, the pod shimmers with a faint, answering magenta. The new one has felt me. It knows I am here.

This is the life of a Breather: not to build, not to hunt, not to conquer, but to wait. To watch a small, translucent sphere in a dim, woven room, and to feel the slow, patient architecture of a new being knitting itself together, one cell at a time, inside a membrane I grew from my own body and the body of the city. The subsonic creak of Sibilant is my lullaby and my clock. My obsidian flake rests in my palm, ready for the moment when I must sever the tether. My hands, those strange, symmetrical hands, are steady. My light is a steady, mothering magenta.

The new one turns in its sleep. A tiny hand unfurls, presses against the inner wall of the pod, and I see the pads of its fingers, already sensitive, already tasting the world. I raise my own hand and press it to the outside of the membrane, aligning my fingers with its fingers, the barrier between us thinner than a thought.

*Soon*, I hum into the kelp. *Soon, little architect. The city is waiting for your hands*.
