Sigrid-of-the-Rustle presses her palm flat against the wall of her dwelling and feels the hum. It is not the spindle's hum, which sits in the hand like a held breath, but the deeper thing—the vibration that lives in the mineral body of the cleft, that has always lived there, that will outlast the last grub-harvest and the last fiber-spinner and the last child calling across the tiers in the click-speech of her people. She has no word for it. The old ones call it the *under-song*, and they say it was the first sound the First Carvers heard when they hollowed the dwellings from the chalcedony, the sound that told them where to cut and where to leave the walls untouched. She does not know if this is true. She knows only that when she places her palm flat against the pale, soap-smooth mineral, she feels something answer.

Today is a gathering day. The word for it in the click-speech is a rolling, rhythmic pattern of three syllables that translates roughly to *the-time-when-the-deep-gives*. It happens once in every cycle, when the wall-glow is at its brightest and the beetle-streams run thickest in the lower thatch. The mat-wardens have been watching for it—she knows this because Feran told her, pausing at her entrance this morning with a grub in each hand and excitement brightening his broad face. "The streams are turning," he said, and that was all she needed to hear.

She descends.

The foot-ledges are crowded today. Everyone on the third tier is climbing down, and the second, and the first; the ladders are alive with pale bodies and pale garments and the soft, rhythmic sound of bare feet finding worn stone. The air thickens as she descends, the sweet scent of the thatch growing dense enough to taste, and underneath it something else: a sharper, earthier note she has come to associate with the gathering days, something the mat releases when it is disturbed in a particular way. The children are already shrieking with excitement on the lower ledges, their click-voices high and rapid. Lirra is somewhere below—Sigrid caught a flash of her pale hair a moment ago, the child climbing down with the reckless speed of the young, her small hands and feet finding holds that an adult would pause at.

The mat at ground level is different today. The wardens have been working since the first brightening, clearing a wide circle in the vegetation, cutting the gorth-vines back from the walls and trampling the thatch into a springy, level floor. The circle is ringed with oil-lamps on tall carved stands, their flames steady in the motionless air, casting a warm, golden light that makes the wall-glow seem pale and ghostly by comparison. The people are gathering around the edges, finding places on the trampled thatch or on the lowest ledges, their faces lit from below. The wardens stand at the center—three of them, Eldest-Mother Tanith and her two apprentices, their garments marked with the crimson chevrons of their calling, their hands stained dark from cycles of working in the deep mat.

And at the center of the circle, there is a hole.

It is not large—wide enough for a person to climb into, but not comfortably—and its edges are raw, the cut vegetation still seeping pale sap. The hole goes down through the thatch, through the compressed layers of dead vegetation, into darkness. Sigrid can feel the warmth rising from it even from where she stands at the edge of the circle. It is a different warmth from the constant, gentle heat of the cleft. This is a focused thing, a breath from somewhere deeper, carrying a smell she cannot name: mineral and organic at once, old and new, the scent of something that has been waiting.

The gathering is a ritual, but it is also practical. The deep-grub, the *kith-mother*, rises from below once in every cycle to lay her eggs in the thatch. She is vast—the size of a curled child, Eldest-Mother Tanith says, though Sigrid has only ever glimpsed parts of her emerging from the hole: a segmented body of pale, translucent white, legs that end in delicate, probing fronds, eyes that are not eyes but patches of darker tissue sensitive to the vibration of the world. She comes to the surface only to spawn, and when she does, the people gather. The eggs she lays are taken and incubated in the dwelling tiers, where they will hatch into the next generation of beetle-grubs, the foundation of the city's protein. The spawning is a gift, and the gathering is a receiving of the gift, and Eldest-Mother Tanith is the one who receives.

Sigrid finds a place near the front of the circle, where she can see. Lirra appears at her side, breathless, and tucks her small hand into Sigrid's. The child's palm is damp. "I saw her," Lirra whispers, the click-words so soft they are almost subvocal. "Last time. I saw her come up. She was singing."

The kith-mother does not sing—not in any way the people understand. But Sigrid knows what Lirra means. She has heard it too, on other gathering days: a vibration that emerges from the hole, a frequency that sits just below hearing, a pressure in the bones and the teeth. The spindle hums at the same pitch. She has noticed this before, and she has never spoken of it, because speaking of it would mean explaining something she cannot explain, a connection she cannot trace.

The gathering begins.

Eldest-Mother Tanith raises her arms, and the click-speech of the crowd fades into silence. She is old—the oldest person in the cleft, older than anyone can remember, her face a map of fine lines, her hair white as the gorth-vine fiber, her skin glowing with the deep, steady luminescence of someone who has spent more cycles in the wall-glow than anyone else alive. Her voice is soft, but it carries. She speaks the words of the Calling, the old syllables that are not quite the click-speech, that belong to an older tongue, the one the stories say was spoken by the First Carvers when they first found the kith-mother in the deep and made the covenant with her kind. Sigrid does not understand all the words. No one does. But the rhythm of them is familiar, a slow, rocking cadence that makes her think of the spindle's hum, of the under-song, of the deep vibration that lives in the walls.

And then the mat heaves.

It is a slow, rolling motion, a wave that passes through the trampled thatch and makes the oil-lamps flicker. The people draw back from the hole, a collective intake of breath that sounds like wind through the gorth-vines. Sigrid tightens her grip on Lirra's hand. The warmth from the hole intensifies, and the smell—the strange, deep smell—grows stronger, and then the kith-mother rises.

She is beautiful. Sigrid has seen this before, but it always surprises her, the sheer, slow majesty of it. The kith-mother emerges from the hole in sections, her pale body glistening with the moisture of the deep, her many legs unfolding with a delicate, jointed precision. Her fronds extend, testing the air, and her dark eye-patches turn toward the light of the oil-lamps. She makes a sound—not a song, but something like it, a low, thrumming vibration that Sigrid feels in her chest, in her palms, in the soles of her feet. The spindle, back in its niche on the third tier, is humming. She knows this without knowing how.

Eldest-Mother Tanith steps forward. She holds a woven basket in her hands, a deep, bowl-shaped thing lined with damp thatch-fiber. She speaks to the kith-mother in the old tongue, and the kith-mother's fronds reach toward her, brushing her face, her shoulders, her upraised hands. The eggs come slowly, one by one, pale and translucent as the mother herself, dropping into the basket with a soft, wet sound. The people watch in silence. The children stare with enormous eyes. The mother's thrumming continues, a steady, gentle vibration that seems to hold the whole cleft in a kind of suspension, a moment outside the ordinary rhythm of chitter and click-speech and the rustle of the mat.

Sigrid feels tears on her face and does not know when she started crying. It happens every time. The gathering is a thing that bypasses language entirely, that speaks to something in her that has no name, that has only the hum of the spindle and the glow of her own skin and the endless, patient warmth of the place.

When it is done—when the eggs are gathered, and Eldest-Mother Tanith has spoken the closing words, and the kith-mother has withdrawn into the hole with the same slow, majestic grace—the people disperse slowly, their voices rising again into the familiar click-patter of daily life. The oil-lamps are carried away. The hole is covered with fresh thatch, to be uncovered again in another cycle. The children run off to play chasing games on the ledges, their excitement still bright and unspent.

Sigrid climbs back to the third tier, Lirra beside her, still holding her hand. The child is quiet, thoughtful. At the entrance to Sigrid's dwelling, she pauses. "Auntie," she says, using the informal click-word for *mother's-sister*, the one she has used since she could speak. "Why does the mother come up? Why does she give us her eggs?"

Sigrid considers this. There are answers she could give—the practical answers, about the protein and the beetle-streams and the covenant—but they are not the answers the child is asking for. She knows this because she remembers asking the same question of her own mother's-sister, cycles and cycles ago, and she remembers the answer she received.

"Because we are part of the same thing," she says. "The mother, and the beetles, and the vines, and the walls, and us. We are all part of the same thing. The cleft is a body, and we are its voice. The mother is its heart. The warmth is its breath. Do you understand?"

Lirra is silent for a long moment, her enormous eyes fixed on Sigrid's face. Then she nods, slowly, the way she does when she is learning something that will take cycles to fully understand. She lets go of Sigrid's hand and runs off down the foot-ledge toward her own family's dwelling.

Sigrid enters her home and sits at the entrance, her spindle in her lap. She places her palm flat against the wall. The hum is there, steady and patient. Through the woven screen of a nearby dwelling, she can hear Feran's booming click-laugh, and the softer voice of his pairing-partner, and the high, excited chatter of a child. Below, the beetle-streams are running, their chitter rising up through the tiers. The wall-glow is beginning its slow fade toward the dim time.

She spins. The thread emerges from the spindle golden and fine, catching the light of the walls, humming with the under-song. Tomorrow she will soak fibers and beat them against her smooth stone. She will teach Lirra the difference between a strand that will hold and one that will snap. She will eat her cake and grub-paste and drink the mineral-tasting water. She will live the ordinary, extraordinary life of her people, in the sealed and living city folded into the ancient, waterless stone.

She is Sigrid-of-the-Rustle, fiber-spinner of the third tier, and this is enough. This is everything.
