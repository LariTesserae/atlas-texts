Her name is Thread-Set as Istra maps the sky, and she is sixteen years old, which means she has seen approximately four hundred and ninety thousand rains. She knows this not because she’s counted, but because her mother once told her the math—seventeen-minute cycles, sixty of them in a waking period, and she’s lived sixteen years—and the number lodged in her head like a beetle in a wall-crevice, immovable and faintly irritating.

She is Corin’s daughter, the youngest, the one who sings to the beetles.

Her given name is Mira, three generations back on her father’s side—names echo in the Set, are picked up and set down and picked up again when the previous bearer has been rendered into oil and the name has sat fallow long enough to lose its grief-weight. She doesn’t mind being called “the youngest” or “Corin’s daughter” or even, occasionally, “Singer,” which is what the lamplighters call her when she passes their territory with her beetle-dredge in tow. What she minds is that her eyes are still too dark, still the deep amber-brown of a surface-dweller seven generations removed, still not pale enough to mark her as truly belonging. Her mother’s eyes are the color of phosphor-dust. Her father’s are the color of the Underting’s breathing pores just before they open—a milky, luminescent gray. Mira’s are stubborn. Mira’s refuse to change.

She is, at this moment, hanging upside down from a ceiling-fiber eighteen meters above the town square, her bare feet wrapped around the strand and her hands free for the work. The work is a baby dredge-beetle, no bigger than her torso, who has gotten its forelegs tangled in a tangle of old copper wire some renderer dropped three rains ago. It’s chirring in distress—a high, grinding whine that cuts through the engine-thrum—and its mother, a massive old female painted with her family’s white pattern, is pacing below in the runoff channel, splashing tepid water with every step, her mandibles clacking in a rhythm that Mira recognizes as *fix it fix it fix it*.

“I’m fixing, I’m fixing,” Mira mutters, not to the beetle but to herself. The wire is corroded and slick with algae, and the baby won’t stop thrashing. She pulls her knife from her belt—a sliver of engine-steel, sharpened on a breathing-pore’s calcified rim—and begins sawing at the wire. A climber swings past her, so close its wet skin brushes her ankle, and it pauses to watch with one large, intelligent eye, head cocked. It chitters a four-note phrase that Mira knows means *problem?*

“Tangled,” she says, in the half-pidgin the Set uses with the climbers. “Tangle-fix, tangle-cut.”

The climber considers this. Then it reaches out with one delicate four-fingered hand and plucks the wire free before Mira’s knife is halfway through. The baby beetle drops, tumbling, but its shell is still soft enough at this age that it bounces once on the root-floor and rights itself, chirring indignation. The mother beetle immediately begins grooming it with her mandibles, rumbling deep in her thorax.

Mira stares at the climber. The climber stares back, then offers her the piece of wire like a gift, like a trade-item, like *I did this and now you owe me*.

“You could have done that three rains ago,” Mira says, taking the wire. “You were just watching.”

The climber trills a phrase she’s never heard before. She doesn’t know what it means. It swings away into the high dark, and Mira is left with the wire and the fading adrenaline and the knowledge that she’s been shown up by a creature with no language and twice her competence.

Below her, the Number Two engine coughs its seventeen-minute steam. The rain begins. The gutters gurgle. Mira hangs there a moment longer, letting the tepid drizzle soak through her hair and her clothes and the pebbled skin of her arms, and she watches the square below resolve into its familiar patterns. The lamplighters are moving through, their suction-poles rasping against the bioluminescent nodules. The climbers are retreating to the upper reaches to avoid the steam. Her father—Corin whose gall faces the eastern duct—is standing in the doorway of their gall-house, his arms crossed, his pale gray eyes scanning the ceiling until they find her.

He does not shout. He claps twice, a particular rhythm—*come down, Mira, come down*—and she claps back, *soon, soon*, and he shakes his head and disappears back into the breathing dark of their home.

She doesn’t come down right away. She never does. There’s a thing she’s learned to do, hanging here in the space between the root-ceiling and the root-floor, in the no-man’s-land that belongs to the climbers and the moths and the occasional lost child. She sings.

Not words. Not language. Just a tone, pitched low and steady, the frequency that makes the dredge-beetles stop their plodding and tilt their great horned heads toward her. The tone resonates in the fiber-strands. It travels through the root-floor. It finds the beetles wherever they are—in the thoroughfares, in the side-tunnels, in the mushroom-farms and the rendering-chambers—and they all pause, for just a moment, and trill back at her in their grinding, subsonic way. It’s not communication, exactly. It’s call-and-response. It’s *I am here, you are here, we are all still here*.

Her mother says she came out of the womb humming. Her father says it’s the only thing that kept her alive as a baby, when she was small and sickly and screamed for no reason except that she was small and sickly, and the only thing that quieted her was being placed on the back of the family dredge-beetle, right between the thorax and the shell, where the beetle’s own trilling vibrations soaked into her bones and gave her something to harmonize with.

She’s sixteen now. She is the only person in the Thread-Set who can calm a panicked beetle with a single note, who can coax a stubborn dredge to pull a load twice its weight, who can whistle a wild beetle out of the dark beyond the lamplight and persuade it to take a harness. The handlers come to her when their beetles are sick or frightened or refusing to eat. The weights—the old ones whose opinions matter in the way that the root-floor matters, solid and foundational—are beginning to look at her differently. Not as Corin’s youngest. Not as the singer. As something else, something they don’t have a word for yet.

The problem is that she doesn’t want to be whatever they’re looking for. She wants her eyes to pale. She wants to belong. She wants to be Set in the way her parents are Set, in the way Istra is Set, in the way the city itself is Set—woven, breathing, permanent. And every time she catches her reflection in a copper gutter or a polished engine-part, and she sees those amber-brown eyes staring back at her, she feels like a surface-dweller who hasn’t earned her stay yet. A stranger. A temporary thing.

The rain stops. The steam dissipates. The breathing pores on the gall-buildings flutter open again, sighing out a faint vegetable warmth. Mira unwinds her feet from the ceiling-fiber and drops, catching herself on a lower strand, then another, then the root-floor, landing in a crouch with a soft wet slap. The mother beetle trundles over to her and leans her great horned head against Mira’s hip, a gesture of thanks or affection or both, and Mira scratches the soft place between the beetle’s eyes until her hand is coated in phosphor-dust and the beetle’s trilling drops into a contented rumble.

“You’re welcome,” she says. “Tell your baby to stay out of the wires.”

The beetle doesn’t answer, of course. But it bumps her hip one more time before plodding away with its offspring, and Mira takes that as acknowledgment.

She walks home through the town square, past the great distillation engines with their blackened iron and their coughing steam, past the gall-buildings with their breathing pores and their weeping sap-gutters, past Istra’s house with its fungus-garden shaped like a window to nowhere. The old woman is sitting outside, as she always does between the rains, her near-white eyes fixed on something Mira can’t see. She raises one gnarled hand as Mira passes, and Mira raises hers in return, a greeting and a blessing and a question all at once: *is the Underting restless today?*

Istra’s hand twists in the sign for *peace, for now, for always until it isn’t*. Then she points at Mira’s eyes and makes the sign for *waiting*.

Mira flinches. Keeps walking.

Her father is waiting in the doorway, arms still crossed, pale eyes unreadable. He’s a head stoker, a weight in his own right, the man who’s kept the Number Two engine running for forty years. He smells like grease and steam and the bitter oil that runs through all their lives. He doesn’t say anything at first, just looks at her with that particular expression that means he’s been worried and is now trying to decide whether to be angry or relieved.

“The lamplighters said they saw you up near the ceiling again,” he says finally. “Beyond the safe fibers.”

“I was untangling a baby beetle.”

“You were untangling a baby beetle *beyond the safe fibers*.”

“It was where the beetle was.”

Corin sighs. It’s a deep sound, a weight-sound, the kind of sigh that carries decades of engine smoke and maintenance cycles and the slow, creeping fear of a parent who has already lost one child to the Underting’s restlessness and cannot bear to lose another. Mira’s older sister fell into a breathing pore twenty years ago, before Mira was born, and was never found. Her name was also Mira, three generations back. Their father never speaks of her.

“Your mother wants to see you,” he says. “She’s in the back chamber, working on the new valve.”

Mira nods and slips past him into the gall-house, into the warm breathing dark, into the familiar smell of fungi-porridge and beetle-chitin and the faint, sweet reek of the Underting’s sap. The walls of their home are studded with breathing pores the size of dinner plates, and they open and close in a slow, hypnotic rhythm, inhaling and exhaling, inhaling and exhaling. The floor is worn smooth by generations of feet, and the ceiling is low enough that Mira has to duck under the place where a root-strut has grown crooked over the last decade and no one has bothered to cut it back yet. Her mother believes that cutting the Underting’s growth invites restlessness. Her mother believes a great many things.

She finds Pella in the back chamber, hunched over a copper valve the size of her torso, sweat beading on her pebbled gray-pink skin. Pella who tends the Number Three condenser, a weight in her own right, a woman whose hands have shaped metal and root and fiber for forty years and never once flinched from a task. Her eyes are the color of phosphor-dust, pale and luminous, and when she looks up at Mira there is something in them that Mira can’t read.

“The baby beetle,” Pella says. Not a question.

“Untangled.”

“The climber helped you.”

Mira blinks. “How did you—?”

“Istra saw it. She sent word.” Her mother sets down her wrench and wipes her hands on a rag that has been a rag so long it’s more oil than fabric. “The climber offered you the wire.”

“It was mocking me.”

“Maybe.” Pella’s voice is careful, measured, the voice of someone who has spent decades diagnosing engine problems and knows that the obvious answer isn’t always the right one. “Or maybe it was trying to tell you something. The climbers don’t mock. They’re not clever enough for mockery. They’re clever enough for a lot of things, but not that.”

Mira leans against the warm wall of the gall-house, feeling it breathe against her back. “What would it be trying to tell me?”

“That’s not my question to answer.” Her mother turns back to the valve, fitting a new gasket into place with fingers that never tremble. “The climbers don’t talk to just anyone. They talk to Istra, because she dreams the Underting’s dreams. They talk to the renderers, because the renderers smell like the oil. They talk to *you*, Mira, and you’re neither of those things. You’re just a girl who sings to beetles. Why would a climber want to talk to you?”

Mira doesn’t have an answer. She stands there in the breathing dark of her home, listening to the distant thrum of the engines, the high chitter of the climbers, the percussion of another rain beginning outside. The water patters on the roof of the gall-house and runs in rivulets down the curved living walls and pools in the copper gutters her great-grandfather installed three generations ago. Everything is old here. Everything is established. Everyone belongs except her, with her stubborn amber-brown eyes and her voice that can reach the beetles and her inexplicable connection to a creature she cannot understand.

“I don’t know,” she says finally. “I don’t know why it talked to me.”

Pella looks at her over her shoulder. Her clouded, luminous eyes are unreadable, but her mouth is soft. “That’s a good answer,” she says. “That’s the best answer. Hold onto it. The people who *think* they know why things happen are the people who miss the things that are actually happening right in front of them. Now come help me with this valve. Your father will have a fit if the Number Three condenser fails during the next processing cycle, and I’ve only got two hands.”

Mira crosses the room and kneels beside her mother, taking the wrench when it’s offered, fitting her smaller hands into the spaces where the copper coil meets the breathing pore. The valve is warm and faintly pulsing, alive with the Underting’s slow circulatory rhythm, and as she works she finds herself humming again—not the beetle-call, not the song that reaches the dredges, but something else, something lower and stranger, a frequency she’s never tried before. It resonates in the valve. It resonates in the wall. It resonates in her mother, who pauses and tilts her head in a way that makes her look, just for a moment, very young.

“What is that?” Pella asks.

“I don’t know,” Mira says. And this time it’s not an admission of ignorance. It’s a discovery. It’s the first note of something that hasn’t been sung before.

Outside, the rain falls. The city breathes. The climbers, high in the false sky of interwoven roots, pause in their swinging to listen. And somewhere in the dark beyond the lamplight, the Underting itself—vast, indifferent, alive—shifts in its slow green sleep and dreams of a voice it almost recognizes.
