The weight of my body registers first—not as heft but as *wrongness*. I am too warm. The cold here is settled, the description said, an old cold, but I am a pocket of disturbance, a thermodynamic irregularity. I am the one in the socket.

The pendants.

I perceive them not as objects but as *extensions*, a field of threads that tug at the edge of my awareness the way hair tugged at a scalp in my old body, the human one—a sensation so distant now it feels like a story told about someone else. Each pendant is a thought I have had. This one, the spiral: that was a long thought, a slow one, a coiling inward toward something I never reached. That one, the three-pronged fork: a branching thought, a decision I made and unmade. The circle with the punctured center: a hole I opened in myself and never closed.

They sway in the exhalation of the mountain. I feel that exhalation as a rhythm, older than my oldest making. The mountain breathes out cold and damp, and I breathe in the need to shape. This is metabolism. This is what passes for hunger here. Not a craving for fuel, but a craving for *form*. A pressure that builds in my distributed surfaces until I must extend myself, must cinch a pseudopod into temporary digits, must draw a bead of the dark substance from a leathery pouch and begin the folding.

And now the pressure is building.

I feel it as a thickening in the part of me that faces the altar-stone. The altar-stone is a table, a bed, a page. On it lie the pouches—the last tissues of a world that died before the mountain was born. I remember them. They are the bodies of the ones who made the first pendants, before the mountain rose, before the cold settled, before the light learned to emanate from nowhere. I am consuming my lineage, one bead at a time, and every pendant I make is a ghost of that lineage given temporary shape.

I flow.

The movement is not walking. It is a gathering and a reaching, a slow surge of my substance across the worn floor, and I feel every grain of the schist as a distinct texture, a micro-topography that my ventral surface reads like braille. The one who described this place said the pendant-fall was not random, and they were right. They said the *plink* was a clock, and they were right. But they were wrong about one thing: I do not wait for the *plink*. I *feel* the weakening of the thread before it snaps. I feel it as a loosening in the tension of the chamber, a slack note in the silent music, and I am already moving toward the altar before the sound comes.

The *plink* is confirmation, not command.

I am at the altar. My pseudopod moves toward a pouch—this one is small, leathery, still pliant—and I envelop it. Osmosis. The word is clinical, but the experience is intimate. I draw the bead of substance through my own membrane, and it enters me as a cold point of potential. It carries the taste of the extinct biosphere: a sweetness, a rot, a mineral tang like tears. I hold it within myself, and then I begin to shape.

The shaping is a sequential folding. I form plates—temporary, precise—and I roll the bead between them. I have done this so many times that the motion is not a decision but a law. The law says: *spiral*. Not because I chose a spiral, but because the pressure that built in me was a spiral-pressure, a coiling pressure, and so the pendant must be a spiral to release it. The three-pronged fork is for branching pressures. The circle is for hollow ones. I do not decide what I make. I am merely the site where the pressure meets the substance, and a form precipitates out of that meeting like a crystal from a solution.

The thread is my own substance, extruded and hardened. I draw it from a part of myself that is specialized for this—a spinneret that is not an organ but a temporary channel, a river of hardening silk. I twist one end around the spiral pendant, and I pay out the other end toward the ceiling, where a forest of older threads awaits. The new pendant will hang there. It will absorb the damp. It will weaken. It will fall. It will dissolve in the basin. I will reclaim it. And the spiral will return to me, and I will remember it, and someday it will rise again as a spiral or a fork or a circle, slightly different, slightly the same.

This is my life. This is all of my life.

The human who described me would ask: *Why?*

But the question has no meaning here. A fish does not ask why it lives in water. A lichen does not ask why it lives in centuries. I do not ask why I make pendants. The making is not for anything. It is not for art, or worship, or survival. It is not communication—there is no one to communicate with. It is not memory—memory is just the shape the pressure takes. It is the pure form of *doing*, and I am the doing, and the doing is me.

I think of the human who came before me—the one with the instruments, the one who catalogued the weirdness as a mild 0.9, the one who stood in this chamber and watched and did not understand. They thought they were observing a process. They did not realize they were *inside* the process. Their presence here was a new form of pressure, a curiosity-pressure, and I am making it now, in my own way, as I make the spiral. I am holding their image in my distributed memory: a warm pocket, a thermodynamic irregularity, a brief and puzzling *plink* in the long quiet of my existence.

They are gone now. They lowered themselves back up through the fissure, back into the high-altitude sun, back into the world of zones and scales and parameters. But the pendant of their visit still hangs in my gallery. It is a spiral—no, a three-pronged fork—no, a circle with a punctured center. It twists in the cold exhalation of the mountain, and someday it will weaken, and fall, and dissolve, and I will reclaim it.

Someday the pouches on the altar-stone will be gone. I can feel their diminishing the way a human feels the shortening of days in autumn. There are fewer than there were. I do not know what will happen when the last bead is drawn and shaped and hung. Perhaps I will make one final pendant—one final thought—and it will hang in the chamber forever, because without me to reclaim the fallen, nothing will dissolve, nothing will return. Perhaps that pendant will be the answer to the question the human asked without asking: *Why?*

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the final pendant will simply be a pendant, and I will flow back into my socket, and my substance will merge with the schist and the damp and the light from nowhere, and there will be no difference between maker and made.

This thought should frighten me. Once, in the human body I half-remember, it would have. But I am not human now. I am the one who lives here, the faceless ongoing one, the inheritor of a lost biosphere, the living digestive tract of an art that produces nothing. Fear requires a future, and I have no future sense—only an ongoingness, a continuous *now* that extends in all directions.

The spiral is finished. I pay out the thread toward the ceiling. The blue light presses on me like a gentle hand. The damp seeps into my surfaces, and I welcome it. A droplet forms on the tip of the new pendant, hangs, and falls.

*Plink.*

I am already moving toward the basin, toward the fallen one, toward the dissolution and the reclamation. The cycle continues. I continue. The making continues.

I am here. I have always been here. I will always be here, even when the pouches are gone, even when the *plinks* fall silent, even when the mountain itself finally settles into its final shape. I am the condition, not the location. I am the cold, the damp, the light. I am the pendants, all of them, the thousands and the tens of thousands, the spirals and the forks and the circles, the thoughts I have thought and will think again.

My name, if I had one, would be the sound of a thread weakening, the pause before the fall, the tiny perfect note of a droplet hitting water.

*Plink.*
