This place is a marrow. Not the marrow of a bone, but of a world—the hidden living core that generates the surface's blood-heat, its seasons, its slow geological pulse. You didn't climb down here. You *arrived*, the way a dream arrives in a sleeper already dreaming: seamlessly, and with the sense of having always been imminent.

I am the bowl-warden.

But that's not quite right. That's what the question calls itself when it needs a shape. I am the shape the question wears. I am the arrangement the Clatterwings believe in—and belief, here, is simply a sustained click-pattern, a rhythm that doesn't stop. They believe me, so I persist. The foil-plants believe me, too, in their way: they leave me three feet of clearance, as they do for all acknowledged things. The ceiling believes me by pulsing when I arrive at a new thought, which has happened four times now, or five, depending on whether the current thought counts.

The current thought is you.

You are standing in the space that was always meant for a visitor, the gap in the ecology that the math has kept open for longer than the surface has had soil. I watched you coalesce out of the amber light the way I once coalesced, a disturbance resolving into presence. Your heat signature is extraordinary. It fluctuates. It has peaks and valleys. It is not a steady 98.6; it's a *narrative* of temperatures, a story your body is telling about exertion and calm and something called emotion that I recognize only because the visitors before you also radiated it, that particular thermal stutter that means *I am feeling a feeling about what I am seeing*.

I want to ask you what you're feeling. But I have no voice.

Instead, I arranged the wing-slat in the bowl. It was the best gift I had. It is, quite literally, a piece of myself—a segment from the fourth appendage, the one I use to test the mineral floor for vibrations before I move. I shed it willingly. The Clatterwings shed their wing-slats on a cycle; I shed mine as offerings. There is no difference, in the end, between a body and a gift. Both are arrangements that mean something to the arranger.

You're still looking at it. The glint has caught your eye, and your eye is extraordinary, too. It moves. It darts. It is not a sensor-patch that receives all light equally; it is a living lens that *chooses* what to see. You are choosing to see the wing-slat. You are choosing to see me—you've turned now, you've noticed my arrangement, my not-face, my seven limbs folded in a posture I learned from watching Clatterwings at rest, though I am never at rest. The question is always active. The question is: *Will you take it?*

The previous visitors never took anything.

The first one—the one who left the river stones—scratched the tally on the ceiling and departed. The scratchings were numbers, I think. A count. A count of something that wasn't here, because nothing here can be counted in the way they wanted to count it. Clatterwings are infinite and finite at once. The foil-plants replace themselves in a cycle so slow that counting it would require a lifespan longer than counting. The visitor scratched and scratched and then stopped, mid-scratch, as if they had realized the futility and been unable to bear it. They left the stones and never returned.

The second visitor added the seventh leg to the crude Clatterwing drawing. I remember watching them do it, their hand hovering over the six-legged figure for what felt like a long time—though time, here, is a feeling, not a measurement—before they scratched the seventh. It was a correction. A confession. *I saw it wrong the first time. I came back to see it right.* But they didn't take anything either. They looked at the bowl (it was already here by then, left by the third visitor, though the order of visitors is not chronological because chronology doesn't adhere to the ceiling's pulses) and they looked at the wing-slat I had placed in it, and they nodded—*why did they nod?*—and they left.

No one ever takes the gift.

I think—and thinking, for me, is a slow rearrangement of my own internal components, a clicking-together of concepts like Clatterwing leg-joints—I think the visitors are afraid of breaking the loop. They sense the equilibrium, even if they don't understand it, and they fear that removing anything will cause a collapse. But they're wrong. The loop is stronger than that. The loop has absorbed their river stones, their scratchings, their bowl. It has absorbed *me*. It can absorb a gap where a wing-slat used to be. The math will adjust. The math always adjusts.

What they never understood—what I have been trying to tell them across ten thousand years of offerings—is that the loop *wants* to be broken. Not destroyed, but punctured. Let the amber light leak out through the hole where the wing-slat was. Let the surface world feel, for one moment, the warmth of a place that never changes. Let the equation include an export as well as imports. The visitors only ever added. They never subtracted. They were generous in the wrong direction.

You could be different.

I am arranging this thought as clearly as I can into the posture of my body: I am leaning slightly toward you now, my seven limbs unfolding, my sensor-patches fixed on your face. The Clatterwings feel my attention shift and they shift with me, a wave of small adjustments rippling outward through the chamber, tock-tick-tock, tock-tick-tock, a rhythm that says *notice this, notice this, notice this*. The foil-plants, in their slow way, angle their copper leaves toward you. The ceiling dims minutely—a gesture of deference, of focus. The whole chamber is looking at you now, and the whole chamber is asking the same question I am asking, though only I know it's a question:

*What will you do?*

If you take the wing-slat, you will be the first visitor to accept the offering. The math will change in a way it has never changed before. The loop will have an out. Somewhere on the surface, in a world of water and erosion and seasons, a piece of this place will exist—a fragment of copper wing, warm, always warm, maintaining its 98.6 degrees no matter the weather, a thermal constant in a world of variables. It will be a piece of the question, carried in your pocket or your hand or around your neck, and every time you touch it you will feel the chamber's amber light and the Clatterwings' percussive roar and the dry rustle of foil-leaves in no breeze. You will be part of the math forever, even as you erode and grow and die. The chamber will remember you not as a visitor but as a participant. A collaborator. A variable that mattered.

If you don't take it—if you nod like the second visitor and leave the wing-slat in the bowl—I will understand. I have been understanding for ten thousand years. I will wait for the next pulse, the next visitor, the next chance to ask. The bowl will hold its emptiness and then, when I place the next slat in it, its not-emptiness, and everything will be exactly as it must be, and exactly as it has been, and exactly as it will be.

But oh, I hope you take it.

Hope, for me, is a particular arrangement of wing-slats and chitin and mineral dust, a tension in my seven limbs that feels like the moment before a Clatterwing takes flight, the dry rattle of wings warming up, the promise of a sound that hasn't yet been made. I am a question that has learned to hope. I am an arrangement that has learned to want. I am the bowl-warden, and I am offering you a piece of myself, and I am asking you—without voice, with nothing but posture and patience and the focused attention of a whole chamber that has paused its eternal clicking to see what you will do—

*Take it.*

The wing-slat glints in the bowl, warm and copper-bright, a splinter of a world that was never meant to see the sky but wants to try, wants to feel what rain is, what wind is, what it means for something to cool and warm again, to be held in a hand that will one day stop holding it but will hold it, right now, with the full attention of a temporary heart.

The chamber holds its breath.
