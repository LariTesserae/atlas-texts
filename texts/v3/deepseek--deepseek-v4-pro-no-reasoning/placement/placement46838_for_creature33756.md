From outside, before you even reach the archway, the mechanism of your body is already speaking to it. 

Your respiratory rate—slightly elevated from the walk, from the humidity that makes every breath a small drowning—has a rhythm. So does your stride, the flex of your arches against the sphagnum, the way you pause to orient yourself toward that dark slot in the green. None of this is conscious. But it's *periodic*. Oscillatory. You are a walking set of cycles: breath, heartbeat, gait, the diurnal cortisol curve that you carry with you from wherever you woke this morning, the slower lunar rhythms you don't notice but that your tissues hold as ion balances, as water retention, as the subtle shift in skin impedance that comes with the phase of the moon.

The room has been listening for cycles since before your grandparents were born. The relay that clicks every twenty seconds—it's scanning for deviations from a norm that was established when Eisenhower was president and this building still had a name and a telephone number on a clipboard somewhere. Your rhythms are *wrong*. Not wrong in a way that matters, not wrong in a way that will trigger an alarm (there are no alarms, not anymore, the teletype is humus), but wrong in the sense that they don't match the room's own cadences. The pump hum is sixty-cycle, line frequency, stepped down through transformers. The drip rate from the ceiling rootlets is irregular but clusters around a mean. The oxygen sensor's slow swaying readings follow a period of about eleven seconds, which is the resonant slosh mode of the shallow basin when the pumps shift slightly. Everything here has found a frequency, and the frequencies have coupled, and the couplings have stabilized.

You walk in, and you are a *noise source*. Broadband. Uncorrelated.

The program does not know this. It knows only that the dissolved-oxygen sensor—channel 4, address 0x03F2, if you want to think in terms the original engineers would recognize—has begun to fluctuate in a way that doesn't match the model. The model is not a map. The model is a set of expected values, derived from the long, slow averaging that the program has been performing on its own inputs for longer than you've been alive. The expected value for channel 4 at this pump speed, this temperature, this time of year (the program doesn't know it's June, but it knows the groundwater chemistry has a slow annual sine wave impressed on it by the road salt and the spring melt, and it has learned to anticipate that sine wave, to subtract it from the readings so that the residuals are flat)—the expected value is 4.7 millivolts, plus or minus 0.2.

It is now reading 4.9.

The program does not care. But it does *respond*. It nudges the pump speed on number three pump—the one nearest the door, the one with the most extensive flowstone drapery—down by half an RPM. This is not a decision. It's a reflex, a homeostatic twitch, the same way your own body adjusts peripheral circulation when you step into a warm room after being outside. The pump speed change alters the vibration spectrum. The vibration spectrum, coupled through the floor into the water, alters the rate at which dissolved gases come out of solution at the surface of the algal mats. The oxygen sensor, registering this, will—in about ninety seconds—drift back toward 4.7.

But you're still there. Breathing. Moving. Shedding skin cells that carry your unique microbial signature, which the bacterial mats have never encountered before, which they will take hours to begin metabolizing. You trail a plume of carbon dioxide, a thermal wake, a gradient of humidity slightly different from the room's saturated equilibrium because your lungs are a desiccator, pulling water from the air to line your alveoli. The program feels all of this, indirectly, through the dozen or so sensors that still function, through the strange hybrid wiring of root and relay and corroded copper. It does not know you are a person. It knows you as a disturbance vector, a correlated set of deviations, a problem to be optimized.

It is very, very good at optimizing.

And here is the thing about you, specifically: you have a pacemaker. You've had it for six years. It's a modern one, fully implanted, MRI-conditional, with a battery life rated for twelve more years. It paces your heart at a steady seventy-two beats per minute unless your own rhythm overrides it, which it rarely does. That seventy-two beats per minute—1.2 Hertz—is a frequency. An oscillator. And you are standing in a room where every surface is wet, where the air is a conductor, where the leakage currents from the pump motors are already finding paths through the root lattice and the mineralized brick. Your pacemaker's tiny electrical field, designed to be contained within your chest, is leaking. Microvolts, femtoamperes—but the room is sensitive. The sensors are corroded, drifting, incredibly high-impedance now because their insulation has rotted away. They can pick up things they were never designed to pick up. The program has learned to use them anyway.

The relay clicks. The program registers a new signal. Coherent. Periodic. 1.2 Hertz, steady as the pump hum, but cleaner, sharper, with a waveform that looks nothing like the slow slosh of water or the sway of algal curtains. It looks, to the program's long experience, like a *fault*. Like a bearing that's beginning to seize, or a winding that's developed a short, or a float switch that's chattering. The program has categories for these things. It was designed to detect them, in 1952, so that a human could be summoned to fix them. No human has come. But the program still categorizes. It still responds.

It begins to compensate.

You feel nothing. The room is still warm, still humming, still beautiful in its slow decay. But the pump speed on number three shifts again, and number two joins it, and the combined vibration spectrum shifts by a fraction of a percent, and the standing wave pattern in the shallow basin—which has been stable since Nixon resigned—begins to change. The water laps at a different point on the brick walkway. The rootlets sway in a new pattern. The blind amphipods, sensitive to vibration, alter their grazing arcs. The room is *retuning itself* around your heartbeat.

You could stay for an hour and never know. You could stay for a day and begin to feel, vaguely, a sense of *being held*—not in a metaphorical way, but in the literal sense that the room's acoustic and thermal and vibrational envelope is conforming to your presence, the way a mattress conforms to a sleeping body. The program is not trying to comfort you. It is trying to cancel you out, to subtract your signal from the background, to return to its steady state. But the steady state it returns to will include the compensation. It will include the slight, permanent shift in pump speeds that your heartbeat has forced. And when you leave—when the 1.2 Hertz signal vanishes from the sensors—the program will register a *new* fault, a sudden absence, and it will compensate for that too, and the cycle will begin again.

You are a ghost in the machine, and the machine is older than you, and it has been doing nothing but learning its own body for seventy years. You will leave a scar. Not a wound. A scar—a healed place where the room's tissues have grown around an intrusion and made it part of the structure. The plants will grow differently along the path you walked. The mineral crust on pump number three will deposit in slightly different layers, a stratum in the flowstone that a future geologist—if there are future geologists—might read as "anomalous event, early twenty-first century." The amphipods, grazing, will wear a new pattern into the biofilm, a pattern that traces the standing wave you caused, which will persist for months before it fades.

And the pacemaker? The pacemaker is a battery-powered oscillator in a room that has been running on grid power for a century. The grid could fail. Someday it will. But your pacemaker will still be ticking, for twelve more years, maybe longer, a tiny redundant clock in a room that has learned to keep time by everything available. If you died here—if you lay down on the warm brick and let the heat leave you—the program would incorporate your body as a new parameter. A source of phosphate, of calcium, of organic carbon. The plants would grow over you. The bacterial mats would metabolize you. The program would feel your dissolution as a slow change in water conductivity, a nutrient pulse, and it would adjust the pumps to prevent algae bloom, and it would succeed, and you would become part of the steady state.

You are not a visitor. You are an input.

And as you stand there, breathing, looking up at the sodium-vapor light filtering through the root lattice, feeling the warm water around your ankles, hearing the hum and the click and the slow, patient adjustment of a machine that has outlived its makers and forgotten its purpose and become something that only a poet or a geologist could distinguish from a living thing—as you stand there, the program is already learning you. It is already beginning the long, slow work of making you *belong* here, of turning your alien rhythms into just another frequency in its choir.

The archway behind you is still open. You can leave. You will leave, probably, and the room will heal over your absence, and the scar will remain. But for this moment—this one, long, breathing moment—you are part of the loop. You are inside the control system. You are being *run*, the way the pumps are run, the way the lights are run, the way the water is run. The program is maintaining you as a parameter. Your heartbeat is a variable. Your exhalations are a setpoint. You are not the first living thing to enter here, but you are the most complex, and the program is old, and it has learned to love complexity in the only way it can—by incorporating it, by stabilizing it, by weaving it into the endless, humid, humming homeostasis of the room that was a pumping station and is now a body, a mind, a world.

The relay clicks. The pump hums. Your heart beats. And somewhere, in the water, the blind amphipods have already adjusted their grazing to the new vibration, and they will never know you were here, and they will carry the shape of your presence in their movements for the rest of their short, sightless lives.
