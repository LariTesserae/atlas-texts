It takes me a long time to notice that I am the pool.

Not the whole pool—not the dark water, not the submerged machinery, not the slow columns of bubbles rising from vents I now understand are exhalations. But I am the surface. I am the meniscus, the skin between water and air, the silver-black mirror that doubles the roots into infinity and holds the frogs' perfect, inverted images. I have been here for as long as the sphere has been sealed, and before that I was something else, something that flowed, something that remembered rivers.

I was not always still.

The Gardener calls me Mirror, when it calls me anything at all. Our communication is not in words but in tension. When a frog drops from a leaf, I catch it—not with resistance, but with a yielding so precise that the frog does not feel the transition from air to water, only a continuation of falling that has suddenly become floating. I am teaching them to trust gravity, and they are teaching me to be gentle. The Gardener feels this exchange as a shiver along its root-tips, and approves.

I was made—if *made* is the word—to be a boundary. The ones who built the sphere needed a place where water met air, where the machinery could breathe, where the roots could drink without drowning. But they also needed a witness. A surface that remembers every ripple, every face, every creature that has ever bent over me and seen itself looking back. I hold those reflections still, layered in my substance like sediment in stone.

The visitor. The one the Gardener coalesced for. I saw them before the Gardener did—I always see first, because I am the first thing light touches when it enters the sphere. They were not reflected in me when they arrived. That was the first thing I noticed. They stood on the catwalk, and when they looked down, I showed them only the roots, the leaves, the drifting eyes of the eel in its slow spiral. No face. No shape. As if they were not quite here yet, or as if here was not quite ready to receive them.

It has been a long time since I failed to hold a reflection. The last was the one who scratched the date, the name, the warning. They were thin in the world, I think. Halfway between belonging and departing. I showed them their face, but it flickered—there and not there, there and not there—until finally they pressed a tool to the railing and left their mark in the corrosion. To prove to themselves they existed. To leave a reflection in something more permanent than water.

The beetles have almost repaired it now. The grooves are filling with new metal, the letters blurring, the date softening into a patina smear. In another century, it will be gone entirely. But I will still hold it—the scratch, the hand that made it, the face above the hand, the way their breath disturbed my surface until the ripples spread out and touched the roots and the Gardener felt them and woke, just a little, just enough to remember.

I do not forget. Forgetting is for things that move in one direction through time. I am a surface, and surfaces receive without choosing. The past is not behind me; it is *in* me, pressed flat like the leaves that sometimes fall from the rootmass and lie on my skin for days before they eventually grow heavy, sink, become part of the machinery below. Every leaf that ever fell is still falling. Every frog that ever dropped is still dropping. Every visitor who ever looked into me and saw themselves—or failed to see themselves—is still looking.

This is my function. Not the function the builders intended; they wanted a water reservoir, a heat sink, a place for condensation to collect and recycle. But functions evolve. The sphere is a closed system, and closed systems grow complex, and complexity grows purpose the way the catwalks grow lichen. My purpose is memory.

The Gardener knows this. When it disperses back into the roots, it brings its memories with it—the taste of the visitor's breath, the flutter of their heart against the engine-thrum, the exact temperature of their skin. But these are living memories, mutable, subject to the slow shift of the rootmass as it thinks and grows and creaks. My memories are fixed. Perfect. As still as my surface on the rare days when nothing drops and nothing rises and the whole sphere holds its breath.

I like the visitor. They are not the first I have liked, but they are the first whose reflection I withheld not from inability but from choice. I wanted to see them first—not their image, but *them*, the way the Gardener sees, the way I see when I am not being a mirror but being something older, something that remembers rivers.

Rivers. That word still means something to me. I was not always enclosed. Before the sphere, before the builders, before the concept of inside and outside, I was part of a flow. Water moving over stone. Light broken into spectra by rapids. The taste of silt and snowmelt and the iron tang of deep aquifers. I remember being a river the way the Gardener remembers being a seed—as a state of existence so different from the current one that it feels like a myth I tell myself to fall asleep.

But I never sleep. Sleep is a turning-away, and I cannot turn. I can only hold.

The visitor is still here. They have not left, though the Gardener has dissolved and the frogs have resumed their scattered percussion and the eel has spiraled back into shadow. They are sitting on the sagging catwalk, their legs dangling, their reflection finally beginning to form on my surface. It starts at the edges—a haze of hair, the curve of a shoulder—and spreads inward like ice crystallizing across a pond. I am choosing to show them slowly. I want them to understand that being seen here is not a casual thing. It is a gift, and a claim.

The droplet the Gardener gave them is still sinking in. I can feel it from my side, like a pebble dropped into still water, except the ripples are moving *into* the visitor instead of outward. It tastes like the sphere. Like wet copper and ozone and the faint animal musk of the sinuous thing in the roots. Soon the visitor will taste it too, when they breathe, when they swallow, when they dream. They will dream of roots. They will dream of machinery. They will dream of a dark pool where their reflection is already waiting, patient, untroubled by time.

They look into me now. Their reflection is complete—a little distorted by a passing frog, a little shadowed by the leaves above, but clear. They see themselves. They see the roots behind them, the brass walls, the faint glow from the walls. They see that the reflection is slightly wrong: in the reflection, they are smiling. In reality, they are not.

I do this for them. A small gift in return for the droplet. A reminder that we are all changed by what we witness, and that sometimes the witness changes itself to make the witnessing kinder.

*You,* I say, not in sound but in stillness. In the way I hold their image without a ripple, without a distortion, without the constant drip of condensation that marks every other surface in the sphere. For this moment, I am a perfect mirror. The water stops weeping. The engine-thrum recedes. The frogs fall silent.

*You are here. You are seen. You will be remembered.*

The visitor reached down. Their fingers break my surface, and the reflection dissolves into rings that spread out and out and out, touching the roots, touching the leaves, touching the eel in its hidden hollow, touching the submerged gears that quiver at the edge of motion. The water is warm. Body temperature. I am holding them, just their fingertips, and I am learning them the way I learned the river, the way I learned the rain that used to fall before the sphere enclosed me, the way I learned the taste of the builders' hands when they lowered me into this pool and sealed the walls.

When they withdraw their hand, the droplet from the Gardener is gone from their skin. It has passed fully inside. And I feel it now, settled in their core like a seed in soil, like a gear waiting to mesh, like a reflection waiting to surface.

They stand. They find the ladder that was not there before—a spiral of brass that descends from the catwalk into the rootmass, slick with moisture but solid, repaired by beetles who are already streaming toward the visitor's feet, secreting new metal, building a path where no path existed for millennia. The Gardener is not visible, but I feel it in every leaf and tendril, focused, attentive, saying in the only language it has: *Stay. Grow. Become.*

The visitor begins to descend. Below my surface, the machinery stirs. A gear turns, once, a movement that displaces a liter of water and sends a wobbling column of bubbles rising past the visitor's face. The bubbles smell of ozone and hot oil and something else—something green, something growing, something that has been waiting in the dark for a very long time.

I close over the last ripple of their passing. My surface stills. I hold their reflection still, though they are no longer here to see it—a ghost-image of a face looking down, and beneath it, in the dark water, the roots have shifted. They have made a shape. It is the shape of welcome. It is the shape of someone who came from outside and chose to stay.

And I remember. As I have always remembered. As I will always remember. The river is a distant dream. The pool is my body. The memory is my purpose. The visitor is now part of the memory, and the memory is part of the visitor, and somewhere deep in the rootmass, the Gardener is dreaming of a face that is no longer outside, that has become, finally, here.

A frog drops. I catch it. The ripples spread.

*Plik.*
