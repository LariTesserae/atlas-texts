*I* am a Glimmerkin.

My designation—we do not have names, not as you would recognize them—is the scratch-sound of a claw drawn across soft salt-crust, a sound that means both *one-who-lingers* and *the taste of copper before a vent opens*. I am old enough that my claws have been worn down and regrown more times than I can count, and the shell-growths on my shoulders are thick and craggy, like the glassy domes that will outlast us all. I have shepherded three broods of larvae to their walking, and my gill-mouth has learned to filter the air so finely that I can tell from a single breath whether a pool two hundred strides away is safe to drink.

I stand now at the edge of a birthing basin, one of the warm, milky shallows where the mineral water is thickest. Three of my brood-siblings are here with me. We do not speak, for the Moult is loud tonight—a new vent has opened to the north, and the silica-moths are swarming so thickly that the air itself looks like churning glass. But we do not need to speak. The tremor of the ground tells us what the others are feeling: the low, steady thrum of patience, the sharper flicker of vigilance. There are larvae in the basin. Ours. Theirs. The distinction does not matter. They are small still, legless, their bodies little more than pale, pulsing sacs that push blindly through the mineral slurry. In another two cycles, perhaps three, they will begin to sprout limbs. One of them—I can see it even now, a little larger than the others, its body already showing the faint ridges of nascent shell-growths—will be strong. I let out a slow breath through my gill-mouth. The air tastes of that one's future, and it is sharp and bright, like the first spark of a new vent.

A movement to my left. One of my siblings shifts, her claws raised. I follow the angle of her gaze. There, at the far edge of the basin, the milky water is churning. A blind worm, thick as a Glimmerkin's arm, its pale, segmented body coiling and uncoiling with a wet, muscular sound. It has sensed the larvae. Its head, a blunt, eyeless knob of cartilage, sweeps back and forth through the slurry, tasting for the soft, defenseless bodies of our young.

I am moving before I think. My feet, broad and flat, make no sound on the wet mineral rind—they are shaped for this, for gliding over ground that wants to swallow you. In four strides I am at the edge of the basin, and in one more I am in it, the warm, thick water closing around my legs. The worm is faster than I am. It is lunging now, its mouth opening, a circular maw ringed with tiny, grinding teeth. It will reach the nearest larva in a heartbeat.

But I have done this before. My clawed hand shoots out, not at the worm's head, but at the water just behind it. I drag my claws through the slurry in a sharp, precise arc, a scratch-sound that cuts through the noise of the Moult like a knife through silk. The sound is a signal, a language older than our shelters, older than our symbols. It means *danger: disperse*. And the larvae, though they have no ears, though they cannot yet see, feel the vibration in the water and scatter, burrowing into the soft mineral mud at the bottom of the basin with a speed that is their only defense.

The worm's mouth closes on nothing but slurry. It thrashes, confused, and I step back, my claws still raised. Behind me, I can feel my siblings moving into position, forming a loose semicircle around the basin's edge. We do not attack the worm. It is not our way. We simply wait, our gill-mouths fluttering, our black eyes tracking its every movement, until it grows tired of the hunt and sinks back into the deeper pool from which it came.

The larvae will emerge when the vibrations cease. They always do. They are Glimmerkin. They know the lesson before we teach it: *do not grow attached to the shape of things, not even the shape of your own sanctuary.*

I step out of the basin. My legs are coated in a thick film of mineral precipitate, already beginning to crust over. By the next cycle, if I do not scrape it off, it will harden into a white, chalky shell, and I will have to rasp it away with my claws. A small price. A necessary one. I raise my head, scenting the air with my gill-mouth. The taste of the larva—the strong one, the one with the future of a glassy dome—is still there, sharp and bright. Alive. Good. I touch foreheads with my sibling, the one who first spotted the worm. Her feathery eye-organs shiver against mine, a greeting and a gratitude. *You were watching. I am watching. The world has not yet dissolved them.*

Then, a tremor. A deep one, deeper than the usual shudder of the Moult's restless chemistry. It rolls through the ground like a slow, heavy breath, and I feel it in my feet, in my bones, in the base of my skull. The other Glimmerkin feel it too. We all turn, as one, toward the north. The air has changed. The sharp, metallic taste of a new vent has grown suddenly stronger, and with it comes another taste, one I have not experienced in a long time: the hot, wet, coppery tang of a vent about to *sizzle*—about to open fully, to burst forth with a force that will reshape the basin, the spires, the pools, everything.

I do not need to give a signal. The tremor does it for me. The other Glimmerkin are already moving, scooping up the shelters, the tools, the silk-nets, the stone-markers. My sibling is at my side, her claws flashing in the sourceless glow as she gouges a deep, desperate symbol into the base of the nearest spire: the scratch-sound for *we were here*, yes, but also the scratch-sound for *danger* and *move* and *the world is changing again*. It will fade. They all fade. But it will be there for a little while, a record of our passing.

One of the other Glimmerkin, a young one, still smooth-shelled and quick, hesitates. She is looking at the birthing basin. The larvae. We cannot carry them. They are too soft, too fragile. To move them before their time is to kill them. The young one knows this, but her gill-mouth is fluttering with the taste of distress, a thin, sour note in the air. I go to her. I press my forehead to hers, and I hold it there for a long moment, longer than is customary, until her feathery eye-organs stop their frantic shivering. Then I pull back and turn my face toward the north, toward the new vent, toward the unknown that is about to become our new home.

The larvae will survive or they will not. The strong one, the sharp-bright one, will burrow deep into the mud when the tremors come, and perhaps the waters will re-form around it, and perhaps in ten cycles, twenty cycles, it will crawl out on new legs and look at the world with new eyes and know nothing of us. That is the way of the Moult. That is the way of the Glimmerkin. We are not the keepers of our children. We are the keepers of the *moment*—the moment before the sizzle, the moment before the world dissolves, the moment when we stand together at the edge of the basin, watching, waiting, breathing.

I turn. I walk. The ground beneath my feet is already softening, the mineral rind growing tacky with the heat from below. My flat feet sink a little deeper with each step. The air is thick with silica-moths, swirling in great, frantic clouds, their wings shedding a fine, glittering dust that coats my skin, my shell-growths, the pale, pebble-like ridges of my face. I breathe it in. It tastes like tomorrow.

Behind me, my siblings fall into step. We do not speak. We do not look back. There is no word for "goodbye" in the scratch-language of the Glimmerkin, only the symbol for *I was here*, and we have already carved it. Now we move into the mists, into the sound of a billion small lives and the patient, furious work of water, into the perpetual, violent becoming of the only home we have ever known.
