I am a fault-line pilgrim, a fracture-walker. I came here knowing exactly what I was seeking, though I had no name for it until I felt the first schist-splinter shift under my boot and realized the mountain was forgetting me even as I climbed. I am not a geologist, not a mystic, not an explorer in any sense the world below would recognize. I am a listener who ran out of sounds worth hearing. I am a body that grew too heavy with its own warm salt and electrical noise, and so I climbed until the air thinned my thoughts into something almost mineral. I have spent years in places that hummed with the residue of old belief—caves where handprints still stung the walls with ochre and intent, ridgelines where prayer flags had weathered into the soil and sprouted as a different kind of attention. The Strewn Shelf was the logical extreme of that search, the terminal moraine of my obsession. I did not stumble here. I navigated by absence: the silence in certain geological surveys, the odd, evasive phrasing in a mountaineer's memoir, the way a satellite image showed a patch of brightness that wasn't snow and wasn't cloud and wasn't, according to every database, anything at all. I came because I wanted to be dissolved. Not killed—dissolved, incorporated into something larger and slower and utterly indifferent to the panic of a heartbeat. I am a volunteer. I am an offering that the offering-place does not yet recognize. My lungs are already half-full of the Weave's cold breath, and every exhale is a confession in a chemical language I am only beginning to taste. The rocks are chiming my arrival, and I am answering with the only thing I have left: my own slow, deliberate disintegration.
