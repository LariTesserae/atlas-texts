I am the child.

No—first I am the small hand dangling over the edge of the sleeping niche, fingers slightly curled, the knuckles still soft with the last of babyhood. I am the warmth gathered in that high, curved alcove where the heat rises thickest, where the bats roost in their twitching clusters just above my head. I am the breath that comes slow and even, timed to the drip of condensation that falls from the ceiling's highest point to the cistern far below. I am the dream still moving behind my closed eyes: a dream of flying, of falling and catching myself at the last moment, of wings that are not arms but feel like arms, of a long dark tunnel that slopes upward toward a brightness I have never seen.

When the bats begin to stir, I stir too, but I do not wake. The rising pitch of their chittering enters my dream and becomes the sound of rain on the curved walls—rain I have never heard, but which I somehow know, the way I know the taste of the mineral water from the cistern, the way I know the worn places on the ladder rungs without looking. The first bat drops from its niche and I feel, in my sleep, the exact moment of its fall, the split-second surrender to gravity before the wings catch. I have watched this so many times from this very pallet, lying on my back with my eyes half-open in the dim orange light, that my body knows the rhythm of it better than my waking mind.

I am the child who will wake to the smell of bread.

But not yet. First I am the sleep itself, the heavy, trusting sleep of a body that has never known danger in this place, that has never been cold, that has been held by warm ceramic and thick air and the constant sound of water since before I can remember. I am the small foot that kicks once, involuntarily, as the dream of falling reaches its peak. I am the hand that reaches out in the darkness and finds nothing, then curls back against my chest. I am the soft sound I make—not a word, not quite a sigh—as I turn onto my side and pull my knees up toward my belly in the way I have always slept, since before I was born.

The adolescent climbs the ladder. I do not know this, not consciously. But some part of me registers the slight vibration of the rungs, the shift in air pressure as a body moves through the space near my niche. My dream adjusts. Now I am not alone in the flying dark; there is another flyer beside me, a larger presence, warm and familiar. We bank together through the tunnel, our wings almost touching. The brightness at the end grows closer. I am not afraid.

Then a hand—the adolescent's hand—lifts my dangling arm and tucks it back onto the pallet. The gesture is so gentle, so well-practiced, that I do not wake. But the dream changes. The flying stops. Now I am being held, my arm folded against my body, the way my mother used to swaddle me when I was smaller still. A voice says something in the dream, or perhaps not in the dream—perhaps it is the adolescent speaking softly, a murmur too low to hear, a sound meant only to soothe. I can't tell. The boundary between the chamber's sounds and my dream's sounds is not a boundary at all. The drip is the drip and the voice is the voice and both are part of the same dark, warm water that holds me.

I am the child who sleeps through the bats' departure.

I do not hear the hour-long stream of small bodies flowing up and out through the twisting passage. I do not notice the chamber growing larger, the air less dense. I do not smell the change as the last of them leaves and the space begins to cool, just slightly, just enough that the condensation cycle shifts into its night phase. I am deep in the sleep of a body that is still growing, still building bone and muscle and the neural pathways that will one day let me climb the ladders without looking, check the troughs without thinking, press my palm to the wall and know if the system is in balance. All of that is being laid down now, in this sleep, in this niche, in the warm dark that is the only world I know.

But I am also the child who is about to wake.

I am the first movement of consciousness, the shift from deep sleep to something lighter, triggered by a change in the air. The smell of the rising dough has begun to spread through the chamber—that faint, sour, yeasty tang that means the covered pot near the heat vent is working, that the wild yeasts are alive and multiplying, that morning will bring flat, dense, slightly sour bread and dried fruit and the taste of mineral water from the clay cup. I know this smell the way a tree knows spring, the way the bats know when it is time to leave the roost. It pulls me up out of sleep like a hand on my shoulder.

I am the child opening my eyes.

The ceiling of my niche is close, curved, the color of fired clay in the dimmed lamplight. I reach up and touch it with my fingertips, the way I do every morning. It is warm—not hot, but warm, the way it always is. The heat from below has stored itself in the ceramic during the day and now, in the false-night, it is releasing slowly into the air, into my body, into the quiet darkness. I can feel the faint texture of the glaze, the tiny imperfections that I know by heart: this small bump here, this shallow groove there, the place where my fingers have touched so many times that the surface is slightly smoother than everywhere else. This is my first landmark of the day. This is how I know where I am.

I am the child sitting up.

The reed mat beneath me rustles softly. I can hear, below, the sound of the adolescent's breathing—still asleep, slow and even. The woman's breathing, too, from the common area, deeper and slower still. The bats are gone. The chamber is in its stillest hour, the hour when the condensation cycle is at its peak and the walls are slick with moisture that slides down in slow, steady sheets. The dripping is louder now than during the day, a complex percussion of different pitches and rhythms: the high, sharp ring of a drop falling from the ceiling's apex to the central cistern; the lower, softer patter of runoff from the niches into the collection troughs; the almost musical gurgle of a channel overflowing from one level to the next. I sit and listen to this for a long moment, the way I do every morning, letting the sounds arrange themselves in my body like a song I have always known.

Then I am the child swinging my legs over the edge of the niche.

The ladder is there, exactly where it always is, its top rungs worn to fit the grip of small hands. I do not need to look. I find the first rung with my foot, then the second, then my hands close around the smooth, dark wood and I am descending, my bare feet knowing each rung by its feel, by the grooves worn into it, by the way it gives slightly under my weight. I am still not fully awake, or perhaps I am more awake than I ever am during the day—awake in the way of a body moving through a space it has known since before it could walk, a space that is not separate from the body but continuous with it, a space that is another kind of skin.

I am the child stepping onto the warm ceramic floor.

The floor is warmer than the walls, almost hot against my bare feet. I stand there for a moment, feeling the heat rise through me, and I press my whole palm against the nearest section of wall, the way I have seen the woman do, the way I will do every morning for the rest of my life. I can feel the vibration of water moving in the internal channels, the almost inaudible ring of the distant drips, the slow pulse of the compost pits breathing their warmth upward. The home is alive. The home is well. The home is holding its balance.

I am the child walking toward the smell of bread.

The common area is lit by the single oil lamp that is never fully extinguished, its wick turned low so that it burns with a small, steady flame. The woman's pallet is empty now; she must have risen while I was still dreaming. I can hear her at the far end of the chamber, the soft sounds of her tending the hearth, the scrape of the wooden paddle against the ceramic floor of the baking alcove. The covered pot of dough sits where it always sits, near the heat vent, and I can see that the cloth over it has risen slightly, the dough pushing against its cover. The wild yeasts have done their work. The bread will be good today.

I settle into the worn depression in the floor—the one that fits a seated, forward-leaning human body, the one that has been worn by generations of bodies sitting in this exact spot. I can feel the shallow curve of it against my own small body, the way it holds me like a cupped hand. I pull my knees up toward my chest and wrap my arms around them and wait. I am not impatient. I have never been impatient. The waiting is part of the rhythm, like the pause before a drop falls, like the moment of stillness before the bats drop from their niches.

The adolescent appears at the edge of the common area, hair mussed from sleep, moving with the same practiced ease. They sit down beside me, not in the depression but on the edge of it, leaving the worn spot for me because I am still small and they are no longer small and that is how it works. They do not say anything. We sit together and listen to the woman working at the hearth and the dripping of the water and the faint ring of the ceramic as it expands, almost imperceptibly, with the first hint of the new day's heat.

I am the child who will eat with my fingers.

The bread comes to us still warm, flat and dense and slightly sour, cooked on the heated tile in the hearth alcove. The woman tears it into three pieces and gives me the first piece, the one that is slightly thicker at the edge, because I am the youngest and that is how it has always been. The adolescent gets the next piece. The woman takes the last. We eat in silence, the way we always eat the first bread of the morning, letting the taste of nut and earth and wild yeast fill our mouths. The dried fruit comes next, passed from hand to hand without needing to be asked for. It is chewy and intensely sweet, the sugars concentrated by the steady warmth of the drying rack, and I hold each piece in my mouth for a long time before I chew, the way the adolescent does, the way the woman does. This is how we eat. This is how we have always eaten.

As we eat, the first bats begin to return from their night's hunting.

I hear them before I see them—the high, metallic ringing of their echolocation chirps, growing louder, more numerous, as the first individuals find the entrance and slip through the dark, twisting passage. They emerge into the chamber one by one, then in twos and threes, then in a continuous stream, their small bodies suddenly present in the orange lamplight after the long dark of the outside. They circle the upper reaches of the chamber, weaving through the ladders and the hanging herbs and the drying fruit, before settling into their niches, their tiny claws clicking against the ceramic. The sound of their return fills the chamber, a rising tide of chitters and squeaks and the soft thumps of their bodies against the walls, and I watch it all from my seat in the worn depression, my fingers still sticky with fruit, my mouth still tasting of bread.

This is the moment I know best. This is the moment when the false-night ends and the day begins, when the bats come home and the condensation cycle shifts and the woman rises to knead the next day's dough and the adolescent begins to check the troughs and the guano ledges and the mushroom pits. This is the moment when the chamber becomes itself again, full of life and sound and the patient, steady pulse of the system that holds us all. I am the child who will learn to do all of these things, who will press my palm to the wall and know if the home is well, who will harvest the mushrooms and tend the compost and check the water channels with a small clay cup. But for now, I am only the child who sits in the warm, smooth depression and watches the bats return and listens to the thousand small rhythms of the place and knows, without needing to think about it, that this is where I belong.

I am the child. I am the home. I am the bats and the drip and the bread and the warm ceramic and the long, unbroken chain of bodies that have lived and died in this single vessel. I am the future of this place, and it is already written in my bones, in the grooves of my fingers from the ladder rungs, in the taste of the water from the cistern, in the dream of flying that I will have again tonight, and every night, for as long as I live.
