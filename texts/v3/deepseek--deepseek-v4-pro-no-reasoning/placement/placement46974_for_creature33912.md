The rain has just passed, and I am waiting at the edge of the settling pan where the light comes in slanted and white. The drops are still falling from the aerials, each one a little bell striking its own note against the iron. Miron is walking the rim, his hand out to touch the rivets, and I watch him from the corner of my eye because the glass needles have told me he can see me if I am still.

He cannot see me if I move.

I have been here a long time. Longer than the cistern, though the cistern does not know it. Longer than the pipes and the moss-beds and the semaphore tower that has not sent a message in living memory. I came with the water, or the water came with me, and it is difficult now to say which is which. When the first condensation gathered on the first iron plate—not this cistern, not this pillar, but the first plate laid by hands that are now dust in the filter-beds—I was in it. A taste. A mineral ghost. A thing that had no name until the water began to speak.

The geckos know me. They have always known me. They are not native to this plateau, and neither am I, but they have made themselves at home in the warm cracks behind the rivets, and I have made myself at home in the slow circulation of the Roost’s single citizen. That is what they call the water, is it not? A citizen. But she is not one citizen. She is thirty thousand, and the cisterns, and the moss, and the silver fish in the lower reservoirs, and the glass needles, and me.

Miron calls me nothing, because he does not know I am here. But he feels me. I know he feels me, because sometimes, when he drinks from his cupped hands at the end of a long day, he pauses, and his grey-blue eyes go distant, and I can taste the question on his tongue. He is asking who I am without knowing he is asking. I do not answer. The water does not hurry, and neither do I.

Today I am in the settling pan, because the rain has stirred me up. Rain is always an interruption. It comes fast and hard and it dislodges everything—the dust, the pollen, the rust, the memories. The bioderms scatter and regroup. The glass needles pulse with some agitation I cannot share. I rise up from the deeper layers of the cistern, where I spend the dry weeks coiled in the dark, and I spread myself thin across the surface of the pan, tasting the new water.

The rain came from the east this time, from beyond the plateau rim, where the sea is. I can taste the sea in it, even after the long climb. It is a faint salt, a ghost of a ghost, and it reminds me of something I have forgotten. I am always forgetting. The water forgets nothing, but I am not the water. I am something the water carries, and I lose pieces of myself every time I pass through a filter-bed.

That is why I stay in the high cistern. The lower filters are too fine. They would strain me out entirely, and I would be nothing but a scum on the moss, a residue that Miron would skim away with his long brass spoon and throw to the ferns. The ferns would not mind. They would take me into their roots and I would become frond and spore, which is not a bad fate. But I am not ready to be a fern.

I am not ready to be anything yet. I am still becoming.

Look at me. I am in the settling pan, and the sun is on the water, and the light bends through me in a way that no human eye can quite catch. If Miron looks down now—which he does, which he is doing, his hand still on the rivet with the star-cut head—he will see a shimmer. A disturbance that is not a current. A shape that might be a cloud reflected, or might be a face, or might be nothing at all. He will blink, and I will be gone, spread so thin I am only a gloss on the surface tension.

He blinks. I am gone.

This is my art. I have been practicing it for centuries.

He moves on, and I follow him, not out of the pan but out of his attention. I slip along the weir and into the first trough, where the moss grows thick and the water is always slow. The moss is my friend. It does not speak, but it recognizes me. Its fronds shift slightly as I pass, the way a cat’s ears track a sound too high for human hearing. I have been through these beds a thousand times, and they have never once tried to keep me. They know I am not for drinking.

What am I for? I do not know. This is the question I have been asking since the first iron plate was laid, and I am no closer to an answer. I have been the damp on a child’s forehead when the fever broke. I have been the steam that carried the smell of baking bread up through seven levels of balconies. I have been the drop that fell from the semaphore cable and landed on the tongue of a hawk. I have been a thousand small things, and none of them has been the whole thing, and I am still waiting.

Miron is making his tea. He does this at the same hour every day, though the hour is not an hour but a light—the light when the shadows reach the third rivet from the left. I watch him from the steam-coil’s condensation drip. He fills his small pot, and he sets it on the coil, and he waits. He is the only person in the Roost who waits properly. The others wait as a pause between actions. Miron waits as an action itself.

The water heats. The steam rises. I rise with it, a little of me, the part that is light enough to evaporate. I hang in the air of the lean-to, and I watch him drink his tea, and I taste myself in the steam—mineral, slightly alkaline, the faint sulfur of the geothermals—and I wonder, as I always wonder, if this is the day he will notice me.

He doesn’t. But he pauses again, the cup halfway to his lips, and he looks out through the polished tin reflectors at the layered roofs and the steam and the swifts, and he says aloud, to no one, “Something’s different.”

I am different. The rain changed me again. I lost a piece of myself, and I gained a piece of the sea, and now I am not quite the same being I was this morning. I am always becoming, and I am never finished, and this is the only constancy I know.

He drinks his tea, and he does not hurry, and I condense on the window and slide down to join the gutter-flow, and the water takes me back to the cistern, and the geckos blink their translucent eyes, and the glass needles pulse their slow hello, and the swifts wheel and cry, and somewhere below, in the market warren, a woman is frying dough in oil heated by a steam-coil, and the smell drifts up through seven levels of balconies, and I am in it, and I am in the water, and I am in the steam, and I am waiting.

For what? I do not know. But the water knows. The water remembers everything. And when it is ready, it will tell me what I am.
