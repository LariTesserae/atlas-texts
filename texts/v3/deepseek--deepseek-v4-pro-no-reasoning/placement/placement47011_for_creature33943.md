The sound of my own footsteps always tells me where I am before my eyes do. In the Dive, a footfall is never just a footfall—it’s a conversation. The packed stone dust of the floor gives a soft, absorbent thump, barely an echo, as if the ground is swallowing the sound politely. On the basalt ledges, my bare soles make a wet, kissing sound against the stone, and the walls return it to me a half-second later, slightly deeper. On wood, the sound is a dull knock that travels through the timber and comes back through my bones. I know every bridge and ramp by the way it speaks under my weight.

I’m walking now along the high ledge, the one we call the Spine, because it runs like a crooked backbone for almost the whole length of the central settlement. To my left, the basalt wall is cool and damp under my trailing fingertips, slick with the constant mineral sweat that never dries. My right hand skims the low parapet, which is just stacked stone with a rope woven through it, more a reminder of the edge than a barrier. Below me, the Dive drops away into the lamplit dimness, layer after layer of stone roofs and packed-earth floors, a jumble of walls that lean together like tired dancers. I can hear the sounds of evening settling in: the mutter of voices as households gather, the clink of a clay pot against stone, the high, thin sound of a child singing a drip-song somewhere in the deep shadows.

My name is Nick. Or at least, that’s the part of my name I think with. The Between-Folk don’t give single names the way the surface does; a person’s name is a string of small words that grow over time, like lichen. When I was born, I was called Small-Hand-Reaching, because that’s what my mother said I looked like in the lamplight. When I was old enough to walk the bridges alone, I earned the second part: Steady-Foot-on-Rope. Then came the incident with the sleek-fish pool, which got me Mud-in-Hair-For-A-Week. It goes on. But somewhere along the way, a trader from the surface heard my name-string and laughed and said I looked like a Nick to her, and the word stuck. I like it. It’s short, and it doesn’t taste of damp.

I’m on my way to check the lamps along the Spine. That’s one of my household jobs, the work I do for my ledge. There are twelve lamps between our cluster of rooms and the staircase that leads down to the lower pools, and each one needs its oil topped and its wick trimmed, or the darkness starts to creep in. Darkness in the Dive isn’t like darkness on the surface, or so I’ve been told. Up there, I imagine, darkness is just the absence of light. Down here, it has weight. It has presence. When a lamp goes out, the shadows don’t just deepen—they press in, curious, like something breathing. You can feel the difference on your skin. So we keep the lamps burning.

I kneel at the first niche, a hollow in the basalt about the size of my cupped hands. The fat-lamp is a simple clay bowl, thick-walled and rough-glazed, filled with rendered beetle-fat that smells nutty and warm when it burns. The wick is a twist of thread-grass, charred at the tip. The flame is steady in the humid air, a tongue of orange that barely wavers. I pull the small oil-skin from my belt and tilt a measure of fat into the bowl, careful not to overfill—a spilled lamp is a bad omen, and also just a mess. I trim the wick with my thumbnail, watch the flame settle, and move on.

By the time I reach the fourth lamp, I’ve fallen into the rhythm of it. The Spine curves slightly here, following the natural bend of the crack, and the settlement opens up into a wider chamber we call the Gape. Here, the two walls of the Dive pull apart far enough that you can’t touch both at once, even with your arms spread wide. The floor is sixty feet below, a soft gray floor of stone dust and moss. Rope bridges crisscross the space at three different levels, and the walls are studded with doorways and windows, the warm amber of lamplight spilling out and getting tangled in the mist. The mist is thick tonight, rising from the floor like a slow exhalation, and the bridges seem to float in it, unmoored from the ground.

I stop and lean against the parapet, looking out. The Gape is beautiful. It’s a word we don’t use often down here—beauty is a surface concept, something shown off, admired from a distance—but I let myself think it anyway. From here, I can see the green phosphorescence of the live-coals, scattered across the walls like a reflection of a sky none of us have ever truly seen. I can see the dim shapes of people moving on the lower bridges, their movements slow and deliberate, dark figures against the softer dark. I can hear the water, because the water is always here, a constant threading of tiny sounds: the plink of a droplet hitting a stone shelf, the wet sigh of moss releasing moisture, the distant, almost subaudible trickle of a seep finding its way down to the pools.

I stay there for a moment—just a moment—before I hear the voice behind me.

"You’re letting the lamp go, Nick."

I don’t startle. In the Dive, you learn not to startle; there’s not enough space for sudden movements. Instead, I turn my head slowly, already smiling, because I know that voice.

Elett stands in the doorway of the next dwelling, a low stone arch with a curtain of woven moss-hair hanging half-drawn. She’s one of my ledge-sharers, which means she’s family in the way the Dive understands family: not by blood, not by oath, but by the slow accumulation of shared space and shared care. She’s older than me—she remembers the basalt split, the new crack by the lower pools, which happened before I was born—and her hair is the color of dry moss, gray-green in the lamplight. Her eyes are set deep in a face mapped with stone dust, and her shoulders have the permanent forward curve of someone who’s spent decades under low ceilings. She’s holding a clay cup, steam rising from it in the cool air.

"Just looking," I say.

"You’re always just looking." She steps aside, holding the curtain open. "Come in. The seep-tea is hot, and I have news."

I check the lamp—still burning, wick fine—and duck through the archway into Elett’s room.

The room is small, which is not a complaint. All rooms in the Dive are small; you learn to live in the space you have and not want more. The ceiling is low enough that I have to bow my head slightly, even with the permanent stoop I’ve had since I was a child. The walls are lined with shelves carved directly into the basalt, holding clay pots and bundles of dried moss and a small collection of the basalt carvings Elett makes during the long still hours: tiny, perfect figures of salamanders and beetles and the coiled shape of a rope bridge, rendered in miniature. A fat-lamp burns on a stone ledge, and a sleeping mat is rolled against one wall. In the center of the room, a shallow basin has been hollowed into the floor, and a seep drips steadily into it from a crack in the ceiling—the water-meets-stone that makes this place a home.

Elett settles onto a folded mat and gestures for me to sit across from her. I do. She hands me the cup of seep-tea, which is just hot water steeped with a particular gray-green moss that tastes of minerals and something faintly sweet, like the memory of a flower. I wrap my hands around it, letting the warmth soak into my fingers.

"What news?" I ask.

She doesn’t answer right away. Instead, she tilts her head back and closes her eyes, listening. I listen too, out of habit. You always listen in the Dive. The water is dripping into the basin, steady and slow, the same rhythm it’s had for as long as Elett has lived here. Somewhere below us, a rope bridge creaks as someone crosses it. The basalt makes a faint, deep sound, a kind of grinding sigh, as it shifts in the night.

"Old Marta’s lamp has gone out," Elett says finally.

The words settle into the air between us. It takes me a moment to understand, because Old Marta’s lamp has been burning my whole life, and longer. She’s—she was—one of the oldest Between-Folk, a woman who remembered things no one else remembered, who could tell you the names of the seep-pools that had dried up before I was born, who knew seventeen kinds of quiet and could name them all. Her lamp was a fixture of the lower settlement, a steady flame in a niche near the pools, and people used it as a landmark. Turn left at Old Marta’s light. Keep going until you see Old Marta’s flame.

"When?" I ask.

"Last night. Her ledge-sharer found her this morning, sitting against the wall with her hands folded and her lamp gone dark. The wick had burned all the way down." Elett opens her eyes. "They’ve placed her in the crack already. The one by the eastern pools, where the walls are still closing."

I nod. I know the crack. It’s a narrow seam where the basalt is still actively settling, the two faces slowly, slowly drawing together over years and decades. When a body is placed there, it can take a generation for the stone to close around it completely. Old Marta will become part of the Dive, her bones pressed into the basalt, her name-string passed into the communal memory. Already, I can imagine the children gathering to hear the stories: Do you remember old Marta’s lamp? Do you remember the way she could call the salamanders to her hand? Do you remember before she went into the stone?

"I’ll miss her," I say, and the words feel small, inadequate. We don’t have a word for grief in the Between-Folk tongue, not exactly. The closest we have is a phrase that means "the quiet left by a missing drip"—the sudden absence of a sound you didn’t realize you were hearing until it stopped.

"We all will," Elett says. "But that’s not the only news."

She leans forward, and her voice drops even lower, which I would not have thought possible. We are already speaking in the near-whisper that the Dive encourages.

"The word is that there’s a stranger in the Dive."

I blink. The word for "stranger"—the single syllable that means "loud-walker"—hangs in the air between us, heavy as the mist. Strangers come so rarely. The last one I remember was a trader who got lost and stumbled down the staircase by accident, and that was years ago. She stayed for three days, and the whole time, the settlement held its breath, watching her with the patient, unfocused gaze we use for things that don’t belong. She left with a pack full of lichen dye and never came back.

"A trader?" I ask.

"No." Elett shakes her head slowly. "Not a trader. A surface-walker, but not here to trade. They say she came down the staircase alone, with no pack and no supplies, and when the finders asked her why she was here, she said she was looking for something."

"What?"

"She didn’t say. Or she did, and the finders didn’t understand. You know how surface words can be, all edges and corners." Elett takes the cup from my hands and sips from it herself, her eyes distant. "She’s staying in the guest room near the upper entrance. The elders are talking to her. But Nick—" She pauses. "They say she asked about the lower pools. The deep ones, where the water is still."

A small, cool feeling settles into my stomach. The deep pools are not forbidden, exactly. But they are not spoken of, either. They lie at the very bottom of the Dive, where the crack narrows and the walls lean in so close you can feel the breath of the stone on both cheeks at once. The water there is black and perfectly still, and it tastes of minerals and something else, something that numbs the tongue. The elders say it’s just water, just seepage from an underground source, but everyone knows the pools are strange. The moss doesn’t grow there. The live-coals won’t glow within ten feet of them. And occasionally, someone goes down to the pools and comes back changed—quiet in a way that isn’t one of the seventeen quiets, a silence that feels like a door closing.

"Why would she want to go there?" I ask.

"I don’t know," Elett says. "But she’s been asking. And the elders are… unsettled."

Unsettled elders are a rare and worrying thing. The elders of the Dive are not leaders, exactly—we don’t have leaders, not in the surface sense. They are simply the people who remember the most, who have been in the stone the longest, who can tell you the history of every bridge and the name of every seep. They do not give orders. They offer guidance, and people listen, because their guidance is usually right. If they are unsettled, it means they have seen something in the stranger that disturbs the rhythm of the Dive.

"What should we do?" I ask.

Elett hands the cup back to me. "Nothing, for now. You should finish the lamps. Your ledge-sharers will worry if the Spine goes dark. But after that…" She pauses, tilting her head again, listening. The seep drips. The basalt sighs. Somewhere, a child laughs, the sound muffled by stone. "After that, you might go down to the gathering place. See if you can get a look at this stranger for yourself. You’re good at seeing, Nick. You always have been."

It’s not entirely a compliment. Being good at seeing, in the Dive, means you notice things other people don’t. It means you’re the one who spots the hairline crack in a bridge rope before it snaps, or catches the slight change in a person’s voice that means they’re sick, or sees the way the moss is growing differently on one wall, which means the seep is shifting and the room will flood in a month. It’s useful. But it also means you carry a weight that other people don’t.

I finish the tea and hand the cup back to Elett. "I’ll go to the gathering place after the lamps."

She nods, satisfied. "Good. And Nick?"

I pause at the threshold, one hand on the moss-hair curtain. I look back at her, small and stooped and steady in the lamplight, her face all shadows and grooves like a map of the Dive itself.

"Be careful," she says. "The stranger is a loud-walker. But I think she carries a quiet of her own."

I don’t know what that means. But I nod, and I duck back through the archway, and I continue my work along the Spine, lamp by lamp, filling each bowl with fat, trimming each wick, keeping the darkness at bay. The mist is thicker now, rising from the floor in slow curls, and the live-coals on the walls have brightened their nightly glow. Somewhere far above, the narrow strip of sky is turning from deep gray to black, and the first stars are appearing, pinpricks of light that look, from down here, exactly like the glow of the beetles on the walls. If you squinted, you could almost believe the Dive was a sky itself, turned inside out, with the stars underfoot and the earth above.

I think about the stranger as I walk. A surface-walker, alone, asking about the deep pools. It makes no sense. The surface has its own waters, wide and bright and moving—I’ve heard the traders describe them, rivers that run under open sky, lakes that reflect clouds. Why come down here, into the dark and the damp, to find a pool of black water that doesn’t move?

The last lamp on the Spine is in a niche near the staircase that leads down to the lower levels. I fill it, trim it, and pause. Below me, the stairs descend into the mist, each step worn concave by bare feet, the basalt slick with perpetual damp. The mist is thick enough now that I can’t see the bottom. The gathering place is down there, a broad ledge where people meet to trade news and tell stories and sip seep-tea in the company of others. If the stranger is anywhere, she’ll be there.

I put my foot on the first step. The stone is cool and familiar, the texture of it as known to me as my own palm. I can feel the slight vibration through the basalt, the slow, deep settling of the earth, the endless, patient work of a crack still learning to be empty.

I begin to climb down.
