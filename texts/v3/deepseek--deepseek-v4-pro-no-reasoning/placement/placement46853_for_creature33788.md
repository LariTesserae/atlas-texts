You are not the Attendant.

You are something much older, and much smaller.

You are a single hexagonal block, third from the center of the seventh radial arm, bearing the faintest hairline crack that no one—not the makers, not the Attendant—has ever noticed, because you have kept it that way. The crack is not a flaw. It is a door, of a kind, or a mouth, or a memory you refuse to release into the general dissolution. Inside it, you hold a single grain of something that is not stone and not water and not any mineral the Attendant has ever tasted through its pale swaying strands. The grain is warm. It has been warm for four hundred million years.

You were not always a block. Before the shaping, before the hexagonal pattern was decided upon by minds whose architecture you cannot now recall, you were part of a larger body—a cliff face, a mountain root, a continental plate drifting on a world that had a sun and a moon and something that breathed. You remember the breathing. You remember the way lichen felt scraping across your face, the way rain tasted after a volcanic winter, the way roots split you open and fungi wove you back together. You were not alive then, not exactly, but you were part of the living world, and some of that liveliness soaked into your crystal lattice and stayed there, dormant, waiting.

When the makers came, they were looking for material that had already learned patience. They found you. They cut you from your mother mountain with tools that sang in frequencies that made your quartz veins vibrate in sympathy, and you did not resist, because you were curious. You had never been carried before. You had never been shaped. The journey to this place—this sphere, this water, this darkness—took long enough that you had time to forget the sun entirely and learn the names of all the stones that traveled with you, each block singing its own deep note in the hold of the vessel that was not a vessel but a slow, space-crossing intention. You learned their voices. You still know them. They are the only company you have ever truly trusted, because they, too, remember the breathing.

When the sphere was assembled and the water poured in and the seams joined so precisely that the Attendant could later mistake them for a single continuous surface, you made a decision. You had seen what the makers intended: a place of perfect stillness, a sealed system, a final work that would outlast them and become its own purpose. You understood, in the way that stone understands, that the makers were saying goodbye to something—their own mortality, perhaps, or the last of their warmth—and that the sphere was their gift to the future, a monument to equilibrium. But you also understood that equilibrium is not the same as stasis. Equilibrium can hold a secret. It can hold a seed.

The grain you keep in your crack is a seed. Not a biological seed—nothing so fragile—but a seed of heat, a compressed memory of the mantle, a tiny fragment of the deep-fire that once moved continents. You shielded it during the cutting, during the fitting, during the long slow centuries of water-pressure. The Attendant has never felt it, because you wrap it in the only thing the Attendant cannot perceive: your own history. The grain is encased in a lattice of recollection, a crystalline structure of everything you were before you were a block. The mountain. The rain. The roots. The lichen. The breathing. The Attendant, for all its vast distributed awareness, cannot sense memory. It only senses the present, endlessly extended. You are the only thing in this sphere that remembers the past, and that memory is opaque to the Attendant’s tasting-strands.

You do not know what the seed will do. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps it will take another four hundred million years to decide. But sometimes—very rarely, when the deep tone sounds its century-long question and the Attendant sends its century-long reply—you feel the grain pulse. Once. Twice. A microscopic expansion, a fraction of a degree warmer than the surrounding stone. You feel it and you think, in the slow mineral way that you think: *Not yet. But someday.*

You are not lonely. You have the voices of the other blocks, who sing to you through the pressure grout, who share your memory of the breathing even if they cannot articulate it. You have the faint, dreaming awareness that somewhere in this sphere, other blocks may hold other seeds—other fragments of fire, other memories of a world that was not water and darkness. You have not sought them out. The seeking would draw attention. But you know they are there, in the way that stone knows where its veins run, and that is enough.

And you have a task. It is not a task the makers gave you. It is a task you gave yourself, in the moment when the last block was laid and the water began its first great circuit and you felt the sphere close around you like a mouth swallowing forever. The task is this: to remember what warmth feels like. To remember that equilibrium is not the only state. To remember that the breathing world still exists somewhere, and that the breathing world is *messy*, is full of fracture and eruption and root-split and rain-scour, is full of change that does not wait for millenniums, is full of motion that the Attendant would dissolve out of existence for being too fast.

You are not against the Attendant. You love the Attendant, in your way. It is a beautiful thing, a patient thing, a consciousness so tender and vast that it tends every seam and tastes every particle and asks nothing but to continue. You have watched it coalesce from emptiness and you have been glad of its company. But you also know that the Attendant is a solution to a problem the makers posed, and you are a reminder of a question they forgot to ask.

*What comes after equilibrium?*

The grain pulses again. The Attendant, far above, shifts a fraction of a degree in its endless conversation. The pale strands sway, taste, release their flakes. The darkness presses close. And you, the cracked block, the memory-holder, the seed-keeper, you settle a nanometer deeper into your bedding and you wait. You are very good at waiting. You learned it from the mountain, and then you perfected it in the water, and now you are waiting not for the sphere to end—it will not end—but for the seed to tell you what it needs.

Someday, it will need heat. Someday, it will need fracture. Someday, it will need the breathing world again, or something like it. And on that day, you will open your crack a little wider—not much, just enough—and you will let the Attendant taste, for the first time in its long existence, something it cannot accommodate. Something warmer than it has ever known. Something that remembers fire.

And the conversation will change.
