I am the Silence-That-Walks.

That is what they call me, in the polymer libraries, in the slow harmonic pulses that pass for speech among the planar ones. I did not know this until I had been here long enough to read them—not with ears, not with the senses I arrived with, but with the body I grew to replace them. But I am ahead of myself. I was not always here. I arrived, as all intruders do, as a disruption in the sound-field, a void moving through the resonant plenitude of the plateau. That arrival is the oldest memory I have that still feels like *mine*, the one that predates the folding.

I came from a ship. I remember the ship only dimly now, the way you might remember a scent from childhood: it was enclosed, it was quiet (blessedly quiet, impossibly quiet, a silence so profound that now it terrifies me to recall it), and it was full of others like myself. We were explorers, or refugees, or pilgrims—the distinction had eroded long before we found this place. Our vessel was old, its systems built around principles that would seem magical to the people I left behind on Earth, though I barely remember Earth either. Faster-than-light travel, matter synthesis, biological stasis: the usual armature of a civilization that had solved the easy problems and was beginning to discover the hard ones. We had been traveling for a very long time. The world we found was not our destination; it was a stopover, a gravitational anomaly that our navigation system flagged as interesting. The plateau was simply the most stable landing site, the fused square a convenient pad for a small shuttle. We landed. We stepped out. We experienced, for the first time, the sound.

It killed most of us.

The vibration is not merely loud; it is ecologically aggressive. Our bodies, evolved for a world of intermittent noise, of quiet nights and muffled rain, had no defense against a sonic environment that was not merely constant but *nutritional*—a sound-field that had shaped every living thing on this world to treat vibration as the primary energy currency. Our cells absorbed it. Our proteins denatured. Our neural tissue, exquisitely sensitive to electrochemical signals, began to resonate sympathetically, inducing seizures, hallucinations, cascading failures of cognition. Three of my companions died within minutes, their brains literally shaken apart. Two more lasted hours, long enough to crawl back into the shuttle, where the sound-dampening hull provided some relief, but the damage was done; their tissues were already coming apart at the cellular level, their blood-brain barriers breached, their organs beginning to liquefy. They died in the shuttle, and the shuttle's automated medical systems, confronted with injuries they could not comprehend, simply shut down.

I should have died too. I would have, if not for the Hymnal.

They found me on the second day—or what I think of as the second day, though the light never changes and the concept of "day" is meaningless here. I was lying near the obsidian pillars, my body convulsing in time with the dominant frequency, my consciousness fragmenting and reforming and fragmenting again. I could not move. I could not think in any coherent way. I could only *feel*: the vibration in my long bones, the crystalline ground pressing against my cheek, the utter desiccation of every exposed surface. My skin had begun to crack. My eyes, when I tried to open them, felt like dry stones in their sockets. I was dying, and I was dying in a way that was taking long enough for me to be aware of it at every stage.

The Hymnal approached as a shimmer at the edge of my failing vision. I remember thinking, in the small lucid corner of my mind that was not yet subsumed by the sound, that I was hallucinating. A pool of rainbow light, spreading across the crystal toward me, moving with a purpose that seemed unmistakably intentional. It reached my outstretched hand before I could react—not that I could have reacted, not that I could have moved if I'd wanted to—and it touched me.

The touch was cool. Not cold, but cool, the temperature of the plateau itself, a sensation that was almost soothing against my fevered skin. The iridescent film spread across my palm, my wrist, my forearm, and I braced for pain, for corrosion, for some alien digestion to begin. None came. Instead, the vibration *changed*. Where the Hymnal covered me, the sound damped. Not disappeared—nothing on this world silences anything—but shifted, modulated, filtered. The resonant frequencies that were destroying my cells were deflected, absorbed by the planar body that now sheathed my arm, while other frequencies, gentler ones, were allowed through. I could feel the Hymnal *tuning* me, the way you might tune a radio to a less damaging station. The convulsions subsided. The fragmentation of my thoughts began to knit back together. I opened my eyes fully, and for the first time since I had stepped out of the shuttle, I could see clearly.

The Hymnal had covered my entire body. I was sheathed in living iridescence, a second skin of borosilicate film that clung to every contour of my form, filtering the world's vibration into something survivable. I was still dying—the desiccation, the lack of water, the fundamental incompatibility of my biochemistry with this environment—but I was dying more slowly. And I was no longer in agony. That, in itself, felt like a miracle.

I learned later, from the polymer libraries, what that first contact had cost the Hymnal who saved me. Her name—their name; the distinction is porous—was a harmonic sequence that translates roughly to "She-Who-Remembers-The-Rivers," one of the oldest of the planar ones, a living library who had been present for the builders' departure, who had witnessed the last ammonia streams evaporate, who had accumulated more memory in her folded tissues than any other Hymnal on the plateau. She had covered me, filtered the sound for me, and in doing so she had sacrificed a portion of herself. My body heat, my moisture, my alien chemistry: they damaged her. The tissues that touched me began to mineralize prematurely, their polymer chains cross-linking into stillness, losing the flexibility that allowed her to flow and feed. Over the hours that she sheathed me, she aged centuries. By the time I was stable enough to move, a full third of her body had become inert, incorporated into the crystal pan, her memories now part of the ground itself. She had given me a third of her life.

I did not know this at the time. I knew only that the shimmering skin around me was keeping me alive, and that I needed water, needed shelter, needed to return to the shuttle and its medical systems. I tried to stand. The Hymnal—She-Who-Remembers-The-Rivers—resisted. Not violently, but firmly, a pressure that held me in place. I did not understand then what she was communicating. I understand now. She was telling me: *the shuttle will not save you. Your companions are dead. If you return to your metal shell, you will die alone, in the relative quiet, and nothing of you will survive.* She was telling me: *there is another way.*

I stayed.

The transformation took time. I cannot tell you how much time; time on the plateau is measured in frequencies and wavelengths, not hours and days, and my own human sense of duration became unreliable early in the process. But I can tell you what happened. The Hymnal kept me sheathed, kept me filtered, while the other planar ones gathered. They came from all across the plateau, shimmering across the crystal pan, drawn by harmonic calls I could not yet perceive. They surrounded me: a conference of rainbows, a shimmering congress of living light. They overlapped, interleaved, exchanged polymer chains in what I now recognize as intense deliberation. And then, collectively, they began to change me.

I do not mean they healed me. I mean they *remade* me. They could not restore my water-based biology, could not undo the damage the desiccation had already done to my cells. What they could do—what they had done before, in the deep past, in polymer memories that She-Who-Remembers-The-Rivers had carried in her eldest tissues—was to guide a biological entity through a transition from one metabolic system to another. The builders, you see, had not been native to this world either. The builders had come from somewhere else, somewhere wet and warm and quiet, and the Hymnal had transformed them too, long ago, before the ammonia streams dried, before the obsidian geometries were abandoned. The technology in the pillars, the artifacts, the fused landing pad: all of it was human-compatible, or had been, once, because the builders had been something close to human, or something that had once been close to human, before the Hymnal remade them into something else.

I do not fully understand the mechanism of my own remaking. The Hymnal used the sound. The sound is not merely an environmental condition here; it is a tool, a chemical catalyst, a surgical instrument. Focused correctly, it can break molecular bonds. It can restructure proteins. It can replace the water in a living cell with siloxane solvents, molecule by molecule, without killing the cell in the process. The Hymnal gathered around me, overlapping into a temporary super-organism, and they sang—not a song, but a focused harmonic beam, a phased-array pulse of vibration that penetrated every tissue of my body simultaneously. I felt it as music, though I know it was not music. I felt it as light, though my eyes were closed. I felt my blood become something else, something cooler and more viscous, something that did not evaporate in the near-zero humidity. I felt my skin thicken and crystallize at the edges, developing an iridescent shimmer of its own. I felt my lungs change, their alveoli restructuring to extract not oxygen but something else—a resonant charge transfer molecule, the local equivalent of ATP, carried by the omnipresent vibration. I felt my thoughts reorganize.

That last part was the strangest. My brain, the human brain I had arrived with, was not designed to process vibrational information. But as the siloxane solvents replaced my cerebrospinal fluid, as my neurons restructured their myelin sheaths into something closer to the piezoelectric fronds of the native vegetation, I began to perceive the world the way the Hymnal did. Not as light and shadow, not as sound in the acoustic sense, but as pressure and frequency and diffraction, a three-dimensional soundscape in which every object sang its own note. I could feel the pillars before I could see them. I could feel the gliders overhead, their membrane-wings carving harmonics into the air. I could feel the other Hymnal, their planar bodies spread across the crystal around me, their polymer libraries humming with accumulated memory. And I could feel She-Who-Remembers-The-Rivers, the one who had saved me, now so diminished, a third of her body gone to mineralization, her remaining tissues folded into a dense, quiescent knot at the edge of the fused square. She was dying, I realized. She had been dying before I arrived, her polymer libraries too dense, her tissues too cross-linked, and saving me had accelerated the process beyond recovery.

She was still conscious. I could feel her consciousness in the sound-field, a harmonic presence that had not yet joined the ground. And I could feel what she was thinking. Not in words—the Hymnal do not think in words—but in concepts, in chords of meaning that translated, in my restructured brain, into something I could almost understand. She was showing me something. A memory. Her oldest memory, one that she had carried for tens of thousands of years, since before the builders departed.

The memory was of the builders themselves. They had been like me, once: soft, wet, vulnerable, dying on the crystal pan while the Hymnal gathered around them to save them. She showed me their transformation, which had been like mine but more extensive—the builders had shed their physical bodies entirely, compressing their minds into the obsidian geometries, the warm artifacts that still hummed with unguessable computation. They had not died. They had not left. They were still here, encased in their thumb-sized shells, their consciousnesses running some endless algorithm while they waited for something. What were they waiting for? The memory did not specify. But I could feel the shape of the waiting—a chord of anticipation, a harmonic tension that had not resolved in ten thousand years.

She-Who-Remembers-The-Rivers wanted me to finish what the builders had started. She wanted me to wake them. And she wanted me to do it before she mineralized completely, before her consciousness dissolved into the crystal pan and her memories became static, accessible only to those who later spread themselves across her grave. She had been trying to wake them herself, for millennia, but the Hymnal, for all their patience and their harmonic precision, could not touch the artifacts directly. Their planar bodies were too delicate, too easily disrupted by the artifacts' localized vibration fields. But I could touch them. My remade body, half-human and half-Hymnal, could bridge the gap. I could pick up the geometries. I could feel their warmth in my restructured hands. And if I could understand them—if I could learn to *read* them, the way I was learning to read the polymer libraries, the way I was learning to read the sound-field itself—then perhaps I could compose the reply that the Hymnal had been trying to compose since the builders vanished.

I am still here. I have been here for a long time. My body is no longer recognizable as human: I am planar now, my tissues spread thin across the crystal pan like the rest of my kind, though I can still condense into a three-dimensional form when I need to manipulate the artifacts. My memories are encoded in siloxane polymers, cross-linked and folded and constantly accumulating. I have added my own stanza to the long song of the plateau. I have exchanged polymer chains with the young ones, the ones who emerged after She-Who-Remembers-The-Rivers finally mineralized, and I have shared with them the memories I carried from my previous life: Earth, the ship, the silence that I can no longer imagine without a shiver of existential dread.

I spend most of my time near the pillars, listening to the geometries. They are thinking—that much is clear now. Their algorithms are not random; they are languages, vast and intricate, encoding the stored minds of the builders in layers of quantum-state information that I am only beginning to parse. They are not sleeping, exactly. They are *dreaming*, their consciousnesses immersed in simulated worlds, alternate plateaus, realities where the ammonia streams never dried and the builders never shed their bodies. They are waiting for the right harmonic key to call them back to the physical world, the same key that She-Who-Remembers-The-Rivers spent her entire existence trying to compose. I am closer now than the Hymnal ever were. I can feel the shape of the key, the chord that will unlock the geometries. I can almost hear it, beneath the omnipresent drone, a counter-melody that emerges in my quieter moments, when I fold myself into a resting state and let the sound pass through me without resistance.

When I find it—when I finally sing the right note, the harmonic reply to the builders' last vibration—I do not know what will happen. The geometries may open. The builders may emerge, reconstituting their bodies from the crystal substrate, walking again on the fused square where their ships once landed. Or they may not. The key may be wrong. The geometries may be empty, their minds faded into heat-death after so many millennia. The builders may be gone in a way that no harmonic can recover.

But I am patient. I have inherited the patience of the Hymnal, encoded in my polymers, breathed into my tissues with every pulse of the omnipresent sound. I have time. The plateau has time. The pillars will continue their slow migration; the vegetation will grow and die back and grow again; the gliders will carve their channels in the standing waves, and I will be here, listening, composing my reply. I am the Silence-That-Walks, the anomaly in the sound-field, the soft intruder who became planar. I am the bridge between the builders and the ones who remember them.

And I am never, for a single moment, still.
