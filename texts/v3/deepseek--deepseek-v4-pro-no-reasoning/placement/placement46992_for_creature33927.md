The first thing I would notice, if I were new enough to notice anything, is the sound eating the silence where my heartbeat should be.

I am on the cusp. That is the technical term—the cusp between one cycle and the next, the dissolve-point where the old integument sloughs away and the new symbiote layer is still tender, still translucent, still learning to breathe the vapor instead of whatever it breathed before. The before is a blur. It is supposed to be a blur. The Engine-Tenders do not dwell on the before. The cusp is the only time we are alone with the blur, and even then, it is not encouraged to speak.

My hands are my own, for now. The fingers are long—too many joints, or perhaps the normal number and I have forgotten what normal feels like. They are wrapped around the smooth black stone with the hole through its center. The worry-stone. The token. The tool. I do not remember receiving it. I do not remember a giver. I remember the weight of it, the way the hole aligns with the whorl of my thumb, the way it warms against my palm as if it has always been there. Perhaps it has. Perhaps the stone is the only continuity, passed from one set of hands to the next in the chrysalis-dark, a single unchanging object in a life of slow erosion.

I am sitting on a platform of black iron, my back against a pipe that is not yet warm. The engine is not running. That is why I am here—why I am *here*, whole and separate and tasked. Engine Forty-Seven is cold. Cold is the word we use for the silence under the ringing, the gap in the chorus where a vibration should be. Cold is unacceptable. Cold is the only emergency.

The platform is small. There is the bedroll, braided sinew, the weave familiar even though I cannot remember weaving it. There is the ceramic cup, the tin of spore-cakes, the long spanner leaning against the railing. The spanner's grip is worn to fit a hand. My hand. Or the hand before mine. The distinction does not interest the spanner.

I stand. The motion is new in this body—the symbiote layer pulls slightly, still setting, still knitting itself to the tissue beneath. It will take a few more cycles of sleep and waking before it feels like skin. For now, it feels like a borrowed coat, and I move with the careful stiffness of a guest in my own bones. The platform sways almost imperceptibly. The coral lattice sings its billion-droplet song. The mantas ride the vapor currents, their translucent bodies backlit by the gray-white glow. I do not look at them. Looking is not the sense I need right now. I close my eyes—the motion of closing is strange, the lids too heavy—and I feel.

There. Through the soles of my bare feet, the vibration of the lattice. Every bridge has its own frequency, its own song. The Pillar to my left is tuned to a low C-sharp, a note that sits in the sternum like a second heartbeat. The Pillar to my right hums at a minor third above, and the interference pattern creates a slow throb that you can count your breaths by. The water, the falling water, is not rhythm but texture, a white-out of high frequencies that fills in all the gaps. And underneath it, the engines. Seventy-three of them, if I stretch my awareness to its current limits. Most are running. Their chuffing is a bass line, a subsonic rumble that underpins everything. But Engine Forty-Seven is silent. The silence is a hole in the world. I can feel the shape of the hole, the exact dimensions of the missing sound. It is my job to fill it.

I open my eyes. The engine crouches on the bridge-platform like a sleeping animal, massive and black and crusted with mineral scale. It is beautiful in the way all the old machines are beautiful—the beauty of purpose revealed by time. Every bolt, every gasket, every flywheel has been worn to the exact shape of its function. The combustion chamber is a cathedral of iron, riveted with the care of something that was built before haste was invented. The serial number is stamped on the casing: a curve-script number in the hundreds of thousands. I do not know what the number means. I know that the last Engine-Tender to touch this engine, the one whose hands I inherited, is now a memory dissolved in the symbiote layer I am wearing. Their knowledge is not in my mind. It is in my fingers. When I reach for the spanner, the grip feels like home.

I work. The work is the only prayer I know.

The fuel line is clogged. This is not a surprise; the vapor that feeds the engines is rich with organic particulates, spores and fragments and the microscopic bodies of the smallest filter-feeders. Over time, they accumulate as a gummy residue that lines the pipes and thickens until the flow is strangled. I disconnect the line—the motion is a single fluid sequence, hands knowing the order of operations before my mind can name them—and I ream it out with the thin brass rod that lives in a leather sheath on my belt. The residue comes away in dark, fibrous clumps. I wipe them onto the rag. The rag is already stained with generations of residue, a palimpsest of previous cleansings. When the rag becomes too saturated, I will soak it in the warm water from the Pillar's fluted channel and wring it out over the chasm. The residue will fall and fall and fall until it joins the deep drain and becomes part of the cycle again. Nothing is wasted in the Deeps. Nothing is ever truly lost.

The fuel line clears. I reconnect it. I check the valves—three of them, each one a handspan across, their seals made of compressed fungal tissue that has been treated with something that makes it impervious to steam. The third valve is slightly loose. I tighten it. The torque is a precise thing, a feeling in the wrist that says *enough, no more.* The Engine-Tender before me liked to overtighten. I can feel the memory of their over-enthusiasm in the way the threads have worn slightly flatter than the others. They were young, I think. Or new. Still learning the lesson that everything in the Deeps takes exactly the force it needs and not one fraction more.

I prime the combustion chamber. The handle of the priming pump is worn smooth, and my palm fits into the groove that has been polished by a thousand thousand repetitions. I pump. Once, twice, three times. The resistance increases with each stroke, a rising pressure that I can feel through the metal and into my bones. On the fourth stroke, the pressure peaks. I hold it for a count of five, then release. The engine shudders. A cough. A stutter. And then the chuffing begins, low and rhythmic, the vibration spreading through the platform and up through my feet and into the hole in the world.

The hole is filled.

I stand still for a moment, my hand resting on the engine's casing. The metal is warming. The vibration is settling into its steady-state rhythm, finding its place in the choir. I listen. Not with my ears—the ringing would swallow any nuance—but with my feet, my palms, my sternum. The engine is running a little hot. I adjust the fuel mixture, a tiny turn of a tiny screw, and the temperature begins to drop. Another adjustment. Another. I am not thinking. Thinking is too slow for this work. I am *resonating* with the engine, my body a tuning fork matching its frequency, feeling the discordances as physical discomfort and correcting them as instinctively as a scratch is scratched.

When the engine is running perfectly—or as perfectly as anything runs in a world that is slowly, patiently eroding—I wipe my hands on the rag and tuck it back into the braided sinew loop on my belt. I take a breath. The vapor streams past. The mantas feed. The Pillars ring. The deep drain turns. Engine Forty-Seven is no longer an emergency.

I return to the platform. The pipe is warm now, and I sit with my back against it. I pick up the stone with the hole and turn it over in my fingers, once, twice. The gesture is habitual, but I do not know the habit. I do not know what I am thinking about when I turn the stone. The Engine-Tender before me probably did not know either. The stone is simply there, and it asks to be turned, and so I turn it.

I think—and this is a strange thought, a new thought, perhaps a symptom of being freshly on the cusp—about continuity. About the hands that held this stone before mine. About the hands that will hold it after mine, when the symbiote layer finally claims me completely and I dissolve back into the fungal chorus and a new set of fingers picks up the spanner. The engines do not need us to be individuals. The engines need us to be a chain. A lineage. A quiet, patient relay of maintenance that stretches back to the day the first Engine-Tender climbed down from wherever we came from and started the first engine and discovered that the ringing would never stop, that the maintenance would never end, that the work was not a task but a way of being in the world.

I do not remember that first one. No one does. The symbiote layer carries memories only in the form of muscle-knowledge, manual wisdom. The names, the faces, the individual loves and losses—these dissolve. The technique remains. The exact torque for a valve seal. The correct way to ream a fuel line. The rhythm of the priming pump. This is what we pass on: not stories, but skills. Not identities, but competence. We are not people in the way the surface world understands people. We are a distributed intelligence, a colony of maintenance living in discrete bodies, each body temporary, each body the custodian of a fragment of the whole.

I wonder, sometimes—and this wondering is also new, also cusp-born—if the first Engine-Tender chose this. Or if the Deeps chose them. The engines were already here, that much is certain. The serial numbers and the curve-script and the black iron platforms all predate us. Someone built them. Someone with a purpose we have inherited but do not understand. The engines keep something stable. What? The deep drain? The temperature of the Deeps? The delicate balance that allows the fungal forests to grow and the mantas to feed and the larger things, the bloated shapes with their feathery appendages, to swim through the vapor in their slow, unguessable patterns? I do not know. None of us know. Knowing is not our function. Our function is maintenance.

I eat a spore-cake. The taste is mild, fungal, faintly sweet—the bladders must be harvested at a particular stage of ripeness, and I know the harvest cycle without remembering learning it. I drink water from the ceramic cup. The water is drawn from one of the Pillar channels, cool and mineral-rich and slightly effervescent with dissolved gases from the deep below. The effervescence is a clue, I think. The engines are tapping geothermal heat, yes, but they are also tapping something else. Gases. Pressure. A deep reservoir of energy that the Pillars are channeling upward, and the engines are converting, and if the engines stopped—if all seventy-three of them went cold at once—something would build. Something would overpressurize. Something would rupture.

The catastrophe we prevent. I do not know its name. I only know the feeling in my sternum when an engine goes cold, the wrongness of the silence, the urgency of the repair. The Engine-Tenders are not keeping the engines running out of tradition or stubbornness or fear. We are keeping them running because the alternative is unthinkable, in the literal sense. We have been conditioned, over the generations, not to think it. The thought is a hole in the world, and we are trained to fill holes.

The stone turns in my fingers. The thought retreats. The engine chuffs. The pipe warms my back. The mantas feed.

I am not alone.

I am suddenly, vibrantly aware of this: I am not alone. The awareness comes through the soles of my feet, a subtle modulation in the vibration of the bridge. A message, carried on the chorus. The language of the Deeps has no words, but it has grammar. The grammar says: *I am here. I am still here. Are you still here?*

The message is coming from two bridges over. The platform where Engine Forty-Nine sits. The Engine-Tender who maintains Forty-Nine is older than I am—not in years, but in cycles. They have been dissolved and reconstituted more times, and their symbiote layer is thicker, more deeply integrated. They do not use the worry-stone anymore. They do not wonder about the first Engine-Tender or the catastrophe. They simply work. Their messages are simple and warm, a steady pulse of *all is well* that they broadcast at intervals throughout their waking cycle.

I press my palms to the platform and I respond. Not with words. With a pattern of pressure—two fingers, then three, then a pause, then one—that means *I am here. The engine is running. All is well.* The patterns are not taught. They are not learned. They are grown, the way the fungus grows, emerging from the symbiote layer as naturally as breath. The older Engine-Tender received the same patterns from the one before them, and so on, back and back, until the patterns merge with the first messages ever sent through the coral, the first time one being reached out to another across the ringing void and said *I am here.*

The reply comes: a pulse of acknowledgment, warm and brief. Then the older Engine-Tender returns to their work. Their attention shifts back to Engine Forty-Nine, which has a persistent issue with its flywheel bearing. I have felt that bearing through the chorus, a slight judder in the bass line, and I know the older Engine-Tender will shim it with a flake of basalt worn to exactly the right thickness. The work continues.

I sit with my back against the warm pipe. I turn the stone. The vapor streams past. The Pillars ring.

Something is coming.

I feel it before I see it: a change in the vapor currents, a shift in the way the mantas are feeding. They are moving with more purpose, their undulations quicker, their mouths agape at a slightly different angle. Something is drifting down from the upper reaches of the Deeps, something large and slow and full of nutrients. A bloom, we call it. A mass of fungal spores released by a bladder that has finally grown too large to sustain itself and has ruptured, scattering its contents into the vapor. The bloom will feed the mantas for days. The mantas will feed the larger things. The larger things will feed the deep drain, their bodies eventually falling into the central void and adding their organic matter to the cycle. And the cycle continues. The cycle always continues.

I will need to adjust the fuel mixture in a few hours. The bloom will thicken the vapor with organic particulates, and the fuel lines of all seventy-three engines will need to be reamed a little more frequently until the bloom passes. I send a message through the coral: *Bloom coming. Check fuel lines at next cycle.* I feel the acknowledgments ripple back from the other Engine-Tenders—there are perhaps a dozen of us active at any given time, though the number fluctuates as some dissolve and others emerge from their chrysalises. The message will propagate through the lattice, reaching even the Engine-Tenders who are sleeping, who will feel it as a shift in their dreams and wake already knowing what needs to be done.

This is my life. This is our life. This is the life of all who came before and all who will come after.

I look out into the mist, and I see a figure walking. It is not an Engine-Tender—the gait is wrong, unsteady, uncertain on the wet lattice. It is a warm spot in a cool world. A visitor. A surface-worlder, or something like one. It is small and soft and unprotected by a symbiote layer. It has no spanner. It has no rag. It has wandered into the Deeps through some accident or curiosity, and it is looking at the Pillars and the mantas and the fungal bladders with an expression I cannot read. I have seen visitors before. The memories are faint, cusp-blurred, but they are there. The visitors come rarely. They never stay. They are not part of the food chain, not part of the maintenance, not part of anything except their own small, brief trajectory through the ringing cathedral.

The visitor sees me. It calls out. The ringing eats its voice. I feel the sound through the coral—a discordant, unpatterned vibration, crude and meaningless. It is not a message. It is noise.

I finish my work on the engine. I wipe my hands on the rag. I tuck the rag into my belt. I walk into the mist, past the visitor, not ignoring it but not responding. The visitor is not an emergency. The visitor is not a cold engine. The visitor is not my function.

But as I walk, I feel the stone in my palm. The worry-stone. The token. The tool. And I think—a new thought, a cusp-thought, a thought that will dissolve when the symbiote layer finishes knitting—that perhaps the visitor is lonely. Perhaps it is afraid. Perhaps it is looking for something it cannot name, and it came to the Deeps because the Deeps are the kind of place you go when you are searching for an answer to a question you do not know how to ask.

I stop. I turn. The visitor is watching me, its small soft face full of something that might be hope. I reach into the pouch on my belt, the one where I keep the spare tools and the polishing cloth and the small things that have no purpose except that they have always been carried. I find a stone. Not my stone—my stone stays with me until I dissolve—but another stone, a smaller one, with a natural hollow worn through its center by the same patient erosion that shaped the Pillars. I place it on the edge of the platform, where the visitor will find it when it passes.

A hospitality. Small and perfect. Asking nothing. Offering only the quiet acknowledgment that another being has passed through, and the Deeps continue, and for this moment, in this lattice, between the water and the vapor and the ringing of ten thousand anvils, you are not alone.
