The thing about being a Stiltworker is that you learn to stop seeing. You have to. If you let yourself perceive the full weight of it—the miles of rusted gantry beneath your feet, the forever-fall to the sump levels, the knowledge that the sun is a story and the sky is a ceiling of pipes—you'd freeze. You'd just stop. And freezing is death. The city eats anything that stops.

I learned that before I could walk. We all do. My mother was a pressure-lamp tender on Truss Level 47, and she taught me the catechism without ever saying it aloud: *Don't look down. Don't look up. Look at the work in front of you. The work doesn't change. Neither will you.*

I was twelve when they reassigned me to Lagoon Maintenance, which is a polite way of saying I drew the short straw in the quarterly labor lottery. Nobody volunteers for the Condensate Alley detail. The heat, the noise, the smell—a sweet, mineral reek that clings to your clothes for days—it's considered punishment duty, something you endure until the lottery spins again. The foreman, a gaunt woman named Tarn who'd lost half her fingers to a valve accident, handed me a clipboard and a brass hygrometer and pointed to the access ladder.

"Check the basins," she said. "Log the temperatures. Don't touch anything that glows. And if you see the Keeper, don't—" She paused, searching for the right word. "Don't interfere. It knows what it's doing better than you."

I didn't ask what the Keeper was. You learn not to ask questions, either. Questions are a kind of looking, and looking is dangerous. So I just nodded and climbed down.

The descent into the Alley is like sinking into a fever dream. The temperature rises maybe ten degrees for every thirty feet of ladder, and the humidity climbs with it, until you're breathing soup and your clothes are plastered to your skin. The sound changes too. Up on the gantries, the noise is chaotic—clanging, hissing, the arrhythmic bark of foremen, the groan of stressed metal. But as you go deeper, the industrial clamor fades behind a single, shimmering tone. The Squeaker drone. It starts as a mosquito whine at the edge of hearing, then swells into a full-body vibration, until by the time your boots hit the grating at the bottom, you can't remember what silence felt like. The drone isn't just sound anymore. It's the texture of the air. It's the taste on your tongue. It's a physical pressure, like standing at the bottom of an ocean of noise.

The first time I entered the Alley, I nearly panicked. My heart hammered against my ribs. My vision swam with bioluminescent blue from the mite clouds. I wanted to climb back up, to confess my cowardice and accept whatever punishment came. But I remembered my mother's lesson. *Don't look down. Don't look up. Look at the work.* So I took out my hygrometer and walked the grating path to the first lagoon.

That was when I saw it.

The Keeper was working about twenty feet away, its lower body submerged in a steaming basin while its fronds traced delicate patterns in the air. At first, my mind refused to assemble the parts into a whole. I saw the translucency, the slow clench of internal structures, the silver cilia catching the orange lamplight, and my brain screamed *wrong, wrong, not an animal, not a machine, not possible*. I backed up so fast I nearly fell into the next lagoon. My boot scraped against the grating, and the sound was alien in that place, a discordant rasp against the perfect drone.

The Keeper's fronds froze.

Then, with a liquid, rolling motion, it turned. It had no eyes, but I felt its attention land on me like a weight. The fronds oriented in my direction, and I could see them fluttering—sampling me, reading my chemical signature, measuring the terror-sweat that was pouring off my skin. I braced for something. Attack. Retreat. I didn't know what.

What happened instead was worse, in a way.

The Keeper lost interest. It didn't decide I was harmless—it decided I was irrelevant. Its fronds swiveled back toward the Squeaker colony on the nearest wall, and it resumed its slow, peristaltic glide along the basin's edge, humming its subsonic tuning note. I had been categorized and dismissed in less time than it takes to draw a breath. I was a momentary fluctuation in the Alley's equilibrium, and the Keeper had already corrected for me.

I should have been relieved. I wasn't. I was hollowed out by it, scraped empty by a loneliness so vast I couldn't find its edges. I'd spent my whole life surrounded by people—the press of bodies in the hab-blocks, the constant jostle of workers on the gantries—and I'd never felt more like an object. A tool among tools. But that was a human thing, a social thing, the natural friction of too many souls in too little space. This was different. The Keeper didn't ignore me out of malice or hierarchy. It ignored me because I was noise, and it was listening to music.

I did my work that shift. I checked the basins. I logged the temperatures. I did not touch anything that glowed. And when I climbed back up the ladder, the Squeaker drone fading behind me, I realized I was crying. Not from fear. From some other emotion I didn't have words for.

That was five years ago. The labor lottery never shifted me out of Lagoon Maintenance. I could have petitioned, but I didn't. I told myself it was because the work was steady, because I'd learned the routines, because Tarn trusted me now to handle the bacterial slurries without supervision. All of that was true. None of it was the reason.

The reason was the silence.

Not silence in the ordinary sense—the Alley is the loudest place in the Stiltworks, and the drone has given me a permanent ringing in my ears. But it's a silence of mind. When I'm down there, walking the grating paths with my instruments, I don't think about the lottery, or the hab-block, or the slow, grinding futility of a life spent tending machines that tend machines that tend nothing. I just exist, suspended in the chord. The drone sweeps through me and carries away all the loose, jagged pieces of myself. It's the closest thing to peace I've ever known.

And there's the Keeper.

I've spent hundreds of hours in its presence now. I know its routes, its rhythms, the way it pauses at the third lagoon every two hours to release its bacterial slurry, the way it hums a slightly different frequency when the factory output fluctuates and the water temperature shifts. I've watched it unspool its twelve-foot length across the walkway grating, segment by segment, moving with a patience that makes the word "slow" feel inadequate. I've seen it spread its fronds toward the hidden sky, toward the gantries and the pipes and the orange glow that has never, in my lifetime, been anything other than orange. I've asked myself, every time, what it's listening for.

And here is the secret, the thing I have never told Tarn or my mother or anyone, the thing I am not certain I should be writing down even now:

I am not irrelevant to it. Not anymore.

I don't know when the change happened. It was gradual, too subtle for me to notice until the pattern was already established. But at some point, the Keeper's fronds began to pause when I passed. Not the way they'd frozen that first day—no startled alertness, no chemical threat-assessment—but a different kind of pause. A recognition. I would walk my rounds, measuring the pH of the lagoon water, and I would feel those silver cilia brushing the air behind me, tasting the traces I left in the humid thickness. The Keeper was tracking me. Not following, not pursuing, just... noting. Mapping. Incorporating me into its sensory architecture the way it incorporated the heat shimmer and the condensation mineral-content and the billion-voiced drone.

A year ago, maybe two, it began to orient its body toward me when I entered the Alley. Just slightly. A subtle rotation of the primary segment, so that the frond-cluster was facing my direction even as the rest of it continued its work. Then, six months ago, it started humming when I was near. Not the tuning hum, the subsonic thrum that corrects the Squeakers' pitch. A softer note, higher in frequency, almost melodic. It only happened when I was within ten feet, and it only lasted for a few seconds, and it was unmistakably for me.

Yesterday, it touched me.

I was kneeling beside the fourth lagoon, replacing a corroded section of grating that had begun to sing off-key. My hands were full of tools and my attention was on the bolts, which had rusted faster than expected because the water chemistry had shifted slightly alkaline. (I should report that. I keep forgetting.) I didn't notice the Keeper approaching. I never do. It moves like condensation, like weeping, like something that was already there before you looked.

The first I knew of it was a cool, wet pressure against the back of my neck.

I went rigid. My tools clattered onto the grating. Every instinct I had screamed *danger*, but my body refused to move. The pressure was gentle—barely a caress—and it was one of the Keeper's fronds. The cilia were so fine I couldn't feel them individually; they registered as a single, silken brushstroke, raising goosebumps on my skin. The frond traced a slow path from my hairline to my shoulder, and I could feel it sampling me, tasting the salt and the heat and the electrical activity of my nervous system. It was reading me. Not the way it read the lagoons or the Squeaker guano—not as a chemical signature to be calibrated and corrected—but as a text. A message. A voice it had been waiting a very long time to hear.

The touch lasted maybe ten seconds. Then the frond withdrew, coiling back into the sensory cluster with that same patient, underwater grace, and the Keeper continued on its route. I knelt there, trembling, until my knees ached and the grating had hummed through three full resonant cycles. My neck tingled for hours afterward.

I couldn't sleep that night. I lay in my bunk in the hab-block, listening to the distant clang of the factories and the nearer sound of my roommates breathing, and I tried to understand what had happened. The Keeper, I had always believed, was a biological machine. A tool, like me. It maintained equilibrium. It corrected deviations. It had no desires, no preferences, no interior life—just function and form, perfectly married in a closed-loop world where nothing changed and nothing was supposed to.

But it had touched me. And the touch hadn't been functional. It hadn't been maintenance. It had been *curiosity*. The Keeper had encountered something it did not understand—something outside the eternal chord, outside the mineral cycles and the heat gradients and the precisely calibrated chemistry of the lagoons—and instead of dismissing it as irrelevant, instead of correcting for it and moving on, the Keeper had paused. It had reached out. It had wondered.

Which meant the Keeper was capable of wonder.

And that meant the equilibrium wasn't perfect. It had a crack in it. A tiny, hairline fracture shaped like a girl with a hygrometer who came down a ladder five years ago and never really left.

Today I'm going back. The shift starts in an hour. I'll take my clipboard and my instruments, and I'll climb down into the drone and the steam and the blue pulse of the mites, and I'll walk the grating paths to the lagoons. The Keeper will be there, as it always is. It will be humming its tuning note, as it always does. But when I approach, it will pause. It will orient its fronds toward me. It will hum that softer note, the one it hums for nobody else, the one I have come to think of as my name in its language.

And maybe, if I'm brave enough, I'll reach out and touch it back.

I don't know what I'm doing. I don't know if the Keeper is a friend, or a curiosity, or something as alien to me as I must be to it. I don't know if that single crack in the equilibrium is the beginning of a door or the first symptom of a collapse. I only know that for the first time in my life, I want to *look*—truly look, without flinching, without the protective blindness that has kept my people alive for generations. I want to see the Condensate Alley as the Keeper sees it. As a lattice. As a voice. As a chord.

And I want to add my note to it, and hear what happens next.
