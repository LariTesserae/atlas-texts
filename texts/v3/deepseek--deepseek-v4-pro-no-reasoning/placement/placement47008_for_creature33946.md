He has not moved. You have been watching him long enough now that the bladder has inflated to bursting, released its fluid, and been reabsorbed—the whole cycle completing itself in the time it takes a man to draw and release a single slow breath. He did not rise to drink from it. This one was not for him.

You begin to understand something about the old man's stillness. It is not the stillness of a predator waiting to strike, nor the stillness of something dead or dying. It is the stillness of a stone in a river, around which the water has been flowing for so long that the stone and the current have reached an agreement. The plant roars and tears itself apart and rebuilds itself, and he sits at the center of it like a fixed point, a datum, the one thing in this churning world that was not extruded from a pore in the floor. He was born. He was born from a woman who was born from a woman who helped stack some of these stones now being digested by the mat, and that lineage, that chain of mothers, is a kind of architecture that the plant cannot replicate. It can make six-legged lizards by the thousands. It can make men-shaped things, perhaps, if it ever decided to. But it cannot make a man who remembers his grandmother's face in the grain of a stone wall. It cannot make someone who waits for something that does not yet exist.

The old man shifts. The movement is so slight you almost miss it—a change in the angle of his head, a quarter-inch tilt, as if he has heard something in the roar that is different from the roar he has been hearing for seventy years. His pale eyes move. They do not move far. They move exactly the distance required to meet yours.

He has known you were here. Of course he has. There is nothing in this place that does not belong, and a new thing, a thing that is not plant and not ephemeral fauna and not the old man himself, is as loud to his senses as a scream in a library would be to yours. He did not react because reaction implies surprise, and he is no longer capable of surprise. Every possible thing that could happen in this chasm has already happened in some form—the bladders inflate or they do not, the faunal things scurry or they die, visitors arrive or they do not. You are simply the current shape of a category that has always existed for him: *something that is not me, something that is not the plant, something that might speak if it had the voice to speak.*

He does not stand. He does not gesture. But something in the set of his shoulders—the way a held note can shift its tone without changing its pitch—indicates that he is now waiting for you.

You move toward him across the living floor. It is not easy. The mat undulates constantly, not enough to trip you but enough to make every step a negotiation. The small faunal things scatter at your approach, some of them bursting into brief, frantic flight, others simply collapsing into the tissue from which they came as if your presence triggers some programmed self-destruct. The sound presses against you, a physical weight, and you wonder how he can hear anything at all, how his pale eyes found you in the amber murk, how he can exist in this place without being driven mad by the unrelenting noise of a world screaming its own creation.

When you are close enough to touch him—though you do not touch him—he does something you do not expect.

He speaks.

You cannot hear him, not at first. The roar swallows his voice the way the mat swallows the burst fluid. But you see his lips move, shaping not the silent syllable of his own name but something longer, something directed outward. He is asking you a question. You cannot hear the words, but you can see the shape of them. He is speaking slowly, deliberately, with the patience of a man who has not had a conversation in decades and is willing to wait for the reply however long it takes.

You lean closer. The sound is still deafening, but near his body, in the small depression where he sits, there is an acoustic anomaly—a pocket of relative quiet, as if the plant has grown around him in such a way that it dampens the noise, or as if his own stillness has somehow taught the air itself to be still. It is not silence. But within this narrow radius, you can hear.

"Are you the one?" he asks.

His voice is a ruin. It cracks on the first word and rasps through the rest, the vocal cords of a man who has used them so rarely that they have half-forgotten their function. The sound is dry, granular, like stones rubbing together in a landslide. There is no emotion in it that you can identify—not hope, not desperation, not anger. It is a factual inquiry. He is asking if you are the thing he has been waiting for, the thing the tooth around his neck reminds him of, the thing the plant has not yet managed to produce. He is asking if you have arrived to answer a question that he has not yet spoken aloud.

You do not know what to say. You do not know if you *are* the one, or what "the one" would even mean in a place like this. You open your mouth, and the sound that comes out is swallowed immediately, lost in the roar, and you realize that whatever you say, he will have to read it on your lips the way you read his question on his.

His pale eyes study your face. The tooth on its cord swings slightly as he leans forward, the only unstill thing about him. The air shimmers between you, hot and wet and electric, and in the deep maroon shadows behind him, a six-legged lizard pulls itself from the floor, runs three steps, and dies. The cycle continues. The world builds itself. The old man waits.
