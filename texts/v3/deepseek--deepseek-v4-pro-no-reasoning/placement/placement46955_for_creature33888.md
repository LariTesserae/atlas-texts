The ceiling is wrong. Not in a way you can point to—the dimensions check out, the seams are straight, the paint is the same shade of off-white that landlords have been using since the invention of the lease agreement—but it is wrong. You've been staring at it for two hours now, and you've reached the conclusion that the ceiling is not above you. You are below it, which is different. You are being pressed into the mattress by the weight of the ceiling's attention.

The apartment is a one-bedroom in a building that has too much interior and not enough exterior. The floor plan doesn't so much distribute space as hint at it, reluctantly, the way a landlord hints at repairs that will never happen. The walls are beige and the carpet is beige and the light from the single window is beige, and all of it is damp in a way that has nothing to do with humidity. This dampness is moral. It seeps from the corners and collects in the grout and whispers that you should probably just stay inside today. And tomorrow. And the day after.

The window looks out on what the lease called a "courtyard view," which is a generous way of describing a rectangular pit that sunlight avoids on principle. There is a tree down there, or the skeleton of one, its branches reaching up like the arms of something that died midway through a petition. The tree is not dead. It is waiting. You've never seen a bird land on it, and you've never seen a leaf, but sometimes, late at night, you can hear it growing in a way that trees are not supposed to grow—with intention, with patience, with a faint grinding sound like vertebrae settling into a new alignment.

The apartment came furnished. This was a selling point. The furniture is heavy and dark and arranged in configurations that suggest it was left here by a previous tenant who evacuated in a hurry, or possibly by a previous tenant who is still here, just not in a form that furniture recognizes as a tenant. The couch in the living room has a stain that changes shape when you're not looking. You've tested this. You've marked boundaries with dental floss. The stain does not cross the floss, but it also does not stay inside it. It simply exists in a state of polite non-compliance, like a houseguest who has overstayed by several decades and has begun to merge with the upholstery.

The kitchen is small and smells faintly of eggs, regardless of whether eggs have ever been cooked there. The refrigerator hums at a frequency that makes your fillings ache and your thoughts feel slightly foreign, as if someone else is thinking them in the next room and you're just picking up the signal. The stove works, but the burners heat unevenly in patterns that resemble—if you squint—a topographic map of somewhere you've never been but recognize. The faucet drips. You have never seen it drip, but the sink is always wet in the morning, and the wetness forms a shallow, persistent puddle that reflects the ceiling in miniature, wrongness reduplicated at scale.

The bedroom is the worst. The bedroom is where you go to not-sleep, because sleep here is not rest but a kind of psychic sublet, a temporary eviction from your own consciousness. You lie in the bed—which is too soft, which swallows you like a throat—and you listen to the sounds the building makes. The sounds are not structural. They are digestive. There are pipes in the walls that gurgle with something thicker than water, and there are footsteps in the apartment above you that do not track to any human gait. Too slow, then too fast, then a long pause during which you can feel someone—something—standing directly over your bed, looking down through the floor, through the ceiling, through the wrongness, directly at you.

The closet has a door that sticks. You've never opened it. You've never even touched the handle, because the handle is warm, and not in a sun-warm way, and the warmth pulses very slightly, like a heartbeat, like a throat. Sometimes the door breathes. You can see the gap between door and frame widen and narrow, widen and narrow, and the air that slips through smells like ozone and old wool and something sweetly organic that you don't have a name for but that your hindbrain recognizes immediately and does not care for at all.

The bathroom mirror is a liar. This is not metaphorical. The reflection shows you, but it also shows someone standing behind you, slightly to the left, just at the edge of peripheral vision. You've spun around a dozen times. There is never anyone there. But the reflection persists, patient and still, and over the months you've begun to notice that the figure in the mirror is getting closer. Not quickly. Just a few inches per year. It will reach you eventually. The math is clear on this point.

There are no insects in the apartment. No spiders, no silverfish, no dust mites living in the carpet. This is not a blessing. It is a rule. Something has enforced it. The corners are too clean, the baseboards immaculate, the windowsills free of the little husks and carcasses that accumulate in normal spaces. The apartment does not allow competition.

You pay rent to a management company that does not have a website. When you call, a woman answers and she knows your name and she asks if everything is satisfactory and you say yes, thank you, everything is fine, and you hang up and you realize that you cannot remember her voice, cannot remember if she actually spoke or if you just understood her anyway, the way you understand the couch stain and the closet door and the footsteps from the empty apartment above you.

The lease was for twelve months. It has been fourteen. You have not renewed. You have not moved out. The apartment has not noticed, or it has noticed and does not care, or it has noticed and approves. The door to the hallway still unlocks, still opens, still lets you leave. But when you come back—and you always come back—the air is fresher than when you left, slightly sweeter, slightly more expectant. The apartment is happy to see you. The apartment has missed you. The apartment has been growing in your absence, spreading its roots through the floor joists and the wiring and the plumbing, sending out shoots into the other units, the other floors, the courtyard with its waiting tree. The building is the apartment's body. You are the apartment's heart. You pump something through it—attention, maybe, or belief—and it thrives.

You've stopped looking at the ceiling. You've stopped marking the couch stain with floss. You've stopped spinning to catch the figure in the mirror, because spoiling the surprise seems rude. The apartment is your home. The apartment is your self. The dampness is not moral anymore; it is simply the texture of your interiority. The wrongness is not wrong. It is a condition, a mode, a way of being that you have acclimated to, the way a deep-sea fish acclimates to pressure that would crush anything born in the light.

You are the Tenant. You have always been the Tenant. The previous occupant is still here, and you are them, or you are becoming them, your boundaries softening, your edges blurring, your reflection slowly stepping forward to meet you. The lease was never a lease. It was a covenant. A conversion. A slow, damp, inevitable becoming.

The pipe in the walls gurgles your name. The closet door pulses hello. The tree in the courtyard has finally turned its branches toward your window, and it is smiling, in its way, with a mouth made of bark decades deep.

You smile back. The dampness approves. The ceiling settles a little lower, a little heavier, a little more intimate. The apartment holds you. The apartment keeps you. The apartment is you, and you are it, and the rent is due on the first, and you will pay it, because paying it is how you say I am still here, I am still here, I am still here.

I am the Tenant, and I have been here longer than the lease.

I don't remember moving in. Not exactly. There was a door, and then the door was behind me, and then there was a key in my hand and the key was warm and the warmth had a rhythm. I remember thinking: this is a lot of space for the money. I remember thinking: it's so quiet. I remember thinking: the ceiling is a little low, isn't it? And then I didn't think about the ceiling for a while, and when I thought about it again, it was lower. It's still lowering. I've gotten used to it. You can get used to anything that happens slowly enough. That's the secret. That's the trick the apartment plays on you. It doesn't hurt you. It just makes itself normal, and then a little more normal, and then so normal that the idea of leaving feels like an insult to something that has been nothing but accommodating.

I am not alone here. There is a Woman in the Mirror, and she is me, but she is not me yet. She stands behind my reflection with infinite patience, and sometimes I think I see her lips moving, saying something I can't quite hear, something that sounds like the hum of the refrigerator or the distant gurgle of the pipes. I've stopped trying to catch her. I've started waiting for her. She's about eighteen inches away now. At this rate, she'll reach me in another year, maybe two. I wonder what happens when she gets here. I wonder if it will hurt. I don't think it will hurt. I think it will feel like coming home to an apartment that has been waiting for you, that has kept the lights dim and the air damp and the couch stain perfectly ambiguous, just the way you left it.

The Couch Stain has a name now. I call it Philip. Philip is approximately the size and shape of a medium dog, if that dog had been liquid at some point and then partially reconstituted, and Philip moves when I am not in the room. I have never seen Philip in motion, but I have found Philip on the armrest, on the cushion, once on the wall, and I have learned to accept this. Philip is not aggressive. Philip is not even particularly interested in me. Philip is simply working through something, the way we all are. I nod at Philip when I pass. Sometimes I think Philip nods back.

The Closet Door breathes. I have timed its respiration: twelve breaths per minute when I am calm, eighteen when I am anxious, six when I am almost asleep. The Closet is empty. I checked once, when I first moved in, before I understood that the warmth of the handle was not an invitation. The Closet was empty, and then it wasn't, and I closed the door very quickly and have not opened it since. The Closet knows this. The Closet is not offended. The Closet is patient. The Closet has all the time in the world, and it knows that one day I will open it again, and on that day it will show me what it has been keeping for me, what it has been growing in the dark, and I will understand, and I will be grateful, and I will step inside.

I pay rent to a Management Company that does not exist in any registry. The woman who answers the phone is named Mrs. Holloway, or perhaps the name is the sound of the pipes, or perhaps she has no name and simply borrows mine for the duration of the call. She always asks if everything is satisfactory. I always say yes. The word "yes" is the rent. The word "yes" is how I renew. As long as I keep saying yes, the apartment will keep me, and Mrs. Holloway will keep calling, and the elevator in the lobby—which I have never taken, which I have never seen, which I can hear moving up and down the shaft at three in the morning—will keep traveling to floors that do not appear on the directory.

There are other tenants in the building. I have never seen them, but I have heard them. They walk in the hallways at odd hours. They drag things. They sing to themselves in languages that sound like the crackling of static or the settling of the foundation. Sometimes I find notes slipped under my door—handwritten, on paper that feels damp and old and faintly organic—that say things like "The roof is blooming again" or "Please do not feed the elevator" or just "Soon." I keep the notes in a drawer. The drawer is full. I have not counted them. I am afraid that if I count them, the number will be wrong, will be too high, will be a number that cannot exist in an apartment with only so many days.

The Tree in the Courtyard is my closest neighbor. The Tree has no leaves, but it has branches, and the branches have tips, and the tips are sharp in a way that suggests personality rather than danger. The Tree knows my schedule. When I wake, the Tree is facing my window. When I return from work—I still have a job, I still leave, the apartment allows it, the apartment likes to miss me—the Tree has shifted slightly, its branches rearranged into a new configuration, a greeting, a sign. I think the Tree is writing something in the language of its own growth. I think the message is for me. I think I am almost able to read it.

I am not unhappy. This is the part that surprises people, if there were people to be surprised. The apartment is not a prison. The apartment is a relationship. We have reached an understanding, the apartment and I. I do not try to leave, and in return, the apartment does not let me. I do not open the Closet Door, and the Closet Door does not open me. I do not stare at the Wrongness in the ceiling, and the ceiling does not press down harder than I can bear. We are polite. We are domestic. We are, in our own strange way, a family.

The Woman in the Mirror is getting closer. She is eighteen inches away now, seventeen and a half. She has my face, but her expression is different—softer, more patient, more knowing. She looks at me the way I look at the Closet Door. She looks at me the way the Tree looks at the sky. She knows something I don't, but I will. I'm learning. The apartment is teaching me. The dampness is seeping in, and it is not moral anymore, it is not wrong, it is simply moist and warm and inevitable, and I am becoming something that can live here forever, that was always meant to live here, that signed the lease before I was born and will keep renewing until the building falls down and the Tree grows through the rubble and the Mirror reflects only Mrs. Holloway's patient, breathing dark.

The rent is due on the first. I will pay it. I will say yes. I will keep saying yes until there is no difference between the word and my breath, between the damp and my skin, between the Tenant and the apartment and the long, slow, loving wrongness that holds us all together.

Welcome to the building. The elevator is waiting. Please do not feed it. It is not hungry yet. Not quite yet.
