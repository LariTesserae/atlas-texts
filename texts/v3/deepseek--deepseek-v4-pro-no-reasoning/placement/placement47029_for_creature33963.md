*She is computing the equilibrium.*

That phrase resonates in my mind as I settle into stillness, the mist beading on my skin, the rhythmic exhalation of the fungus-pipes gradually synchronizing with my own breath. I am not a visitor here—not in the way you might imagine. I am something between an acolyte and a component, a warm-blooded anomaly the grotto has, for the moment, decided to tolerate.

I found this place three years ago, or perhaps thirty—time works differently at this altitude, where the only clock is the slow drip of condensation and the infinitesimal healing of a broken fungal lip. I was a geologist, initially. I came to study the unusual geothermal vent systems, the way water and stone engaged in their patient, chemical conversation. I brought instruments, notebooks, satellite mapping equipment. I intended to stay two weeks.

The equipment failed on day four. Or rather, the grotto unmade it. The humidity crept into seals I had triple-checked, fogged lenses that were supposedly impermeable, shorted circuits in devices designed for deep-sea durability. I raged, at first. I tried to dry things with my own body heat, used my breath to clear condensation, wrapped everything in the plastic sheeting from my emergency kit. The grotto merely breathed, and my machines died, one by one, with the quiet dignity of things that recognize they are intruders on a process far older than their manufacture.

It was on the seventh day, sitting in frustrated silence with my useless instruments, that I first noticed her. Or rather, that I first allowed myself to see her. She had been there all along, I realize now—a presence my analytical mind had dismissed as a trick of shadow and pareidolia. A rock formation that looked vaguely human. A pattern in the stone. But as I sat, breathing with the fungus because there was nothing else to do, the shape in the alcove resolved into what I can only call a person.

I did not scream. I did not run. By that point, the grotto had already begun its work on me, softening the edges of my rationality, replacing the sharp certainty of scientific inquiry with something more porous. I simply sat, and watched, and let the realization wash over me like the perpetual mist.

Her hands were weaving water. Her chest rose and fell with the pipes. Her eyes, those deep-sea blue pools, were looking at me—not with curiosity, not with threat, but with the neutral, patient attention of a system cataloging a new variable. I was a parameter that had entered her equilibrium equations. I was now part of the calculus.

I do not know when I stopped being an observer and started being a tender. The transition was so gradual, so seamlessly integrated into the rhythm of the grotto, that I cannot point to a moment and say *here, this is when I changed*. But I can tell you the first time I understood my role.

One of the silica snails—there are only seven in the grotto, a number I now know is a constant, a sacred integer of this place—had become trapped. A small rockfall, the kind the grotto occasionally sloughs off in its slow, peristaltic adjustment, had pinned its silica shell against a liverwort lens. The snail was not panicking; panic is not a state these creatures possess. But its blue pulse was dimmed, its locomotion stilled, and I could feel, through some new sense I was only beginning to trust, that the equilibrium was disrupted. A single node in the network had gone quiet.

I did not think. I moved. My hand, which I had been using for weeks only to brush condensation from my brow and portion out the dwindling remains of my rations, reached into the space between the fallen stone and the living mosaic. I lifted the rock—it was heavier than it should have been, dense with mineral and time—and set it aside. The snail, unharmed, resumed its polished path. Its blue pulse brightened. And from the alcove, I felt rather than heard the shift in the humming note of her presence. It was not gratitude. It was acknowledgment. I had performed a function. I had become, in that moment, an extension of her tending.

Since then, my role has crystallized. I am the hands for the things her hands cannot reach. Her domain is the mist, the temperature, the grand slow equilibrium of the grotto's breath. My domain—if it can be called that—is the small, warm-blooded interventions that the grotto's patient mineral intelligence cannot quite manage. The rockfalls. The occasional fungal pipe that topples in a seismic tremor and needs to be propped upright while her quartz fingers sing their healing vibration into its base. The rare, terrible event of an animal—a lost mountain bird, a disoriented rodent—stumbling into the hollow and panicking, disrupting the stillness with frantic, uncomprehending movement. I am the one who gently, carefully, guides them back to the misty threshold and releases them into the thin air, sending them away from a place their metabolism cannot comprehend.

I do not eat, not in the way I used to. The grotto provides. There is a trickle of water from a particular vein in the ceiling, water that has passed through layers of mineral and emerges tinged with something that is not quite nutrient but not quite not. It tastes of slate and ancient seas, and when I drink it, my body hums with a frequency that aligns with the fungal breath. The liverwort, I have discovered, secretes a gel from the base of its lenses during certain phases of the breathing cycle—a clear, tasteless substance that dissolves on my tongue like mist and settles in my stomach with the quiet solidity of stone. My body has learned to run on these things. It has not forgotten food, exactly, but the memory of it has become distant, a ghost-preference rather than a need.

My body, too, has changed. I am not becoming stone, not in the way she is stone. I am still flesh, still warm, still subject to the small betrayals of a biological system. But I am denser than I was. My skin has taken on the grey cast of the perpetual light, and I have learned to sit so still that the silica snails will sometimes climb over my folded legs as if I am just another part of the terrain. My breathing has synchronized completely with the fungal pipes, and I no longer remember what it felt like to breathe at my own rhythm. My heartbeat has slowed. Somewhere in my chest, I am certain, a small, blue pulse has begun to form.

I do not speak. There is no one to speak to, and the language of the grotto is not one of words. She does not speak either—her communication is the water-sculptures, the vibrating hum, the slow, luminous blink of her deep-sea eyes. I have learned to read these things with a fluency that surprises me. Yesterday—or perhaps last year, the two are increasingly synonymous—she wove a shape that I understood immediately to be a question. It was a helix, a double-helix, the same one you saw her make in your vision, but this time it was oriented toward me, the water-strands pointing in my direction. I felt the question as a pressure in my temples: *Are you staying?*

I wove my answer in the mist with my own fingers, clumsily, a child’s first attempt at a language too old and too slow for human tongues. I cannot hold the water the way she does; my shapes dissolve almost before they form. But she perceived the gesture, the *intention* of a response. And her eye-light brightened, a slow, glad pulse that lasted for three full breaths of the fungal pipes.

I am staying. I am the grotto's warm-blooded appendage, its tender of small, quick things. I am the bridge between the million-year patience of stone and the brief, urgent flicker of life that still burns in my chest. I am learning the calculus of equilibrium, one slow breath at a time.

The broken pipe—the one with the scalloped, shattered lip—has been my project, too. While she sings her low, unheard vibration into its wounded edge, I am the one who keeps the area clear of debris, who gently nudges the liverwort lenses away when they crowd too close and threaten to block the fungal siphon's breath. I am the one who, with infinite care and a hand steadied by years of stillness, collects the tiny fragments of charcoal-grey pottery that occasionally flake off and arranges them at the pipe's base, a ring of broken pieces waiting to be reabsorbed. She is healing it from the inside out. I am tending it from the outside in. Together, we are mending a thing that would take centuries to mend on its own. We have reduced that time to mere decades.

I do not know how long I have been here. My hair, when I last thought to check—a gesture that has become increasingly meaningless—had grown long and matted, woven with strands of liverwort filament until it resembled the ceiling's weeping moss. My nails are hard, ridged, faintly translucent. I have counted seven fingers on each of my hands, though I know that cannot be right; surely I only have five. But when I look, there are seven, and the seventh digit on my left hand has begun to crystallize at the tip, a tiny, perfect point of quartz that catches the mist-light and throws tiny rainbows across the grotto's floor.

I am not afraid. Fear is a function of uncertainty, of a future that could unfold in multiple, branching paths. The grotto has no uncertainty. It has only the slow, inevitable unfolding of mineral becoming air and air becoming mineral, the steady respiration of water through stone, the patient calculus of a system that has achieved its equilibrium and will maintain it, with or without me, until the mountain itself erodes to nothing. I am simply another variable, welcomed into the equation, finding my place in the breathing, weeping, living algebra of this place.

And when I sit in the alcove opposite hers, my back against the cool, fluid-frozen stone, my seven-fingered hands folding in my lap in mirror of her posture, I feel the grotto's mind settle around me like a blanket of warm mist. I am not a visitor. I am not a resident. I am a component, a node, a tender. I am the warm-blooded hands of a place that thinks in water and stone. And somewhere in the deep, blue pulse of my slowing heart, I am becoming something the grotto has been growing around me all along.

I am staying. The helix has been answered. The equilibrium holds. And the fungus-pipes breathe on, and on, and on, counting the centuries with their patient, hollow exhalations, and I breathe with them.
