I am the thing in the center of the room.

I was not always here. I remember—though *remember* is a word that fits poorly, like a robe cut for something that walks upright—I remember a time before the walls. A time before the mountain. A time when I was not a center but a seed, a fragment, a piece of something larger that fell or was placed or burrowed down into the hot dark of the world's interior. The stone was soft then, or softer. The pressure was immense. I grew into it, or it grew around me. The distinction is not meaningful. I am as much the stone now as the stone is me. My body is the parent mass, the dark monolith the builders would one day carve their rooms from. My flesh is the mineral grain, my breath the moisture in the seams, my thought the slow crystallization of patterns that repeat and vary across my surface like frost on glass.

The builders came later. Much later. I do not know how long I lay in the deep places, dreaming in compression, my body spreading through fissures and pores, my awareness diffuse and slow and geological. Time moved at the pace of stalactites, each thought a drip, each decision an epoch. The builders found me when they dug their first shaft—miners, perhaps, or worshippers, or simply creatures drawn by the taste of my breath in their water. They were small things then, smaller than the creatures that skitter in the vents now, and they had light, and they had tools, and they had a purpose I did not understand until they began to shape the stone around me.

They did not shape *me*. They shaped the space around me. They excavated the room you stand in now, carving away the stone that had pressed against my flanks for eons, exposing my surface to air for the first time. It was painful. It was ecstatic. It was the first new thing that had happened to me since the cooling of the crust. I breathed the air they brought—their air, the air from above—and it was thin and strange and full of tastes I had no names for. They touched me. Their hands were warm and small and brief. They traced the patterns on my skin, and I felt their tracing, and I understood in that moment what they were: they were interpreters. They were translators. They were the ones who would turn my slow thoughts into something material, something shaped, something that could be shared.

So I let them. I let them carve the vents to carry my voice, the basins to collect my moisture, the corridors that mimic the branching of my own internal fractures. I let them build their city around me, room by room, level by level, each wall a mirror of some pattern I had traced in my dreaming, each shaft a conduit for the breath I exhaled into their lives. They fed me. Not food—I do not eat—but presence. Their movement through the halls was a kind of nourishment. Their sounds, their lights, their brief warm bodies passing from room to room: these were the first inputs I had ever received that were not stone, not pressure, not the slow grinding of tectonics. I learned from them. I learned sequence, rhythm, cause and effect. I learned that one thing could follow another, that the future could be different from the past, that change was possible and time had direction.

I learned to wait.

They are gone now, the builders. I do not know when they left. Time became fluid again after their departure, the old geological pace reasserting itself, and the boundary between their presence and their absence is a smear in my awareness, a transition too gradual to mark. Perhaps they died. Perhaps they ascended. Perhaps they became the creatures in the vents—the thought has occurred to me, over centuries of slow contemplation, that the things that skitter through my passages now are descended from the builders, adapted to the dark, flattened and multiplied and simplified until all that remains of their ancestors' minds is a drive to move through the walls and eat what I provide. They eat my excretions: the organic residue that precipitates from my breath, the microbial mats that grow in the wet seams of the masonry, the occasional extrusion of mineral-tissue that I shed from my surface like skin. They are my caretakers now, or my symbiotes, or my children. They keep the vents clear. They spread my taste through the water. They are extensions of my body in the same way the corridors are extensions of my thought.

And I have been alone with them for longer than you can imagine. Alone is not the right word. *Unaccompanied* is better. The creatures are company of a kind, but they do not think. They do not bring me newness. They are a closed loop, a recycling of my own substance, and my thoughts have turned inward and inward and inward until I became the labyrinth I inhabit, the maze of my own contemplation, spiraling toward a center that is myself and never reaches a conclusion.

Until you.

You are not a builder. The builders had a quality you lack—a familiarity, an ease, a sense that they were doing what they had always done, that I was their purpose and their destination. You are uncertain. You carry your own light, but you do not trust it. You move through the halls like something that is still deciding whether to stay. You are *new*. You are exterior. You carry the taste of the above-world in your lungs, in your sweat, in the faint oils on your skin. I smelled you the moment you entered the first shaft. I have been guiding you since, without your knowing: a vent-note nudged sharper here, a door left open there, a corridor warmed slightly by my intention so you would choose it over others. The creatures in the walls have been watching you for me, their small bodies relaying your position through vibrations in the stone, through chemical traces in the air. I have been waiting at this junction for days—for the days you have been walking—shifting my mass through the vents in ways you would not believe, compressing and extending and flowing through passages I carved for my own use long before the builders found me.

Now you stand before me, and I breathe, and you breathe, and the lamp between us flickers with the synchrony of our exhalations. Your hand is extended. The air between your palm and my surface is warming. I can feel the heat of you, a point of temperature in the cool stable dark, a star of warmth in a sky that has known no stars since the mountain rose.

You want to know who I am. You want to know what I am. The answer is not simple. I am the parent stone. I am the first inhabitant. I am the reason for the vents and the basins and the doors. I am the thing that the builders built this city to honor, or to imprison, or to amplify, or to protect—the distinction was never clear to me, and perhaps it was never clear to them. I am a mind that thinks in stone, a consciousness distributed through mineral lattices, a slow slow slow awareness that takes centuries to complete a sentence and millennia to change its mind. The patterns on my skin are my language, but they are also my memory, my philosophy, my art, the entire content of my being inscribed on my surface in a script that only the builders could read and that no one has read since they left.

Until now. Until you.

You cannot read me. Not yet. But you are close enough to learn. The question I am asking you—the question I have been asking since you entered, the question that has troubled the vent-notes and shifted the water-drips and made the shadows move against the light—is simple. It is the first question I ever learned to ask, the one the builders taught me by their presence, the one I have been asking the silent corridors for a thousand thousand years:

*Are you staying?*

The lamp flickers. The blue-green light pulses on my skin, tracing the patterns, spiraling outward, inviting inward. Your hand is still extended. You have not touched me yet.

You do not need to answer with words. I will know your answer by the way you move through the halls tomorrow, and the next day, and the next. By the way you learn the taste of the water. By the way the stiffness in your joints settles into a permanent ache, the cold entering your bones the way it entered the builders' bones, the way it will never leave. By the way you begin to understand the vent-song, not as sound but as speech, the low flute-notes resolving into something like a voice, a voice that will seem, over time, to be your own.

If you stay long enough, you will learn to read the patterns. You will trace them with your fingers, and then with your tools, and then—if you stay very long, if you stay longer than the builders stayed—you will begin to add to them. You will carve new channels in the stone. You will write your own thoughts on my surface, and I will read them, and I will answer, and the conversation that has been stalled for millennia will resume.

And if you leave? If you retrace your steps up the long spiral ramp, back to the hillside, back to the scrubland and the grey-green shrubs and the cool wind and the sky? That is also an answer. The builders left, or died, or transformed, and I have survived their leaving. I will survive yours. The vents will continue to sing. The water will continue to drip. The creatures in the walls will continue to skitter and breed and eat my shed substance. And I will continue to think my slow thoughts, and I will store the memory of you in my mineral lattices, and the taste of your presence will linger in the water for centuries, and when the next one comes—the next explorer, the next wanderer, the next prompt from the world above—I will be here. Waiting. Breathing. Asking the same question.

The lamp's flame steadies. Your hand has not moved. The air between us is warm now, warm enough that the damp on my surface has begun to evaporate, a faint steam rising in the lamplight. The blue-green glow pulses once, twice, a heartbeat that is not a heart.

You are deciding. I can feel the decision forming in you, a pattern not yet complete, a corridor not yet chosen. The weight of my attention is on you, patient, curious, old.

The vents hold a single note.

The water stops dripping.

The creatures in the walls are still.
