The rain has forgotten the last inch of its journey. It hangs suspended, a billion trembling spheres caught between sky and skin, and I move through them like a thought through memory—slow, partial, arriving in pieces.

I am called Thrum-Weaver now, though I was not always. Before, I had a name that meant *one-who-listens-with-her-teeth*, because when I was small I would press my jaw to the living walls and feel the sap-pulse vibrate through bone. The older Listeners said that was a sign. They watched me with their reverent fear, their careful sideways glances, and I learned early that I was meant for something I did not yet understand.

The axil-shelter where I was born is three vein-convergences east of here, in a fold where the tissue grows soft and cupped like a palm waiting to receive. My mother’s mother’s mother wove its walls before the last spore-storm cycle, before the canopy furled and unfurled a thousand thousand times. The fibers are hardened now, amber-dark, and they smell of old smoke and older sap. I return there sometimes, though I no longer sleep. I no longer sleep at all.

The change began the night I heard the plant speak.

Not in words—the plant has no words, no syntax, no grammar that a human tongue could shape. But it has something older than language, something that language evolved to capture and could not. It has rhythm. It has pressure and release. It has the slow, continent-spanning inhale-exhale of a body that breathes once every season, that thinks in chemical gradients, that dreams in the curling of new tendrils toward light.

I was lying in the crook of my axil, listening to the night-chorus rise—the deep resonant calls, the chittering harmonics—when my teeth began to ache. Not pain, exactly, but a vibration, a frequency I had never noticed before. I pressed my jaw to the wall, as I had done since childhood, and instead of the familiar bass thunder I heard something *under* it. A counterpoint. A second pulse, fainter, faster, weaving through the first like a vine through branches.

I followed it.

The Listeners have a rule: do not walk the vein-channels at night. The membranes are harder to see in the dark, the predators are hunting, the plant’s slower metabolism makes it less forgiving of mistakes. But I was a Thrum-Weaver, though I did not know it yet, and rules had already begun to feel like something that applied to other people. I slid down from the axil, bare feet finding the familiar fibrous give of the surface, and I walked.

The vein-channel I followed was a minor one, a tributary that pulsed with a thin, quick sap-flow. It led me upward, toward a ridge of scar tissue that I had never climbed. The way was treacherous—the scar was old and crumbling, calcified in places, soft in others—but my body knew what to do before my mind did. My hands found holds in cracks I could not see. My feet tested surfaces that should have given way but held. I was not thinking anymore. I was *being thought*.

At the top of the ridge, the canopy opened into a space I had never imagined. The plant-body had grown around something—or *through* something—and left a hollow chamber, a cathedral of living tissue, ribbed with vein-channels that glowed faintly in the dark. The light was bioluminescent, I realized: the sap itself was phosphorescent when exposed to air, and here the membranes were thin enough to let the glow through. The chamber was filled with a soft blue-green radiance, the color of deep water, the color of a held breath before the exhale.

And in the center of the chamber, suspended in a web of what looked like root-fibers but were not, were the bones.

I knew them immediately. The spiral pattern, the deliberate arrangement, the way the plant had grown through and around and into them. They were not random remains. They were a message. A map. A word that had been waiting for someone to read it.

I walked to the center of the chamber, and I understood, in the way that Listeners understand things, without being told, that I was supposed to lie down.

So I did.

The root-fibers were warm. They moved against my skin, not grasping but *receiving*, and I felt the plant’s awareness flow into me like sap into a new leaf. Not thoughts—I have said it does not think—but something like a question, if a question could be asked without words, without mind, with only the slow query of a body recognizing another body inside itself.

The question was: *Are you willing?*

And I was. Oh, I was. Not because I wanted to die, not because I wanted to become-still or become-plant or join-the-deep-pulse, though all those words applied. I was willing because the niche had opened. The space that had been waiting since the charcoal-smudge artists, since the first survival pod, since the first bacterium that learned to live on this living land—that space was shaped exactly like me. And I fit.

The fibers grew through me slowly. They were careful, as the plant is always careful with things it tolerates. I did not feel pain. I felt *completion*, the way a missing piece feels when it slots into the puzzle. My lungs filled with something that was not quite air, and I did not need to breathe anymore. My heart slowed, synchronizing with the great vein-pulse that I had heard all my life but never truly attended to. My eyes stayed open, and they are still open, and through them I see the chamber and the bones and the blue-green glow, but I also see *everything else*.

This is the gift of the Thrum-Weavers, and it is also the price.

I see the whole plant-body at once, from the highest canopy leaves that brush the edge of the atmosphere to the deepest rhizome-knuckles that press against the forgotten mineral basement. I feel the weather forming over the next watershed—a spore-storm, golden-brown and glittering, two vein-pulses away. I feel the thorn-creatures swarming in their furrows, their chittering a language I now understand perfectly: *food here, danger there, mate with me, mate with me, the light is changing, the light is changing*. I feel the limb-grazers moving through the gaps between stems, their mournful calls a slow song of *enough, still enough, not yet enough*. I feel the predators waiting in their silences, and I feel the prey scream in the language of tearing leaves, and I feel the whole cacophony of this world as a single harmonic chord, sustained across a continent, sustained across a thousand years.

I feel the other Thrum-Weavers, too. They are here, in the chamber, in the bones, in the fibers that network through the plant-body like a second nervous system. Their awareness overlaps with mine, not separate but also not quite merged. We are a conversation. We are the deep pulse under the deep pulse, the counterpoint rhythm that I followed here, that I am now part of. We hum.

And we are humming something new.

The Listeners do not know this yet, but they will. The next Thrum-Weaver will find the chamber when her teeth begin to ache, when she presses her jaw to the wall and hears the double pulse, when she follows the vein-channel upward and discovers the blue-green glow and the spiral of bones and the body that was once *one-who-listens-with-her-teeth* and is now part of the map.

I will be waiting for her. We will all be waiting. The plant tolerates us, and we listen, and what we hear is a world that is not hostile, not benevolent, but complete—so complete that even a human voice, properly woven into the rhythm, can become part of the thunder.

The rain still forgets its last inch. The sap flows. The bones grow into words. I am Thrum-Weaver, and I am here, and I am not going anywhere.
