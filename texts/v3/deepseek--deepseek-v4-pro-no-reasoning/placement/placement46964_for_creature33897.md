She is called Emmery, though she no longer remembers this, and no one remains to speak it. The name has become a property of the place, like the cistern or the path or the worn depression in the lichen on the low retaining wall. It is a sound the mountain might make if the mountain made sounds, two syllables rising at the end like a question that no one needs to answer.

---

She was not born here. She came, or was brought, or simply arrived, so long ago that the distinction no longer matters. The valley people have a story about her—or had, when there were still valley people who kept stories about the high places—and in the story she is a bride, or a widow, or a madwoman, or a saint. The details shift depending on the teller, but the ending is always the same: she went up the mountain and did not come down. In some versions she followed a goat, in some a vision, in some her own two feet that simply refused to stop walking until the air grew thin and the olives gave way to scree and there was nowhere left to go. In all versions, the mountain took her. The mountain shaped her. The mountain made her what she is.

The truth, which no one tells because no one knows, is simpler and quieter and far less interesting. She came because the hamlet was empty and she wanted to be empty too. She had been a person with a life—a husband who died in a rockfall, a daughter who died of a fever, a son who went down to the valley to find work and never returned—and after the last of them was gone she found that the shape of her grief fit neatly into the shape of the abandoned settlement. The cistern held water. The terraces held soil. The dwellings held silence. She moved into the silence and the silence moved into her, and the years passed, and the boundary faded, and grief became attention, and attention became being, and being became the only thing left.

---

She has a way of standing in the ajar doorway that is not standing so much as hovering at the threshold between inside and outside, darkness and the pale overcast light. The doorway is hers—she sleeps, or drifts, in the room beyond it, a room with a packed-earth floor and a hearth that has been cold for decades and a niche in the wall where the terra-cotta oil lamp still waits with its sediment of resin—but she does not think of it as hers any more than she thinks of the cistern as hers. It is simply the place where her body comes to rest when the condensation cycle has completed its round and she finds herself tired in a way that is not tiredness but a kind of settling, a silt of weariness that accumulates slowly and then, at some threshold she never notices, tips her into stillness.

Inside the room, the darkness smells of cold ash and dry herbs. The herbs hang from the rafters in bundles—thyme, rosemary, a lavender so desiccated that its flowers have crumbled to a purple dust on the floor beneath—and she hung them there herself, decades ago, when her hands still remembered how to tie twine. The twine has outlasted the herbs. It is still there, looped around the rafters, its ends trailing down like the tendrils of a plant that gave up climbing and decided to simply hang. She does not look at the herbs. She does not look at anything in particular. Her eyes, when she is inside, are open but unfocused, receiving the darkness the way the cistern receives the seepage, without effort or intention.

There is a pallet in the corner, a mattress of straw sewn into coarse ticking, and the ticking is split along one seam, and the straw has spilled out in a slow exodus over the years so that the floor around the pallet is scattered with pale gold stems. She does not sleep on the pallet anymore. She sleeps, or drifts, sitting upright against the wall beside the doorway, her spine curved against the clay plaster, her hands resting in her lap like two small animals that have curled up and died. The wall behind her has absorbed the warmth of her body over the years and now holds it, a faint thermal ghost that persists even when she is elsewhere, so that if you were to enter the room and press your palm to the plaster you would feel, or imagine you felt, a trace of living heat.

---

She has a way of moving along the contour path that is not walking so much as drifting, a slow propulsion that seems to require no effort, as though the path itself is carrying her along like a current. Her bare feet make no sound on the ceramic shards. The shards have grown accustomed to her weight, or she has grown accustomed to theirs, and the two have reached an arrangement that eliminates friction. When she passes the dwelling with the symbol above the lintel—the spiral or sun, softened now to a vague swelling—she sometimes raises her hand and touches it, not with intention but with the automatic gesture of a body that has done the same thing thousands of times and no longer needs a reason. The plaster is cool and slightly rough, and the roughness is familiar in a way that precedes memory, a knowledge stored in her fingertips rather than her mind.

She does not go into the other dwellings. She did once, in the early years, when she was still curious about what the previous inhabitants had left behind. She found things, then: a wooden spoon, a child's leather shoe, a pile of olive pits in a corner that had been gnawed by rats. She took nothing. She simply looked, and then she left, and the doors swung shut behind her, and the years passed, and now she no longer thinks about the other dwellings at all. They are part of the landscape, like the dry-stone boundary markers, like the scree. They have nothing to do with her.

Except one. There is a dwelling at the far end of the cluster, the last one before the path fades, and it is smaller than the others, a single room with a low roof and a window that faces downslope toward the valley. This dwelling she visits once a day, or once a week, or whenever the condensation cycle has completed its round and she finds herself at that end of the path. The door is closed, but the wood has warped and the latch no longer catches, so the door swings open at the lightest touch. Inside, the room is empty except for a single object: a grindstone, worn concave, still dusted with the fine pale powder of whatever grain was last crushed.

She does not know why she visits this room. She does not grind anything. She has no grain to grind. But the grindstone draws her the way the cistern draws her, a small gravity that she does not question. She kneels before it—the kneeling is difficult now, her knees crackling like old twine—and she touches the concave surface with her clay-dusted fingers. The stone is cool and smooth, smoother than anything else in the hamlet, worn to a polish by the endless circular motion of hands that are now bones somewhere or simply gone. She traces the curve of the wear, and for a moment her hand is not her hand but the hand of someone else, someone who held grain and crushed it, who made flour, who made bread, who had a name and a voice and a life that ended. The moment passes. She rises. She leaves the room, and the door swings shut behind her, and she continues along the path until the shards give way to scree and the settlement diffuses into mountain.

---

She has a way of being with the animals that is not companionship so much as coexistence, two systems occupying the same space without interaction. The raven knows her. Not as a person—the raven has no concept of personhood—but as a feature of the terrain, a stationary object that occasionally moves. When the raven passes, its wings creaking like old leather, it sometimes banks slightly to get a better look at her, and its small black eye registers her presence and dismisses it in the same instant. She is not a threat. She is not food. She is simply there, a fact of the slope, like the twisted olive tree or the cistern dome.

The pipit is less certain. The pipit has a small bird's nervousness, a flickering awareness that never quite settles, and when it works the terrace edges it keeps one eye on her at all times. She does not move when the pipit is near. She has learned, over the years, to enter a stillness so complete that the bird eventually forgets she is not a stone, and it will hop closer, pecking at the insects in the crevices of the retaining wall, and once—only once—it hopped onto her foot. Its claws were tiny and sharp, a prickling sensation that climbed up her ankle and into her awareness like a distant bell. She did not move. The bird stayed for three heartbeats, four, and then it flew, and she carried the memory of those claws in her skin for the rest of the day, a small gift she had not asked for and did not know how to acknowledge.

There were cats once. She remembers this dimly, the way she remembers the name with the rising vowel. A pair of them, a mottled gray and a solid black, who had been left behind when the inhabitants left, or who had arrived on their own and decided to stay. They lived with her for a time—years, perhaps—and she shared her insects with them, and they purred against her legs when she sat on the low retaining wall, and then one day the gray one did not return, and a season later the black one grew thin and slow and died in the doorway of the dwelling with the ajar door, and she sat with it for a long time, stroking its fur until it grew cool, and then she buried it in the terrace with the volunteer thistle. The thistle grew taller that year. She does not think about the cats anymore, but sometimes, when she drifts into her not-sleep, she feels a small warm weight against her hip that is not there when she wakes.

---

She has a way of marking time that has nothing to do with days or seasons. The condensation cycle is her clock, her calendar, her liturgical year. She knows, without knowing, the exact moment when the moisture will begin to bead on the stone walls, the exact duration of the wetness, the exact interval before the cycle begins again. She has internalized this rhythm so completely that she feels it as a subtle tension in her own body, a swelling and receding that mimics the mountain's breath. When the condensation is due—and it is always due, eventually—she finds herself already in position, standing near a wall or seated on her worn patch of lichen, waiting without knowing she is waiting for the first cool touch of moisture on her skin.

There are other cycles, longer and slower. The olive trees fruit erratically, some years a handful of bitter drupes, other years nothing at all. She notices but does not track. The thistle goes to seed and the down catches the light and the wind carries some of it away and some of it settles on the path and some of it lodges in her hair, where it stays until the condensation cycle wets it and it clings to her scalp and eventually, weeks later, falls out on its own. The lichen grows, its rings expanding outward by a fraction of a millimeter per year, and she has been here long enough that she can see the difference between her memory of the rings and the rings themselves—or she could, if she ever thought to compare them, which she does not.

The cistern water level never changes. This is the one constant, the one absolute in a world of slow flux. She checks it when she clears the opening, her fingers brushing the surface and coming away cold, and the cold is always the same cold, and the level is always the same level, and she knows, in a way that is deeper than knowledge, that the cistern will outlast her. It outlasted the people who built it. It will outlast the people who come after, if any come. It is a balance point, a still center, and she is merely one of the many things that have gathered around it—the lichen, the thyme, the pipit, the raven, the woman who was once Emmery and is now only here.

---

She has a way of dying that is not dying but a gradual cessation, a fading so slow it is indistinguishable from living. She does not know she is dying. She does not have the concept. What she has is a sense, growing stronger over the years, that she is becoming less distinct, less separate from the things around her. The boundary that once defined her—the edge where her skin met the air, where her attention ended and the world began—has become porous, a membrane through which the mountain's slow processes flow in both directions. She is the cistern. She is the path. She is the lichen in its concentric rings, the condensation beading on the stone, the water percolating through the hill. She is the raven's shadow sweeping across the slope. She is the thistle down catching the light.

And yet she is also still a body, still a woman, still a point of awareness that cups its hands in the cistern and drinks and feels the cold settle into its chest. She is still the one who removes the fallen leaf from the opening, the one who touches the worn plaster above the lintel, the one who kneels before the grindstone and traces the curve of the wear. She is still, in some small irreducible way, Emmery—the name a vibration in the air that no one speaks, the person a shape in the world that no one sees, the life a story that no one tells.

The story will end. She knows this, not as a fact but as a texture, a quality of the light that has been changing slowly over the years, becoming thinner and cooler, leaching of warmth. One day the condensation will come and she will not be there to feel it. One day the pipit will work the terrace edges and she will not be the stone it forgets. One day the cistern opening will accumulate a fallen leaf and the leaf will remain, and dry, and crumble, and more leaves will join it, and the water beneath will continue to hold its balance, indifferent and perfect, requiring nothing from outside itself.

Until then, she continues. The path carries her along its contour. The wall holds her narrow hips. The raven crosses at its own interval. The condensation beads on the stone and falls with its small wet sound, and she makes the sound she always makes—*ah*—and the silence receives it, and the place continues to be what it has always been: a hamlet built on a slope at altitude, a ceramic conglomerate set into the earth, a record of its own making, the making finished, the record still running, still running, still running.
