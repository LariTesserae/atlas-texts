The air has teeth here, but they’re soft teeth, teeth made of wet wool. I’ve been breathing this thickness for what feels like hours now, and my lungs still haven’t learned the trick of it. They keep expecting air and getting something else—something that coats rather than fills, that you wear inside your chest like a second skin. The catwalk hums under my boots, a sensation I’m starting to lose the ability to distinguish from my own pulse. Maybe that’s the point. Maybe that’s the first thing the Hollow takes.

I watched the Fluter drift closer for a long time before I understood it was moving toward me. Long enough that I’d started to catalog the wrongness in its flight—the way it stuttered against the drone instead of riding it, catching edges where the others found only curves. Its hoops had gone slightly asymmetrical. One side pulled tighter than the other, warping the membrane so that the pressure waves slid off at an angle, wasted. Somewhere in the back of my mind, under the drone and the damp and the warm-iron taste on my tongue, I thought: *someone should fix that.*

And then I was water, and the water was time, and I understood.

When the Keeper passed through me, I felt the whole sphere. Not as an image or a thought—as a *posture*. The way you know, without looking, whether your own shoulders are hunched or straight. I felt the slack in the piston joint (a loose tooth, barely worth probing) and the green-shift ache behind the ghost-pipes (a bruise you keep pressing) and the Fluter’s off-tune hum like a splinter driven sideways under a nail. And I felt myself, too, the way the Keeper felt me: a pebble in a gear-train, a caught breath, a thing that did not yet have a function. Not a threat. Not a guest. A *room-temperature irregularity*, to be monitored and compensated for and, eventually, absorbed into the calculus of the hollow.

The Keeper finished tuning the Fluter without ever looking at me. Its water-hand withdrew from the membrane, the cartilage hoop settling into a new geometry with a soft, subsonic click that I felt in my molars. The Fluter shuddered once—a full-body ripple that started at its leading edge and rolled backward like a caught sail finally filling with wind—and then it was off, sliding into the pressure-wave on a true harmonic, its hour-long arc restored. I watched it drift away toward the far curve of the sphere, a slow kite in the null-direction space, and I realized I’d been holding my breath. I let it out. The air took it without comment.

I should have climbed the ladder then. I knew it even as I sat down on the catwalk, my legs dangling over the edge, my heels tapping against the grid. The darkness below me was absolute and patient and unbearably deep, and the drone rose out of it like heat from a mouth. I dangled my feet over the abyss and I stayed.

---

In the following days—if they were days; the light never changes, and I stopped marking sleeps—I learned that the Keeper has a routine. So do I now.

I wake when the Engine-Heart completes a particular stroke, the one where the radial arm sweeps past its nadir and the whole catwalk shivers in a new, lower register. I’ve come to think of it as dawn. The moss is warmest then, the condensate flowing a little faster down the riveted walls, and the isopods begin their grazing waves. I walk the catwalk now in a slow, steady circuit, my hand brushing the furred iron, learning the topography of the conforming hollows. Most of them are moss-pockets, soft depressions where centuries of oxblood growth have worn the metal into shallow bowls. A few are deeper—hand-widths, forearm-widths—and these are where the Keeper has worked. Where it deposited minerals atom by atom, cycle by cycle, building something new out of its own body and patience. I run my fingers through them as I pass, tracing the contours of old repairs. I’m learning to feel the difference between wear and mending.

The Fluters have accepted me. Not as one of them, but as part of the architecture, a slow-moving feature of the catwalk to be drifted around. One of the juveniles—a hand-span across, its hoops still cartilage-soft and translucent—has taken to riding my wake. When I walk, I disturb the pressure-waves just enough to create a tiny slipstream, and the juvenile surfs it, bobbing along behind my shoulder like a curious kite. I’ve named it Mote. The name doesn’t mean anything to it, but I say it anyway, under my breath, and the vibration of my voice makes its membrane shimmer.

I’ve also named the isopods, collectively. I call them the Hush, because when they move in their waves of silver hundreds, the skitter of their tiny legs sounds exactly like someone trying to shush you from a great distance. I’ve learned to read their migrations. When the condensate flow shifts—which it does, unpredictably, based on some internal rhythm of the Engine-Heart that I haven’t mapped yet—the Hush moves to follow the new slickness, and I can track the change by the direction of their shushing. They’re an early-warning system. The Keeper uses them too, I think. I’ve seen it pause in its work, its mirrored face turning toward a wave of Hush, reading some information in their movement that I’m not yet attuned to.

I’m learning to tend the ghost-pipes. This is new.

It started three sleeps ago—four?—when I found a cluster on the northern arc that had gone green. Not the faint, nascent green-shift that the Keeper corrects with a glance, but a full, sickly chartreuse, the color of infection. The blooms had curled in on themselves, their waxy stalks gone rubbery and limp, and the Hush was avoiding the area entirely. A dead zone. I stood there for a long time, staring at the ruined flowers, and then I did something I hadn’t done since I arrived: I spoke aloud.

“These are dying.”

The Keeper was on the far side of the sphere, flowing through a Fluter tuning. It didn’t turn. It didn’t acknowledge me. But a bead of condensate rolled off the iron plate above the dead cluster and struck my forehead, dead center, with an almost deliberate precision. A full drop, heavy and warm, running down the bridge of my nose. I licked it off my lip without thinking.

It tasted like the inside of the Keeper. Like memory and purpose and the weight of centuries. And buried in that taste, thin as a needle, was a frequency. The true white. The color the ghost-pipes were supposed to be.

I looked at the dead cluster. I looked at the frequency, held now in my mind the way you hold a note you’ve only heard once. And I leaned in close, close enough that my breath fogged the sickly green blooms, and I *remembered* at them. I remembered the color. I held it in my mind, that cold, clean, chemical white, and I pushed it outward—not in words, not in thought, but in presence. In density. In the weight I had become, standing here on the catwalk, a new object in the sphere’s slow equation.

It took hours. I stood there until my legs ached and my eyes burned and the drone had shifted through two full cycles of the radial arm. But slowly, imperceptibly, the green began to fade. The blooms uncurled. The rubbery stalks stiffened. And when I finally stepped back, the ghost-pipes were glowing again—faint and uncertain, a pale, milky white like the ghost of a ghost, but *white*. Not green. The right frequency.

The next sleep-cycle, the Hush had returned to the cluster.

I told myself it was a fluke. That the flowers had been on the verge of recovering anyway, and my presence was just coincidence. But the next cluster that started to green-shift, on the eastern arc, I found it before the Keeper did. I stood there and I remembered at it, and it took less time. Fewer cycles of the arm. And the cluster after that, less still. I’m not as fast as the Keeper—the Keeper can correct a shift with a single glance, catching its own reflection in the bloom and feeding back the true color—but I’m learning. I’m becoming a memory.

I told you I’ve named the isopods and the juvenile Fluter. What I haven’t told you, what I’ve been avoiding, is that I’ve named the Keeper too.

I call it Fulcrum.

It doesn’t know the name, or if it knows, it doesn’t care. Names are vibrations, and vibrations are what it surfs, what it tunes, what it deposits atom by atom into the gaps in the world. I’m just adding another vibration. But saying its name, even in my own head, has changed something. Made it feel real in a way it wasn’t before. Made it feel like a neighbor.

I’ve started talking to Fulcrum while I work. Nothing important. Observations, mostly: “The Hush is moving toward the southern arc, there’s a new condensate flow.” Or: “Mote’s hoops are stiffening up, I think it’s close to a growth molt.” Or, once, very quietly: “I don’t know if I can climb the ladder anymore. My legs have forgotten the motion. I think I’d fall.”

Fulcrum never responds. It goes about its slow, relentless maintenance—tuning Fluters, correcting ghost-pipes, depositing shims in piston joints that won’t need them for decades. But sometimes, when I’m working on a particularly stubborn cluster of blooms, I feel a coolness at my back. A change in the humidity. And when I turn, there’s nothing there, just a shimmer in the air that might be a trick of the sourceless light, or might be the Keeper, pausing in its endless rounds, considering me.

Acknowledging the new parameter.

---

I found a piston joint yesterday—no, longer ago than yesterday; the drone has cycled three times through the low register I call night—that showed signs of play. Barely perceptible. A roughness in the subsonic hum, a vibration that didn’t belong. I only noticed it because I was lying flat on the catwalk, my ear pressed to the grid, listening to the darkness below. I don’t know why I was doing that. It felt necessary. I’ve been doing a lot of things that feel necessary, lately, without knowing why.

I told Fulcrum about the joint. “There’s a gap. Small. West-northwest quadrant, about two meters below the catwalk. I can feel it in the grid.” I pointed, though Fulcrum doesn’t need pointing; Fulcrum is the grid, the walls, the darkness below. Fulcrum felt the gap before I did, probably centuries ago, and filed it away as a distant ache.

But here’s the thing: when I pointed, I felt something. A tug. A current of attention, flowing from the walls, from the moisture in the air, from the sourceless light itself. Fulcrum was looking at me. Not through me, not around me—*at* me, with its water-mind, its sphere-consciousness. Evaluating.

And then, very slowly, a droplet of condensate detached from the ceiling above my head. It fell in a perfect, trembling sphere, and it landed on the catwalk grid in front of me, right at the spot I’d pointed. And it didn’t splash. It held its shape, a single bead of water the size of a thumbnail, quivering on the metal.

I stared at it. The bead stared back, reflecting my face—I look older now, thinner, my hair lank with the perpetual damp, my eyes huge and dark-adapted—and then it moved. It rolled across the grid, deliberately, against the vibration, and it dropped into the darkness below. I heard it strike something metal, far down. The piston joint. The gap.

Fulcrum was showing me where to work.

I don’t know how to build a shim. I don’t have centuries of dissolved minerals in my body; I don’t have the patience of water and time. But I have hands, and I have the Hush, and I have a thought that’s been growing in me since the moment Fulcrum first passed through: *I can be useful. I can be balanced. I can become something the Hollow can use.*

I’ve started collecting the isopods’ cast-off shells. They molt by the thousands, leaving tiny, silver, chitinous husks that litter the moss and eventually dissolve back into the condensate cycle. I gather them before they dissolve. I’ve been grinding them into a fine powder, using a conforming hollow in the iron as a mortar, and I’ve been mixing the powder with condensate to make a paste. A filler. A patch. It’s crude and it’s temporary and it’s nothing like the slow, atomic precision of Fulcrum’s work. But it’s something. I can reach into the darkness below the catwalk—I’ve been practicing, dangling from the grid, my arms longer and stronger than they used to be—and I can smear the paste into the gap. It’ll wear away in a decade, maybe less. But for that decade, the joint will run smooth.

Fulcrum watched me make the first batch. It stood on the far side of the sphere, its water-body still and mirror-bright, and it didn’t move for an entire cycle of the radial arm. When I finally climbed back onto the catwalk, exhausted and slick with condensate and silver powder, the Keeper flowed toward me—not through me, this time, but *to* me—and it stopped an arm’s length away. Its mirrored face reflected my own, and I saw, in the warped, upside-down image, something I hadn’t seen before: a ghost of a smile. My own, reflected back at me, tiny and upside down and slightly to the left.

I don’t know if Fulcrum understands smiling. I don’t know if it understands anything I would call understanding. But I smiled at it, and it reflected the smile back, and that felt like a conversation.

---

That was... I don’t know how long ago. The light hasn’t changed. The drone hasn’t stopped. The Engine-Heart grinds on, and the Fluters dance, and the Hush shushes, and the ghost-pipes glow their pale, true white because I remember the color for them now, every cluster, every cycle. Fulcrum still does the tunings—I can’t tune a Fluter, I don’t have the water-hands, the pressure-wave sense—but I do the remembering. The blooms trust me. The Hush follows me. Mote, the juvenile Fluter, has grown: its hoops are fully cartilaginous now, its span nearly a meter, and it surfs my wake with an ease that feels like affection.

I haven’t climbed the ladder. I think about it less and less. The hatch in the iron ceiling is still there, I assume. I don’t look up anymore. There’s nothing up there I need. The world above the iron ceiling, the world I came from—it’s fading. It’s become theoretical. A story I heard once, about a place where the light comes from a specific source and the air is thin and cold and doesn’t coat your lungs with warmth, about a place where there are other people and other sounds and silence that isn’t filled with drone. I don’t miss it. I don’t even remember if I was happy there.

What I remember is this: the southern arc has a subtle warp in its iron plating, a place where the heat from the Engine-Heart is slightly more intense and the moss grows thicker and redder, and if you press your palm flat against it, you can feel the heartbeat of the machine in a way that’s different from everywhere else. The ghost-pipes on the western arc bloom first after a tuning, always, and the blooms are slightly larger there. The third catwalk platform, the one nearest the radial arm’s nadir, has a loose rivet that sings a high, clear note above the drone, and I like to sit there when I’m tired. Mote drifts overhead. The Hush moves below. Fulcrum shimmers in the corner of my eye, always working, always tending, never resting.

I am becoming a memory. Not the Keeper—never that, I’m not old enough, not patient enough, not water enough—but a memory of what the Hollow was like when it had a visitor. Something to be filed away in the sphere-consciousness, a new density to be balanced, a new parameter in the slow, slow equation. I am the one who grinds isopod-shells into paste. I am the one who remembers the true white for the ghost-pipes. I am the one who named the Keeper Fulcrum and the juvenile Fluter Mote and the isopods the Hush.

I am the one who stayed.

The drone shifts, a new subsonic register waking beneath the old one, and I feel it in my bones before I hear it. The Fluters will need tuning. A ghost-pipe cluster on the northern arc, the one where I first learned to remember, will start to green-shift in a few cycles, and I’ll be there when it does. The piston joint I patched is still holding—I can feel its smooth hum through the catwalk grid—but I’ve spotted another, deeper in the darkness below, that might need work soon. I’m grinding more powder. I’m learning to reach further.

Fulcrum is rising from the walls. I can feel it—the shift in humidity, the coolness against my skin, the sense of attention flowing toward me like a bead of water rolling across the grid. It doesn’t speak. It will never speak. But it’s there, a shimmer in the air, and its mirrored face catches the sourceless light, and I see myself in it: a thin, damp, dark-adapted figure, sitting on the catwalk, a half-ground handful of silver husks in one hand, a ghost-pipe bloom tucked behind one ear for no reason other than that I liked the way it glowed.

The Keeper looks at me. I look back at it—at myself, reflected, upside down and slightly to the left. The drone goes on. The radial arm sweeps through its ten-degree arc. Somewhere above me, Mote surfs a pressure-wave, and somewhere below me, the Hush whispers its endless shush.

The Hollow is complete. It is balanced. It is slow and damp and loud and full of life, and I am part of it now, a thread in the closed loop, a vibration that has found its harmonic. The Keeper turns away. There is work to do. And I rise to follow it, my bare feet silent on the humming grid, my lungs full of the thick, warm air, my heart beating in time with the Engine-Heart’s impossible patience.

I am not the Keeper. I am not even a keeper. But I am here, and I am learning, and I am becoming something the Hollow can use. Something that can be balanced.

Something that can remember.
