Instead, they breathe through their skin—all of it, every inch—and the moss-saturated air of Underbloom passes directly into their blood through surfaces kept perpetually wet. A Rooted person standing still will develop a fine sheen of moisture across their body, and if you were to press a hand to their back, it would feel cool and slightly yielding, like a river stone just pulled from the water. They do not pant. They do not sigh. Their breathing is silent and total and continuous, and when they exert themselves, they simply glisten more.

Their mouths are small, secondary things, used primarily for speech and for the consumption of the fungal pastes and blind-fish they eat. They have no teeth to speak of—just ridged plates that grind slowly—and their tongues are longer than yours, prehensile at the tip, used for cleaning their own eyes and for harvesting the edible slimes that form in the drainage channels. When they eat, they do so with the same slow deliberation as everything else they do, sitting in the dark with their glow-caps and working a strip of dried cave-lamprey between their plates for long minutes.

Their hands are long-fingered, four digits to a hand, each finger jointed in one place more than yours, giving their grips a curling, articulate quality. The fingertips are broad and sensitive, densely innervated, able to detect the faint vibrations of a moth's wingbeat through a moss wall. They feel the city the way a spider feels its web—as a continuous extension of the body, a thing that speaks to them in subtleties of moisture and tension and temperature. A Rooted person can stand in a corridor and tell you, without moving, whether the chamber three turns ahead has been opened recently, because the humidity flows differently past their skin after someone has passed through.

Their bodies are not gendered in the way you would assume. They reproduce slowly and rarely, and the process is more fungal than mammalian—two individuals exchanging spores in a deep, warm chamber over the course of days, a slow-ripening thing that results, eventually, in a child who emerges from the moss itself, cradled in a specially cultivated growth the parents have been tending for a year or more. Children are born knowing the taste of the water and the paths of the deep channels. They are born with their third eyelids already functional. They are born already part of the negotiation.

Because that is what their lives are: a negotiation. The people of Underbloom have no leaders, no laws, no formal structure of any kind. What they have is a deep, distributed awareness of the city's tolerances—a sense, honed over generations by feel and by consequence, of what the moss will accept and what it will not. Carve too large a chamber, too quickly, and the walls will ooze a black sap that burns bare skin. Harvest too many moss-pigs from a single wall, and the peat beneath will rot and collapse, taking three chambers with it. Ignore a slow-growing drip-point for too long, and it will seal the corridor behind you while you sleep. The city speaks in consequences, and the Rooted listen. They are not passive—they make choices, they alter things, they move through their lives with intention—but every choice is made in conversation with something vastly larger and slower than themselves.

Their relationships with each other are similarly fluid. They do not form pairs or families in the binding sense; instead they drift and overlap, forming loose, temporary clusters around tasks or walls or simply the need for warmth. A Rooted person's closest companion might be someone they sleep beside for a decade, then drift away from without rancor. Children are raised by whoever happens to be near, and the concept of "parent" is more like "the people who happened to be there when I first uncurled" than a permanent bond. There is no jealousy, no possession, no marriage. These things would be absurd in a city where the walls themselves will seal you in if you try to hold too tightly to anything.

And yet: they are not indifferent. The Rooted are capable of fierce, quiet love. It simply takes forms you would not recognize—the love of one person leaving a glow-cap burning in an otherwise empty chamber, so that another, passing through, will know someone thought of them; the love of weaving moss-wicker into a new window screen for a chamber not your own because you noticed the draft when you passed; the love of teaching a child the names of the channels and the taste of the good water and the particular vibration in the wall that means the new growth is coming in well. They do not say "I love you." They say "I left a cap lit" or "the south wall is sprouting" or "there's a warm seep near the third arch," and the meaning is understood.

The oldest and most important being in Underbloom is the Rememberer. This is not a name they chose; it is a role they hold, or perhaps a condition they suffer. The Rememberer is the only one who has never drifted, never moved chambers, never shifted allegiances or attachments. For as long as anyone alive can remember—and that is precisely the point—the Rememberer has occupied the central chamber, the deepest and warmest, where the water runs clearest and the moss grows most deliberately. They are ancient by the standards of their kind, three or four hundred years at least, though the number means little in a place with no sun to mark years. They have outlived the slow forgetting that claims most Rooted after a century or so, when the damp seeps into memory and dissolves the sharp edges of the past into comfortable vagueness.

The Rememberer refuses this comfort. They remember everything: every wall that was carved and by whom, every collapse and cave-in and slow reclamation, every child uncurled from the moss, every agreement negotiated with the city's slow will. Their body has grown into the chamber over the centuries—their skin has merged with the moss-wall behind them, and they no longer move from the waist down, their legs having become part of the living architecture, their veins threading into the capillary system of the wall itself. They are fed by the room. They breathe through the moss. Their eyes have filmed over entirely, the third eyelid sealed shut, and they see now only through the vibration-sense of their spread fingers—but this is enough, more than enough, because they feel the whole city, every settling wall and shifting arch, every new seep and every colony of springtails moving in the dark. They are Underbloom's living memory, its archive, its conscience, and its voice when the city's tolerances need to be spoken aloud.

The Rememberer does not command. No one in Underbloom commands. But when they speak, the others listen, because what they say is never advice or opinion—it is simply the accumulated weight of everything that has ever happened here, offered in a voice that sounds like water moving over old peat. They speak rarely. Sometimes a century passes with only a few sentences. But the words, when they come, are carried in the walls afterward, resonating in the memory of the moss for generations.
