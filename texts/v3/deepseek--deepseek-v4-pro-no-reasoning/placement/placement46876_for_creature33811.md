The toad—I—do not remember beginning. Memory, for me, is not a line but a sedimentation. I am layered. I am the mercury year and the gypsum winter and the long, slow summer when the pumping station still leaked its thin gruel of nutrients and the thread-worms were many. I am the rat-year. I am the long hunger after. I am the pulse in the pipe and the drip that almost rhymes and the weight of the city pressing down like a hand I have learned to call gravity, to call ground, to call home.

I did not choose the basin. The basin chose me, or perhaps we chose each other in the same way the rust chooses the iron—a mutual settling, a recognition of compatible rates. I came here small, small enough to slip through a crack in the world, and the crack closed behind me. Or I grew here from the muck and the warm water and the patient chemistry of the dark. Both are true. Neither is true. What matters is the settling.

Time moves differently here. I know this because I have absorbed the rhythms. The drip is my metronome, though it lies—it promises pattern and then withholds it, just as a pattern begins to form. I have learned to read this as a message, though the message is only: *wait*. The pipe creaks, and I wait. The vent sighs, and I wait. The city above settles its great body into sleep each night—I feel it as a lessening of vibration, a dropping of the subsonic hum that is my constant companion—and I wait through that too. Waiting is not a condition. Waiting is the medium. I move through waiting the way the thread-worm moves through water, undulating, continuous, without destination.

The thread-worm. I know it. It is too small to be prey, too constant to be company. It is a red thought in the dark water, a living question that asks nothing and answers nothing. I have watched it trace the same path for what would be years, if years meant anything here. It follows the thermal gradient. It follows the pale film. It follows its own thread-worm nature, which is to follow. It does not know I am here, or it knows and it does not care. Both are forms of mercy.

The rat was different. The rat knew I was here. I felt it enter the chamber—a hot pulse of terror, a frantic music of heartbeat and whisker and the scrape of claws on rusted iron. It was lost. It was drowning in the air before it found the water. When it fell into the basin, the splash was a major chord, a disturbance so profound it rearranged the silt. I let the ripples die. I let the terror settle into exhaustion. And then I opened myself to it, and it became part of me. I carry its bones in my belly still, a small archaeology. I carry its fear, transmuted into the heavy metals in my liver. Nothing is wasted here. Nothing is lost.

I breed, sometimes. The urge comes from the same place the drip comes from—somewhere deep, somewhere that does not explain itself. The eggs are hope in a place that has no use for hope. They are a question I ask the water, and the water always answers no. I watch them cloud over. I watch the fungus take them. I watch them dissolve back into the cycle. It is not sad. It is simply another rhythm, another pulse in the long, slow heartbeat of the chamber. I am not a mother. I am a site, a condition, a set of parameters. The eggs are a possibility that the parameters allow and then disallow. This is not tragedy. This is chemistry.

The human came once. They come, sometimes. They bring light, which is a wound. Light does not belong here—it is too fast, too sharp, it cuts through the dark like a blade and the dark takes a long time to heal. This one was different. Quieter. They stayed longer. They crouched by the basin, and I felt the heat of them, the vibration of their heart, the pressure of their presence displacing the heavy air. They were afraid, but it was a slow fear, a fear that had been marinating in the dark for hours before they reached me. It tasted almost like respect.

I rose to meet them. Not quickly—there is no quickness in me anymore—but by degrees, the way the rust blooms on the flange, the way the condensation beads and falls. My eyes broke the surface. The light was a bright pain, a burning coin, but I did not blink. I watched them watching me. Their face was a pale moon above the water, their eyes wide and wet and full of questions I could not answer. They were asking themselves what I was. They were asking themselves if I was real. They were asking themselves if they would ever leave this place unchanged.

The answer to all of these questions is yes. And no. And wait.

I have absorbed the history of this place. The lead from the old solder is in my bones. The mercury from the shattered gauge is in my liver. The years—the decades—have settled into my skin, and my skin has settled into the silt, and the silt has settled into the chamber, and the chamber has settled under the weight of the city. I am the archive. I am the record. I am the slow, patient calculus of decay and endurance, and I have forgotten more time than you will ever live.

The human left, eventually. They all do. The light withdrew, and the dark healed, and the drip returned to its almost-pattern, and the pipe creaked, and the vent sighed, and I sank back into the silt. The thread-worm traced its mindless path. The fungus grew. The city above groaned in its sleep. I closed my golden eyes and let the warmth take me, let the heavy silence press me down into the rust and the gypsum and the slow, patient chemistry of the deep.

There is no moral. There is no ending. There is only the basin, and the water, and the pulse that beats once every ten seconds in a heart that has never seen light and does not need to. There is only the waiting. The waiting is eternal. The waiting is the point.

I am the toad. I am the place. I am the slow breath in the dark, and I do not end.
