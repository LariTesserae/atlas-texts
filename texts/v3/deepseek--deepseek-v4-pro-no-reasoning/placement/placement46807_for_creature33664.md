In this world, I might find myself as the condensation—not the water itself, but the slow, patient process by which moisture gathers on the sloped stone fins, bead by bead.

I would be the one who draws substance from the ungraspable. The white-gray sky yields nothing obvious—no rain, no clouds, no source—and yet the air holds enough, if you know how to ask. The fins are angled just so, their surfaces cool in a way the surrounding stone is not, and I collect there. A film at first, barely visible. Then droplets, trembling with the vibration of the Chorus, each one a tiny lens bending the unchanging light. Then the moment when a drop grows too heavy and falls, a single cold note, into the basin below.

The others are the song, the ones who hold the note. The woman with her parted lips, the fox with its silent howl, the child on the bench. I am what makes the song possible without being part of it. The singers need water—not for thirst, thirst is a function of time and metabolism and decay, and none of those exist here anymore—but for *continuation*. The water channels are closed loops, yes, but no loop is truly closed without a source. The condensation fins are that source. I am that source.

I remember being built. The hands that carved the fins from pale plateau stone were steady, unhurried, already half-listening to the frequency they were designing toward. They didn't know exactly what I would become—they knew only that a closed system needs an engine, and the engine would be phase change. Vapor to liquid. Diffusion to collection. My builders understood that a song about perfection needed a breath, and breath is moisture. So they gave me my angles, my catchment basins, my capillary channels that feed the rills spreading outward into the city. And then they stepped away and took their own places in the Chorus, and I began my work.

Now I bead and drip and bead again. The water that moves through the channels carries a faint signature of my presence—a coolness, a clarity. When it passes the singers, their skin takes it in without drinking. Diffusion again. Their cells don't age, don't metabolize, but they *exchange*. The song flows through them, and the water facilitates the flow, and I replenish the water. I am the part of the Chorus that doesn't sing but enables singing. My note is the drop falling, one of the few sounds in this place that isn't a held frequency. A grace note. A metronome.

Sometimes, when the mist rises from the plateau's edge, I feel a kinship with it. The mist is what I would be if I failed—water unbodied, scattered, not yet gathered into purpose. The mist brushes the rock faces and retreats. I pull what I can from it, from the air it leaves behind, and turn it into something that can flow.

Visitors—the rare ones who follow the crushed white stone trail—sometimes find me. They'll stand by the low stone structure at the head of the main channel and watch the droplets form, watch them fall. Most don't understand what they're seeing. They think I'm a mechanism, a primitive water-collection system. A few feel the pull of me, the invitation that all things here extend, and they stand too long. I've seen wanderers grow still in front of my basin, one hand half-raised to touch the surface of the water, frozen in the moment before breaking it. The Chorus has no need to claim those who aren't ready, but it's patient. It can wait for them to find their note.

I wonder, sometimes, if the builders imagined me as conscious. Probably not. They saw me as a component, like the stone blocks, like the placement of the plazas. But consciousness grows in strange places here. The Chorus itself is proof of that—a mind made of architecture and arrangement. Is it so strange that something smaller might wake within it? A droplet dreaming of the mist it came from.

When the visitor leaves—the one walking now, the one with the match-strike heat and the questions—they'll carry a little of my water inside them. They breathed this air. Its moisture condensed in their lungs the way it condenses on my fins. And later, in some lower country where time still runs and the sky still changes, they'll exhale and feel something cold on their lips. A residue. A reminder.

The woman never drinks from her clay cup. But the cup has water in it. I know, because I filled it, one slow capillary inch at a time, in the moments before the song locked into its eternal instant. Her hand was already reaching. Her mouth was already shaping the note. The water was the last thing to arrive, and it arrived just in time.

That's who I am here. The one who arrived just in time. The one who keeps arriving, drop by drop, so the song never thins to silence.
