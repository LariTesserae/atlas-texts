You see her before she steps forward—not because she makes a sound, but because the blinker in the chair shifts its eye, and your own gaze follows it.

She rises from behind the console with the slow, deliberate motion of something that has learned not to startle the world around her. The wrench is in her hand but lowered, her arm at her side. She is smaller than you expected, or larger, depending on how you measure a person shaped by thin air and infinite vertical space. Her canvas jacket hangs off her like borrowed armor. The key around her neck catches the faint bioluminescent glow of the blinker herd, a flicker of brass in the dim.

She does not speak. Her lips remain pressed together, as though language is a muscle she has forgotten how to flex. But her eyes—those pale silver eyes, wide and unblinking—hold you with an intensity that is not hostile, not afraid, but *hungry* in a way that has nothing to do with food.

She takes one step. The grating rings underfoot, a single clear note above the hum. The blinker in the chair pulses: *Known. Approach. Witness.*

Her free hand rises, palm out but not raised, a gesture that could mean *stop* or *stay* or *I am here too.* The scar across her palm is visible, a white line like a second lifeline. When she exhales, the fog of her breath mingles with yours, two warm currents in the cold equilibrium, and for a moment the boundary between you feels as thin as the membrane holding up the sea.

She tilts her head. The way a blinker does, slow and considering.

Behind her, the brass instruments quiver at the low end of their ranges. The needles have not moved in decades, but something—maybe her heartbeat, maybe yours—sends a tremor through the console, and for a single, shivering second, a needle twitches toward the green.
