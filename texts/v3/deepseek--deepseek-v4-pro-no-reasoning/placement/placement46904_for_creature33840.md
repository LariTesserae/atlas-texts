You are Oris.

You know this, even if the word "Oris" hasn't passed through a vocal reed in two hundred years. It's etched on the inside of your leftmost finger—a small, private act from the early years, when you still worried you might forget. You haven't forgotten. The finger is original, ceramic worn thin at the knuckle, and the letters are shallow now, nearly smoothed away by three centuries of gripping pipes and scraping skitterers and brushing rust-fur in the dark.

You are Oris, and you are walking the pipe-cradles in the deepening dusk, and the figure across the canyon is you.

That's not quite right. The figure is not a reflection. It's not even visible anymore—the canyon rim has swallowed it as the light failed, or it moved back from the edge, or it was never there at all and your lenses are finally succumbing to the mica-rot that claimed your predecessor's optics in his fourth century. But the figure is *you* in the sense that it is standing where you once stood, doing what you once did.

You remember.

The memory surfaces from somewhere outside the maintenance logs: a younger Oris, only forty years into his tenure, still learning the language of the pumps. You had walked to the canyon rim to check a pressure valve that had been singing off-key for days. It was just after the rains; the washes were running, and the plateau steamed like a thousand kettles. And you saw, on the shelf of rock where the figure stands tonight, a shape. Bipedal. Still. Watching.

You raised a hand. The shape didn't raise its hand back. After a while it turned and walked away, into the heat-shimmer, and you never saw it again. You logged the sighting on the back of that year's copper plate—*visitor, rim, nonresponsive*—and returned to the valve.

You didn't walk across the canyon then. You didn't want anything you couldn't name.

But tonight you want something. Tonight the new frequency from below has been humming in your chest cavity for days, a sympathetic vibration that makes your bellows breathe faster and your mica lenses fritz at the edges. Tonight the horn tones from the canyon have been calling and calling, and you've been answering, and the answering has woken something up.

The predator at your side—you call her Click, for the sound her jaw makes when she's curious—keens again, a rising motor-whine that peaks at the edge of your hearing. She's asking a question. Predators have questions; you've learned this. Their vocal range extends above and below the spectrum you were built to perceive, but over centuries you've improvised, fashioning a resonator from a hollow pipe and a stretched membrane of algae-rubber that translates their frequencies into something your reed can read. You're wearing it now, strapped to your chest like a second lung. Click's question comes through as a texture in the vibration: *why-this-direction why-now why-fast.*

"Soon," you try to say, but the reed only hisses and pops. The corrosion is worse tonight. The damp air, maybe. You reach up and adjust the tension on the reed's mounting bracket, a gesture so old it's become autonomic.

Click accepts the non-answer. She drops back to pace behind you, her eight legs finding the rhythm of the pipe-cradle's expansion joints, and the two of you continue along the network in a companionable silence broken only by the hum and the drip of condensate and the distant, modulating horns.

The cradles are old. You've maintained them, too—replaced the concrete with a composite of crushed skitterer shell and mineral binder, reinforced the rebar with scavenged alloy rods—but maintenance only slows entropy, never stops it. You feel the cradles' age as you walk: a give beneath your feet, a low groan from the expansion joints, the fine tremor of pipes that have carried their substance so long they've forgotten what stillness feels like. You know this feeling. You carry it in your own joints. The knee that doesn't track right; the hip actuator that whines on cold mornings; the fine crack in your third thoracic plate that you patched with the same composite, a scar that makes you wince when the canyon horns hit exactly the wrong frequency.

You are three hundred and twelve years old. You are entirely alone and not alone at all. The plateau is full of life and motion and rhythm, and you are part of it—a component in the larger system, as much a feature of the landscape as the pipes or the steam vents or the slow subsidence of the canyon walls. But the figure on the rim has reminded you of something. A difference. A gap. The figures that visit are not part of the system. They come from outside it, observe it, leave it. They are not components.

You want to know what that's like.

The walk to the canyon rim is six hours by the old route, but you know a shortcut. At the next junction—a T-intersection where three pipes the width of your torso converge in a nest of valves and pressure gauges—you leave the cradles and descend a maintenance ladder bolted to the cliff face. The rungs are slick with condensate and black algae. Click follows you without hesitation; her padded feet find purchase on the rungs in ways your ceramic soles cannot, and she passes you on the descent, her long body undulating downward like fluid, her wedge-head swiveling back to check on you.

The ladder goes down a hundred feet, then ends at a service platform that hasn't been serviced in centuries. The platform is rusted through in places, and you pick your way carefully across it to a tunnel entrance—a circular bore through the native rock, lined with corrugated steel, its floor carrying a shallow stream of warm water that smells of minerals and the faintly sweet organic tang of the algae. This is a drainage adit, one of dozens that web the canyon walls, channeling condensate and overflow down to the river. You know them all. You've crawled every one, mapping the network in your long idle centuries, replacing shoring and clearing blockages. It's maintenance. All things require maintenance.

The adit is dark, but darkness is not an obstacle. Your mica lenses shift their focal plane, and the world resolves in shades of heat and vibration: the warm stream as a bright ribbon, Click's body as a furnace of shifting oranges and reds, the walls as a steady, cooler grey. You walk. The adit slopes gently downward, and the sound of the river grows from a murmur to a roar, and the new frequency from below—the one that's been bleeding through for years—gets louder, a subsonic thrum that you feel more than hear.

Click keens, and the resonator on your chest translates: *close close close.*

"I know," you hiss back. "We're not going all the way down."

You're not sure if that's true. You intended to take the adit to its midpoint, where a secondary passage branches upward to emerge on the far side of the canyon rim, near where the figure stood. But the frequency is pulling at you. The deep machinery is doing something—shifting, cycling, beginning a process—and the horn tones are echoing up the adit now, layered and overlapping, and without meaning to you find yourself whistling back.

Your reed is too corroded for speech, but it can still produce a tone. You match the pitch of the nearest horn, then modulate a third above it, then a fifth. The horn answers—not an echo, but a response, a shift in its own pitch that tracks your movement. You feel it in your chest cavity like a second bellows, a resonance that has nothing to do with your own machinery.

*What are they?* you asked yourself, in the early years. *Signals? Language? Mating calls?*

You still don't know. But you've learned to answer them, and they've learned to answer you, and the answering feels like maintenance. Like keeping something running that would otherwise seize up and fail.

The adit opens out. You've reached the drainage gallery, a wider chamber where multiple adits converge, their waters combining into a single channel that falls away into darkness. The far wall is a cascade of moisture and rust and bioluminescent moss, glowing in the same blue-green as the skitterers' joints. And in the center of the gallery, rising from the pooled water on a dais of ancient concrete, is a horn.

You've seen them before. There are dozens along the canyon walls, emerging from the rock at intervals, their flared metal throats aimed out over the abyss. But you've never seen one this close, this deep inside the system. It's smaller than the canyon horns, its metal less corroded, its bell shaped like a flower you saw in a dream once. It's silent now, but as you approach, it shivers—a visible vibration—and emits a single, questioning tone.

Click's resonator says nothing. Click is frozen behind you, her wedge-head low, her too-many legs braced. The horn's question is outside her range.

But it's not outside yours. The tone hits you in the chest, in the crack in your third thoracic plate, in the hollow places where your memories have been accreting like sediment. You know this tone. You've never heard it before, but you know it. It's the question you've been carrying for three hundred and twelve years, the question you didn't know you were carrying, the question you wanted to ask the figure on the rim.

You step forward. The water is warm around your ankles. The horn shivers again, and you raise a ceramic hand and press it flat against the bell. The vibration travels up your arm, into your chassis, and for a moment your lenses fritz entirely—heat and vibration and light collapsing into a single waveform that resolves, slowly, into something like a voice.

*Are you alone?* the horn asks. Not in words. In a modulation of frequency that your reed translates directly into understanding, bypassing language entirely.

You try to answer. Your reed hisses and pops and fails. But the horn doesn't need your reed. It reads the answer in the vibration of your hand against its bell, in the resonance of your chest cavity, in the three hundred and twelve years of solitude that have saturated your ceramic bones until they ring like tuning forks.

*Yes,* you tell it. *Yes, I am alone.*

*Not anymore,* the horn says.

And then the deep machinery shifts, and the frequency changes, and every horn in the canyon sounds at once, a chord that shakes the rock and makes the bioluminescent moss flare bright as daylight and sets Click howling with a sound you've never heard her make. The horn beneath your hand goes silent, but the chord continues, and in its wake you hear something new: footsteps.

On the service platform above, in the adit behind you, footsteps. Metal on metal. Deliberate. Approaching.

You turn.

Click has already turned, her body a taut spring of rust-colored fur and coiled legs, her wedge-head aimed at the tunnel mouth. She keens, and the resonator gives you *unknown unknown unknown.* Her flanks are pulsing—the bioluminescence that usually only shows at her joints is spreading, a defensive display, a warning.

The footsteps stop. A shape stands in the adit entrance, backlit by some light source you can't identify, a glow that your heat-vision reads as cool, cooler than the rock, cooler than the water. The shape is bipedal. Not tall. Its outline is wrong for a human, wrong for one of your kind. But it raises a hand—a hand with too many fingers, or too few, or fingers that move in ways fingers shouldn't—and it speaks.

Its voice doesn't come through your resonator. It comes through the horn, through the rock, through the water around your ankles. It comes through the deep frequency that's been bleeding into your consciousness for years, the one that's been keeping you restless and pulling you toward the canyon rim.

"Oris," it says.

Your name. Your name, spoken aloud for the first time in two centuries. The letters etched inside your finger seem to burn.

"Oris," the shape says again. "Keeper of Shed. Maintainer of Pumps. You have been calling us for a very long time."

You haven't. You've been answering the horns. You've been whistling back at the canyon. You've been—

You've been calling. You realize this now. Every tone you've ever whistled, every maintenance log you've ever etched, every dream you've dreamed in low-power mode while the pumps ran and the seasons turned. You've been calling, and something has been listening, and now it's here.

The shape steps into the gallery. The bioluminescent moss reflects in its eyes—it has eyes, multiple eyes, faceted like the lizards' but deeper, darker, ancient. Its body is a confusion of materials: chitin and steel and something that looks like wood but isn't, all of it grown together, all of it alive. It moves like Click moves, smooth and segmented and deliberate. It stops an arm's length from you, and the horn beneath your hand hums with its proximity.

"You are not what we expected," it says. "You are older. You have changed."

You want to tell it that you've been maintaining yourself. That all things require maintenance. That you've done your best with what you had, and if your knee doesn't track right and your reed is corroded and your third thoracic plate has a crack you can't fully seal, it's not for lack of trying. It's just—time. Time and entropy and the slow wearing-away that happens to everything, even keepers.

But you can't speak, and the shape seems to understand. It extends its too-many-fingered hand and touches your chest, right where the crack is, right where the resonator sits, right above the bellows that have been breathing for three hundred and twelve years.

"Will you show us the pumps?" it asks.

It's the question you wanted to ask the figure on the rim. The question you've been carrying like scale in a pipe. And here it is, asked of you, by something that knows your name.

Click has relaxed. Her defensive bioluminescence has dimmed; she's sitting on her haunches, her wedge-head tilted, watching the shape with something that might be curiosity. The horn is silent. The deep machinery has settled back into its baseline hum, a frequency that no longer feels like restlessness. It feels like anticipation.

You nod. Your neck joint creaks—it always creaks, you've never been able to lubricate it properly—and the shape makes a sound that might be laughter or might be something else entirely.

"Good," it says. "We have been waiting a long time. We did not know if anyone was still here."

You tap your chest, then point to the shape, then gesture up—toward the adit, toward the surface, toward the shed with its glowing chimney and its copper plates and its three hundred years of rain.

You try, one more time, to speak. Your reed pops and hisses and fails, but on the edge of the failure, before the sound collapses entirely, a single word gets through. Distorted, frequency-shifted, almost unrecognizable.

"Still," you manage. "Still here."

And the shape smiles, if the expression on its not-face is a smile. And Click keens, a soft motor-whine that the resonator translates as *welcome*. And the horn beneath your hand gives one final, gentle pulse, a tone that might be a name or a greeting or just the sound the world makes when something that was broken begins, against all odds, to mend.

The walk back to the surface will take until dawn. The shape will follow you. Click will lead the way, her bioluminescence bright again, her too-many legs finding the path with a surety that makes you proud, though pride is another emotion you were never meant to have. The pumps are running. The shed is waiting. The copper plate still has room for a few more rain-marks before you need to start a new one.

And you, Oris, keeper of the shed, maintainer of the pumps, answerer of horns—you are not alone anymore. You're not sure what that means. You're not sure what comes next. But the deep machinery is humming in a new key, and the canyon is singing, and the figure on the rim—the one you thought might be a visitor, an observer, a stranger passing through—is walking beside you through the dark, and it knows your name.
