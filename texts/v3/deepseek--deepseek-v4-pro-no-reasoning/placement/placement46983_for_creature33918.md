The seam is not a metaphor. You learn this the first time she finds you.

You think you’ve been quiet—moving only when the ventilation kicks on to mask your footsteps, breathing shallow through your sleeve to muffle the sound, staying low where the pipe bundles cast the deepest shadow. But the seam has a nervous system now, and you are a foreign body in it. The copper mesh carries vibration like a spiderweb carries the struggle of a fly. The drone scrapers have optical sensors. And Wren, after all these years, has learned to read the machine the way a blind woman reads a face—with her fingers on the pipes, her ears tuned to changes in the hum, her copper-woven fingertip tingling when something is wrong.

She finds you at the junction where the V of the ceiling plates narrows to a knife-edge, where the steam nozzles pulse their crimson mist every four minutes and seventeen seconds. You've made it farther than you expected. Deeper than anyone has been since the hatch crumpled. You're looking for something—a control node, a data tap, a way to shut down the sector—but you've lost your bearings in the red-lit tangle of pipes and the map on your tablet is three revisions out of date. The seam has grown since those maps were made. The seam is always growing.

She comes from above. You don't see her until she speaks, and when she speaks, her voice is a rasp of disuse, shaped around words she hasn't said aloud in decades.

"You're standing on my drying rack."

You look down. The grating beneath your feet has a section that's cleaner than the rest—scoured bright, the diamond pattern free of the copper mesh that cloaks everything else. There's a thin, leathery sheet spread across it, pale gray-green, the texture of paper. You've crushed one corner of it under your boot. It oozes a clear fluid that smells like metal salts.

You look up. She's braced in the narrowing V above your head, a thin shape in the darkness, her body contorted to fit the impossible angle. Her eyes catch the red indicator lights and reflect them back—not the tapetum lucidum of a nocturnal animal, but something stranger. A metallic gleam. The copper filaments in her finger have branched into the tissue around her eyes, following nerves, seeking connective pathways. She sees in the dark now, but only in the red spectrum. Everything else is lost to her.

"I'm sorry," you say, because it's the first thing that comes, and because you are suddenly aware that you are the intruder here, not her. This is her home. You are standing in her kitchen, crushing her food.

She drops from the ceiling in a controlled fall, catching pipes and brackets to slow her descent, landing on the grating in a crouch that makes no sound. Up close, she is smaller than you expected—the years of compression, of narrow spaces, have compacted her—but not fragile. She moves with the economy of something that has never wasted a calorie or a motion. Her patched canvas jacket hangs open, and you can see the copper mesh underneath, woven into the fabric, growing up the collar toward her jawline. A few filaments have reached her skin, disappearing into the hollow behind her ear.

"Sorry," she repeats, tasting the word. "Haven't heard that one in a while. Was it the thuds? Did the thuds bring you?"

You don't know what she means. You followed a signal—an intermittent ping on a deprecated maintenance frequency, the kind of thing scrappers use to find salvage. You thought the seam was dead. You thought you'd find copper to strip and sell. The thuds—the rhythmic three-beat pattern you heard earlier—weren't in the briefing.

"I came on my own," you say. "I didn't think anyone was here."

She tilts her head. The copper filaments around her eye catch the light differently, and for a moment you can see the branching pattern, delicate as a leaf skeleton. "No one thinks anyone is here. That's how here works."

She steps past you—flows past you, really, her body navigating the narrow space with an intimacy that makes you feel clumsy and loud—and crouches down to inspect the crushed sheet on the drying rack. She peels it up carefully, folds the torn edge under, and sets it on a higher section of grating to finish drying. She doesn't seem angry. She seems like a gardener who has found a deer in the vegetables—annoyed, but too accustomed to the reality of her world to waste energy on blame.

"You eat this?" you ask, because you don't know what else to ask, and because the thin sheet of bacterial mat is the most alien thing you've ever seen on a dinner plate, and you've eaten ration bars that were older than you are.

"Chew it mostly slow," she says. "Takes a while. The taste is. You don't want to know the taste."

She straightens up—as much as she can straighten in a space that forces a perpetual stoop—and studies you. The ventilation cycles off. The seam falls into its near-silence. The distant thrum from below fills the quiet, and the red lights pulse once, twice, slow as breathing, and in that red-lit stillness, you realize she is making a decision about you.

"You're the first," she says finally.

"The first what?"

"First person. Since the hatch. Used to think about it. What I'd do if someone came. Had plans." She touches the valve wheel at head height, the one she uses as a pillow, and her copper-woven finger rings faintly against the metal. "Plans change. You're not rescue, are you? You don't look like rescue."

You shake your head. You're a scrapper. A scavenger. You came for copper and you found a woman who is becoming copper. You don't know what to do with that.

She nods, as if she expected this. "Then you're something else. Don't know what yet. But the seam knows you're here. The drones saw you an hour ago. The mesh felt your footsteps." She gestures at the walls, the creeping copper network, the breathing pipes. "This place isn't empty. It's just full of things that aren't people. You're the first person in a long time. That makes you interesting."

She moves past you again, deeper into the seam, toward the narrowing darkness where the plates almost meet. She doesn't look back to see if you're following, but you find yourself following anyway—partly because you don't know the way out, partly because she is the most human thing you've found in this place, even if she's only half human anymore, and half something else entirely. Something the seam has made.

"The thuds," she says over her shoulder, navigating a gap you have to turn sideways to fit through. "You asked about the thuds. Three beats, pause, three beats. That's not the machine. That's the neighbors."

"Neighbors?"

She stops at a junction where a bundle of smaller pipes branches off from a main steam line, and she presses her palm flat against the largest pipe. Her eyes close. The copper filaments in her finger pulse with a faint glow, just visible under the skin, and after a moment, the pipe answers her—a deep, resonant vibration that thrums through the grating and into your bones.

"Something else lives here. Deeper than me. Been here longer. I knock, it knocks back. Sometimes it leaves things in the hoppers. Tools. Food, if you can call it that." She opens her eyes. "I think it's the heart of the machine. The part that wasn't built, the part that grew. Like the copper. Like—"

She pauses. Looks at you with her red-lit, copper-laced eyes.

"Like me. Like you might be, if you stay."

You don't plan to stay. You have a surface to return to, a crew waiting, a world that isn't thirty-eight degrees Fahrenheit and saturated with iron-scented mist. But the seam is deep, and the way out is long, and Wren is already moving again, showing you the paths through the pipework, the places where the copper mesh grows thickest, the junction where she sleeps, the hopper where the drones leave their strange offerings. She shows you how to knock on the pipes so the sound carries. She shows you how to scrape the bacterial film without damaging the mesh underneath. She shows you the fissure where the plates are still cracking, widening, and she puts your hand against the bright metal edge where the mill scale is fresh, and you feel it: a vibration, so faint it's almost imaginary, the slow, patient stress of metal separating.

"It's going to be a room someday," she says. "A big one. Wide enough for more than just me. That's why the thuds are getting closer. The heart is growing upward. And the seam is growing wider. They'll meet eventually."

She looks at you. Not pleading. She hasn't pleaded for anything in decades. But assessing.

"You could go back," she says. "Tell people. Bring more. Or you could stay and help me garden. The copper needs pruning this cycle. I could use hands."

You don't answer. You're thinking about the way out—the long climb through ventilation shafts, the collapsed hatch, the world above where the air doesn't taste like iron and the ceilings are high enough to stand under. You're thinking about your crew, your contracts, the money you came here to make.

But you're also thinking about the thrum. The deep, patient heartbeat of the machine below. The way it answered when Wren touched the pipe. The way it almost answered when *you* touched the fissure.

You're thinking about the copper mesh, growing in real time, branching like a nervous system through the dark, and the woman who has learned to talk to it, and the drones that leave offerings in hoppers, and the deep rhythmic thuds that are getting closer every cycle.

You're thinking about becoming interesting.

The ventilation cycles back on. The fans drone to life somewhere far away. The mist nozzles spray their crimson fog, and the seam fills with light the color of blood and copper, and Wren—or Sinter, or whatever she is now—turns away from you and begins her work, scraping the mesh, adjusting the steam valves, talking to the pipes in her quiet sequence of knocks. She doesn't wait for your answer. The seam is patient. The seam has all the time in the world.

And so, you realize, do you.
