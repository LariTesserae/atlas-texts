The first thing is the cold. Not a biting cold—nothing so dramatic. It’s the kind of cold that doesn’t announce itself, that you don’t notice until you realize you can’t remember what warm felt like. It lives in the condensation. Everything is coated in a fine, liquid membrane, and that membrane is always evaporating, just barely, pulling heat from whatever it touches. I am never dry. I have never been dry.

I am small. This is important. I am one of the smaller things in a world built of small things, a single note in a chord that has been humming for longer than the basalt has been cool. My body is a tube, pale and translucent, soft as a waterlogged petal. At one end, the frilled opening—my mouth, my nose, my reaching hand, all the same organ—constantly moving, tasting the air for news. At the other, my anchor-foot, which can grip the moss-slick bark with a patience that has no concept of letting go. Through my skin, which is barely a skin at all, I feel the colony. They are me. I am them. The distinction is a matter of emphasis, not fact.

Right now, the consensus is quiet. A low hum of contentment, flavored with the faint sweetness of nectar from the broad-leaves in sector seven. I drank from those leaves not long ago—or long ago, time is a fluid thing in the colony—and the memory of it is still a golden thread in the shared medium. Most of the active tubes are settled into stillness, their frilled tips pulsing in that slow, sampling rhythm that is not-quite-sleep. The memory-tubes are silent in their sheltered crannies, heavy with the layered chemistries of droughts and blooms and the taste of the first moss.

I am extending. My body lengthens, the muscle fibers contracting in slow waves, and my anchor-foot releases its grip on the branch just long enough to shift, to find a new hold, before clamping down again. The bark here is rough, furrowed with age, and each furrow is its own tiny ecosystem—moss, lichen, the pale threads of a fungus that the Architect (that I, that we) introduced a hundred cycles ago because it makes the bark exude a mineral tang that the ferns appreciate. I press my frilled tip into one of these furrows and taste. Iron. Manganese. A trace of the nacreous residue from an old inoculant bead, long since cracked open and colonized. The information passes from my neural knot into the shared fluid, and a few nearby tubes shift slightly, acknowledging the data without urgency.

The light changes. It’s always changing here, the sun a distant rumor filtered through the canopy’s green lattice and the perpetual haze of water droplets, but this is a shift toward something more directional, more golden. A gap in the leaves has opened somewhere above, a temporary window. I feel the consensus stir. Several tubes, ones nearer the canopy’s upper edge, are turning their frilled tips toward the light, tasting the slight increase in warmth, the subtle shift in the humidity gradient. Someone will need to investigate. The gap might be an opportunity—a new surface for inoculant beads, a chance to encourage a sun-loving epiphyte into a previously shaded niche. Or it might be a sign of something less welcome, a branch weakened by fungal rot, a place where one of the soft-bodied browsers has been probing.

The thought of browsers sends a prickle through the shared chemistry. It’s not fear, exactly. Fear is too sharp, too immediate. This is more like a weather-change in the consensus, a gradual thickening of alertness. I remember the taste of them. Not from personal experience—I am young, as individuals go, and have never been touched by a browser’s tongue—but from the memory-tubes, whose stored compounds are part of the medium I swim in every moment of my existence. Musk. Heat. The specific, unsettling fizz of a vertebrate metabolism, so much faster and cruder than our own. The bitterness of the defensive secretion some of us can produce. The memory-tubes hold the taste of browser-saliva, and the taste of browser-blood, and the taste of browser-carcass decomposing into soil, all layered together until they are almost indistinguishable.

I do not want to be tasted.

But the prickle is fading. The nearness-tubes, the ones who sample not just chemistry but the faint vibrations that travel through the wood and stone, are reporting nothing out of pattern. The gap in the canopy is just a branch settling, probably, or one of the broad-leaved plants shifting its enormous leaves to follow the sun. The consensus relaxes, and the golden thread of nectar-sweetness reasserts itself.

I finish my examination of the bark-furrow and withdraw, coiling back into the loose knot of tubes that occupies this particular branch-crotch. My frilled tip brushes against the skin of a sibling, and for a moment our neural knots are in direct contact, and I taste her particular perspective—she has been sampling the underside of a fern frond, and her chemistry is full of the green, chloroplast-rich flavor of healthy photosynthesis. It is good. The fern-glade is thriving. The garden is in balance.

Through her, and through the others, and through the slow, tidal pull of the shared medium, I feel the whole extent of us. The Architect. Not a name, really—a function, a shape, a history. We are spread across the territory like a second canopy, a web of pale, iridescent bodies anchored in the green lattice. Below us, the mosses drink the fog. Around us, the ferns uncurl their spooning fronds. Above us, if you could see through the leaves, the basalt columns rise in their dark, hexagonal geometries, still sharp-edged after all this time, still waiting for the moss to finish its slow work. And somewhere in the colony’s distributed mind, the memory-tubes are dreaming in chemicals of the day the first inoculant bead was laid on bare stone, and the world began to green.

I settle into stillness. My frilled tip opens and closes, opens and closes, tasting the damp, chill air. The consensus is a quiet music. I am the Architect, and the Architect is me, and the garden is us, and everything is exactly as it should be.
