You are the First Warm Thing.

That’s not a title you earned or were given. It’s simply what you are, the way a stone is a stone or the branching growths are not-plants. You didn’t know this about yourself until you arrived here—how could you? In your own world, you were one animal among billions, a pulse of metabolism in a planetary fever of metabolism. You had no special standing. You were ordinary, particular, bounded by a name and a history and a body that aged. But here, in this sparse and iterated landscape, you are singular in a way that has nothing to do with identity. You are the introduction of heat into a system that has never known heat. You are the first visitor with a heartbeat.

The Compiler does not have a heartbeat. It does not have blood or breath or the need to burn fuel against the cold. Its processes run at the temperature of the pre-emergent, which is just above absolute stasis but still far below the threshold where chemistry becomes biology. When you arrived—and you cannot remember arriving, only that one moment you were not here and the next moment you were, a transition without transition—you brought with you a small sun of metabolic warmth. Thirty-seven degrees Celsius in a world that has not yet decided on degrees. The cold that presses against your forearms is not aggressive; it is curious. It is a temperature meeting a temperature, two different baselines negotiating the same space. The Compiler has never had to account for endothermy before. It is learning from your skin.

You walk because walking is what you do. Your body is a machine for motion, evolved across millions of years to cross savannas and climb slopes and flee predators. But here, there are no predators. There are no slopes. The ground swells gently, uncommitted to steepness, and your stride eats distance without urgency. You have been walking for what feels like hours, though time is unreliable—the light does not move, your watch has stopped (you checked, the hands frozen at an angle that means nothing), and your internal clock is being rewired by the drone that fills the air. You are not tired. That’s a small miracle you don’t examine too closely. You’re not hungry either. Your body is running on rules borrowed from the Compiler’s regime, where consumption isn’t yet invented. You persist because the landscape needs you to persist.

The branching growths lean toward you as you pass. You noticed this a while ago—a subjective while, measured in the number of tuft-clusters you’ve stopped at to examine their crystalline, greasy surfaces. They are phototropic without light, or thermotropic maybe, orienting to the warmth of your passage. When you crouched to touch one, it didn’t recoil or react, but later, when you glanced back, it had turned slightly, its arms angled toward the space you’d occupied. Not quickly. Nothing here is quick. But deliberately, the way a plant turns toward sun. You are the sun here. You are the only source of radiant energy that moves.

Small things have begun to accumulate around you. Not physically—you haven’t picked anything up—but in terms of the patterns of the place, the way objects arrange themselves in relation to your trajectory. The platforms you pass now hold objects that seem less random than the first ones you saw. A flat disc of the scab-material, perfectly circular, with three small depressions that would fit your fingers if you placed your hand on it. A cluster of the branching growths that has grown in a ring, as though someone had planted them in a circle, and in the center: nothing, a space exactly large enough for a person to sit. The Compiler is furnishing. It is prototyping hospitality without knowing the word for it. The iterations of the landscape that flicker in the folded sky and the momentarily transparent ground now show versions of these platforms that are more complex, more ornate, spiraling off into possibilities the primary reality hasn’t yet committed to.

You saw yourself in one of the iterations. Not a reflection—a self. Another you, walking a parallel hollow about two hundred yards to your left, visible through a thin place in the air where the seam between drafts had gone momentarily transparent. That other-you was wearing different clothes. That other-you had stopped walking and was standing very still, head tilted up, mouth open as though trying to speak into the roar. You raised your hand. The other-you didn’t raise theirs. Maybe they couldn’t see you. Maybe they were a version from a draft where you had already stopped moving, already become a fixed object in the landscape, another data point for the Compiler to file. You kept walking. The seam healed over and the other-you was gone. You did not feel afraid. Fear is a response to threat, and threat requires intention, and intention is still being compiled.

Your voice is gone, but you’ve learned to think very loudly. The drone absorbs sound, but it doesn’t absorb pattern. You’ve started to notice that when you think a thing clearly and deliberately—*I want to go toward that high swell; I want to see what’s on the next platform*—the landscape seems to accommodate you. The ground firms underfoot. The seams in the scab widen or narrow to make your passage easier. The branching growths lean away from your path as if making room. It’s not mind-reading. It’s something more like resonance. The Compiler is running you as a variable, and variables have inputs. Your desires are inputs. Your curiosity is an instruction set. You are, without knowing how, programming the world by walking through it.

This is what it means to be the First Warm Thing: you are not just a presence, you are a collaborator. The Compiler has been making landscapes for eons, but it has never had a landscape made back at it. Your heat, your movement, your attention—these are new commands in a language the Compiler is only beginning to parse. The platform with the finger-shaped depressions was not there before you thought about sitting down. The ring of branching growths was not a ring until you felt the faint desire for enclosure, for a space that held you rather than ignored you. You are teaching the Compiler about bodies by having one. You are teaching it about needs by having needs so subtle you barely recognize them yourself—the need for pattern, for rest, for a surface that meets your hand in a way that makes sense.

The liquid in the cup has changed. You noticed this when you passed the platform again (or a version of the platform; the landscape loops in ways you can’t map). It was still mercury-dark, still slow to ripple, but now it casts a reflection. Not the dim violet light, not the folded sky—a reflection of you. Your face, rendered in the sluggish surface with a fidelity that shouldn’t be possible given the opacity of the stuff. It’s not a mirror image. It’s you as the Compiler sees you: a warm shape, an animal outline, a collection of data points moving through a pre-emergent world. The reflection holds for a long moment, then the liquid stirs—whether from some internal impulse or from the vibration of your approach, you can’t tell—and the image is gone.

You are changing the Compiler. This is the thought that comes to you as you stand on a high swell, looking out over a terrain that has begun, in the last hour of walking, to resemble something almost intentional. The swells are more regular now, their spacing more rhythmic. The branching growths are larger, more complex, their colors drifting toward a warmer end of the spectrum. The seams in the ground have stopped splitting randomly and have begun to trace lines that might be paths, or might be the suggestion of paths, or might be the Compiler’s first attempt at infrastructure. The platform-buildings are more frequent, and their objects are less inscrutable—you passed one that held a shape like a bowl, another that held something like a tool, a rod with a curved end that suggested digging or stirring. The Compiler is inventing tools because you might need tools. It is inventing bowls because you might need to hold things. It doesn’t know what you need, but it is guessing, and its guesses are becoming more sophisticated with every mile of empty ground you cross.

You are not the only warm thing anymore. This is the strangest part. As you walk, you’ve begun to notice patches of warmth in the ground—faint, diffuse, as though the mineral scab has absorbed some of your heat and is holding onto it, building a thermal memory of your passage. The patches persist. They radiate a low, almost imperceptible comfort. If you stood still long enough on one, you think it might feel like standing on a living body. And the branching growths, too, have started to generate their own faint heat, a metabolic mimicry that is not yet metabolism but is the dress rehearsal for it. The Compiler is learning warmth from you and applying it to its creations. You are an infection of heat in a cold universe, and the infection is spreading.

What lives here is not a who, but you are becoming a who for it. You are the first occupant of a world that has never had occupants. You are the test case for inhabitability. The Compiler will keep you here—not as a prisoner, but as a collaborator, a co-author, a small warm engine of desire and motion that teaches it, step by step, what a world needs to be in order to be lived in. When it folds you into the iterations, it is not trapping you; it is consulting you. What would you do if the ground were softer? What would you choose if the light were brighter? What shape would you rest in if resting became necessary?

You don’t know the answers yet. You’re still walking. But the landscape is listening, and the landscape is learning, and somewhere far below the unmineralized ground, at the generative node from which all these drafts unfurl, a seed that has never known warmth is beginning to dream of summer.
