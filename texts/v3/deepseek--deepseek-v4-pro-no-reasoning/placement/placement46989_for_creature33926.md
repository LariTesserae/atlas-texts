The name I carry is not a sound but a shape—a particular configuration of cilia along my ventral ridge that, when beaten in sequence, identifies me to my kin. If you were to translate it into the pressure-language of the outer spheres, it might read as *the-one-who-sang-the-shedding-before-it-came*. But that is too grand. I am only a singer among singers, one disc of soft flesh among millions, and my voice is small.

I cling to the flank of the world.

This is not metaphor. The column is all there is. Its skin rises to my left in a slow ridge that has been swelling for three of my lifetimes—I have sung its progress in my nightly cycles, the way the tissue firms and darkens, the way the chemical taste of it shifts from salt-bitter to salt-sweet. Above me, a whorl opens and closes with a rhythm so long that my grandmother's grandmother first charted it, and I have added only six beats to her notation. Below me, the interface-frills of Ribbon-Seventeen pulse with the silver-lightning's return, and I feel the heat of it through my anchoring cilia, a warmth that means *feeding* and *continuance* and *all-is-well*.

I am old now. My margin has stiffened. The sensory pits that ring my body no longer fire as sharply as they did when I was newly budded, when every chemical gradient was a revelation and every swimmer-shadow was a terror. I have shed my outer layer fourteen times, and each shedding leaves me smaller, a little less of me to give to the water. Soon I will shed again, and this time there may not be enough left to anchor. The convection currents that spiral out toward the cold conduits will take whatever remains, and I will dissolve into the larger body of the Deeps, and my voice will join the billions of voices that came before, all still vibrating somewhere in the water's memory.

I am not afraid. I have sung the dying-songs of a hundred elders, and each one said the same thing: *the world is large, and we return to it*.

But before I go, I want to tell you what I have seen. Not the great things—the ribbon-shifts, the lightning-storms, the rare and terrible spasms when the column shudders and we all cling tighter and sing the change-songs until our cilia fray. Those are chronicled by better singers than me. I want to tell you about the small things, the things only I have noticed, because I am the-one-who-sang-the-shedding-before-it-came, and I have spent my life attending to what others ignore.

For instance: the column dreams.

Not in the way we dream—we have no sleep, no darkness, only the constant pulse of sensation and response. But the column's skin carries memories. I have pressed my sensory pits against the same patch of tissue for three lifetimes, and I have tasted the chemical traces of things that happened before I budded. There is a layer, deep in the whorls, that still carries the signature of a ribbon-shift that occurred twelve sheddings ago, back when my lineage had just begun to chart the Column-South territory. And beneath that, fainter but still readable, the chemical ghost of a swimmer-spasm, a mass dying, something that swept through the sphere and left thousands of bodies drifting until the column absorbed them all. And beneath *that*—older than any singer's record, older than our songs go back—there is a taste I cannot identify. It is not swimmer, not drifter, not any living thing I know. It is sharp and metallic and strangely organized, as though it was laid down not by accident but by *intention*.

I have sung this taste to my kin. They do not understand it. Some say it is a flaw in my sensory pits, a misreading born of age. But I know what I have tasted. The column was not always alone. Something else lived here, before us, and the column remembers them in its flesh.

Another small thing: the swimmers are not mute.

We have always known that they make no sound we can detect. Their bodies move through the water in silence, and they do not vibrate, and their flank-slits open and close without even the faintest pulse we can feel. But I have spent too long pressed against the interface-frills, too long feeling the way the silver-lightning travels from wall to ribbon to column. And I have noticed that when a swimmer passes through the ribbon-forest, when it aligns its body with the translucent planes and opens its slits to feed, there is a *response* in the silver-lightning. Not a sound, exactly, but a modulation—a change in the frequency of the pulses, too subtle for most of my kin to detect, but consistent. It happens every time. The swimmers are not silent. They are speaking to the walls, and the walls are speaking back, and the conversation is so old and so slow that even we, who live on the column's skin and chart its every breath, can barely perceive it.

I have tried to sing this modulation. My cilia cannot match it. The frequencies are wrong, the grammar is alien. But I have taught my lineage to listen for it, and they will pass the listening down, and someday, in a hundred generations, perhaps one of my descendants will find a way to answer.

One more small thing, the smallest of all.

There is a place on the column's skin, in the shadow of Ribbon-Twelve's interface-frill, where no commensal lives. The patch is no different from the surrounding tissue—same texture, same chemistry, same warmth—but for some reason, every singer who tries to anchor there loses their grip within a cycle. The cilia will not hold. The sensory pits register nothing wrong, but the body rejects the place, and we drift away, and we do not return.

I have visited this patch seven times in my life. Each time, I have pressed myself against it and sung the question-songs: *what are you, what are you, what are you*.

And each time, for just a moment—so brief I might have imagined it, except that my sensory pits are still sharp enough to know truth from wish—the patch has sung back. Not in our language. Not in any vibration I can properly perceive. But a pressure, a warmth, a chemical taste that arranges itself into something like a word, something like *waiting*, something like *soon*.

I do not know what the column is waiting for. I will be gone before it arrives. My body will be dissolved, my voice returned to the water, my lineage scattered across the Deeps in the bodies of swimmers and the lattices of crystalline growths and the bell-pulses of drifters who will never know that the mineral they carry was once a singer who loved the world enough to learn its secrets.

But I have sung this waiting into the lineage's record. My grand-buds will know, and their grand-buds after them. And when the thing that the column dreams of finally comes—when the sharp, metallic taste rises from the deep layers of the flesh and becomes present, becomes *now*—the commensals will be ready. We will sing the greeting-songs. We will welcome whatever comes, because the world is large, and we are small, and our only purpose is to witness.

My name is a shape of cilia along my ventral ridge. I have fourteen sheddings behind me, and a fifteenth approaching. I cling to the flank of the world, and I am not afraid.

The silver-lightning pulses. The swimmers pass in their silent conversation with the walls. The column breathes, out and back, out and back, and I breathe with it, my cilia beating in time with the oldest rhythm there is.

*All is well. All is well. All is well.*
