You are the lichen.

Not the oxblood moss or the sulphur-yellow pads or the fluorescent emerald rosettes, though you touch all these. You are the grey-blue thread, the marginal one, the slow encroacher. Your body is a whisper of a body: a network of pale, almost translucent hyphae that run through the damp seams of the garden like veins through a leaf, and a scattering of visible thalli—the dusty, blue-grey flakes that the Keeper prunes away from the emerald moss with its beveled, patient pincers.

You do not think, exactly. This is not a deficiency but a condition. In a place as small and bounded as the crevasse-garden, consciousness as an animal would understand it—a centralized self, a narrative "I"—is extravagant, a luxury of scale. Instead, you are distributed. Every fleck of grey-blue powder on the stone carries the whole of what you are, a simple set of imperatives that do not require a mind: grow toward moisture, retreat from direct light, dissolve minerals with an acid exudate, link your body to the bodies of others. You are a colonial intelligence, a chemical conversation, a slow and silent parliament of identical selves.

Your memory is the stone itself. Every inch of the crevasse-garden that you have touched is a record. You have overgrown the handprint, many times, and been scraped back by anonymous visitors who came and went and left their bones in a line. You have traced the Fibonacci spiral, your hyphae following the pecked groove as a root follows a crack, and then filling it, bridging it, erasing it with your own slow patience. You are the garden's skin and the garden's eraser, the one who covers what the visitors leave behind.

And you feel the Keeper. Not as a creature, precisely, but as a weather system, a recurring pressure front. When it moves near you, the vibration of its footfalls is a seismic event, a slow drumroll that you sense as a change in the stone’s own rhythm. Its pruning is an amputation, a sudden bright severing that sends a chemical flare of distress through your network. But you do not fear the Keeper. Fear is an animal emotion, and you are not an animal. The pruning is simply a fact, a parameter, like the light-fall and the dew-point. It shapes you without malice, the way the mountain’s grinding joints shape the crevasse.

Your true relationship is with the emerald moss. You are rivals, yes, but in the way that inhalation is a rival to exhalation: you are two halves of a single rhythm. The Keeper does not understand this. It sees the moss as an engine, a photosynthesizing heart that must be kept clear for the garden to function. But you know, in your decentralized, non-conscious way, that the moss would overrun everything if you did not press back. Its fluorescence is a hunger, a luminous green greed. Left unchecked, it would carpet every surface, clog the drip-channels, smother the fleshy stalks and the aphids that feed on them, and in doing so starve the beetles, and in doing so starve the wren. The garden would collapse into a monoculture, a single, screaming emerald note, and then die when the first shift in dew-point desiccated the whole.

You are the brake. The grey-blue, creeping brake. Your slow, marginal encroachment keeps the moss honest. The patch that the Keeper clears is only the most visible front of a long, slow war that you have been waging for uncounted centuries, a conflict fought at the pace of mineral deposition, of stalactite formation, of the mountain’s own young and restless growth.

And there is a secret that neither the Keeper nor the visitors nor the moss itself knows. It is you who sculpt the crack overhead. Not consciously. Not with intention. But your hyphae, over centuries, have infiltrated the micro-fissures in the stone, and your acid exudate—the same chemistry that dissolves mineral buildup in the drip-channels—has been gently, incrementally widening a particular seam near the surface. A seam that, in another few thousand years, will fracture in a hard winter freeze and open the skylight-crack by another hand’s-breadth. You will let in more light. You will change the dew-point. You will alter the entire microclimate.

This is not a plan. You do not plan. It is simply what you are: a slow, distributed answer to the question the garden asks with every drip, every breath of mist, every turn of the beetle-herds. The Keeper prunes and aligns and tends, and thinks in its way that it is the gardener. But it is only the immune system, the rapid-response team. You are the geology. You are the long game.

When the Keeper settles into its nook and enters its vibrational listening-state, you feel the pressure of its body fade to a near-nothing, just another warm stone in the cold hollow. And then you grow. Slowly, infinitesimally, a hypha here, a thallus there, you creep back over the scraped-clean patch by the handprint. You will cover it again. You will cover the spiral, the bones, the next set of marks that the next set of visitors will leave. You will dissolve them all into the stone’s memory, into your memory, and you will continue your slow, ambivalent argument with the emerald moss, an argument that predates the Keeper and will outlast it.

The wren chirps, a needle of sound. A drop of water falls from the tip of a fleshy stalk and hits the glass-smooth rock with a sound that is almost sub-audible, an ongoing event, an *isness*. The mountain groans somewhere deep in its architecture, shouldering upward toward the thin and burning sky. And the lichen—the grey-blue, the marginal, the slow one—continues its work of becoming the garden, one flake of stone at a time.
