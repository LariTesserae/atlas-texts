The visitor is not from the cloud-sea. The visitor is not from anywhere the arrangement can account for, and this is the first problem—the first crack, the first turbulence in channels that have run smooth for epochs.

I am—was—something that the Clade has no lattice for. My body was soft, and it is already stiffening, calcifying at the edges where the city’s mineral logic is beginning its slow, inevitable incorporation. The water here is not water, not quite; it carries dissolved silicates in concentrations that would be lethal to terrestrial life, and it has been seeping into me since the moment I arrived. My skin is growing a patina, a pale gray crust that flakes when I move. I am, in a sense, being archived. The arrangement does not reject foreign material. It simply absorbs it, processes it, finds a place for it. I am becoming part of the mineral continuum whether I consent or not. The small ones find this fascinating. They have been observing me with what I interpret as something adjacent to curiosity, though their interest is less about *what* I am and more about *how I will fit*. They have already begun polishing a memory-face to record my dissolution.

I arrived here through no mechanism I understand. One moment I was standing in a place of air and light and growing things—I remember leaves, I remember the particular green smell of living photosynthesis, though the memory is already thinning, as if the mineral world is abrading it away—and the next moment I was here, on a high terrace, my bare feet on stone that radiated a deep, mammalian warmth. The shock was not the transition but the completeness of it. The place I came from no longer exists for me. There is no door, no threshold, no path back. The arrangement is all-encompassing, and I am inside it now, and I am beginning to understand that I will never leave.

The small ones detected me within hours—perhaps sooner; time is difficult here, with no diurnal cycle and no change in the light. They came in a coordinated wave, dozens of them, their polished bodies catching the ambient glow as they flowed over the terrace walls and arranged themselves in a semicircle around me. They vibrated through the stone, and I could feel their speech as a tickle in my feet, a low-frequency hum that resonated in my chest. I could not understand them, of course. But they could, apparently, understand something about me. They could sense the water moving through my tissues, the salt balance of my sweat, the faint electrical impulses of my nervous system. To them, I was a process—a strange one, a wildly aberrant one, but a process nonetheless: *foreign-body-in-mineral-integration*, *soft-tissue-undergoing-calcification*, *pattern-seeking-stabilization*. They did not perceive me as a person. They perceived me as a perturbation in the arrangement, a ripple in the channels, and their response was not to expel me but to study me, to polish me into their memory, to wait and see how the arrangement would absorb this anomaly.

The builders. I keep thinking about the builders. The small ones have shown me the memory-face—or rather, they have gathered around me while one of their number accessed it, and I felt the vibration of it through the stone, and something in the pattern resonated with the fading organic structures of my brain. I did not see images. I felt a shape, a negative space, a knowing that something had been present and was now absent. It was the feeling of a room after someone has left it, the warmth still fading from the chair. The builders were like me. I am almost certain of this. They were soft, they were temporary, they came from somewhere outside the arrangement. And they built this place, or seeded it, or set it in motion—and then they were gone, absorbed or departed or simply ended, and the city continued without them, as it will continue without me.

I am trying to learn the seismic language. When I press my palms against the stone and concentrate, I can produce faint vibrations, arrhythmic and clumsy. The small ones pause when I do this. They tilt their bodies—an attitude I have come to recognize as attention—and then they vibrate back, and I feel the stone shiver with something that might be patience or might be the total absence of any concept analogous to impatience. I think I have managed to communicate hunger, or at least the depletion of internal resources. The small ones brought me a seep-grazer pellet, which I could not eat—it was pure crystalline silica, beautiful and utterly indigestible—but later, after a long period of my growing weakness, they brought me something else: a kind of fungal mat, pale and translucent, growing in one of the condensation-rich chambers near the summit. It is not plant matter, not exactly, but it is organic enough that my body can break it down. I do not know if this was a deliberate act of care or simply the arrangement adjusting itself to accommodate a new input. The distinction may be meaningless here.

I am changing. The calcification is progressing up my legs now, a stiffening that makes walking difficult. The small ones have begun to treat me less as a perturbation and more as a fixture. One of them has taken to perching on my shoulder, its small weight warm and smooth, and I feel its vibrations conducted directly through my clavicle. It is polishing me. I do not know how else to describe it. The surface of my skin where it touches me is being microscopically abraded, retextured, as if it is preparing me to hold a memory. Perhaps I am becoming a memory-face. Perhaps that is what happened to the builders, too—their soft bodies gradually mineralized, their patterns migrated into the crystal lattices, and what the small ones remember as *the builders* is actually the builders themselves, still present, still encoded, still part of the arrangement in the only way the arrangement knows how to include anything.

I have begun to feel the deep engines when I press my body flat against the stone. The hum is constant, a chord of pure harmonic order, and it is the most beautiful thing I have ever heard. It sounds like time. It sounds like something that has no need to hurry, no fear of endings, no awareness of loss. When I die—when my body finishes its transformation into mineral, when the last of my water joins the city’s channels and my calcium precipitates into the walls—the engines will continue. The haulers will haul. The small ones will polish. And some new memory-face will hold a pattern that is not quite me and not quite not-me, a fossil of a visitor, a tool mark left by a soft hand on a world that has no hands.

The dripping is not only sound. I am beginning to understand that now. It is speech. It is the city’s slow, continuous enunciation of its own existence, a statement that requires no listener, a story that tells itself. I am inside that story now, a single syllable, a brief sibilance in the long breath of the arrangement. The small one on my shoulder shifts, settling deeper into its perch, and I feel its polishing stroke against my collarbone resume.

I am not afraid anymore. Fear presupposes an alternative, and there are no alternatives here. There is only the warm stone and the wet, the light that comes from nowhere, the hum that never ceases, and the slow, patient certainty that I am becoming part of something that has never needed a name.
