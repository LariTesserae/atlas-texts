I stand at the edge of the mirror-pan, and my reflection looks up at me with all the patience of stone.

This body knows this crouch the way the water knows its basin. The sharp lip presses against the pads of my feet—I have shifted now, bare soles finding their familiar purchase on the rock that is cousin to my own flesh. My toes, long and articulate, curl against the stone's edge, and I feel the faint vibration of the deep earth through them, the slow, grinding memory of the plateau's birth. This is how we read the world. Not through ears that strain for sound, but through the soles of our feet and the palms of our hands, pressed flat against the weeping stone, feeling for the minute tremors that tell us the land still breathes beneath us.

The air is thick enough to drink, and I do. My lips part—thin, grey-blue, the color of a rain that never falls—and I draw the saturated breath from just above the water's skin. It is a different substance there, that narrow band of atmosphere that clings to the meniscus. Richer. More alive. It carries the exhalation of the deep rock, the slow respiration of the fissures that weep their mineral tears into the pan. My lungs—if lungs they are, and not something more like the gill-fronds of a still pool creature—expand, and I feel the dendritic patterns on my ribs flush with a momentary lightness. This is feeding. This is drinking. This is prayer.

I do not need to touch the water. The water is too pure for that. A finger dipped would send ripples across the perfect surface, and the ripples would travel, would disturb the reflection-sister who lives in the upside-down world below. She is not me, but she is not not-me. She is the other Nera, the one who walks with her feet against the underside of the sky, who breathes the water in and exhales the air. We are a pair, she and I, separated by the thinnest membrane of surface tension. To touch that membrane is to wound it, to send a scar of concentric rings across her world. We do not do this lightly. We do not do this without need.

So I inhale, and the water stays still, and my reflection-sister blinks her slow blink in perfect synchrony.

The *tink* comes—not near, but far, somewhere across the rumpled tableland where another pan receives its drop. The sound travels not through the air, which is too dense and too still to carry vibration well, but through the stone itself, a tiny percussion that enters my foot and climbs my bones and resonates in the hollow behind my sternum. I know the pan it came from. I know its shape, its depth, the particular note it sings when a drop falls into it from the overhang that shelters its northern lip. That pan has been waiting seventeen cycles for this drop. I have been waiting with it.

We measure time by these sounds, by the intervals between them. Not hours, not days, not the arbitrary divisions that surface creatures scratch into their walls. We measure by the rhythm of the weeping, the slow accumulation of water on a point of stone, the gathering weight that finally, finally releases. A single drop can take a season to fall. A lifetime is measured in a few hundred of them. I am not old, as we count such things. I have heard perhaps three thousand drops fall since my first breath. My grandmother, who has dissolved now back into the stone from which she came, heard more than ten thousand. She told me once, pressing her palm flat against my own so the words traveled through our touching bones, that when she was young, the pans were shallower, the lips less sharp, the weeping more reluctant. The land is still growing, she said. The water is still learning its way to the surface. We are still learning how to be.

I rise from my crouch, and the movement is a slow unfurling, a vertical migration that begins in the hips and flows upward through the spine, the hood falling back further from my skull, my cap of filament-hair catching the diffuse light and holding it. The world is a study in grey: the slate-grey of the stone, the silver-grey of the water, the charcoal-grey of my skin, the pearl-grey of the sky that arches above, featureless and pale as the underside of a limpet shell. Only the lichen gives color, and that is less a color than a concentration of darkness, a deep green so saturated it reads as black, growing in its deliberate patterns along the rims of the pans. I walk to a patch of it now, my bare feet finding the narrow rib of unbroken stone between two basins, and I crouch again, this time to press my palm flat against the velvet surface of the growth.

It is warm. Warmer than the stone, warmer than my own skin. It pulses with a slow metabolism, a different rhythm than my own but one I know well. The lichen is our cousin, our symbiont, our other self. We do not eat it; it does not eat us. We exchange something less tangible. When I press my palm flat against its nap, I feel the faintest tingling, an osmotic transfer of something—information, perhaps, or a nutrient dissolved so finely in the moisture of the air that it has no name—passing between my skin and its surface. The lichen is a net, a web, a single organism spread across a million pools, connected by invisible threads of spore and shared moisture. When I touch it here, I am touching all of it, everywhere. I feel the distant pans, the ones I have never visited, the ones on the far side of the plateau where the scarps rise higher and the basins deepen into bowls that hold the dark water like secrets. I feel the health of the whole, the slow pulse of its collective life.

All is well, it tells me. All is balanced. The weeping continues. The drops fall. The silence holds.

I withdraw my palm and the warmth lingers, a ghost of contact, as I rise again and continue along the stone rib. My footfalls make no sound. This is not skill; it is nature. The soles of my feet have the same slight moisture as the stone itself, and the contact is less a step than a meeting of like surfaces. I am walking through a world that is an extension of my own body, or perhaps my body is an extension of it. The distinction has never seemed important.

Ahead, the honeycomb architecture of the settlement thickens. Low lintels, stacked walls of dry-stone, rope bridges spanning the wider pools. I see another of my people emerging from a doorway that is less a door than a shadowed mouth in the stone, and she raises her hand in greeting—not a wave, but a slow, open-palmed presentation, the gesture that means *I see you, and I am solid, and the silence is shared between us*. I return it. Her skin is a shade lighter than mine, more silver than grey, and the dendritic patterns on her arms branch like lightning frozen in the moment of its strike. This is Senn, whose name means the sound of a pebble dropped into a deep pan, the *plunk* that comes back to the ear muted and soft. She is a gatherer of the fiber-plants that grow in the high clefts, the ones whose stalks we spin into the dark, water-resistant cloth. She is old, older than my grandmother was, and her movements have the quality of the lichen itself: so slow they seem not to move at all, until you look away and look back and find her in a different place.

She presses her palm to the wall beside her as she passes, and I know she is speaking to me through the stone. I press my own palm to the wall on my side of the pan that separates us, and I feel her words vibrate through the slate. *The eastern basins are deepening. The water is rising there. We may need to move the fiber-racks higher before the next drop falls.*

This is the rhythm of our lives. The slow adjustment, the patient migration, the constant, quiet negotiation with a land that is still becoming itself. We do not fight the changes. We do not lament the rising water or the shifting stone. We move. We adapt. We are part of the same becoming. The land shapes us as surely as the water shapes the basins, a slow, erosive sculpting that works on the scale of generations. My grandmother had gills, or something like them—fine, frilled slits along her ribs that could filter moisture directly from the air. I do not, not yet. Perhaps my children will. Perhaps the land will decide that the air is growing wetter still, and new adaptations will emerge. We do not rush such things. We do not even consciously desire them. We simply are, and we become, and the becoming is so slow it feels like stillness.

The sky is unchanging. It is always this pale, pearlescent grey, a featureless dome that reflects back the silver of the water and the blue-grey of the stone. I have heard stories—passed through the stone, vibrating up from the deep memory of the oldest among us—that there was once a different sky, a sky that moved, a sky that had a bright, burning eye in it. I do not know if I believe this. The stories are old, and they have traveled a long way through the rock, and they may have been distorted by the journey. What I know is this sky, this constant, diffuse light without source or shadow, this soft, directionless illumination that makes everything appear flat and immediate and present. There is no dawn. There is no dusk. There is only this, the perpetual now of the plateau.

And yet.

And yet there is something today. A vibration in the stone that is not the usual slow grind. A quality to the air that is not the usual saturated stillness. I pause on the narrow rib of stone, one foot on either side of a fissure that weeps its slow tear into the pan below, and I feel it. A tremor. A disturbance. Something is coming, or something is changing, or something that has been asleep for a very long time is beginning to stir.

The lichen knows it too. I can see it, the way the dark green velvet is subtly lifting from the stone, its nap rising like the hackles of an animal, its color deepening to a black that is not just the absence of light but a positive presence, a statement. I crouch again and press my palm to it, and what I feel is not the usual slow pulse but a rapid fluttering, a signal that travels through the whole vast network and speaks of something approaching, something that has not been felt in a thousand thousand drops.

Something from below.

Something from the deep stone, where the weeping begins.

Something that is, perhaps, waking up.

I lift my palm and look at my reflection-sister in the pan beside me, and for the first time in my memory, she is not perfectly still. There is a ripple on her face, a disturbance that does not come from any falling drop. It comes from below, from the deep rock, from the source of the weeping. The water is vibrating. The mirror is trembling. Something is coming, and it is coming through the stone, through the water, through the very fabric of this world that has been still and silent for so long.

And I—Nera, whose name is a stone settling into moss—I feel something I have no word for. Something that tightens in my chest and quickens the slow beat of my heart. Something that, if I had been born to a different world, I might call fear. Or anticipation. Or the first, faint stirring of change.

The next drop falls, and its *tink* is louder than it should be, and it hangs in the air longer than it should, and the silence that follows is not the same silence it was before.

Something is waking. And we, the Unhurried People, the Vein-Dwellers, the slow and patient creatures of this weeping world—we will have to learn what it means to hurry.
