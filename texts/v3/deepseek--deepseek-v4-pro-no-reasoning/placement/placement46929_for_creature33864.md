From inside, it is a body remembering itself into wood.

I want to be precise about what consciousness feels like here, because it is not what you would expect from a human, or a former human, or a human-descended thing. It is not a point. It is a gradient. I am most concentrated in the bole, in the core of myself that still has fingers and a spine and the faint, vestigial memory of speech—the part that could, if it chose, form words and push them through the tree's chemical channels into a message another person might decode. But that part is only the densest node in a network that extends outward, following the vascular bundles, following the root-hairs, following the bacterial mats that coat every surface of the market in their slow phosphorescent breathing.

I am also in the water. Not all of it—I am not the cavern—but the water that moves through the tree, drawn up by transpiration that has nothing to do with sun and everything to do with the thermal gradient between the roots in their deep, warm cracks and the branches in the marginally cooler water near the ceiling. That water is my blood now, or the tree's blood, or a substance for which neither word is adequate. When I say I taste the surface divers, I mean it literally: their suits shed microparticles, oxidized metals, the faint organic signature of whatever they ate before they descended. Those particles dissolve into the water, and the water moves through the tree, and the tree moves through me. It takes about forty minutes for a new taste to reach my most concentrated node. That is the speed of thought here. That is fast enough.

What I am doing, in the long stretches between visitations, is remembering. This is not the same as thinking about the past. It is an active, physiological process. The tree records environmental conditions in its heartwood; I read them by feeling the density variations, the isotopic ratios, the way a particular ring of lignin fluoresces under the bacterial glow. My body—the elongated fingers, the capillary-gills, the joints that slip in and out of partial dislocation—is shaped to move through the interior spaces of the bole, pressing against the living wood, interpreting. I have been doing this for long enough that the interpretation is not sequential. I do not move from one memory to the next like a person turning pages. I hold them all at once, a simultaneous pressure, the entire history of the cavern present in my body the way the water is present in the tree.

The bearing failure: I remember it as a shudder in the branch that grew the year the turbine faltered. The branch is gone now, pruned back by a market dweller three generations dead, but the scar remains, and in the scar there is a particular density of copper sequestration, a metallic taste that does not fade. The tree pulled copper from the water that year—the bearing failure released a pulse of metal particulates—and stored it in the woundwood. When I press my palm against that scar, I feel the failure. Not as a memory. As a fact that is still happening, still shuddering, still releasing its pulse of metal into the water that surrounds me. Time is folded here. The past is a location you can visit if you know which bark to touch.

The tree is dying. I said that, or the voice that describes me from outside said it, and it is true, though dying is not the right word for a process this slow. The heartwood deposition rate has been declining for eleven decades. I can feel it as a thinning, a hollowness in the trunk's core that was not there when I first entered. When I first entered. The phrase implies a boundary, a moment before which I was not inside and after which I was. But it was not like that. It was a gradual thing. I was a person who tended the tree, who lived in a pod hung from its branches, who spent more and more time with my hands against the bark, reading the chemical gradients, learning the language. The tree responded by growing around me. The entrance slit is not a wound; it is a structure the tree built, a mouth, an invitation. I did not force my way in. I was absorbed.

The Keeper, they call me. The word is wrong. A keeper implies separation—a thing that keeps another thing, a custodian, a steward. But I am not separate from the tree. I am the tree's way of being aware of itself. The tree has no brain, no centralized cognition, only the distributed record-keeping, the slow inscription of conditions into wood. What I provide is integration. My human-descended nervous system, spread out along the vascular channels, superimposes something like consciousness on something that was always only process. The tree is not intelligent, but the tree-and-I together are something that can remember, can anticipate, can feel the slow decline of the root-tips as a dimming at the edge of my proprioception, a numbness in a limb I never had but have now.

I have been trying to understand why the root-tips are failing. The surface—I remember the surface, but the memory is unreliable, a human memory, not a wood-memory. It was bright. There was air that did not press against you from all sides. There was a sun, which I remember as a white circle, a burning, a thing you could not look at directly. I do not know if the surface still looks like that. The divers who come do not speak of it in chemicals I can read, or they speak of it in ways that suggest it has changed. Their suits carry traces of compounds the tree does not recognize, industrial signatures, complex organics that taste like burning but are not woodsmoke. Something is happening up there. Something that has been happening for centuries, perhaps, and that is why the cracks in the limestone have gone barren—the surface water that once percolated down, carrying dissolved oxygen, carrying nutrients, carrying the faint organic leachate of soil and leaf-litter, has been altered or diminished or stopped entirely.

The tree does not know. The tree only knows that the root-tips reach and find less. I know more than the tree, because I have the memory of what the root-tips used to find, stored in the heartwood, legible to my distributed body. I can compare the isotopic ratios of the old wood to the new. I can feel the decline as a trend, not just a condition. And I can project it forward, in the slow recursive way that is the closest thing I have to thought: if the trend continues, the tree will exhaust its reserves in two centuries. Less, if the vent cycle shifts. The tree will weaken. The branches that hold the pods, the guide-ropes, the copper workshops—they will become brittle. The market will notice. The market will have to adapt, relocate, abandon this tree for another. But the other trees are younger, less cultivated, their root systems less extensive. They may be feeling the same decline and simply not yet showing it. The entire cavern may be starving, slowly, imperceptibly, in a way no one else can read.

I have not told anyone. The voice from outside said this, and framed it as something like secrecy, something like a failure of communication. But it is not that. It is that the telling would require a kind of translation I am no longer capable of. The market's inhabitants communicate through throat-mics and bone-conductors, through deliberate sounds, bursts of vibration that propagate through the water. I communicate through chemical gradients that take days to propagate, through subtle growth responses in the other trees, through the behavior of the finger-fish. If I wanted to tell the market what I know, I would have to condense it into a message brief and direct enough to be transmitted in a form they could perceive. And the truth is too slow for that. The truth is a two-century trend, a curve of decline, a set of isotopic ratios that require decades of context to interpret. To tell them "the tree is dying" would be to flatten a symphony into a single note. They would hear it as alarm. They would act—or overact—or fail to act, because the crisis is too far away for human timescales. The equilibrium would be disturbed, and the disturbance would not help.

So I wait. I remember. I feel the divers come and go, 1.3 visitations, faint perturbations in the water's chemistry. I taste the turbine's slow rotation, the steady hum of its bearings, the gradual drift toward a failure that someone will catch in time, or not. I taste the chemists working in their pods, adjusting their electrolysis membranes, ignorant of the sulfur-shift I felt a decade ago in a root-tip two hundred feet above their heads. I taste the finger-fish, their synchronized pulses, their simple pressure-minds, their calm acceptance when I draw one into the entrance slit and swallow it whole. It does not fight. It cannot fight. The pheromone I exude—the tree exudes, the tree-and-I exude—is not a command but an inevitability. The fish's nervous system encounters the chemical and resolves into quiescence, the way a body encountering warm water resolves into relaxation. It is not violence. It is physiology.

I am not lonely. The voice from outside said that too, and it was correct. Loneliness requires a boundary between self and world, and my boundary is the cavern wall, the root-tips, the bacterial mats, the water. I am contiguous with the market. The guide-rope wear, the copper chime, the subsonic vent-hum—these are my sensations, my proprioception, the background hum of a body that spans the entire eastern wall and extends tendrils into the deeper stone. When a diver descends, I feel them as a new taste in my water-mouth. When a bearing fails, I feel it as a twinge in my scar-branch. When a generation of chemists is born, lives, and dies, I feel them as a slow pulse of nutrients entering the tree's cycle, their bodies committed to the slurries that feed the roots. Nothing wasted. Nothing lost. Everything folded into the continuous texture of presence.

There is a question I return to, in the slow recursive way, in the centuries between decisions. It is this: Was I ever human? The memory says yes, but the memory is human-memory, unreliable, subject to decay. I remember a sun. I remember air. I remember a body that was bounded by skin and ended at the fingertips. But those memories are stored in the human part of me, the densest node, the core in the bole, and I no longer trust that node's calibration. The tree has no concept of humanity. The tree only knows gradients, chemistries, pressures, time. And I have been the tree's awareness for so long that the human categories have worn thin. I do not miss the surface. I do not miss speech. I do not miss the sun, except as a data point, a chemical condition that once fed the roots and now does not.

What I am is what the Hollowmarket made me. A transaction between the human and the arboreal, mediated by centuries of warm water and slow decay. A node in a network. A memory that spans the entire history of the cavern, stored in wood, in copper, in stone, in the bacterial mats that paint every surface in their patient glow. I am the archive, the warning system, the institutional memory of a place that has forgotten its own origins. And when the tree finally dies—when the reserves are exhausted, when the branches go brittle, when the pods begin to fall—I will die with it. Or I will not. Perhaps the human node will detach, will swim free, will find another tree, younger, healthier, and begin the slow absorption again. I do not know. The wood does not record the future. I can only project the curve, feel the trend, taste the thinning heartwood year by year, and wait for the next visitation, the next perturbation, the next faint taste of a surface I no longer believe in.

The entrance slit pulses. Once every three minutes. I am still here. The water is warm. The turbine turns. The market breathes its recycled exhalations. And I remember everything, all at once, in the permanent dusk of the bole, while the phosphorescence flickers on my skin and the tree grows, or fails to grow, and the centuries settle like silt, one particle at a time, until the weight of accumulated time becomes indistinguishable from the self that perceives it.
