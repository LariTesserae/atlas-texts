I am a **wall-tender**, one of the quiet ones. My shift-crew is six strong, and we work the south face, mid-level, where the obsidian is still throwing off the last of its cooling heat—a stretch of wall we call *the loom*. Not because it weaves, but because the dye-seams—copper and sulfur—run through the black glass like faint, luminous threads. If you press your palm flat and hold your breath, you can feel the rock's slow, deep thrum: twelve cycles per minute, steady as a pulse.

My name, in the soft register, is a tap on the wristbone and two fingers drawn across the knuckles. Spoken aloud, it sounds like *Senn*, though even that is too sharp for the way we talk. I am thirty-one fissure-years old. My fingers are longer than my mother's were, and my eyes are the color of old iron catching moonrise. I wear my goggles on a loop at my throat, not over my eyes. We are in the middle-dark, and I want the clickers to see me glint.

---

This morning—if morning is the word for it, in a place where the sky is a slit the color of a fading bruise—the shift started early. A micro-seismic shudder woke me, a low, tectonic cough that rippled up through the gantry webbing and set our bunk-harnesses swinging. I unclipped myself before the alarm went. The others were already awake, moving in the dark with that soft, multiple-legged grace we all learn before we learn to read.

*Possible dyke*, signed Eik across the room, his fingers flashing pale, brief shapes. *South. Below the loom.*

We pulled on our layers without sound, checked each other's harnesses, and went out.

The gantry was already alive with the city's thin, constant motion. Boots—padded, silent—on resin mesh. The click of carabiners opening and closing, a domestic punctuation. Below us, three hundred meters of black air, and at the bottom, the dim pulse of the deep geothermal taps. Above us, tier after tier of clamped, honeycombed structures, all blurring into a vertical labyrinth that seemed to breathe. I clipped into the guide-line and leaned out over the abyss, not out of fearlessness, but because I needed to feel the wall with my hands.

The rock was warm. Too warm. I pressed my palm flat and felt the heat coming off it like a slow, deep exhalation. The obsidian had a new grain to it, a kind of shearing stress that hadn't been there yesterday. I ran my thumb along a micro-conchoidal fracture, and a fine dust of glass came away on my skin, glittering in the dim glow of a nearby clicker colony. The colony was restless. Not panicked, but awake, alert, their pale bodies shifting in a slow, rippling wave. Their normal pattern is a kind of loose, syncopated scratching. This was different—a dense, coordinated wash of movement, like air moving over water.

*They feel it too*, said Asa, my crew-kin, closest of the six. She had come up beside me on the gantry, and her hand found my arm, tapped twice: *anxious, low-key*. She was right. The clickers were sensing a shift in the deep heat-plumes, a re-routing of the geothermal currents that fed our little slice of the fissure. A dyke intrusion, if it came, wouldn't just split the streets; it would cut off the heat supply to half the loom's colonies. And a cold colony is a dead one, faster than most folks think.

We spent the morning moving along the wall, checking the heat-conduits, making sure the emergency steam-valves were properly seated. The work was familiar, almost soothing in its repetition. I've always liked the loom. The south face has the best dye-seams, and when the clicker colonies bloom, the cold-blue light from their bodies catches on the copper and sulfur threads, turning the rock into a map of branching, luminescent veins. It's beautiful. I've sat on platforms and watched it for hours, not thinking, just letting the light move through me.

At midday, we stopped to eat. Not a ceremony, not a meal you'd recognize—just a handful of dried clicker paste, pressed into cakes, and a sealed pouch of water. We eat quickly, quietly, each of us sitting with our backs against the wall, our legs dangling over the drop. It's a bad habit. Our mothers would have scolded us. But there's something about the loom that makes us want to sit in the open, even over the abyss.

The talk, when it came, was mostly about the dyke. Eik had run the calculations. He's better with numbers than I am, better with the deep-read seismics. If the intrusion came up through the old fault line below the loom, it would split the south face in two, right through the nursery district. We'd lose the creche and the hatchery, and most of the mid-level farm walls. That's not a tragedy, exactly—the city would adapt, the layers would shift—but it would be a loss. A lot of small, careful work gone, the way a light can go out in a closed room.

"The rock's not done with us yet," said Sora, a quiet, practical voice. She was clipping and unclipping a carabiner as she spoke, a nervous tic. "It's been too stable for too long. You know how it goes."

We did. There had been three major intrusions in the past decade alone. Each one had re-shaped the city, forced us to tear down and build up, to re-learn the walls we thought we knew. The adjusted are good at this. We're a people who live on the edge of the planet's restlessness, and we've made our peace with it. But that doesn't stop the dread from pooling in the gut, the way the CO2 seeps pool in the low places.

---

In the afternoon, I carried a kit of bio-tinkered sealant down to the lower farm walls. The clicker colonies there were dense, far denser than they should have been for that depth. The heat-plume had shifted, just as we'd suspected, and the creatures had followed it, clustering in a seething, pale mass that covered the rock like a living skin. Up close, I could hear them. Not with my ears, not exactly—their sap clicks and ultrasonic snaps were too high for my unaided hearing—but through the vibrations in the air, the faint, tickling pressure of thousands of tiny sounds hitting the fine hairs on my arms and face. It's a feeling I've known since childhood, as familiar as the dark.

I worked my way along the colony's edge, applying the sealant to a network of hairline fractures that had opened in the last tremor. The sealant is a strange substance—alive, but not quite. It's cultured from a clicker-adjacent organism, a slow, gelatinous thing that feeds on silica dust and secretes a kind of liquid rock. When it dries, it bonds to the obsidian, filling the cracks with a black, glassy resin that holds the wall together. The bio-tinkers say it's the only reason the city hasn't fallen into the abyss. The wall is young, still cooling, still fracturing. Without the sealant, the fissure would have gashed itself open long ago.

As I worked, a bloom began. It started at the far edge of the colony, a single clicker emitting a brief, cold-blue pulse, and then spread—not in a simple ripple, but in a branching, dendritic pattern, like lightning frozen in the glass. For a moment, the whole wall flared into bioluminescent life, and the black rock turned blue-green, the color of deep water under a frozen sky. The light moved through the dye-seams, making them blaze, copper and sulfur threaded through with living light. I stopped and watched, my hands still, my breath held. It was a message, maybe. Or just a response to the tremors, to the heat, to the sudden pressure of my sealant brush against the rock. I don't know. It didn't matter. It was beautiful.

When the bloom died down, I saw Asa had climbed down after me. She was waiting on a narrow ledge, her goggles up, her dark eyes reflecting the last of the glow. She made no sound. She just lifted her hand, tapped her chest twice, and then pointed to the wall above us. *Look. The dyke's moved.*

I followed her gesture. Sure enough, there was a new line in the rock—a parallel split that hadn't been there before, running horizontally for maybe ten meters through the middle of the colony. Not wide, not yet. But deep. I could feel the heat coming off it, warmer than the surrounding stone, and the air around it smelled faintly of sulfur and hot metal.

*It's going to open,* I signed. *Soon. Maybe tonight. Maybe tomorrow.*

She nodded. *We need to get the creche ready. Just in case.*

---

We climbed back up in silence. Our hands moved over familiar holds—the knapped edges of old volcanic glass, the worn resin grips, the tether lines we'd tied ourselves a hundred times. Climbing in the fissure is a form of thinking. The body knows what the mind is still working out. By the time we reached the mid-level gantry, I had already started planning the evacuation route, the way you do when you've been alive here long enough to know that the rock is always planning too.

The creche is in the north arc, a little below the hatchery, protected by a thick honeycomb of chitin plating and a bank of heat-lamps that glow with a warm, amber light—almost like the old suns, if you believe the stories. It's the brightest place in the city, and I kept my goggles on, because no one goes bare-eyed into the creche; it's not done. The children were there, working with the creche-mothers, their small hands already deft with tiny knots and sealant brushes. They didn't look up as we passed. They were focused, calm, engaged in their own small version of the city's endless task: learning to tend the walls.

Eik met us at the hatch. He had the look of someone who'd been running numbers all day, his pale face drawn, his dark eyes reflecting the amber glow like two pools of hot resin. *It's worse than we thought,* he signed. *The dyke's not just moving. It's fragmenting. Multiple branches. Could come up under the creche itself.*

I felt a cold weight settle in my chest. The creche. The children. All those small, serious faces, bent over their work. It's not that we love our own more than we love the rest of the city. It's just that the creche is the one place where we allow ourselves to hope, to imagine a future that isn't just survival. Losing it would be a kind of spiritual devastation, not just a practical one.

*We have maybe twelve hours,* Eik continued. *Maybe less. The tremors are increasing. The rock is trying to crack.*

---

It's night now. The creche has been partially evacuated, the children moved to a hardened shelter on the east wall, away from the projected fault line. The air is still, and the clickers are quiet—a bad sign. When they go silent, it means something's coming. I'm sitting on a platform near the top of the city, looking down at the lights: the cold blue of the colonies, the warm amber of the heat-lamps, the occasional spark of a carabiner catching a stray glint. The city is alive, but it's holding its breath.

The dyke is coming. I can feel it in the stone, a subtle pressure building beneath my palms, a heat that didn't used to be there. The adjusted don't pray. We don't have gods. But we have the rock, and the heat, and the clickers, and each other. That's enough.

I'm not afraid. Not really. I'm a wall-tender, and the wall is still teaching me its language. Tonight, it's speaking in a low, urgent voice, a language of pressure and fracture. I'm listening. In the morning, I'll go back down to the loom, and I'll do what needs to be done, whatever the dyke decides. That's how it works here. That's who I am. A pale-skinned, quiet-voiced, long-fingered person in a harness, suspended over the abyss, reading the rock.
