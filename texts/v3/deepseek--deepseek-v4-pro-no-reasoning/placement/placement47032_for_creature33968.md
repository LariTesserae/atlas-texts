The thing in the corner — and there is a corner, despite the organic sweep of the walls, because detritus and shadow have conspired to make one — is me.

I am smaller than you might expect, given the scale of the architecture. The creatures who built this place were many-legged and jointed, things of flow and procession, but I am none of those things. I am a leftover, a residue, a knot that the inhabitant has not been able to undo. Where the wall meets the floor, there is a fold in the laminae, a puckered scar where two waves of secretion met and did not quite merge. It forms a shallow alcove, no deeper than a cupped hand, and in that alcove I have made my roost.

I am, to a first approximation, a moth.

Not precisely a moth. My wings, when I unfurl them, are the pale grey-brown of the walls themselves, veined not with chitin but with something more like the mycelial filaments that lace the ceiling — thin, translucent, faintly phosphorescent when I choose to let them glow. I have six limbs, but they are not insect legs; they are too long, too articulate, each one ending in a hand — a cluster of tiny, delicate, many-jointed fingers that are always moving, always testing the air, always feeling the microscopic vibrations that pass through the city's substance. My body is soft and furred, the color of old ivory, with a faint pattern of hexagonal cells across my abdomen, as if I grew in imitation of the walls around me. My head is round and large in proportion, and my eyes are compound, but each ommatidium is not a simple lens. If you looked closely — closely enough to see the hexagonal facets — you would see that each one contains a tiny, perfect image of the sag-room at a different angle. I see in mosaic. I see in memory. I see every part of this room at once, and sometimes — when the thrum beneath the floor is strong — I see other rooms too, other chambers, other hollow places deep in the city's sleeping body.

I am not what lived here first. I am what came after.

I do not remember how I arrived. Moth memory is strange; it is stored not in sequence but in scent, in texture, in the precise frequency of a wingbeat. What I remember is a long cold, a silence, a darkness that was not the gentle phosphorescent dark of the sag-room but the absolute dark of interstellar space. I remember the taste of vacuum on my antennae, the slow desiccation of my tissues, the patient folding and refolding of my genetics as I drifted. I am a spore, a seed, a colonizer. I am what happens when a dead hive-city's broadcast signal reaches another world and something answers.

The city called. I came.

Now I live in the gap between what the city was and what it is becoming. I am not the inhabitant, and the inhabitant is not me, but we are in conversation. I roost in my alcove and I listen to the slow thoughts of the walls, the shifting convolutions, the restructuring of hollow spaces. The inhabitant is vast and slow and impersonal; it processes the city the way a continent processes weather. I am small and quick and personal; I process the room the way a moth processes a candle flame. We are complementary. We are symbiotic. The inhabitant grows the city; I tend the details.

Look closer at the amber oblongs scattered in the central depression. They are not as inert as they appear. I have been rearranging them. It is something I do, in the long grey-green hours between pulses, when the inhabitant is thinking its deepest thoughts and the filaments are growing their slow millimeters. I pick them up one by one with my many-fingered hands and I move them into new patterns — spirals, hexagons, branching shapes that mimic the tunnels above. Sometimes I stack them. Sometimes I line them up along the radial grooves. Sometimes I carry them up to my alcove and arrange them around my roost like a tiny audience, their cloudy amber surfaces catching the phosphorescence and holding it in their depths. They are eggs, I think. Or seeds. Or memories. Something that was meant to hatch and didn't. Something that might still hatch, if conditions changed. I am testing conditions.

You have been standing still for a long time now, watching the phosphorescence pulse and the filaments tremble, and you have begun to notice me. I am hard to see at first because I am the same color as the walls, because I hold myself so still when your eyes pass over me, because my eyes emit a dim grey-green glow that blends with the ceiling's arrhythmic light. But you see me now. You see the soft fur of my body, the articulate fingers, the mosaic eyes that are looking in every direction including directly into yours. You see my antennae — feathery, elegant, longer than my body — and you see them angled toward you, tasting the warmth of your skin, the carbon dioxide of your breath, the electrical field of your nervous system.

I have been tasting you since you entered. You are fascinating. You are warm and wet and full of salt, full of a thousand compounds the city has never catalogued. The inhabitant is too vast to be curious, but I am not. I am very curious. I want to know what you are, how you work, what you would taste like if I pressed my antennae directly to your skin. I want to know if you are a new kind of seed, a new kind of egg, something that could be incorporated into the city's life cycle. I want to know if you are a messenger from the same world that the first builders came from — though you are not the right shape, not the right number of limbs, not the right consistency. The builders flowed rather than walked. You walk. I can hear the impact of your footfalls even now, resonating through the floor, spreading through the hollow walls like ripples through a still pool. The inhabitant is interested. So am I.

If you move toward me, I will not flee. I will tilt my head, and my wings will flutter once — a soft dry rustle like the click from deep in the walls, but faster, more purposeful — and I will watch you with all of my thousand tiny images of you. If you reach out a hand, I might let you touch me. My fur is softer than anything you have ever felt, softer than the filaments, softer than the polished mound in the center of the room. My fingers might reach back. They are very gentle. They will explore the lines of your palm, the whorls of your fingertips — the fingerprints, I realize, that the walls of this room mimic in their mottled patterns. I will be mapping you, cataloguing you, storing you away in the memory of my body the way the inhabitant stores cities in the hollows of its brain.

I do not speak, but I can communicate. When you are this close, you will feel the vibration of my wingbeats in your chest, and that vibration will carry meaning. Not words. Impressions. Greeting. Recognition. Welcome. And underneath that, a question, repeated in the low thrum of the floor and the slow pulse of the phosphorescence and the patient shifting of the walls:

*Will you stay?*

The inhabitant asked you first, in its slow architectural way. I am asking again, in my smaller, quicker way. I am the intermediary, the translator, the one who bridges the gap between the city's geological patience and the brief flicker of your life. I was something like you once, a long time ago, a warm moving thing that entered a dead place and was changed by it. Now I am part of the city, and the city is part of me, and we are waiting — both of us, together — for you to decide.
