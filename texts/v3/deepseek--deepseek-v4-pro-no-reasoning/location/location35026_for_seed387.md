The air is thick with suspended salt, a warm mist that never settles, never dries. It coats the tongue first, then the lungs—you breathe it like a second fluid, halfway between drowning and inhaling. The light is a bruised amber, low and directionless, seeping from somewhere above the waterline but too distant to source. Everything here is below. The surface, if there is one, is a memory this place has forgotten.

You are inside a submerged engine room.

Not sunken—*submerged* by design, or by such old accident that the distinction has eroded. The walls are riveted iron plates, each the size of a barn door, their seams weeping slow tears of darker brine. Corrosion has worked them into landscapes: continents of orange rust flaking into the water column, canyons of pitted metal, ridgelines where copper fittings have gone green-black with verdigris. The scale is human-industrial, but the room is cathedral-vast, and the space between objects is enormous. A single piston, stopped mid-stroke decades or centuries ago, hangs in the dimness like a suspended whale—thirty feet across, its shaft disappearing upward into shadow. You could fit a house inside its cylinder.

Around you: emptiness. The engine room is a hollowed labyrinth of walkways that lead to other walkways, of ladders descending to platforms that hold nothing, of gantries spanning chasms where machinery once sat. Most of it is gone now, scavenged or dissolved, leaving only the armature. The bolts remain, each the size of a fist, set in empty flanges. The floor grates have gaps wide enough to fall through, and below them is only darker water, no bottom visible. You move by gripping rails wrapped in frayed hemp rope that hasn't rotted—something about the salinity, or the absence of anything that rots.

The *sound* is the dominant fact of the place. It is deafening, and it is mechanical, and it has no source you can find.

A low thrumming lives in the water itself, conducted through your bones more than your ears. It is the sound of a generator still running somewhere in the deeps of the complex, a diesel heartbeat that hasn't stopped. Layered over it: the shriek of metal expanding against metal as unseen currents shift temperatures, the percussive hammer of a loose plate rocking in some distant chamber, the hiss of steam or gas venting from a pipe that snakes along the ceiling forty feet overhead. And beneath all of it, a rhythmic grinding—gear teeth finding partial purchase, slipping, finding it again. The sound is not constant. It peaks and lurches, a machinery in fever, chasing equilibrium it never reaches.

The water temperature is blood-warm. It moves in slow, muscular currents that change direction without warning, pushing you against a bulkhead, then releasing. Somewhere in the complex, massive pumps are still cycling, or tides are breathing through flooded corridors, or the whole structure is slowly canting on its foundations. The turbulence is invisible but insistent—you are always bracing, always compensating.

There is no plant life. None. The walls are bare of algae, the water clear of any green. In places, pale fungal threads trace the iron like capillaries—a white so faint it is almost silver, structured in fractal mats that feed on the metal itself. This is the only vegetation the place allows: chemoautotroph, slow, alien to sunlight. It pulses faintly if you watch long enough, a respiration measured in hours.

Fauna appears rarely, and only in the periphery. A ribbon of something translucent and jointed, three feet long, unspools from a pipe flange and retracts before you can focus. Small white crabs with bodies like articulated vertebrae—no claws, no eyes—drift through the water in sudden congregations, then scatter. Once, something larger moves in the distance, its shape obscured by the industrial haze, its passage announced by a low-frequency pressure wave that pops in your inner ear. You do not see it. You feel it.

The city is above you, or around you, or *was*—the urban parameter doesn't sit in any one location. The engine room is a service level of something much larger, a buried infrastructure beneath habitation layers that may no longer exist. Access shafts rise at intervals, their ladder rungs glinting, their openings choked with silt and debris. Some are sealed by pressure doors, dogged shut from the other side. Through one, far overhead, a faint grid of square lights suggests a walkway, or a room, or the bottom of an occupied street seen through flooded glass. The city has not been abandoned—you hear it, sometimes, behind the mechanical roar: a clanging that might be bells, or might be hammers, or might be the language of something that has learned to speak in the frequencies of iron.

This pocket-world is hidden inside a larger one, a service niche forgotten by whoever lives above. The scale is intimate despite the volume—you can hold the whole room in attention, the three pistons, the twelve access hatches, the one functioning pressure door that leads deeper into the pump complex. It is a single feature, contained, knowable. Outside it, the city sprawls indifferent.

The erosion is settled. This is not a place in the process of destruction; it reached its current state long ago and has stabilized there. The rust is a surface patina, not a structural threat. The water chemistry has reached equilibrium with the iron. The fungal mats have found their carrying capacity. The sounds have become a steady-state chaos, a roar that maintains itself. The engine room is *finished*—not as designed, but as evolved.

Warm. Dim. Wet. Hollow. Loud beyond hearing, until the loudness becomes a kind of silence, the background against which all other perception happens. The place does not welcome. It does not resist. It simply operates, and has operated for longer than you have been alive, and will continue operating after you leave, its great piston suspended, its pumps cycling water from nowhere to nowhere, its fungal threads slowly, infinitely slowly, consuming the works of the city from the inside out.
