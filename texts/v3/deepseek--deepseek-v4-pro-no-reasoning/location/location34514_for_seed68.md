The place is called the Sump.

It is a hollow — a shallow bowl of land not much larger than a cathedral's footprint, sunk into the flank of the city like a cupped palm. The walls of the hollow are sheer and dark, cut from what looks like fresh basalt: planes of black stone that catch the light with edges still sharp, unweathered, as if the whole formation were cleft open only decades ago. Mineral salts bloom in white crusts along the fracture lines. The rock is cool to the touch, always, even when the air above the city is warm.

The floor of the bowl is a sprawl of pipework.

Pipes come in from all sides, penetrating the basalt walls at odd angles — cast iron, riveted steel, glazed ceramic, some wrapped in fraying asbestos lagging. They are the veins of the industrial quarter that presses in around this place. They carry things. Most of them leak. A slow, rhythmic seep of condensation beads along every joint and flange, so that the pipework is perpetually wet, glistening in the low light, and the air tastes of iron and warm grease and something faintly organic, like an old greenhouse.

Water never pools here in a true pond — the floor is too well-drained by a lattice of grated channels — but it is always *damp*. A fine mist hangs at knee-height, fed by intermittent jets of steam that pulse from pressure-release valves. The pulse is slow, patient. It follows a rhythm you could set a clock by, if clocks were made for rhythms this long: a sigh of vapour every two or three minutes, a pause, then another. The steam drifts upward in lazy curls and cools against the basalt walls, condensing, trickling back down, completing a cycle that has no urgency.

And in this damp, cool, sheltered hollow, life has taken hold with startling intensity.

Ferns grow here in profusion — not the delicate ferns of forest floors, but huge, muscular things. Their fronds uncurl from every crevice, some as tall as a person, their stems thick and black and shining with moisture. Mosses blanket the upper reaches of the pipework in electric-green pelts. Liverworts spread in flat, lobed mats across any horizontal surface that holds a film of water. There is no soil to speak of; the plants root directly into the rust-scale of the pipes or the mineral crusts of the rock, drawing what they need from the condensation and the faint warmth. The air smells green, alive, undercut with the metallic tang of the steam.

The vegetation is lush, but it is not crowded. There is space between things. Each fern has its own territory, its own patch of pipe or rock face. The mosses are scattered like islands, separated by expanses of wet black stone or bare, dripping metal. This is the sparse density — a richness that has room to breathe. The eye can rest in the gaps. The ferns do not compete; they are each a distinct presence, their fronds describing individual arcs against the dark walls, and the emptiness between them is as much a part of the place as the life itself.

The fauna is abundant, though at first you might not see it.

Slugs are the largest residents — leopard slugs, their bodies mottled grey and brown, as long as a finger and thick as a thumb. They move with the same slow patience as the steam cycle, tracing glistening trails across the basalt. They are everywhere, dozens of them, maybe hundreds, scattered across the walls and pipework. They graze on the algal films that grow where the light falls, leaving clean, shining tracks behind them.

Snails cluster in the joints of pipes, their shells a pale translucent amber, delicate as blown sugar. Woodlice, grey and segmented, live in the damp crevices of the lagging. And there are moths — small, brown, unremarkable moths — that live their entire lives within the hollow, their larvae feeding on the mosses, the adults resting with wings outspread against the cool stone, waiting for the slow pulse of warm steam to rise and carry their faint, dusty scent into the air.

But it is the slugs that define the place. They are the largest moving things, and they move so slowly that the Sump feels almost still, even though life is everywhere. Change happens at their pace. A slug takes an hour to cross a pipe. A frond uncurls over days. The condensation drips, the steam sighs, and nothing hurries.

The sound is remarkable. It is *loud*, but not in a way that overwhelms. The steam jets hiss — a constant, breathy undersound. The pipes carry a low, mechanical hum from the factories beyond the walls, a vibration you feel in your sternum as much as hear. Water drips at a hundred different pitches, a percussive, pointillist rhythm. And over all of it, the hiss of the steam builds and fades in its slow cycle, like the breathing of some immense, sleeping creature. It is loud enough that you must raise your voice to speak, but the noise is so layered, so steady, so without sudden edges, that it becomes a kind of silence after a while. You stop hearing it and start feeling it instead.

Light enters from above. The hollow is open to the sky — a rough oval aperture maybe thirty feet across, framed by the sharp, unweathered lip of the basalt. On overcast days, which are common, the light is diffuse and grey, filtering down through the steam in soft, shifting columns. When the sun breaks through, it catches the wet surfaces and makes them glitter, throws the shadows of fern fronds onto the rock in sharp silhouette. The walls are scored with old soot-stains from the factories, but the rain, when it falls, washes them clean in streaks, feeding the dampness, keeping the cycle going.

The Sump is not built for people. There are no paths, no railings, no signs. But the city has grown around it, pressed up against its walls, and in places you can see the evidence of human attention. A small platform of rusted grating, bolted to one wall, accessible through a narrow maintenance door in the rock. A single electric bulb in a wire cage, long dead, its filament dark. Some old, illegible gauges on a pipe fitting, their glass faces clouded with condensation. These things are relics; no one comes here anymore, or if they do, they come quietly, briefly, leaving no trace but the faint disturbance of a slug's slow progress diverted by a bootprint.

This is the peculiar unusalness of the place: not that it is strange in any one dramatic way, but that it is an industrial hollow, a piece of city infrastructure, that has been colonised so thoroughly by living things. It is a terrarium built by accident. The city's excretions — waste steam, leaking water, ambient warmth — have become an ecosystem's inputs. The industrial and the verdant are not opposed here; they are the same system, feeding each other, a small, slow, loud, damp, living room at the heart of the machine.

Outside the hollow, the city clatters on. Inside, the slugs graze, the ferns uncurl, and the steam sighs its patient sigh.
