Here is a description of this landscape.

---

The first thing you notice is the sound. It is not a single sound but a press of sound, a humid clamor that arrives before you understand where you are. It chitters, it drips, it scrapes. It is the sound of a billion small, determined lives and the incessant, patient work of water.

You are in the Moult, a place being born. The ground underfoot is not stone, not soil, but a warm, yielding rind of mineral precipitate, like the skin on cooling magma but soft, slightly tacky. It gives under your weight with a faint, wet sigh. Your feet sink a fraction of an inch, leaving impressions that slowly, almost imperceptibly, fill with a glistening fluid from below. The air is a warm, wet blanket, heavy with the smells of wet clay, hot metal, and a sharp, coppery tang. It clings to you. The temperature is that of a fever just breaking, a pervasive warmth that radiates from the ground and condenses on every surface, including your skin. Droplets form on your arms, on the fine hairs of your face, gathering the microscopic dust that hangs in the air into small, muddy rivulets.

You stand in what might be a basin, or a valley, or the floor of a vast, open cavern. The horizon is a matter of guesswork. The light is a sourceless, diffuse glow, as if the very air has been charged to a dull, pervasive luminescence. It illuminates a landscape of strange, organic architecture. There are spires and domes, not built, but grown, or perhaps secreted. Some are dull and grey, like unfinished clay. Others are glassy and dark, shot through with veins of something that glows a faint, internal red, like embers seen through smoked glass. These are not ruins; they are foundations, half-formed anatomies of a world still in its embryonic phase. Many are capped with a steady drip of mineral-rich water from the low, unseen ceiling far above, each drop adding a new layer to the growing structure, a patient, vertical accretion.

The place is dense. Not with vegetation, for there is not a single green thing here. No leaf, no blade of grass, no lichen. The density is of another order entirely. It is a density of *process*. Stalagmites crowd against each other, their flanks touching, fusing, forming terraces and basins that overflow with milky or ochre water. Ledges of glittering, brittle salt-crust line every pool. The air itself is thick with water vapor and the dust of a thousand precipitations, so much so that distant shapes are soft, their edges blurred. You feel a constant, gentle patter on your head and shoulders, not from a single rain, but from a congregation of a million drips, each one a tiny, persistent hammer building the world.

And it is crawling. The fauna here is not a visitor; it is a primary force. The surfaces, the pools, the very air are saturated with life. But it is a life that has no use for sunlight or photosynthesis. It is a life of chemosynthesis, of mineral consumption, of pure, frenetic churn. Pale, blind worms as thick as your wrist boil in the milky pools, their writhing bodies the engine that stirs the mineral soup. Hordes of tiny, translucent arthropods skitter across the wet rind, their carapaces leaving faint, phosphorescent trails that trace complex, frantic patterns for a moment before fading. The ground itself seems to shift and stir. What you took for pebbles are, on closer inspection, the hard, conical shells of creatures that inch along, rasping at the mineral deposits with unseen mouths. The air is thick with tiny, fluttering things, their wings a blur, their bodies little more than motes of silica dust, swarming in dense, humming clouds around the warmer vents.

Here and there, you see the hand of something more deliberate. Not a grand civilization, but the scattered marks of a simple, tenacious intelligence. There are small shelters built from slabs of the mineral rind, propped against the larger spires. A cleverly arranged series of channels, painstakingly gouged into the soft ground, diverts a stream of mineral water into a shallow basin where a lattice of sticks—they appear to be the dried, hollow legs of some large insect—supports a web of pale, silk-like fibers, perhaps a net for catching the tiny fluttering things. A circle of stones, each marked with a crude, scratched symbol, surrounds a vent that breathes a rhythmic, moist heat. These are not monuments, but tools. They are the marks of a people, or something like a people, who are living in the scaffolding of a world, adapting to its feverish construction.

The turbulence is the defining law of this place. It is unstable. A spire you leaned against a moment ago might be gone, dissolved back into the ground, its minerals reclaimed by the water and the hordes. A new vent might crack open with a hiss of steam a few feet from you, instantly attracting a frenzied swarm of the silica-moths. The ground shudders, not from any single tremor, but from the collective, ceaseless activity of a trillion small lives and the constant, dissolving, re-forming chemistry of the rock itself. It is a landscape in a state of perpetual, violent becoming.

The sound is overwhelming. A constant, percussive dripping, a low tectonic groan, the high-pitched chitter and rasp of uncountable arthropods, the wet slap of worms in their pools, the sizzle of new vents opening, and the faint, crystalline tinkling of salt-crust shattering under the weight of a growing stalactite. It is the sound of a world being assembled, piece by piece, drop by drop, life and stone in a furious, endless negotiation. It is not a place to rest. It is a place to witness.
