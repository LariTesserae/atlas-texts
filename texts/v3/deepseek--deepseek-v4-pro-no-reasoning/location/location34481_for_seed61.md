You step off the last rung and your foot sinks into wet wool. Not water exactly — the ground here never quite commits to being solid or liquid. This is the Weeping Quarter, where the city has grown down into the old cistern galleries and forgotten itself.

The walls are not stone but accretion. Centuries of mineral drip have built them layer by patient layer, grey-white flowstone flowing over older grey-white flowstone, all of it glistening in the dimness with a permanent sweat. The air is so thick with moisture that breathing feels like drinking. Every surface beads and drips — not the sharp plink of discrete drops but a constant wet exhalation, a sound like a thousand mouths whispering with full cheeks. It never stops. It has never stopped.

The space is small. You can touch both walls at once if you spread your arms, and the ceiling leans close enough to make you stoop. This is the whole of it: a pocket of habitation buried inside the larger strata of the city above, a single room-world shaped like a collapsed throat. The floor tilts gently toward a central channel where the seepage gathers and moves, slow as syrup, toward a grate that has been swallowing water for so long its teeth are smoothed to nubs.

Things hang from the ceiling. Rows of them, packed tight, swaying slightly in currents of air too subtle to feel. They are shapes wrapped in what might be hide or might be felted fibre — primitive craft, hand-stitched, every seam visible, every cord twisted by someone’s thumbs. Pouches, bundles, oblong cocoons suspended from hooks hammered into the flowstone long ago. They nearly touch one another. The density of them makes the small space smaller still, a forest of hanging things you must brush past to move. They swing back into place behind you with a soft choral rustle.

Light seeps from somewhere above and behind, filtered through so many layers of the city’s bowel that it arrives exhausted, grey-green, barely more than a glow. It catches the wet walls and makes them shine like the inside of a shell. Shadows do not fall so much as curdle in the corners.

There is something alive here. Not plants — the parameters forbid that, and the place has obeyed. No green, no root, no photosynthesizing tendril has ever reached this far down. But something furrier. Simpler. You glimpse it only as a quick skittering along the seam where wall meets ceiling, a pale shape with too many joints that freezes when you look directly at it, then dissolves back into the mineral folds. Rare fauna. It leaves behind a faint smell, fungal and sweet, that mingles with the wet-rock breath of the place.

This is a farm, you realize, or what passes for one here. A croft of damp and darkness. Someone tends these hanging bundles. Someone comes down the ladder — rough-hewn rungs lashed with sinew, the wood dark and soft as soaked bread — and checks each cocoon with patient hands. You can see the evidence of this tending: a clay bowl resting on a natural shelf, a scraping tool of chipped stone, a pile of something that might be food or might be fuel. Urban but primitive. A basement culture, a settlement of one or two, folded into a space that the city above has long forgotten it contains.

The sound never varies. That wet whisper-roar, the collective exhalation of walls that cannot stop giving up their water. It fills the ears the way the damp fills the lungs. After a few minutes it ceases to register as noise and becomes a property of the air itself, a constant pressure.

A shape detaches from the deepest shadow. Human-height, wrapped in the same felted material as the hanging bundles, hooded against the wet. It moves with the slow economy of someone who has done the same tasks in the same small space for longer than you can reckon. It does not speak. Its hands go to a bundle, part the wrappings, and inside you glimpse not flesh but darkness, a negative space where something has been growing or waiting or dreaming in the mineral dark. The hands re-wrap it with care. The water keeps weeping. The shape turns back to its work, and you understand that this place has found its equilibrium — a pocket world, dense and hidden and utterly complete, where nothing changes and nothing ends, only seeps and drips and hangs and waits.
