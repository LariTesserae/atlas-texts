The air is not air. It is a medium, thicker than mist, thinner than gel — a warm, slightly resistant substance you push through with your whole body, tasting of copper and ozone and something floral rotting. The pressure is immense but directionless, squeezing not from above but from everywhere equally, as if the atmosphere itself has become a muscle tensing around you.

The ground — if ground is what it is — slopes sharply upward at broken angles, a terrain of dark, glossy spines that are still forming. They emerge audibly: a groan that becomes a shriek as mineral crystallizes out of the saturated air in real time, black needles four feet long materializing with a sound like glass being dragged across bone. Some are warm to the touch, fresh extrusions. Others crumble at a finger's pressure, their structure abandoned mid-formation, already obsolete. The landscape is making and unmaking itself with the frantic indecision of something newborn and panicked.

And through all of it, the sound.

It does not stop. It does not dip. It is not something that happens to the place — it *is* the place. A continuous, wall-of-force noise that seems to come from the medium itself: the low-end rumble of continents grinding, overlaid with a roaring like a thousand waterfalls fed through a broken speaker, and threaded through it all, impossibly, something almost choral — not voices, but harmonics that suggest voices, overtones that resolve into what the ear desperately wants to hear as language before dissolving back into the roar. You feel it in your teeth. You feel it in the soft tissue behind your eyes. After a while you stop hearing it as sound and start feeling it as gravity, a weight that presses thought into simpler shapes.

Water is everywhere but not in familiar forms. There is no surface, no distinction between wet and dry. The medium you move through is supersaturated; droplets condense spontaneously on any surface — including skin — then shake themselves apart again moments later, unable to settle in the turbulent pressure-field that fills the space. Sometimes a pocket of clearer liquid rotates past, a lenslike sphere a foot across that briefly magnifies the spines behind it before it shudders and disperses. Occasionally a wall of water rolls through — an actual vertical sheet, coherent, traveling sideways, slapping against the spines with a sound lost in the greater roar, leaving a brief mineral slick behind.

The light is low and sourceless, a dim amber suffusion that seems to originate from the medium itself, as if the atmosphere is faintly bioluminescent. It flickers arrhythmically, not with any pulse but with an unsteady wobbling, like candlelight seen through disturbed water. Shadows don't behave: they stretch in the wrong direction, they lag behind the spines that cast them, they sometimes turn independently, rotating like slow weather systems across the crystalline surfaces.

Vegetation — if that's the word — amounts to scattered, opportunistic films: a rust-colored crust adhering to the older, more stable spines, something that might be lichen or might be a mineral efflorescence, furry to the touch and faintly acidic. It retreats when touched directly. It advances again when you look away. In one crevice, a single structure rises on a thin stalk — something like a fungal fruiting body the size of a thumb, translucent, veined with a faint internal glow. It sways in the pressure-currents, and as you watch, it completes its life cycle in less than a minute: unfurling, releasing a puff of spores that spiral away in the roar, then collapsing into a black thread that the next rain-wall washes away.

Fauna is rare but present. Something moves between the spines at the edge of perception — not an animal, not quite, but a zone of *different pressure*, a mobile low-front that flows around obstructions and leaves behind a trail where the ambient droplets align briefly into radial patterns before relapsing into chaos. It has no visible body but it has intent; it curves toward warmth, lingers nearby, moves on. Later, perhaps the same entity, perhaps another, sends out a pulse that moves through the medium as a visible ripple, and the spines it touches briefly ring in harmonic response before the main roar swallows the tone.

The sparseness is key. This cathedral-sized volume — maybe a hundred feet across, bounded by a natural bowl of the largest spines curving inward — contains only these few presences. Mostly it is empty medium, churning, loud, full of frantic stillness. The emptiness is the main event. The scattered spines, the occasional biological half-attempt, the roaming pressure-anomaly, the drifting water-lenses: they are notes held in a vast, roaring silence, provisional, likely to be erased and redrawn at any moment.

There are remnants. Not built, exactly — no tool could function here, no structure could hold — but the marks of something that was here before the reality-storm. A flat patch of fused mineral that might have been a floor. A section of spine that isn't like the others: too regular, bearing parallel grooves that could be tool marks, or could be natural, or could be the landscape dreaming that it once had inhabitants. The moment you fix your attention on it, a pressure-front sweeps through and the grooves smooth out, erasing themselves.

The feeling is of standing inside a throat while the throat sings. The world is being dreamed by something that doesn't know what a world is, and it keeps getting the details wrong and starting over. The spines are a forest one moment, a nervous system the next, a mountain range the moment after that — not changing form so much as never fully committing to any form, always in the act of becoming, which here is a violent, deafening process indistinguishable from destruction.

You taste salt and iron. Your own heartbeat becomes a participant in the sound, struggling to find a rhythm that doesn't get torn apart by the larger roar. You realize, with a certainty that bypasses logic, that the place is *listening* to you back — the pressure-fronts adjust around your body, the droplet-walls deviate around your heat-signature, the youngest spines lean incrementally in your direction as they form. The landscape is interested. The landscape has never encountered anything that stays the same shape for this long.

The roar, if you stop fighting it, resolves into something almost like weather — a weather of pure information, pattern dissolving and reforming, and you realize the deafening volume is simply the sound of reality happening very fast, and that "silence" would mean something terrible: that nothing more would ever be created here again.
