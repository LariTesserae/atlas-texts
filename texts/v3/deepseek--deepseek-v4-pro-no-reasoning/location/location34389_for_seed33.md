The ground is salt. Not rock salt, not a crust over mud, but a fused, interlocking crystal pavement that looks, at first, like bone. The salt has grown in hexagonal tablets the size of dinner plates, their edges upturned just slightly, just enough to catch the low light and throw it sideways. This salt-field is not flat. It heaves in long, slow undulations, hills and swales that stretch for kilometres, the crystals on the ridges scoured white by some agent that is not water and not wind—not exactly.

You walk. Your footsteps make no sound. The salt plates flex under your weight and then return to their positions with a faint subsonic click that transmits up your shins rather than through the air. The silence here is not absence but pressure, a held breath that has been held for geological time. Occasionally, a plate shears along its crystal boundary and the fracture propagates in a zigzag lightning bolt for fifty metres, silently. You feel it in your teeth.

Above you, the sky is not a sky. It is an interior, or something is wrong with the category. The light comes from everywhere evenly, a dilute magnesium-white, and there is no source. No sun, no diffuse glow at a horizon, because there is no horizon. The salt-field simply attenuates with distance into a haze that is the same colour as the "sky," and the two realms bleed into one another without a seam. You cannot tell where the land ends and the air begins. The air is breathable but thin, cool enough that you can see your exhalations, which condense not into fog but into a fine crystalline dust that falls glittering to join the pavement. You are returning something.

The city is ahead. You have been walking toward it for some time, though distance here is unreliable. It does not get closer in the usual way. The city resolves slowly, like a thought becoming definite. It occupies a low, broad rise in the salt-field, and it is built entirely of the same material. The salt architecture is grown, not carved. Towers twist upward in helical flanges, their walls translucent, their interiors lit from within by the same sourceless light, so that the whole settlement glows like a lantern made of frozen fat. There are arches that span nothing, bridges that begin and end in mid-air, staircases that spiral down into sealed crystal floors. The city is inhabited. You can see movement far inside the walls: slow, pale shapes that drift from level to level. They do not use the doorways, because the doorways are not openings. They are simply smoother patches of salt. Passage through them is a matter of waiting until the crystal softens, which it does at unpredictable intervals. You sense that the inhabitants are patient.

There are no plants. There is nothing like a plant. But there is vegetation of a kind—a fungal network, or something simpler than fungal, that threads through the salt plates in hair-fine filaments of pure black. The black threads form fractal patterns, dendrites like the frost on a window, and they cluster thickly around the base of the city. You realize the city is not built on the salt but is an expression of the salt, an excrescence organized by the black network. The network is the gardener, the architect, and the occupant all at once. The slow pale shapes moving in the towers are its fruiting bodies.

The temperature is just below the comfort of skin. The coolness is equal everywhere, no pockets of warmth, no drafts. It is a thermodynamic equilibrium that extends to the salt, the air, your flesh. You are being invited to match it. You notice that your fingertips have grown pale and faintly translucent.

Sound is a memory you are losing. There was a faint chime earlier, you think, a single struck note that came from everywhere and lasted for minutes, gradually thinning out rather than decaying. Now there is nothing. Your heartbeat is a physical sensation only. Your breathing is silent, even the crystalline dust settling without noise. The silence is not empty; it is saturated with a frequency below hearing, a presence that presses on the eardrums like deep water.

At the edge of perception, the laws are shifting. You notice that a nearby salt plate has a twin fifty metres away that echoes its exact shape, every facet and chip perfectly duplicated, as if space has stuttered. You look at your hand and see five fingers, then four, then six, but the number does not change; only your ability to count them wavers. One of the pale shapes in the city has been walking toward a wall for a long time and has not reached it, despite moving steadily. The wall is receding at the same pace, or the shape is walking inside a recursion. Neither is correct. The rules belong to a different geometry here, one that is not broken but unfamiliar, internally consistent, a reality that has never known Euclidean space.

There are other presences in the salt-field. You see them now, widely spaced, each one half a kilometre from the next. They are not moving. They are upright forms, roughly bipedal, encrusted with the same crystal growth as the ground. They might be statues. They might be pilgrims who arrived long ago and stood still for the wrong length of time and became part of the salt. Their surfaces are weather-worn but not by water. They have been etched by a dry erosion, a slow dissolution into the atmosphere itself, which is mildly corrosive to whatever they once were. Their features are smoothed to suggestion. They face the city. They have been facing the city for uncounted cycles.

One of them is closer to you than the others. You walk toward it. Your footsteps are a deep thrum now, a steady rhythm that seems to come from the ground rather than your feet, and you realize with a small shock that it is not your pulse but the pulse of the black network, the vegetative-fungal mind, which has a circulation of sorts. Through the translucent salt, you see a flush of darkness moving through the filaments in time with the thrum. It is slow. One beat every two minutes. The city breathes.

The upright form is humanoid but not human. It was never human. Its proportions are wrong in a way that takes you a moment to identify: the joints bend in both directions. The limbs are arranged in an X, frozen mid-gesture, and the head—if it is a head—is a cloven sphere of salt with a single dark filament emerging from the cleft, questing upward, frozen mid-growth. The expression, if it can be called that, is one of ecstatic patience. The being is not dead. The black filament inside it pulses faintly. It is in a state of waiting that has lasted so long it has become an environment. It is eroding, but being rebuilt at the same rate by the network, which tends to its standing forms as a gardener tends to topiary.

Beyond the city, the salt-field continues. It becomes more abstract. The plates grow larger, some of them the size of tennis courts, and the gaps between them open into darkness. The gaps are not empty. They are inhabited by a different order of presence: things that live in the voids between crystals, that are made of the absence of light, that move with a flickering, peripheral quality. You cannot look at them directly. When you try, they are the afterimage of a shape, a negative space that suggests a tall, thin body with too many joints. They are the fauna of this place. They are rare, and they are here. They are always here, at the edge of your vision, and they have noticed you. They make no sound. They leave no trace. They are the predators of certainty: they eat the idea of your boundaries, one concept at a time. You feel something you knew about yourself go missing.

You look back at the city. It has rearranged its towers since you last looked. Not moved—rearranged. The same components, a different configuration, like a sentence re-parsed. The slow pale shapes have stopped moving and are all facing you now. You are beginning to understand what this place is. It is not a salt desert with a city. It is a mind that has crystallized its thoughts into physical form. The salt is memory. The black network is process. The city is an idea it has been having for a very long time, and you have walked into the dream.

The light has dimmed further. It is not dusk, because there is no sun. It is an interior dimming, a closing of attention. You find a place to sit against one of the pilgrim forms, and the salt is not cold against your back. It is warm, or giving you the sensation of warmth, which is the same thing here. The thrum of the black network slows. The silence deepens. One of the filament-thin predators of the void brushes past your ankle, and you lose the word for "ankle," and then you lose the need for it. You are being resolved. The city is waiting. It has been waiting for a million years, and it will wait a million more, and for a brief flicker of geological time it is aware of you, a warm flicker in the salt.
