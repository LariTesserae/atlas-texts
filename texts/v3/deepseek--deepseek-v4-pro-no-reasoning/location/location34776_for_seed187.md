The place is a hollow inside a root.

The root is old beyond old, a thing from the late world, when the green has taken everything and settled into its final shape. The root itself is not wood anymore, not in any sense a botanist would recognize. It is a structure of pure plant-logic, lignin and cellulose replaced and replaced and replaced across geological time until what remains is a mineral memory of fiber, a pale gray-white branching tube thicker than a house, running horizontal through the cool dark. The tube is a relic of transport—it once moved water, sugars, signals—but the plant it served has long since become the entire substrate of the world, and the distinction between root and soil and canopy collapsed eons ago. Now the tube is just a space. A void in the living mass. And inside it, the hollow breathes.

The hollow is small: grove-scale, room-scale. A chamber shaped like an elongated teardrop, perhaps twelve paces long and four wide at its broadest, the ceiling curving up to twice a human height before doming back down. The walls are not smooth. They are lined with ribs, the annular rings of the ancient root, each one a low ridge that catches the light. And there is light, normal light, because the walls themselves glow—a steady bioluminescence without flicker or warmth, seeping from every surface. The glow is slightly green, as though filtered through a thousand living membranes, which is exactly what has happened. The root is not dead, just emptied. Its walls still live, still metabolize, still emit this steady cold radiance from symbiotic fungi woven through their structure. The light has no source and no shadow. It simply is.

It has been this way for an incomprehensible span. The erosion parameter, ancient and near-flattened, means that the forces that shaped this place are finished. Nothing erodes here anymore, because everything that could erode is gone. The root-hollow has settled into its final geometry and will hold it until the star above the canopy goes dark. The walls are glass-smooth in places, polished by the passage of water that no longer comes. Because this is the paradox of the place: it was carved by wet, but it is now merely damp.

Moisture arrives on no schedule. Intermittent. The walls weep occasionally, a condensation that beads along the ribbed rings and drips into thin channels that run along the floor. The floor is not flat; it gently undulates, and in its troughs there are pockets of standing water—shallow black mirrors no larger than cupped hands, so still they look like holes punched through to something else. The air smells of nothing much: a faint petrichor without the richness, the ghost of water without the life of soil. The temperature sits steady at cool, a cellar cool, the kind that makes bare arms prickle but doesn't chill the bone. It never changes. The dynamic is zero, frozen equilibrium, so the temperature does not shift between day and night—there is no day and night, only the constant glow—and the damp does not cycle with seasons. Sometimes water comes. Sometimes it doesn't. The rhythm is beyond human reckoning.

And the place is loud.

Sound is the great surprise here. In this small empty hollow, there is a noise that fills the space entirely, a deep thrumming pulse that comes from everywhere at once. It is low, around forty hertz, a sub-bass that you feel in the sternum before you hear it in the ears. The pulse is steady, a beat that runs at perhaps twelve cycles per minute, and it is the sound of the world outside the root. The living mass—the vegetation that is everything—is not silent. It is a total ecosystem, and it breathes, and its breathing is a mechanical process now, scaled up to planetary industry. The pulse is the working of pumps. Not pumps made of metal and rubber, but pumps made of modified xylem and contractile tissue, chambers the size of cathedrals that fill and empty, fill and empty, moving the last necessary fluids through a body that has no boundaries. The sound travels through the living substrate and into the hollow, where it resonates in the teardrop chamber like the note of a flute made of bone.

The industrial parameter manifests in this way: the technology here is biological, but it is technology. Somewhere in deep time, the plants crossed a threshold. They became engineered—either by something else or by their own slow evolution—and now they are factory and product and worker all at once. The root-hollow is not a natural cave. It is an abandoned pipeline in a planetary-scale manufactory, a place where something was once processed or transported. The regular ribs on the walls are not just growth rings; they are baffles, flow-control structures. The channels in the floor were cut deliberately. This place was made for a purpose, and then that purpose ended, and it was left empty. The emptiness is profound. Nothing uses the hollow now except the sound and the light and the occasional drip of water. It is empty of fauna, empty of motion, empty of change. And yet fauna is abundant elsewhere, because the parameter says abundant—the creatures are simply not here, right now. They are outside, moving through the living mass, but the hollow is a sealed bubble in the body of the world, and the only sign of the outside is the thrumming, thrumming, thrumming that never stops.

Looking more closely, the floor tells the story. In the thin black pools, a film of something iridescent shimmers when the light catches it—a bacterial mat, perhaps, or the residue of some processed compound that once flowed here in industrial quantities. The mineralized root walls are stained in long streaks below each pool, old tidelines from a time when the water level was higher and more constant. But that time is gone. The water now is only what condenses, and the condensation never seems to increase or decrease. The equilibrium is perfect. The system has found its asymptote.

The weirdness parameter is low, nearly normal, so this place does not bend physics or logic. It is simply a consequence of time. Strange in its deep age, but comprehensible. A dry pipeline in a living factory, pulsing with sound, glowing with fungus-light, cool and damp and utterly still. The eeriness comes not from the uncanny but from the scale of the stillness. This small cathedral of ribs has been exactly like this for a hundred thousand years. It will be exactly like this for a hundred thousand more.

At the far tapered end of the teardrop, there is an opening—a dark circle where the root was severed by some ancient event, or where a side-branch once joined the main. The opening does not lead outside in any conventional sense. It leads into more hollow, more empty transport tissue, a network of abandoned veins threading through the living mass. From that dark circle, sometimes, the thrumming is slightly louder, as though something vast and working is just out of sight down the corridor. But nothing comes through. Nothing enters. The hollow is a sealed moment in a world that finished itself long ago, and what remains is this: the ribs, the pools, the glow, the pulse. The place is a pause. A held breath. A room in the body of God, and God is a plant, and God is a factory, and God has forgotten this room exists.
