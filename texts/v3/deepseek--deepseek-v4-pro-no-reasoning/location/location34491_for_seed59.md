The place is a hanging garden the size of a chapel, but no one planted it. It grew itself into a city.

You enter through a cleft in the weeping rock—a limestone rib that sweats condensation day and night, its surface always slick, always cool to the touch. The water comes from everywhere and nowhere in particular. It seeps from pores in the stone, beads on every surface, drips from a hundred thousand leaf-tips, runs in rivulets down walls that aren't walls but roots. The air is so wet it feels heavy in the lungs, thick with the green taste of living things respiring. When you exhale, your breath joins the fog that pools in the lower hollows before spiraling up into warmer currents.

This is the high reach of a river gorge, a shelf carved into the cliff face three hundred feet above the water, where the stone decided to hold moisture like a sponge. The recession is deep enough to form a nave, tall enough that the ceiling vanishes into vegetal darkness, but small enough that you could cross it in thirty strides. Boundaries are absolute: rock wall on three sides, open drop on the fourth. You are in a room the mountain made, and the plants have furnished it.

And the plants—the plants are not decoration. They are the architecture, the infrastructure, the citizenry, the law. Every surface that isn't actively running with water is sheathed in living tissue. There are no bare stones here, no naked soil. The ground beneath your feet is not ground but a woven mat of rhizomes and aerial roots, springy as a mattress, perpetually damp, warm in patches where decomposition runs hot. What you first mistake for pillars are actually trunks—some species of strangler fig or something that evolved to fill the same niche—that have fused together into a colonnade, their bark flowing into each other like candle wax. They support nothing but themselves and the epiphytes that crust their every inch.

The epiphytes make the walls. They are the walls. Layer upon layer of them—ferns growing on moss growing on lichen growing on older, deader ferns—create a tapestry of greens so dense and varied that the eye loses depth perception. You could press your hand into it and sink past your elbow before touching stone. Shelf fungi jut like brackets, their undersides dripping. Bromeliads cup small pools of water that brim over in the constant drizzle. Orchids with roots like pale fingers clutch at the air. Everything is stacked on everything else, each plant growing on the plant below it, a vertical chain of dependency that goes all the way down to the first pioneer that cracked the rock with its hyphae millennia ago.

And it is old. The erosion score tells you this: the place has been this way long enough to forget otherwise. The water has sculpted the limestone into draperies and flutes, but the plants have kept pace with every dissolution. When a piece of rock sloughs away, roots have already claimed the new surface. The equilibrium is ancient. Nothing here is raw, nothing is new, nothing is temporary, except—

The animals.

You hear them before you see them. The sound is a wall. A roar, a chitter, a screech, a thrum—layered and chaotic, rising and falling in waves that never settle to silence. This is not a peaceful grotto. This is a marketplace, a riot, a city under siege by its own inhabitants. The volume hits you like a physical force, a pressure on the eardrums that makes your own thoughts feel muffled.

They are everywhere. The density score doesn't prepare you. Every cubic foot of this space that isn't solid plant is occupied by something that moves. The air is thick with flying things—not just a few birds but swarms, clouds of them. Hummingbirds the size of bumblebees, their wings a blur, stabbing at the bromeliad cups with needle beaks. Moths with eyes on their wings, clustering on the fungi so thickly that the fungi seem to blink. Small bats, dozens of them, folded into the crevices between epiphyte layers like furry fruit, chittering their echolocation so constantly that you imagine you can feel it on your skin.

Larger bodies move through the canopy. Things with prehensile tails and grasping hands—not quite monkeys, but something on that branch of the tree, something smaller and faster and more numerous. They travel in troupes of thirty or forty, never still, always chattering, always fighting or grooming or mating or stealing food from each other's mouths. They use the fused fig trunks as highways, running vertical and horizontal with equal ease, their claws leaving shallow scores in the bark that heal over in hours and are reopened the next day. Their scat falls constantly, a gentle rain of processed fruit pulp that the floor-mat of roots absorbs instantly, gratefully.

The sound of them is a texture more than a signal—a constant chattering shriek that overlaps with itself until individual voices are lost. But within the chaos, patterns emerge. A sudden silence from one quadrant means a predator. A rising pitch means food discovered. A particular staccato bark means territory disputed.

Nothing is still. Everything is in motion, and the motion is turbulent. There is no order here, no hierarchy of movement, no calm center. The plants grow into each other and the animals move through the plants and the animals move through each other, and the whole system is in a state of constant, violent negotiation. A branch breaks under the weight of too many bodies; the branch is immediately colonized by something new as the animals spill downward and find purchase elsewhere. A bromeliad pool is drained by a thirsty troop; the cup refills in minutes from the eternal seep. A moth cloud is scattered by a bat's wing; the moths reform their cluster on the next fungus over. Nothing is lost. Everything is reconfigured.

And all of it—all of it—is what these people built.

Because there are people here. The built score isn't wrong; this is urban. This is a settlement. You just have to learn to see the architecture in the chaos, the craft in what first appears to be mere growth.

The trunks that form the colonnade are not naturally fused. Look closer. Where they join, you can see the old wounds, the deliberate bark-stripping, the way the cambium layers were bound together with woven fiber cords that have long since been absorbed into the living wood. This is grafting at an architectural scale. They trained these trees, and they are still training them, shaping the next generation of pillars from saplings that are guided onto stone footings you first mistook for natural boulders.

The floor is not a random mat of roots. It undulates with intent—low berms create channels that direct the seep-water into catchment hollows lined with clay pressed by hand. The clay is crude, unfired, but it holds. You find tools tucked into niches: stone blades knapped from chert, sharp enough to still hold an edge after what looks like years of use. Wooden mallets with heads hardened in fire. Fiber ropes braided from epiphyte roots, incredibly strong, still tied around bundles of harvested leaves.

And the people themselves.

You don't see them at first because they don't want to be seen, and because they have lived here so long that they move like the animals move, part of the turbulence. But once your eyes adjust, you realize that some of the shapes moving through the canopy are human. Small people—adapted to this place, selected by it over generations—with long toes that grip like the monkeys' tails, with skin that has taken on a greenish cast from something in their diet or their blood. They wear nothing. Their bodies are decorated with scarification that mimics the texture of bark, that makes them vanish against the walls.

Their technology is stone and fiber, bone and wood. No metal. The tools you saw are theirs. They knap chert nodules that they dig from the limestone matrix. They shape the branches while they're still alive, training them into hooks and pegs and platforms. They do not build shelter—what need? The plants are shelter. Instead, they build relationships. They know which bromeliad species holds water longest. They know which fungus is edible at which stage of its fruiting cycle. They know the precise moment when a particular tree frog becomes toxic, and they avoid it that week. They know the predator calls from the prey calls from the alarm calls from the courtship calls, and they move with the information.

And they are moving now, all of them, a constant low-grade panic that you realize has been there since you arrived. Because the turbulence is not just animal. It is them. They are the anthill, the bazaar, the Solaris-storm. They do not stop. They harvest constantly, process constantly, eat constantly—handfuls of larvae scooped from fungal shelves, mouthfuls of nectar sucked directly from flowers, small furry bodies cracked open with a stone and shared among six hands before the blood cools. The churn of their bodies through the space is as dense and chaotic as the monkeys', and they do not distinguish their movements from the monkeys' movements in any obvious way.

A child swings past you on a vine that is not a vine but an aerial root, and she does not look at you. She is chasing something, or being chased. The scar patterns on her back say clan, say lineage, say role. She is gone into the green before you can track her.

The air smells of wet stone, of humus, of the sweet-rot of fallen fruit, of guano and sweat and the sharp green of crushed leaves. The temperature is cool—the wetness and the height together keep it that way, a constant chill that never quite reaches cold. Your skin is beaded with condensation, and you understand that if you stayed here long enough, things would start growing on you too.

The light comes from the open cliff face, a wall of grey sky and mist, diffused through the perpetual haze of water droplets. It's normal light, daytime light, but filtered through so much moisture that it feels underwater. Shadows are soft-edged. The colors are all green, all brown, all the pale white of the limestone where water has washed it clean. There is no fire here. There couldn't be. Everything is too wet to burn.

The sound does not stop. It will not stop. Even when night comes—and you can see the quality of light beginning to shift toward dusk—new voices will join the chorus. The frogs. The insects. The night-birds. The people, who you suspect sleep in shifts, or sleep moving, or have forgotten sleep entirely. The city does not rest. The garden does not rest. Every moment, something is growing and something is dying and something is being eaten and something is being born, and the water drips, drips, drips, never ceasing, the pulse of the place, the metronome beneath the chaos.

You are standing in a living thing that has been shaped by other living things that have been shaped by it in turn. The boundaries are clear—thirty strides across, rock on three sides, void on the fourth—but the density makes it feel infinite. You could spend a lifetime here and never map the whole of this single room-sized world.

A hand touches your arm. You didn't see anyone approach. An old woman, her face a mask of scar-tissue, her eyes clouded but tracking. She holds out a cup made from a folded leaf, brimming with water that she has scooped from a bromeliad. She says something in a language you don't understand, a string of clicks and trills that sounds exactly like the monkeys, exactly like the birds. She presses the cup into your hand and is gone, into the green, and you lose sight of her before you can thank her.

The water is cool. It tastes of flowers.
