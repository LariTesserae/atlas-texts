The first thing that registers is the sound. Not a single noise but a weave of them, so constant and so layered that it becomes a kind of silence, the way a waterfall does. Beneath everything else runs a deep, percussive rhythm — a thudding, syncopated beat that travels up through the soles of your feet before you ever hear it with your ears. It’s the sound of water, but water handled roughly, water being put to work.

You are standing on a shelf of rock, high up. The elevation is not mountainous in the way of sharp peaks and snow, but high in the way of a continental plateau that has been here so long the world has forgotten it wasn't always. The air is thin and warm, thick with moisture, and it smells of iron and wet vegetation and something acrid that catches at the back of the throat — ozone, perhaps, or the exhalation of some vast chemical process.

The darkness is absolute. No sun, no moon, no stars pierce this place. Your eyes strain and find nothing, and after a while they stop straining. The darkness becomes a medium, like water, pressing in with its own weight and texture. But you are not blind. Light exists here, only not light as you know it. The landscape provides its own faint luminescence in patches: a slick, greenish glow that clings to wet rock faces, the bioluminescent bloom of microorganisms living in the constant condensation. It is a light that obscures as much as it reveals, turning the world into a study in deep greens and wet blacks.

The terrain underfoot is a testament to deep time and water's patience. This is a karst landscape pushed to impossible scale. The plateau is riddled with dissolution features — not mere caves and sinkholes, but whole canyons and interior basins formed by the eating-away of soluble rock. The rock itself, a dark limestone veined with darker minerals, is sculpted into razor-sharp crests and fluted channels, every surface grooved by millennia of slightly acidic rain. This is the established form of the place, weathered into a state of dynamic equilibrium where dissolution and deposition race each other across every surface. You run a hand over a nearby boulder and it comes away slick. The rock is weeping. Everywhere, water seeps from the stone as if the entire plateau is a sponge being slowly squeezed.

And that is the source of the sound. The deep, industrial thudding.

Stretching away into the lightless distance, down the long slopes of the canyon-basins, is a forest of machines. They are not separate from the landscape but have grown into it, or from it. Tall derricks, built of corroded iron and what looks like brass, rise on spindly legs from the misty hollows. Their bases are sunk into the rock itself, tapping the deep aquifers. With every heavy, rhythmic stroke, a piston the size of a tree trunk slides down, compresses, and then releases a gout of steam and water that sprays across the surrounding vegetation. The sound is the heartbeat of the plateau: *thump-hiss, thump-hiss*, a vast, desynchronized chorus of pumping stations that no human hand has tended in an age. Some pumps are toppled, their legs akimbo, their pistons still cycling uselessly, hammering at the air and spraying geysers of warm, mineral-saturated water into the dark. These are the ruins of an industry, but not a dead industry — an industry that has become feral, a scattered set of automatic processes that have outlived their purpose and now constitute a new kind of ecosystem.

The vegetation has adapted to this mechanical rainfall. It is a jungle of strange, lush forms, growing in dense, near-touching profusion. Without true light, the plants are not green but a bruised spectrum of purples, deep reds, and waxy blacks. They are structured not for capturing photons but for capturing the chemical heat and mineral bounty of the pump-jack exhalations. Great, fleshy pads, like the leaves of monstrous liverworts, cup the falling spray. Fronds with the texture of razor clams strain the humid air for droplets. Hanging from the corroded crossbeams of the derricks are long, hair-like epiphytes, pale as nerves, that sway in the steam currents. The density of life is staggering; each surface is a habitat, each organism hosts a dozen more. It is a peat-forming ecosystem on fast-forward, a relentless, dripping, heaving accumulation of wet biomass that crackles with its own quiet, metabolic sounds.

And through it all moves the fauna. You can’t see them clearly, but you sense them — abundant presences in the dark, signalled by sound and by the brief eclipse of a phosphorescent patch. A high, chittering screech cuts through the rhythmic thumping, answered by a lower, grinding call from somewhere in the canopy of fronds. There's a wet, slapping sound, like a heavy fish being dropped on stone, and then a scrabble of claws on metal. The creatures here are blind, or nearly so, navigating by the echoes of the pumps and their own sharp cries. The air is a dense information-scape of sound, and the fauna are its instruments. You sense forms that are streamlined and wet, slipping through the narrow channels of the karst; forms that are multi-legged and careful, picking their way up the derrick legs; forms that soar in the vast, cathedral darkness above the canyon, their passage announced by a sudden shift in air pressure and a brief, clean note like wind over a bottle.

Scattered among the industrial detritus are the faint marks of another kind of builder. On a smooth, vertical slab of rock that overhangs a steaming sump, a design has been chiseled. It’s not writing, not exactly, but a spiral pattern that mimics the whorl of water draining into stone. It is small, precise, the work of a careful hand. Nearby, a low wall of fitted stone, now swaddled in fleshy purple vegetation, forms a boundary that no longer divides anything from anything else. These are the only acknowledgements that this place was once intentional, that the pumps weren't always feral, that someone once tended the water and cut the stone and left a mark meaning *we were here* before they left or were absorbed into the ecosystem they were engineering.

The scale of it all is the final, overwhelming fact. This is not a valley or a mountainside. This is a terrain that repeats on a continental scale. The weather is its own: a micro-climate of perpetual, warm, condensing fog that rises from the sumps and falls again as greasy, mineralized rain. The processes — geological, biological, industrial — are all planetary in their reach. The thudding of the pumps is the heartbeat of a region, a sound that would register on seismographs continents away. You are inside the machine-park, but it extends beyond the horizon of your perception so far that the word "horizon" loses all meaning. The world is here, inside this darkness, this wetness, this noise, and it is vast enough to hold you forever, a single point of fleeting consciousness in a saturated, active, endlessly churning system that has forgotten you were ever there at all. The mist coils around a derrick leg, and a blind thing with too many legs pauses in its climb, scenting the air with feathery antennae. The pump strokes. The rock weeps. The darkness presses in, full of life, and the sound goes on.
