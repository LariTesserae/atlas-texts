They call it the Cataract Vaults, though no one alive remembers who named them or when. You enter through pressure doors that weep warm condensation, stepping into a space of constant gentle rain that never touches you.

The water is the air. It holds you suspended, breathable, a gel-thick atmosphere saturated with oxygen so dense your lungs work differently here — slower, deeper. Every inhalation tastes green, wet, alive. The liquid-light filters through it like honey through glass, illuminating a vertical world that refuses both ground and sky.

You are floating inside a cathedral built for nobody.

The walls rise in all directions — above, below, to every side — their surfaces completely obscured by growth. Not plants in any terrestrial sense, but something that learned from them: photosynthetic architecture. Copper-green pads like lily leaves scaled up to blankets. Translucent bladders clustered in spirals, each one faintly luminescent with its own internal chemistry. Thread-fine mycelial nets spanning the gaps between structures, crusted with nodules that breathe. Every surface. Every angle. There is no room between presences — one organism's root pierces another's stem, which cradles a third's fruiting body, which feeds a fourth's slow, thermal metabolism.

The industrial bones are still visible if you look. Catwalks of perforated steel, their railings wrapped so thickly in tendrils they've become cylindrical hedges. Pipes — hundreds of pipes — running in deliberate, engineered parallels before disappearing into the green. They still function. You can feel the deep hum of pumps through the liquid atmosphere, a subsonic pulse that never varies, never stops. Somewhere a turbine turns, patient as a heartbeat, driving this whole saturated machinery of circulation and aeration.

Light comes from nowhere and everywhere. No single source. The entire volume glows at the exact brightness of an overcast afternoon, diffused through the living water-air until shadow cannot find purchase. The photosynthetic mats contribute their own bioluminescent edge — a faint blue-green that cancels darkness. Every pipe, every tank, every slowly rotating fan blade exists in perfect visibility, encrusted, furred, smothered, festooned.

The temperature is skin-warm. Body-warm. If you stayed still long enough you might forget the boundary where you end and the vault begins.

There are no animals. No fish in this wet sky. No insects murmuring. No birds — where would they land? Every niche that movement might occupy has been filled instead by the slow. The patient. The sessile and the vegetative. Fungal towers grow in place of coral, reaching mottled brackets toward the center of the space. Things that are not exactly vines hang in curtains, their tips exuding viscous droplets that drift for minutes before finding purchase on something below.

And below is a direction that matters here.

The Vaults are arranged vertically — layer upon layer of catwalks and platforms and great bulbous tanks, each one an industrial component turned support structure, turned scaffold, turned soil. The water-air grows warmer as you drift downward, richer, thicker with dissolved nutrients. The growth intensifies. At the lowest accessible level, visible through gaps in the lattice, there is something like a root-mass — a solid knot of intertwined biology so dense it has become geography. Is that the bottom? Is there a floor beneath it? The archives are unclear.

Sound is almost absent. The pump-thrum you feel more than hear. The occasional soft pop of a seed pod releasing — you see it, a puff of pale motes dispersing into the liquid medium, drifting with a slowness that borders on the geological. Your own breathing, amplified, intimate. The rustle of your movements through the thick air. Nothing else. The quiet is not empty — it is saturated with presence, a stillness that comes from everything being exactly where it needs to be, doing exactly what it has done for longer than you can measure.

The Vaults are young by geological standards. The edges are still sharp where the original construction shows through — flanges, rivets, the machined precision of early industrial ambition. Erosion has not had time to round them. The water hasn't yet worn channels through the steel. The organisms haven't yet soil-ified the substrate, haven't broken down the human intention into anonymous fertile matter. You can see clearly: here is a pump housing, here is a pressure gauge — its needle still trembling at 2.4 atmospheres — here is a maintenance ladder leading up into a tangle that has made rungs into trellises.

What was this place built to do?

The tech is recognizable — water treatment perhaps, or aquaculture, or some experiment in closed-system habitation. Tanks with inspection windows still visible beneath the green. Control panels beaded with condensation, their buttons miniature ponds. But whatever it was made for has been overwritten by what it became. The purpose changed. Or perhaps the purpose was always this: a womb for a world that doesn't need animals, doesn't need motion, doesn't need sound.

The space is not large — a central chamber perhaps forty meters on its longest axis, with subsidiary alcoves and pipe-chases radiating outward. Boundaries are clear enough: the pressure doors, the hull-like curvature of the outer walls, the knowledge that outside is something else entirely, something this place was built to exclude or contain. But within those boundaries, it is total. Complete. A fully constructed cosmos where every element — liquid, light, temperature, chemistry — has been tuned and is maintained by the humming, invisible machines.

Something changes as you watch. So slowly your conscious mind cannot track it, only your peripheral awareness notes the shift. A fan blade completes its rotation. A photosynthetic mat on the far wall has expanded — no, not since you arrived, but it is expanding, will have covered that pipe junction in another month or so. The central cluster of bladder organisms has begun a new budding cycle: tiny translucent spheres, each one smaller than a fingernail, appearing along the edges of the parent forms. They will detach eventually. They will drift. They will find their own surfaces to colonize.

This is what strangeness means here. Not the shock of the alien, but the slow dawning recognition that you are in a place organized by logics adjacent to your own — recognizable enough to read, divergent enough to leave you permanently outside. The industrial skeleton speaks of human minds solving human problems. The photosynthetic flesh speaks of something else: a continuation, a proliferation, a life that took the invitation of warm water and sealed light and ran with it in directions no one planned.

The Vaults breathe. Not metaphorically. The pressure cycles — a slow sigh that takes seventeen minutes to complete, driven by the pumps, mediated by the gas-exchange membranes, answered by the collective respiration of every living mat and bladder and nodule in the space. Inhale: oxygen saturation rises, the water-air grows fractionally clearer, the growths seem to lift and spread. Exhale: carbon dioxide blurs the light for a moment, the organisms settle, droplets form and fall.

You could stay here. You could watch the fan blades turn for a hundred years and never see the same configuration twice — it's just that the differences would be too small, too patient for your animal attention. This place was not made for you. It has moved beyond the need for you. But it will tolerate your presence, warmly, quietly, while you float in its body-temperature sky and try to understand a world without mouths.
