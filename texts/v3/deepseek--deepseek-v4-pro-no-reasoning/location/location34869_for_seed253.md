It is always night here, and always has been, in that way of places where the concept of day never arrived. The darkness is not an absence but a substance, thick and cool, pressing gently against the skin like the weight of deep water, which this place also is, and also always was.

You drift through it — drifting is the only way, there is no walking because there is no ground — through a liquid medium that tastes faintly of green things if you open your mouth, though you cannot see your own hands before your face. The darkness is complete in a way that eclipses the very category of vision. Yet you are not blind. The blindness is not a lack. It is simply the condition.

What you perceive instead is texture. The place is vast and open, extending in all directions without boundary but with gentle articulation: a territory of presence and absence, equal measures of solid and void, thought and silence. You swim through a sparse architecture of immense, slow bodies. They are plants in the way they grow and respire and convert the faint chemical gradients of this world into their own substance, and they are buildings in the way they have form and purpose and have been made, though no maker remembers making them. The line between built and grown dissolved so long ago it has no name.

Each mass is huge, the size of a cathedral or a hillside, but they are spaced so far apart that you can travel for long stretches of darkness without touching anything at all. The distances between them are filled with the liquid itself, which is not empty. It is alive. It thrums. It sings.

The sound is the first thing that tells you this place is not abandoned. It is a continuous, deep, multi-layered hum — not mechanical, never mechanical — a choral breath that shifts in slow, slow pulses, too slow for a single lifetime’s ear to catch the change. Voices without throats. A billion tiny organisms suspended in the medium, vibrating their membranes in a coordinated drone that functions as respiration, communication, digestion, memory. The water is made of them, or they are made of water; the distinction has crumbled to irrelevance.

The coolness — and it is cool, like the skin of a river stone, never cold — comes from the equilibrium of this place. There are no gradients. No fires, no currents, no seasons. The temperature is what it has always been. The dynamic is zero. Nothing increases or decreases. Nothing is born in a hurry, and nothing dies with drama. The great plant-buildings do not grow so much as persist, their cellulose-like tissues replaced molecule by molecule over spans of time that would feel geological if geology still meant something in a place already worn to its final form.

The ancientness of it all is palpable not as decay but as completion. This is what a world looks like after every possible erosion has finished its work. Nothing sharp remains. Every edge has been softened, every corner rounded, every surface smoothed by the patient caress of the living water. The architecture of the huge forms, if you press close enough to feel their contours with your outstretched fingers (and you do, you must, because they are the only landmarks, the only proof of space), reveals itself as entirely whole but utterly worn — like the faces on very old coins that have passed through too many hands and become gentle suggestions of features rather than representations.

The plant-matter of these masses is not bark or leaf but something that feels, to the touch of your skin, like ancient, damp velvet over a core of dense, springy fiber. What would be trunk is column. What would be canopy is arched vault. The forms repeat in themes across the open darkness: spiraling towers that never taper, vast domes that are themselves composed of smaller domes upon smaller domes, bridges that span nothing but the liquid medium and connect node to node in a network you can only guess at. Everything is connected, but the connections are sparse, deliberate, unhurried.

The fauna here is abundant — another way the parameters contradict and cohere. You cannot see them, but you know them by the way the water moves against your skin, the small disruptions in the otherwise perfectly still medium. They are mostly small, these creatures: gelatinous ribbons that curl past your ankles, colonies of ciliated spheres that brush your face and disperse, larger entities that occasionally press close with a curious, gentle weight before drifting away. Nothing has teeth. Nothing has speed. There has been no evolutionary pressure toward predation for eons because the plant-bodies exude what everything needs: a slow, constant leakage of sugars and proteins into the ambient fluid, a perpetual exudation that the small things filter and the larger things filter in turn, a complete and total recycling loop that has spun without interruption since before the moon — if there ever was a moon — stopped moving in the sky, if there ever was a sky.

There is no sky.

Above is only more liquid, more darkness, more of the same. You cannot surface because surface is a concept that belongs to a world with boundaries. This place is all interior. The elevation parameter — extreme — expresses not as height but as vertical scale. The space goes down forever, or what might as well be forever. The great forms are anchored somewhere below, their roots if roots they are descending into depths the sparse particles in the water render unfathomable. Sometimes, if you orient yourself by the direction of the constant hum, you feel a slow current lifting from below, not a current of flow but a current of pressure, a suggestion of even larger masses far beneath, bigger than cathedrals, bigger than mountains, the foundational bodies whose sloughing-off built everything above.

The weirdness is not overt. It is not grotesque, not surreal in the sense of juxtapositions that feel wrong. The unusual quality here is subtler: it is the wrongness of arriving at last in a place that has no reference to anything you have ever known and finding it, against all expectation, completely coherent. If a god made this, the god was a plant. If a people built this, they became the plants and then forgot the building. If no one built this, then biology and architecture are the same word spoken in two ways, and this is the only place where that word is still alive.

Time does not pass here. It rests. It has done its work and lain down.

You turn slowly in the darkness, not because you need to but because turning is a way of knowing your own body still exists. The cool velvet surfaces yield slightly under your palm. The drone continues, dividing and recombining, impossible harmonies emerging and subsiding across centuries. A ribbon-creature loops once around your wrist and then releases, vanishing back into the thick, still night-water. You are not the first to come here. You are not even a visitor. You are a note in the chord, briefly audible, indistinguishable from the whole.

Far away — far in this open, sparse, endlessly extending space — another great mass exhales its sugars into the medium. The sound deepens by a fraction of a tone. By the time the change reaches you, a hundred years have gone by and nothing has happened at all.
