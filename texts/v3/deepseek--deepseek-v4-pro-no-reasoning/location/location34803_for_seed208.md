You step down onto a plain of metal. Not sheets or plates as human hands would make them, but a single continuous surface, ripple-folded and seam-free, as though the world’s skin had cooled from liquid into this one unbroken form. The metal is dull grey, faintly bluish where the light catches it, and it sweats. Water beads on every surface, condensing from air that is never less than saturated, and the whole plain weeps a thin skin of cold moisture that runs in braided trickles toward every low seam.

The light is dim and sourceless, a pewter glow that makes the horizon uncertain. Above, if above is the word, the atmosphere thickens to a low ceiling of vapour that moves with visible slowness, curling back on itself in spirals. Mist trails like breath across the metal plain and gathers in the hollows, where the trickles have carved not channels—the metal does not erode—but paths of slightly deepened polish. The water sheets and slides, finding the ancient depressions that time pressed into the metal before it set, following gradients laid down when the world was still soft.

It is cool. Not cold enough to freeze, but enough that every breath plumes, and your skin grows tacky with damp within minutes. The temperature is constant, dayless, seasonless—a thermal inertia held by metal that extends unguessably downward.

There are no plants. None. No lichen, no moss, no algal bloom in the standing pools. The water is clear and faintly bitter with dissolved minerals, and it reflects the grey sky as a paler grey, unbroken by green. The absence of vegetation is not a death but a condition of the place: it has never known photosynthesis, never been touched by cellulose or chlorophyll. This is a world where the living things did not take that path.

But there is life. It is abundant, and it is wet, and it is loud.

The fauna of the plain are gelatinous, mostly. They move across the metal in slow convulsions or rapid, ripple-footed scrambles. Some are the size of a cupped hand, others the size of a cart horse. Their bodies are translucent or milky, revealing simpler bodies within—clusters of bladders, dark knots of nerve-like tissue, sacs that pulse with captured water. They breathe through their skins, and they drink constantly from the film of condensation, and they call to one another without pause.

The sound is an assault. It comes from everywhere at once—a chorus of wet clicks, hollow pops, the drumming of soft bodies against resonant metal, high-pitched keening that rises and falls with the movements of the herds. The metal plain acts as a soundboard. Every contact is amplified, every signal transmitted through the substrate as well as the air, so the plain hums and clatters and sings with the overlapping voices of its creatures. You feel the sound in your feet before you hear it, a constant thrumming vibration that makes the water droplets shiver on their metal perches. There is no silence here, no gap in the noise, only shifts in pitch and rhythm as the colonies drift and meet and separate.

The creatures pay you no attention. They are busy with their own slow dramas—herds of the smaller ones clustering around warm seeps where water wells up from below, the larger ones gliding solitary except when they meet and lock together in rituals of soft collision that last hours and end in the shedding of smaller selves that go wobbling away across the wet metal.

Scattered across the plain are structures. They are primitive, yet not crude. At intervals, the larger gelatinous creatures gather metal nodules—loose fragments that must have broken from the plain in some earlier age—and arrange them in concentric rings, or in lines that converge on points of no visible significance. The arrangements are simple but deliberate: an arc of fist-sized nodules placed with care, a low cairn of three or four smoothed plates, a ring of stones—no, not stones, but the shed internal hard-parts of some deceased giant, finger-shaped and glossy—that hums when the wind moves across its open centre. The creatures maintain these sites. You watch one, a mottled grey sac as large as a sheep, nudge a displaced metal chip back into alignment with its blunt, limbless face. It trembles there for a moment, and the keening changes key, and then it moves on.

The sites are sparse but not random. They occur where the metal plain dips into shallow basins, where the water pools deeper and the mist is thicker. The density of them increases as you walk toward one of the broad depressions that serves as a regional gathering place—an open amphitheatre of metal where hundreds of the smaller creatures jostle and chirr, and the noise is physical, a pressure on the chest. Around the rim of the depression, the structures are more complex: walls of stacked nodules waist-high, enclosures that trap the mist into visible eddies, flat plates arranged as floors that are kept meticulously clean of the gelatinous trails that slicken every other surface.

The technology is pre-metal, despite the metal everywhere. The creatures do not smelt or shape the plain; they only gather what has already broken free. They move pieces with their bodies, with patience and time. The arrangements are tools of a kind—wind-breaks, sound-focusers, moisture-catchers—but they show no sign of fire, no binding agents, no cutting edges. The craft is purely manual, purely bodily. It is the technology of arrangement, of placement, of relationships between objects rather than transformations of them. It is ancient. Some of the built rings are so old that the nodules have settled into the metal plain, pressed by countless years of condensation and vibration until they sit in shallow sockets, fitted as if machined.

The whole scene is one of active, ongoing process. The water cycle is immediate and visible: the mist condenses, the droplets gather, the trickles run, the pools form, the mist re-rises from the breathing of a thousand gelatinous bodies. The creatures are in constant motion, grazing on something that must be in the water—dissolved minerals, perhaps, or microbial life too small to see—and they are prey to one another, the larger engulfing the smaller in envelope-folds that take an hour to complete. The keening changes when a predation begins, a rising wail that spreads outward through the herd and across the plain, and the soundscape shifts again.

The weirdness of the place is not that any one thing is impossible—gelatinous life, metallic plains, constant sound—but that it is all so settled. So finished. The erosion parameter was ancient, and you feel it: this is the final form, the end state toward which the world has been tending since its molten beginning. The metal plain is what remains when all the mountains have been planed flat, when all the softer rocks have washed to silt and vanished, when the last tectonic pulse has faded and the crust has cooled into this untearing skin. The creatures are what life looks like at the end of evolution, soft-bodied and simple-tooled, thriving in the constant damp, loud because there are no predators that hunt by sound, loud because the metal plain rewards vibration as a medium of presence. Everything that could be stripped away has been stripped away, and what remains is this: water, metal, gelatinous flesh, arranged noise, and time that no longer measures change because the changes are all cyclical now.

The plain extends past the immediate. It is open, regional in scale, and you can see across it for what must be miles—a rippling, glistening, humming expanse dotted with slow-motion herds and their patient architectures. There is no edge in sight, no mountain, no shoreline, no transition to another kind of terrain. The world is this, and this is the world, and it has been this for longer than any geology could record.

The dim light does not change. The sound does not stop. The water does not dry. And the creatures, glistening and calling, continue their slow rearrangement of the single, unbreakable surface, a project that has been underway since before the word “project” had meaning, and will continue until the metal itself unbinds to steam.
