A plain of salt that stretches beyond the curve of the world, baked to the color of bone under a sun that seems too large and too white for the sky that holds it. The light here doesn’t simply arrive—it presses down, reflects up from the crystalline crust, fills every angle until shadow becomes a memory. Your eyes adjust, and adjust again, and still find nothing soft to rest upon.

The salt isn't natural salt, not exactly. Walk on it and it doesn't crunch—it rings. A faint, singing note under each footfall, as though the entire plain is a single struck bell, impossibly vast, resonating down into depths you cannot see. The surface is patterned in interlocking geometric scarps: hexagonal plates a stride across, each one slightly concave, each one rimmed with a raised lip of harder mineral that glints in colors just beyond the visible spectrum. You perceive them as shifts in brightness, depths in the whiteness, a shimmer that shouldn't be there.

This is the Sediment Crown. A fully engineered landscape, built and then abandoned so long ago that the builders have no name, no fossil record, no trace except the plain itself. Beneath the salt plates lies the true structure: layers of computational crystal, molecular lattices grown over millions of years, still processing—still active—but in cycles so slow that a single thought takes geological ages to complete. The faint ringing is the sound of data moving through stressed lattices, a quiet music of computation that never stops. In the stillness, you can almost hear it as a pressure in the inner ear, a subsonic hum that bypasses hearing and settles directly into the bones.

Low elevation: the plain sits in a basin so broad that the horizon bends differently than it should. The curvature is perceptible if you lie flat and look edgeward—the salt grades into a haze of light that smears sky and ground together without a join. No hills, no mountains, no raised features at all. The builders erased topography as thoroughly as they erased themselves, planed the continent down to a single continuous surface that spans what used to be seafloor and uplift both, indifferent to the former geology.

Warmth rises from the plates in steady exhale. The salt catches heat and holds it, radiates it back in shimmering waves that make the honeycomb pattern dance. Temperature settles around the high end of comfortable, nudging toward oppressive in the still air. No wind to speak of—the scale is too vast for small weather, and the engineered materials don't convect the way natural ground would. Instead there are thermal tides: slow, invisible currents of hotter and cooler air that slide across the plain in laminar sheets, never breaking into turbulence. Sometimes two sheets intersect and you feel the boundary pass across your skin as a momentary cool stripe, a single second of relief, then gone.

Vegetation exists, but it's strange and it's hiding. Sparse—the parameter says 0.7—and what there is has learned to occupy the cracks between plates, the micro-shadows beneath the scarps' raised rims. Look closely and you'll find the saltweeds: hair-thin filaments of silica and carbon, colored the exact white-blue of the salt they grow through, photosynthesizing not from the visible light but from the ultraviolet wash that saturates everything here. They form mats no thicker than lichen, spread in lacy networks across the plate interiors. Touch them and they retract—not quickly, but in a visible crawl, pulling back into their crevices over the course of a breath or two. Sessile life, patient beyond measure, pulling what it needs from light and the slow exudates of the crystal processors beneath.

Fauna: rarer still, but present if you know how to look. The salt-skimmers are flat disks of flexible mineral, no thicker than paper, that use differential heating to float on the thermal tides. They ride the invisible currents for decades without touching ground, absorbing UV through their upper membranes and excreting crystalline waste that falls as a fine, constant dust—the only source of new material on the surface. They're so thin they're nearly transparent, visible mostly as distortions in the geometric pattern below, brief lenses passing overhead. Their life cycles are timed to the computational pulses of the substrate, which occur once every few centuries. In the long intervals, they simply drift, dormant in everything but motion.

The sky is a problem. It's too bright—parameter 2.8—and the white sun isn't alone. There's a ring system, or the remnant of one, a band of fragmented material that catches and scatters light across the entire dome of heaven. No blue. The sky is pearl, is opal, is a nacreous sheet that gives no directional sense of the star. You navigate by the grain of the salt plates, which all orient toward a single point on the horizon: the old convergence, the computational focus, whatever it was the Crown was built to think about for these million years.

Silence, mostly. Sound parameter 0.3—but not true silence. The ringing of your steps fades slowly, absorbed into the counting hum of the deep lattices. There's a faint crackle from the saltweeds as they adjust their filaments in the thermal tides. The skimmers make no sound at all. Rarely, perhaps once in a watch, a deeper note sounds from far away: a plate shifting, a stress fracture propagating through ancient crystal, the ground adjusting its thoughts. It arrives through the feet more than the ears, a seismic whisper that rolls across the plain and is gone.

Total construction, total artificiality—and yet it has become an ecosystem, a thing with plants and animals and weather of a sort, all evolved into the niches the builders left empty. Nothing here remembers a natural world. Everything is native to the Crown, descended from whatever microscopic remnants or deliberate seedings were left when the original project ceased to be tended. The salt is the substrate. The processors are the bedrock. The ring in the sky might be debris from the construction, or might be the builders themselves, dismantled into a monument.

Strange, but not incomprehensibly so. Weirdness 1.5—enough to unsettle, not enough to break the mind. The logic is there if you sit with it: a machine that thinks in geological time, a landscape that has become the thing it was built on top of, life that feeds on computation and crystallized light. The vastness means you can walk for days and see nothing but the same hexagonal plates, the same lacy weeds, the same bright pearl sky—and yet the ground beneath you is working, is thinking, is holding a conversation with itself in a language of stress and resonance that will finish its next sentence long after you are dead.

Active. Dynamic. Not still at all, despite the silence. The thermal tides flow, the skimmers migrate on century-long cycles, the saltweeds spread and retract, the plates slowly realign themselves toward the shifting computational focus, and somewhere deep in the lattice, a calculation older than your species grinds forward one more step. The quiet is the quiet of a heart beating too slowly to hear, not the quiet of death.

You can lie down and press your ear to the salt. It's warm. You can feel the ringing in your skull. If you stay long enough, the saltweeds might grow over you, mistaking your stillness for a new feature of the plain, incorporating your outline into their patterned growth. You'd become part of the computation then, a variable in the long equation, your body a brief sculpture of carbon and water on the bone-white plate, dissolving over centuries into the only monument this place can offer: the faintest shift in a single hexagon's alignment, a correction toward focus, a note in the endless chord.
