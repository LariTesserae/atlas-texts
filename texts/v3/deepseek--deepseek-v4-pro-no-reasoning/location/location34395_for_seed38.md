The place has no name because nothing here has ever needed to name it.

You arrive not by crossing a boundary but by slowly realizing you have been inside it for some time. The ground is not ground in the usual sense—it is a continuous, living mat of compressed vegetation, a thousand generations of plants growing upon the flattened, mineralized remains of their ancestors, thickness upon thickness going down and down. Your feet sink slightly with each step, the surface spongy and warm, exhaling moisture that beads on your skin and drips back down among the leaves.

Everything is wet, but not with standing water. The wetness lives in the air itself, a saturated stillness that makes the distance shimmer faintly despite the dim light. It condenses on every surface: on the broad, thick leaves that overlap like shingles, on the rope-like stems that crisscross the under-reach, on the occasional outcropping of rock that pushes through the vegetation like the knuckle of some buried giant. These stones are dark with damp, their surfaces weeping slow tears from fissures that have not changed in eras. When you touch one, it is warm—blood-warm, as if the heat comes from inside rather than above.

Above. The sky, if it can be called that, is a low ceiling of interlocked canopies, layer upon layer stacked so thoroughly that direct light never reaches here. What illumination exists is a diffuse, green-tinged dusk, filtered through living tissue after living tissue until it arrives as something like deep water light. It does not change perceptibly; if there is day and night beyond, the vegetal roof swallows the difference. You have been here long enough now that you are not sure time is passing at all, or if it ever did.

The sounds are few and widely spaced, each one distinct in the otherwise profound quiet. A low, almost subsonic hum issues from everywhere and nowhere—the combined respiration of millions of leaves, perhaps, or the slow hydraulic movement of sap through stems as thick as your body. Occasionally there is a wet, tearing sound from somewhere far off, a sound that travels strange paths through the dense air, arriving at your ears from a direction that does not match its source. Once, a high, thin note like a single string plucked in a cathedral, held impossibly long and then fading into the hum again.

You begin to notice the structures.

They are not buildings in any architectural sense. They are more like suggestions of geometry within the otherwise seamless weave of life. A vertical plane of stems grown so perfectly parallel that your eye follows it up into the canopy and sees it continue, a wall of living wood that extends further than you can trace. Circular clearings where the ground-mat dips into a shallow bowl, the vegetation around the rim growing inward and interlocking above to form a low dome with a single opening at its apex—you stand in one and look up through the aperture at layer after layer of leaves giving way to leaves, a tunnel of green receding into green. The space feels intentional, or at least consequential, as if something had once needed a room and the ecosystem had slowly grown around that need, preserving its shape long after the need itself dissolved.

Scattered among the living architecture are artifacts, if that word still applies. They are so integrated into the plant-skin of the world that you almost mistake them for natural forms. A length of something that is not wood, not stone, not bone, curving up from the mat and then disappearing into it again, its surface textured with a pattern too regular to be random but too organic to be machined. What you can see of it suggests a larger shape submerged beneath the growth, a buried geometry that the roots and stems have embraced rather than cracked. Another object—smooth, hemispherical, pale as old ivory—rests in the crotch of a massive stem cluster, half-swallowed by the plant's slow sculpting. From its rim, a single drop of water falls at intervals you cannot predict, each drop landing on a broad leaf below with a soft, percussive plink that echoes slightly in the stillness.

The warmth is constant and strange. It does not come from a sun but from the breathing mass itself—decomposition and growth in such perfect balance that the entire realm hums at blood-heat. When you place your palm flat on the ground, you feel a faint pulse, slow as a sleeper's heartbeat, and realize it matches the cadence of your own.

You walk, though "walk" implies a destination and here there are none. The terrain rises so gradually you would not notice except that the composition of the vegetation slowly shifts: the broad-leaved forms give way to something more filamentous, feathery fronds that brush your shoulders and leave trails of condensed moisture on your skin. The air grows slightly cooler—still warm, but no longer body-heat—and the light shifts imperceptibly toward grey-green. You have gained elevation, you realize. The entire landscape is a vast, shallow dome, continental in scale, so gentle in its slope that you could walk for a lifetime and only realize you had climbed a mountain when you began to see the layers differently.

From this slightly higher vantage, you can make out the texture of the canopy-top stretching away. It is not flat but gently undulant, a topography of its own making, with swells and hollows that must be the size of small countries. In one direction, a darker band runs across the green—a river, perhaps, or a seam where two vegetal plates meet. In another, a faint pallor suggests a region where the canopy thins, where maybe something else breaks through. Steam rises here and there in slow, tall columns that dissipate into the overcast, marking places where the inner heat finds a vent.

You see movement. Not discrete animals, not yet—you cannot pick out individual creatures—but a shifting in the middle distance, a ripple passing through the fronds like wind over a wheat field, though there is no wind here. The ripple moves against the logic of any breeze, heading purposefully in a direction that makes you think of migration, of something vast and cohesive flowing beneath the surface. As it passes, the hum deepens briefly, a chord change in the world's slow song.

And then something closer. A shape detaches from a stem at the edge of your vision, something that had been perfectly still and perfectly camouflaged as a knot of wood or a cluster of seed pods. It unfolds—limbs, maybe, or segments, or something else entirely—and reorients itself toward you with a motion too smooth to be called sudden but too deliberate to ignore. You cannot parse its form; your eyes slide off it as if it were made of the same dim green light as everything else. But you feel it attending, a presence that is curious without being hungry, ancient without being tired, strange in the way that something wholly of this place can be strange while belonging here completely.

The creature—presence—being—holds its attention on you for a span of time you cannot measure and then folds itself back into the stem cluster, becoming indistinguishable once again. The hum returns to its prior pitch. The water drops continue their erratic rhythm.

You sit on a warm stone that seems to have been waiting for someone to sit on it, though it was not shaped for a human body. The vegetation around you slowly adjusts, a leaf here and a tendril there shifting to accommodate your presence, not aggressively but with the patient, senseless hospitality of a place that has been itself for so long that it has forgotten anything else ever existed. You feel the slow pulse through the stone. You breathe the saturated air. You become, slightly, part of the hum.

The place goes on. It has been going on for longer than continents. It will continue. You are a brief, warm pressure on its skin, already being absorbed into its memory, already becoming one of the faint marks it holds.
