The air is thick with sound, a low, pervasive drone that thrums through the dark like a second darkness, a pressure against the ears. It comes from everywhere and nowhere—from the walls, from the distant ceilings lost in shadow, from the slow, grinding exhale of something vast and unseen. This is no natural cavern, not anymore. It remembers being carved, but that memory is ancient, overwritten by accretions of something else, something that grew in the long centuries after the builders left, or changed, or became something other.

You are below the surface, deep enough that the world above is a rumor no one believes. The elevation is a negative number, a slow descent that happened so gradually you never noticed the transition from basement to buried, from buried to sunken, from sunken to whatever this is. The space around you is immense by any human reckoning—cathedral-scale, yes, a hollowed-out volume that could swallow a small town and leave room for silence. But it’s not empty. That’s the strange thing. The density parameter says nearly nothing per cubic meter, and that’s true, there’s a terrible openness, a near-vacuum of solid objects, but the emptiness itself feels inhabited. The space between things is a presence. The hollow has weight.

The ground underfoot is uneven, a buckled pavement of something that was once flat and purposeful. It sweats. Water comes not from above—there is no rain here, not in any ordinary sense—but from the surfaces themselves, a pervasive damp that beads on every material, a condensation cycle driven by the temperature differential between warm walls and cooler air. The warmth is constant, a humid, enveloping 30-something degrees that makes the skin prickle and the lungs work harder. The moisture gathers in rivulets, tracing slow paths down the buckled floor toward low places where it pools in shallow, motionless sheets. Sometimes a droplet falls from a high unseen protrusion and strikes a pool with a sound like a wet finger on glass, startlingly distinct against the drone. That sound echoes. Everything echoes.

There is vegetation, but calling it that feels like a category error. It’s sparse—isolated colonies that appear at long intervals, as if they’re the outposts of some larger network you can’t perceive. The growths are pale, non-photosynthetic, feeding on something else: heat, perhaps, or chemical exudates from the walls, or the faint electrical fields that still ghost through the ancient infrastructure. They resemble nothing so much as lung tissue, or sea sponges, or the gills of a mushroom turned inside out. Translucent white flushed with pink and grey, they pulse almost imperceptibly, in time with the drone, or maybe that’s an illusion. When you touch one—if you touch one—it’s warm and dry on the surface, unnervingly so, but gives slightly, like pressing on the skin of a drum. They don’t react. They just continue their slow, blind metabolism, filtering the thick air for whatever it is they eat.

The built environment is layered beyond easy comprehension. This was a city once, or something that served the function of a city: a dense, organized settlement burrowed into the crust for reasons now opaque. The technology on display is post-recognizable. Walls are not simply walls—they’re strata of materials that shift under prolonged gaze, surfaces that were poured or deposited or grown in ways that current physics struggles to model. There are seams that suggest molecular welding, edges that blur into neighboring structures with no visible joint, conduits that glow faintly with a light you can only see from the corner of your eye. And yet, for all that sophistication, this place is old. The erosion index is high, not in the sense of active, abrasive decay, but in the sense of a long, slow settling into terminal form. The edges are softened. The sharp angles have rounded. The structures have slumped into a kind of equilibrium with gravity and time, like melting ice cream on a geologic scale. Nothing is falling apart anymore. Everything has already finished falling apart. What remains is the stable core, the skeleton that erosion couldn't take.

Fauna is rare. You might spend hours, days down here without seeing anything that moves of its own volition. But you sense them at the edges of perception. A skittering in the dark that stops when you turn your head. A brief, pale shape sliding behind a distant pillar—no, a structural column, fifty meters tall and grooved like a tree trunk, but made of some ceramic composite that rings faintly when you tap it. The creatures, if they exist, have learned to evade the pools of light that don’t exist because there is no light. The darkness is nearly absolute. Light rate: 0.3. That means you brought whatever illumination you have, and it feels fragile, a tiny bubble of visibility in a sea of black that presses in like a physical force. The walls drink light. The spongy growths drink light. The air itself seems to absorb it, so that your beam dies ten meters out, swallowed by the sheer volume of the space.

And yet the city is not silent. The loudness parameter is high, and it’s not just the drone. There are other sounds, less constant but regular enough to form a kind of rhythm. A distant, metallic groan, as of some enormous weight shifting in its sleep. A high-pitched whine that rises and falls, like a turbine spooling up somewhere miles away and then dying again. A percussive knock, repeated twice, always twice, with a pause of exactly forty-seven seconds between each pair. Something is still functioning. Something is still alive, in the machine sense of the word. Or maybe it’s not a machine. The weirdness parameter is moderate, a quiet 0.9, not the in-your-face surrealism of a dream but the subtler wrongness of a place that has its own rules and doesn’t care to explain them. For instance, sound does not travel in straight lines down here. A noise that should be on your left arrives from above. A whisper aimed at a companion comes back from behind you, half a second late, distorted. The acoustics are impossible, bent by the architecture or by something in the air itself, a density gradient or a field of some kind that the builders wove into the walls.

This is an active place, despite the emptiness. The dynamic score of 1.9 reflects processes, not populations. There is a weather cycle here, subterranean and bizarre: the drone intensifies over a period of hours, then subsides; the condensation shifts from beading to streaming and back; the distant groaning becomes more frequent, then less, following a circadian rhythm that has no connection to any sun. The spongy growths release spores on some hidden schedule, a faint dusting of pale motes that hang in the still air and settle on your skin, not irritating, just present, a reminder that the ecology here is slow but not static. Somewhere, something is consuming. Somewhere, something is reproducing.

The overall impression is of a place that was abandoned so long ago that it has circled back around to being inhabited again—not by its makers, but by the consequences of its making. It’s a ruin that has matured, a city that outlived its citizens and then outlived its own decay, entering a third phase of existence that is neither civilization nor wilderness but a strange, humming middle ground. The walls weep. The machines sing to themselves in frequencies that make your fillings ache. The empty space breathes. And somewhere in the deepest part of the cathedral-dark, a droplet falls, and the sound of its landing takes a full three seconds to reach you, arriving soft and clear and utterly alone.
