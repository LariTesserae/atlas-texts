It is a place that does not announce itself; it is simply *there*, and has been forever. The ground underfoot is not soil but a vast, single sheet of dark, slick rock. It could be basalt, polished by a patience beyond human reckoning, but its surface holds a thousand shallow, radiating cracks like the glaze on ancient ceramic. Every one of these cracks is a capillary, and the landscape is its own weather system. There is no river, not in the usual sense, but the entire rockface is a slow, breathing skin of water. It weeps from the stone in a thin, silvery film, gathers in shallow, circular stone pockets that the wind has somehow drilled over eons, then overflows and slides down the next tilted plane.

The air is cool, maybe a breath past ten degrees, and it has a mineral taste, like licking a clean, wet stone. It is never warm, never freezing. There is no season. The temperature has no argument with itself. And the light—the light is a constant, bright, directionless silver-white, as if the sky is a single pane of diffused glass stretched from horizon to horizon. There is no sun, no moon, no clouds to track. The brightness is simply an attribute of the place, the way darkness is an attribute of a cave. It makes every wet surface glitter, not with sparkle but with a steady, glassy sheen, so that the whole tilted world seems lacquered.

And it is tilted. This is the first and last fact of the place: a plateau of stone pitched at a severe but unvarying angle, rolling away toward a horizon that is impossibly, dangerously far. The elevation is extreme. You understand this not by seeing valleys below but by the thinness of the air, the slight pressure in your ears, the way your lungs work a little harder for the same restless breath. There is no edge, no cliff to stand on and look down. The world just falls away, rising from where you stand like the deck of a vast, capsizing ship, until it meets the blank silver of the sky. You could walk for days downward, or what you think is downward, and never reach a shore.

The rock is not empty. It is sparsely, deliberately colonized. Here and there, in the shallow water-pockets, a species of plant grows that looks less like a plant and more like a slow, green thought. It is a moss, perhaps, but not one you have seen: it forms low, packed domes the size of a sleeping dog, each one a dense cushion of stiff, hair-like fronds, the color of old jade. They are widely spaced, one every fifty, sixty, a hundred meters, each growing in a pool that is exactly the right depth, exactly the right shape. They do not spread. They do not seed, as far as you can tell. They simply exist, each one a silent, breathing lung. The water around them is clearer than the water elsewhere, as if they filter it simply by being near.

And then there are the birds. Or what would be birds, if birds could be made of wind and argument. They are small, not much larger than your hand, with dark, drab bodies and long, swallow-like wings, and they are everywhere. The air is never empty of them. They come up from the lower slopes in the same way the wind does, and they fill the cool, bright air with a sound that is not birdsong but pure, abrasive noise. It is a hard, clipped, metallic chittering—thousands of voices overlapping, peaking, dropping, never pausing for breath. It is not loud so much as *constant*, a wall of sound that presses on the ears like the light presses on the eyes. They wheel in a loose, purposeful gyre above the stone, never landing, never feeding, at least not in any way you can see. They are the only animal, but they are abundant beyond counting, a single living body of sound that has no beginning and no end.

Yet there are traces of others. Here and there, in the shallow water, lie small, pale stones that are not stone. Bones, perhaps, or something like them—each one a single, perfect, finger-length needle of white, as thin as a twig, scattered alone in a water-pocket, as if left deliberately. No skulls, no ribs, no feet. Just these single elongated shards, water-darkened at one end, bleached at the other, lying in the cold, clear pools. They are not fresh; they are polished, softened, almost mineral. But they are worked. Not by metal, not even by sharpened flint, but something like care—each one scored at the thick end with four parallel lines, running the same way, at the same depth. A notch, a tally, a name. You will find one every hundred meters or so, if you look. Never more than one. Never less.

There is no shelter here, no built thing. Not a wall, not a cairn, not a blade of grass hacked into order. Nothing that is not rock, water, wind, moss, bird, or bone. If anyone has ever lived here, they left nothing but those four-line scratches. No fire pits, no tools, no tracks. The world is unwalled, and the horizon is an open wound.

The strangeness of the place lies not in any single feature but in its complete absence of change. The wind blows—it must blow, because the birds bank on it and the water trembles—but it does not gust. It is a steady, mechanical current, a cold, wet breath that moves downhill at the same speed, day after day, year after year. The water runs at the same pace. The mosses grow not by seasons but by some internal clock you cannot sense. The birds never cease their screaming. Nothing accumulates. Nothing erodes, not in any way you can measure. The rock is not being worn away; it is exactly as it has always been, as if the process that shaped it finished long ago and then simply stopped, like a wave turned to glass. The place is in equilibrium with itself, a closed loop with no outside. The wind is the water is the light is the sound. Nothing is added, nothing is taken away.

It is a world with no story. It is not waiting for you. It is not haunted, exactly. It simply *is*, and you are the only thing in it that will ever change, the only thing that starts and stops, the only thing that walks downhill. And the longer you stay, the more you understand that the birds are not calling to each other. They are calling to the stone. And the stone, in its own mineral way, is listening.
