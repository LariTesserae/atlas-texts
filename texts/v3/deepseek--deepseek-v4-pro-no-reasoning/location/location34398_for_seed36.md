The first thing you notice is the light. It is not sunlight — you are far below the surface for that — but it is comprehensive, sourceless, the colour of overripe citrus. It seems to emanate from the walls themselves, or perhaps from the thin skein of moisture that coats every surface. The glow pulses almost imperceptibly, a slow circadian rhythm without a sun to answer to.

You are inside a chamber of living tissue.

The space is vast — not infinite, but measured in hundreds of metres, a single ovoid hollow whose boundaries curve away into the luminous distance. There is no floor, not in any geological sense. Instead, the ground beneath you is a thick, fibrous mat of interwoven roots and stems, spongy underfoot, warm as skin. It is a composite substance, this floor: a million individual plants have pressed themselves together so tightly and for so long that they have become a single continuous organism. When you shift your weight, a tiny amount of amber liquid wells up between the fibres and is reabsorbed. The air is heavy with the smell of sap and something else, something almost animal — a sweet, lactic richness, like the breath of a sleeping infant.

The architecture of the place is entirely botanical. Enormous structural ribs cross the chamber, curving from the floor up the walls and meeting overhead in a distant latticework. They are not wood, not exactly — they have the look of reinforced petioles, the stems of some colossal leaf scaled up to cathedral proportions. Their surfaces are smooth and slightly tacky, and in places you can see the faint striations of internal vessels, xylem and phloem grown to the diameter of drainpipes, pulsing with the slow, silent movement of sap.

Scattered across the floor are the remains of human presence. They are few and they are small, and they do not interrupt the vast vegetable order of the place so much as they are being gently consumed by it. A stone hearth, its mortar crumbling, half-swallowed by the advancing root-mat. A single wall of dressed limestone, leaning at a drunken angle, its surface furred with a pale moss that glows faintly in the pervasive light. A wooden doorframe standing free, door long gone, now serving as a trellis for a cascade of flowering vines whose blossoms are a deep, wet crimson, almost black. The flowers open and close in a rhythm unrelated to anything you can perceive — a slow, thoughtful breathing.

There are no animals here. Or rather, the animals are the plants, or have become the plants, or the distinction has ceased to matter. If you stand still long enough, you begin to sense a vast, distributed presence — not a mind, exactly, but an awareness. The chamber knows you are here. The light shifts fractionally when you move. The moisture on the walls thickens in your direction. The floor adjusts its give beneath your feet.

The silence is not absolute. It is a thick, living quiet, full of tiny sounds. The constant, almost subsonic hum of sap moving through the great vascular pipes. The faint crackle of new growth, leaves unfurling somewhere in the distant reaches of the ceiling. The occasional wet pop of a seed pod releasing its cargo into the warm, still air. Once, from very far away, comes a long, low creak — one of the great petiole-arches settling under its own enormous weight, a sound so deep you feel it in your chest rather than hear it.

Water is everywhere but it is never free. You can see where it weeps from the walls in a hundred places, clear fluid beading on the surface of the living tissue, running down in rivulets that are caught and channelled by specially shaped leaves, funnelled into the mouths of pitcher plants the size of bathtubs, absorbed back into the system before it ever has a chance to pool. The air is saturated — you are breathing water as much as air — and the temperature is a steady, equatorial warmth that never varies. Heat rises from the floor itself, from the slow metabolic furnace of the roots, from the composting of ten thousand generations of leaves.

This place is young. You can tell, somehow, despite its cathedral scale and its air of ancient, settled purpose. The walls have a raw look to them, a freshly-grown quality. The great ribs are still thickening, new layers of tissue being laid down at a rate just barely perceptible to the eye. The chamber is not finished. It is still becoming itself, still elaborating its form according to some deep, slow plan that has no need of haste. In the far distance, where the curve of the wall disappears into the haze of its own light, you can see the darker mouths of tunnels — new growth, new chambers being opened, the plant-architecture extending itself further into the dark earth.

There is no day or night. There is no season. There is only the slow, steady pulse of the light, the patient construction of form, and the occasional human remnant being quietly absorbed into the fabric of the world. The hearthstone will be gone in another generation. The doorframe will be a trellis for a hundred more years, and then it will be enclosed entirely, a ghost of geometry inside a living wall.

You realise, after a time, that the chamber is breathing with you. The moss on the limestone wall flushes a shade darker, a shade lighter. The moisture in the air condenses on your skin and then evaporates. The floor under your feet shifts by millimetres, accommodating your weight, learning your shape.

This place has never been seen before, not by eyes like yours. It is still inventing what it means to be seen.
