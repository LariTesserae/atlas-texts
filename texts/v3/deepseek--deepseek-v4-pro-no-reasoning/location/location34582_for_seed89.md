The place is a hanging valley, a bowl the size of a cathedral nave carved into the shoulder of an enormous range. The walls are not clean rock but a kind of crumbling conglomerate—rounded stones the size of fists and heads set in a matrix of hardened red mud, the whole mass polished by wind and water into smooth, undulant ribs. The valley floor is a long, tilted shelf of the same material, sloping away to the east and ending in a lip that overhangs a drop of unguessable depth. Beyond the lip, in the dark, there is only a different quality of darkness, less textured, more hollow.

Water arrives here not as a stream but as seepage. The walls weep. In the warmth—and it is warm, a still, humid warmth like the exhalation of a sleeping animal—the stone sweats. Films of water slide down the conglomerate, gathering in shallow, pebbled runnels that braid across the floor. Pockets of standing water sit in basins where the mud-matrix has dissolved, each pool a few hand-spans across, perfectly still, each reflecting a faint smear of skylight that does not actually exist. The air is heavy, almost drinkable, and carries a mineral taste: wet clay, copper, something faintly ammoniac.

The sparseness of the vegetation is a matter of kind, not absence. There are plants here, but they are not plants in any soft sense. They grow in the cracks and in the mouths of the runnels—low, spiral-stemmed growths the color of iron, with leaves reduced to waxy scales. Some are little towers of tight, overlapping foliage, knee-high, each one hosting a colony of something too small to see, a fine silver fuzz that ripples in the air currents. There is one tree, if it can be called that, near the valley's back wall: a single trunk, bone-colored, splitting at the top into five branches that lie almost horizontal, each branch covered in a shag of dry, hair-like fibers. It does not sway. The air is too still. It is, instead, always vibrating slightly, invisibly, as if something inside it is breathing.

The valley is loud, but the sound is not continuous. It comes in slow, overlapping pulses. From the walls—every few breaths—a clatter, sudden and brief, as a fist-sized stone loosens somewhere high and tumbles, bouncing against the conglomerate, sweeping smaller stones with it, all of them faltering, then stopping. The sound echoes, but not far. The air is too dense for echoes to travel; they die into a muffled pattering. Underneath that, a wet sound: the drip of water from high ledges, but irregular, arrhythmic, each drip landing on the stone or into a pool with a soft, swallowed chord. And underneath that, the fauna.

The fauna is everything. The valley is not so much inhabited as filled. The pools are all ringed by gatherings of small, soft-shelled creatures the size of a thumb, their backs blank and glistening, their undersides raked with tiny, rowing legs. They are stacked three and four deep in places, climbing over each other with a slow, patient urgency, each movement producing a faint adhesive click. They do not seem to go anywhere. They simply turn in the ring, a slow eddy of bodies around each black, reflective eye of water.

The walls, too, are alive. The silver fuzz on the plants moves in waves; close up, the fuzz is made of thousands of minute, translucent bodies, each fixed to the leaf by a thread of silk, each with two long, feathered antennae that sweep the air in synchrony. The vibration of the tree turns out to be a cloud of long-legged flies, their wings too damp to lift them, some crawling, some clinging, some rubbing their wings together so that the entire tree emits a low, constant hum, like a promise, or a threat.

And there are larger ones. You only see them when the light changes, which it does rarely and without source—a dimming, a slight shift in the gray-brown dimness, and suddenly the floor is not empty. Shapes detach from the shadows between stones: things the size of a large dog, with too many legs and flattened, disc-like bodies, their surfaces the same mottled red as the rock. They lie in wait, absolutely still, until the light dims, and then they rush—not toward anything visible, but in a programmed, circular path, every creature tracing the same route through the runnels, one after another, their feet making a sound like rain on tin. Then the light shifts back, and they settle, gone again.

There are no constructed structures. Nothing is made here that is not body. The valley itself is the only structure, and the marks on it are all from these endless, patient processes. The surfaces are worn into channels and hollows by the discs that walk the same paths; the pools are ringed by a colorless deposit, a kind of mineral rim left by the bodies of the pool-creatures, layer after layer, so that the pools have grown shallow, raised, like cups set on pedestals of their own making. Some of the oldest pools have closed entirely, filled to the rim with the mineral layers, and now hold only a moist divot and a tight knot of creatures that cannot find the water and so lie still, bound together by mockery of their own exudate.

The air does not move, but the atmosphere is not quiet. It is a place of patient, erosive sound, of slow accumulations. The warmth keeps everything soft, prevents brittleness; the wetness keeps everything from stillness. It is a place that has been going on for a very long time and does not intend to stop.

The only unusual thing, the only note that does not fit, is a place near the lip of the overhang. There, a shallow basin of rock has been worn smooth, and in its center is a single, perfect spiral groove, no wider than a finger, winding inward for nine turns to a small, polished knot in the center. The groove is clean, even now, though the rest of the basin grows a fine patina of slime. It does not look made. It looks grown, the way a shell grows, but there is nothing in the valley that makes a line like that—nothing with that kind of intention. The spiral fills slowly with seepage, and the pool-creatures avoid it, giving it a wide, respectful rim of bare stone. And the sound of the valley, all its clatter and drip and hum, seems to bend around it, as though the air itself is careful not to cross that line.
