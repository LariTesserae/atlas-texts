We are underground.

Not in the way of caves—not karst, not limestone dissolved by patient water into galleries and flows. This is a made underground, a total underground. Every surface has been shaped, laid, stacked, joined. There is no natural substrate here, no bedrock waiting behind the walls. The walls themselves are the world, and they are everywhere, and they are close.

But the space doesn’t feel small. It opens in ways that confuse the body’s sense of scale—rooms that widen without warning into halls, halls that drop into pits that are also rooms, shafts angled in directions your inner ear refuses to accept as vertical. This is a dense place, packed with architecture, layer lapped over layer, passage crossing passage at strange intervals, walls nearly touching across narrow clefts. In some corridors you must turn sideways to move; your shoulders brush both surfaces at once, and the surfaces are always cold and always damp. Not wet. Damp. The air carries moisture like a held breath, condensing in beads that never grow heavy enough to fall. The walls glisten faintly when light passes over them. The damp has a taste: mineral, faintly alkaline, with something else underneath, something organic but not alive, like old wool left in a cellar.

The temperature is cool and stable. It doesn’t vary between rooms or levels. Your body never warms the air around it; the air takes your heat and carries it off into the dark without comment. You breathe shallowly without meaning to. The cool enters your joints after a while, a slow stiffening that makes you aware of your own moving parts.

There is no vegetation. None. No root has ever breached these walls, no spore has found a purchase. This is a post-life place, or perhaps a pre-life place, or a place that life has simply never been invited into. The surfaces are bare mineral—a dull, dark stone, fine-grained, worked smooth in some places and left rough in others. In the rough places, tool marks are visible: parallel grooves, the chatter of a chisel on stone, the scalloped signature of something driven by hand. The craftsmanship is precise but not machined. This is primitive work, if "primitive" can apply to a construction of this scale and complexity. Hand tools, simple tools, but wielded with an obsessive, generations-long patience. There are seams where blocks meet, but no mortar. The weight of the world above holds everything in compression. Some walls are solid masonry; others are carved directly from a parent stone that must have been colossal, monolithic, a single dark mass sculpted into rooms and stairs and channels and vents.

Those vents: they are everywhere, and they are the source of the sound.

The sound is loud and constant. It is a breathing sound, but not like lungs. It is air moving through narrow constrictions, a low flute-note that shifts in pitch as pressures change somewhere deeper in the system. When the wind outside the mountain changes, when some distant opening admits a gust, the whole interior responds. Notes bend. Harmonies emerge and fade. Sometimes a tone deepens into a shudder you feel through your feet, a subsonic throb that makes the damp beads tremble on the walls. Sometimes a high whisper cuts across the drone, a sibilance that seems to come from directly behind you, even when your back is to a solid wall.

The vents are not random. They are channels, deliberate and shaped, cut with the same tool marks as the walls. They follow their own logic. Some run vertically, square-sectioned shafts that drop or rise into blackness. Some run horizontally along the ceilings, slotted like the mouths of flutes. Some are barely wide enough to admit a hand. Some are large enough that a small body could crawl through them, and sometimes, very rarely, something does.

The fauna is rare, but it is present. You don't see it often. You hear it more than you see it: a skittering in the vents, the click of claw on stone, a wet sound like a mouth opening and closing in the dark. When you do see one, it is usually a peripheral thing—a movement at the edge of your lamplight, a shape resolving into jointed legs and a pale, segmented body before it pulls itself into a vent too small for you to follow. They are pale things, sightless things, their bodies flattened for passage through tight stone. They are not insects. They have too many legs, and the legs have too many joints, and the joints bend in planes that seem wrong. They are not large. The biggest might be the size of a human hand. But there are many of them, or there seem to be many; the density of their sounds suggests a population spread through the vents like blood through veins. They are the only living things here. You wonder what they eat. You wonder if they eat each other. You wonder if they are eating the walls, slowly, over centuries, grain by grain. You never find droppings. You never find remains.

The light is not light. It is darkness, mostly, a darkness so complete that your eyes begin to invent their own stimulation—phosphenes blooming and fading, afterimages of nothing. You carry your own light, a simple oil lamp of fired clay, the wick drawing fuel from a reservoir that smells faintly of rendered fat. The flame is small and nervous. It pulls shadows from every irregularity in the stone, and the shadows move more than they should because the flame moves, because your hand moves, because the air from the vents stirs it constantly. The shadows pool in corners and stretch up walls and sometimes seem to detach and flow against the direction of the light. You blink and they return to their proper places. The lamp casts a warm, narrow circle, and beyond that circle the world is absolute black, and in that black the vents continue their low, shifting music.

Water is present but not abundant. It arrives on no schedule you can discern. Sometimes, for hours, nothing. The walls remain merely damp, condensate-beaded, cold. Then a change in the air pressure, a shift in the vent-sound, and a new note emerges: dripping. It starts in one place, then another, then a dozen places at once. Water seeps from seams in the masonry, trickles down carved channels you hadn't noticed before—gutters, really, simple grooves cut into the floor or the base of walls. The water collects in basins, also carved, shallow depressions that hold maybe a few liters each. The water is cold and clear and tastes faintly of the same mineral-organic something you taste in the air. Then the dripping slows, stops, and the damp silence returns, filled only by the drone of the vents. The water in the basins gradually evaporates or drains through unseen perforations. You never catch it draining. The basins are simply empty again after a time.

The elevation is low. You are deep. You entered through an opening somewhere on a hillside, a dark mouth in a landscape you can barely remember now—scrubland, grey-green shrubs, a cool wind. You descended stairs, then ramps, then a long spiraling walkway that seemed to have no landings. You lost track of how far down you went. You feel the weight above you now, not as a physical sensation but as a quality of the silence between sounds, a density in the air itself. The pressure is slightly higher than it should be. Your ears popped once, twice, and then stopped popping, which means you are deep enough that the pressure differential is gone, equalized, you are inside the mass now.

But despite the depth, the place is active. Not in the sense of machines—there are no machines. The activity is environmental. The air moves. The water comes and goes. The vents shift their song. The things in the walls move about their business. There are cycles here, rhythms you can't yet map. Sometimes a corridor that was dry when you passed through it an hour ago is now slick with moisture. Sometimes a door—a stone slab on a pivot, perfectly balanced despite its mass—stands open when you are certain it was closed. You push one, and it moves with surprising ease, the pivot lubricated with something dark and greasy that smells like the vents smell, like the creatures smell, like the faint organic note in the water. The doors are everywhere. Most corridors branch and branch again, and each branch has doors, and the doors have no handles, only recessed pulls carved into their edges. You learn to check both sides before letting one swing shut behind you. They are heavy enough to crush a hand, or a skull.

The weirdness is in the details. The proportions are slightly off. The stairs are too shallow, or too deep, your stride never quite matching the risers. Corridors angle left just enough that you feel yourself listing, compensating, and then you realize you've been walking with your head tilted for minutes. Rooms are never empty: each one contains something, a feature, a function. A single stone chair, too large for a human, facing a blank wall. A pillar carved with grooves that spiral up and vanish into shadow. A floor inset with channels that form a pattern—not a symbol you recognize, but a pattern, deliberate, like a maze compressed into a single plane. A room that is nothing but basins, dozens of them, at different heights, connected by channels, a dry cascade of empty stone bowls. A room where the walls are covered in small niches, each niche just large enough to hold something the size of a fist, and each niche is empty, and the lamplight catches the tool marks inside them, the same chatter of the chisel, repeated thousands of times.

You begin to sense that this place is still forming itself. Not geologically—geology has nothing to do with it—but in some other sense. That word: "emerging." The parameters said emerging. And you feel it now, a sense of incompletion, of a process interrupted or still underway. Some corridors end not in walls but in raw faces of stone where the carving simply stops, tool marks trailing off as if the worker walked away and never returned. Some doors open onto rooms that are only half-excavated, one wall finished and smooth, the other still rough and undefined, the floor a jumble of broken stone and dust. The dust is fine and dark and undisturbed. The air in those rooms is still, somehow, the vents silent here, the only sound your own breathing and the hiss of the lamp. You don't linger in the unfinished rooms. They feel like the place is watching itself, waiting for something to resume.

The scale of the whole is impossible to gauge. You have walked for what feels like hours, maybe days. You have passed through halls large enough to swallow a cathedral, their ceilings lost beyond the lamp's reach, the vent-sound echoing in vast, shifting harmonies. You have crawled through passages so tight you had to push your lamp ahead of you and squirm after it, the stone cold and wet against your belly, the sound of the creatures moving in the walls suddenly very close, very clear. You have found stairs going up, stairs going down, ramps that turn back on themselves, shafts with carved handholds leading into darkness above and below. You have the sense that the complex extends laterally for a distance you can't measure—the parameters said regional, town-or-valley-size—and vertically as well, an open density of stacked spaces, each level connected by its own logic. You could walk for years and not map it all. You wonder if anyone ever did. You wonder if the people who built this are the same people who left, or if they became the creatures in the vents, or if they are still here somewhere, in a deeper level, still carving.

You stop for a moment to rest. You set the lamp on a stone shelf—another niche, empty—and you listen. The vents are singing a chord now, a minor third, slow-beating. Water drips somewhere far above, the sound traveling down a shaft and arriving at your ear delayed, ghostly. A creature clicks in the wall to your left. You feel the cool air on your face, the damp in your lungs, the stone under your palms. The place is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is simply here, and it is still becoming, and you are inside its becoming.

You lift the lamp. The flame flutters. The shadows leap. Ahead, a corridor branches: left, right, center. All three are dark. All three are waiting.

You choose one, and you walk on, and the sound of the vents follows you, and the walls continue their slow, unfinished breathing.
