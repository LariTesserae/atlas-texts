The first thing that strikes a visitor is the sound. Not a single noise but a vast, choral drone, a continuous chord of creaking, sliding, and deep, subsonic thrumming that comes from every direction at once and seems to travel not through the air but directly into the bones of the skull. The air itself is cool, carrying a faint sharpness like ozone and cut grass, and it moves in slow, deliberate currents that push against the skin with an almost liquid weight.

You are high here, though the horizon is a concept you must re-learn. There is no ground as such. What lies beneath your feet—if you still think in terms of feet and ground—is a compressed lattice of pale fibers, a mat of interwoven strands as wide as roads, each one the color of old ivory with a faint, sickly iridescence like oil on water. This is the substrate, the lowest layer you can reach, but it is not the bottom. Press your hand against it and you feel a deep, resonant give, a flexing that travels outward in ripples. The material is cool and dry, yet somehow pliant, like leather soaked in glycerin. It goes down and down, this woven mass, through strata of darker and darker material until, somewhere far below where no light has ever touched, it merges into a singular, continuous living tissue. The entire mountain—for it is a mountain, a vast, regional highland rising from what might be lower plateaus lost in a haze of pale green—is one plant.

No. Not one plant. That implies an individual. The distinction has no meaning here. This is the Khora, a continuous vegetative matrix that has been stable for a timescale that mocks the word "ancient." Individual organisms, if they ever existed, dissolved into the whole so long ago that the very concept of a separate plant is a fossil in the language of the people who live here.

And there are people. The city is not built; it is negotiated. Across the sweeping, open flanks of the highland, structures emerge from the plant-mass with an organic inevitability. Your eye first reads them as knots, burls, or fungal shelves growing from a tree trunk, but this is infrastructure. Towers rise not by stacking but by swelling, smooth-walled and windowless, their surfaces a polished, darker version of the ivory substrate, veined with hairline cracks that glow faintly from within in a cool bioluminescent aquamarine. Between the towers, suspended in the dim, open air, are walkways and plazas made of a different material entirely: a nacreous, semi-transparent film stretched taut like a drumskin, engineered at a scale that blurs biology and architecture. They are sparse, these structures. Much of the vast, sloping landscape is empty air and the pale, curving body of the Khora, punctuated by a settlement here, a cluster of towers there, separated by kilometers of undulating fiber-plain. The density is that of a flock of birds scattered across a wide estuary at dusk.

The light is dim, a perpetual twilight that seems to emanate from the sky itself rather than a point source. Above, the atmosphere is a pale, milky haze, through which larger structures are sometimes dimly visible: colossal, shadowy arches and spheres that might be parts of the plant-body reaching into the upper atmosphere, or something else entirely, something engineered in a previous epoch. The erosion is total. There are no jagged peaks, no talus slopes, no broken rock. The shape of the land has been worn to a state of perfect, flowing equilibrium—every curve is a catenary, every slope an asymptote. It is a world that has been stroked by wind and a strange, dry-chemistry rain for eons until it settled into its final, drape-like form. It is old. Older than continents should be.

And the city is loud with life. The fauna here does not walk on the surface so much as flow and flit through it. Small, dark, streamlined creatures with too many legs and polished, chitinous bodies pour in rivulets along the grooves of the fiber mat, their movement a skittering waterfall of clicks. They are not individuals; they are a process, a nerve signal made flesh, dismantling dead fiber here, depositing some secretion there. Larger forms, vaguely mammalian but with jointed limbs that fold in unexpected directions, hang from the membranous walkways, their bodies pulsing with the same bioluminescent aqua as the towers, and they keen to each other in long, mournful, ridiculously loud fluted calls that echo across the open spaces. The air is full of whirring, translucent pollinators—or perhaps data-gatherers, or both—their tiny, intricate bodies humming with a technology that is indistinguishable from their biology, their wings beating in frequencies that make the very light seem to shimmer.

The strangeness is not in any one thing but in the coherent logic of the impossible place. A figure, human in silhouette but with skin that has the same nacreous sheen as the walkways, steps out from a tower's puckered orifice. It wears no clothes, but its body is traced with the glowing aqua veins, and on its shoulder perches a small, glistening sphere that is clearly a constructed device, yet it purrs. The figure raises a hand, and from the fiber beneath its feet, a shape extrudes—a table, a chair, a terminal—all made of the same living ivory, sculpted in real-time by a request spoken in a language of pheromones and sub-vibrations that you feel as a change in the deep chord of the wind. The figure sits, and begins to commune, and the mountain-city-plant, the oldest living thing in this pocket of the world, shifts imperceptibly in its ancient, active dream.
