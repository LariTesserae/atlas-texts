The city is called Glim, or something that sounds like Glim when the syllables condense on your tongue. It is strung across a high saddle between two peaks that don’t have names because no one is here to name them—no one but the city, and the city doesn’t use language that way. The altitude is a constant pressure against the inner ear, and the cold is the kind that doesn’t come in gusts but simply abides, a patient fact, a density of stillness. Nothing grows here, not in the sense of rooted things reaching for light. There is no light. The dark is absolute and wet, a perpetual condensation that beads on every surface and drips without source, without destination.

And yet the place is loud.

The sound is not chaotic. It is layered and rhythmic and bodily, a low thrumming that travels through the stone itself before it ever reaches what you’d call air. It comes from the fauna. The fauna are the city, or the city is the fauna—the distinction eroded sometime in the city’s slow, embryonic emergence from the mountain. They are everywhere. The density parameter is nearly maximal: the creatures, if they can be called creatures, occupy every surface, every crevice, every plane of the vertical and horizontal built world. Their bodies press together, overlapping, tessellating, a living grout between structures that themselves appear half-formed, as if the geology rose up and stopped mid-gesture, frozen into walls and conduits and arched passages before fully deciding what shape to take.

The creatures are not animals in any Linnaean sense. They are wet and dark and many-limbed, but the limbs don’t belong to individuals the way you’d expect. They fuse and separate with a slowness that makes glaciers look rash. Over spans you’d need different calendars to measure, a single mass of interlocking bodies will reconfigure—a pseudopod of fused torsos slowly extending across a plaza, a cluster of breathing orifices migrating up a spire, a thick rope of tendrils descending into a flooded shaft. The sound they make is respiration and friction and a deep, almost seismic hum that might be communication or digestion or just the noise of being so numerous and so close. It’s loud enough that you feel it in your teeth before your ears.

The city is built, its tech beyond any recognizable lineage. The structures are not constructed so much as crystallized, woven from something that resembles carbon lattice and wet membrane and, in places, a kind of weeping mineral that exudes and sets in fractal patterns. There are towers that don’t taper but bulge, covered in something between scales and scabs, each inch of surface occupied by a smaller instance of the larger fauna, down to a scale where the eye—if there were eyes here—couldn’t resolve them. The built environment merges with the living walls. Doorways are sphincters. Balconies are ribs. Streets are channels cut by the slow, patient passage of bodies over centuries, polished to a high, wet gleam by the constant application of mucus and pressure.

Water moves through everything. It drips from overhead ledges into pools that reflect nothing, because there is no light to reflect. It seeps from porous walls in thin, continuous films. It condenses on the fauna, giving their surfaces a slick, obsidian shine. The whole city is a hydrological cycle in miniature, closed and slow: evaporation from the heat of clustered bodies, condensation against the cold stone, precipitation as a fine, perpetual mist that never quite becomes rain. The pools in the lower courses are deep and still, and in them, the larval forms of the fauna develop over centuries, blind and pale and slowly differentiating limbs.

The weirdness here is not flamboyant. It’s a matter of scale and patience. The city emerged. That’s the only word for it. The erosion score is barely above zero because this place is still becoming itself. The mountain is not yet finished being the city. The city is not yet finished being the fauna. The fauna are not yet finished differentiating into whatever they will eventually become. The whole system is in a state of long, slow embryogenesis, a construction project where the workers are the building materials and the blueprint is a distributed dream held in the humming, synchronous vibration that never stops.

There are no plants. There is no sun. There is only this dense, urban ecology of self-assembling architecture and the overwhelming presence of life that doesn’t recognize itself as separate from the place it inhabits. A traveler—if a traveler were possible here—would stand on a high causeway of fused bodies and look out across a valley of darkness, seeing nothing, hearing everything, feeling the cold seep in and the slow, almost imperceptible movement underfoot, and understand that the entire city is breathing.
