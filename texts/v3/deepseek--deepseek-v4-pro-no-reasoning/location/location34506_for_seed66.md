The city does not sit on the plateau; it is the plateau.

It rises from the cloud-sea in stepped, weathered tiers, each one broad enough to hold its own weather. The stone is warm underfoot—has been warm for longer than recorded time—and everywhere it weeps. Water seeps from the walls, the floors, the undersides of bridges, the ceilings of passageways. It doesn't rain here, not exactly. The moisture condenses directly out of the air, beading on every surface, gathering into rivulets that trace the same channels they've been tracing since before the builders first came. The sound of it is constant: a low, percussive dripping, a hundred thousand small impacts multiplied into a susurrus that fills the city like breath.

There are no plants. Not a blade of grass, not a lichen. The mineral world rules absolutely.

The stone is pale gray in some districts, iron-red in others, and in the oldest quarters it has taken on a translucent, opalescent quality—silicate flesh polished by an eternity of water and the passage of bodies. Streets run between buildings that were not built so much as revealed, excavated from the native rock by minds that understood its grain. The walls are thick, close-set, the corridors narrow. Density here means intimacy: every structure presses against its neighbors, separated only by the seeping channels where the water runs, so that moving through the city means constant near-touching, shoulders brushing damp stone on both sides at once.

This is not a dead city. It overflows with life.

The fauna is the first thing a visitor notices, after the wet, after the warmth. It is overwhelming in number, in sound, in constant motion—and yet the motion is slow, patient, deliberate. The creatures are not animals in any sense recognized by terrestrial biology. They are built things, or perhaps grown things, or perhaps something that blurs the distinction beyond meaning. Their bodies are segmented, jointed, composed of what appears to be the same mineral substrate as the city itself, differentiated only by a faint internal luminescence that pulses in slow, arrhythmic patterns.

The largest of them—the haulers—are the size of locomotives, moving through the broader avenues on hundreds of short, chitinous limbs. They carry loads of raw mineral from the deep quarries in their hollow backs, loads they will deliver to the factories. The factories are everywhere, integrated into the urban fabric: cavernous halls where heat and pressure and chemical baths transform stone into more stone, into the bodies of new creatures, into the delicate mechanisms that regulate the water cycle. The air in the factory districts thrums with a low mechanical vibration, a bass note beneath the dripping. Steam vents in controlled bursts from fissures in the walls. The city is industrious, productive, endlessly making more of itself.

There is a logic to it, an ancient, grooved-in order. The water channels are not random; they are a circulatory system, gathering moisture from the condensing atmosphere and directing it—downward, always downward, through a thousand kilometers of graded channels—to the great engines in the roots of the plateau. There, heat from the world's interior meets the descending water, and the engines turn. They have been turning for epochs. They drive the factories, pump nutrients through the mineral vascular network, sustain the slow metabolic pulse of the city-organism. The system is closed. It is balanced. It has been in its settled form for so long that the concept of change has become almost theological—a thing the city's denizens might dimly contemplate, but never expect to witness.

High up, near the summit terraces, the air is thinner, the condensation less aggressive. Here the buildings grow taller, more elaborate, their opalescent walls carved into spiraling forms that serve no apparent function beyond a kind of mineral aesthetics. This is where the small ones congregate—the quick ones, the thinkers. They are no larger than a human hand, these darting forms, and they communicate in frequencies that make the water droplets shiver on their stone perches. They are the tenders, the adjusters, the ones who remember. Their memories are not stored in any neural tissue—they have none—but in the crystalline lattices they maintain, polishing certain facets to preserve encoded information, letting others weather away when the information is no longer needed. Their work is slow. A single memory might take centuries to polish into permanence, centuries more to be deemed obsolete and allowed to fade.

Standing on the highest terrace, looking out across the city, the scale of it becomes almost unbearable. The plateau stretches to the horizon in every direction, and the horizon itself is wrong—it curves upward, the world bending around its own immensity, so that the distant districts seem to float above the nearer ones, tier upon tier of gray and red and shimmering opal ascending into a sky that is never quite dry. The clouds below are white and soft, a false sea that churns in slow motion, and somewhere in that sea the roots of the plateau descend into depths no surface creature has ever seen.

The temperature never changes. It is always warm—the warmth of a body, of something alive but metabolizing at a pace so slow it barely registers as activity. The sound is always the same: dripping, dripping, the low hum of the engines far below, the subsonic chatter of the small ones, the periodic grinding creak of a hauler negotiating a tight passage. There is no day, no night, only the steady diffuse glow that emanates from every surface, a light with no apparent source, as if the stone itself has learned to shed photons as waste heat from its internal processes.

It has been this way for so long that the water channels have worn grooves a meter deep into the stone. The feet of the haulers have polished the streets to a mirror finish. The factory walls are caked with the mineral residue of ten million production cycles. Everything is weathered, softened, rounded—not decaying, but settling into its final form, the form it will hold for the remaining lifetime of the planet.

The city does not know it is a city. It has no name for itself. The small ones have a concept that translates roughly as "the arrangement," or perhaps "the way things are." They do not distinguish between natural and built, between creature and construct, between water and the channels that carry it. It is all one thing, one vast slow breathing system of mineral and moisture and warmth, and it has been this way for so long that the arrangement is indistinguishable from the laws of physics.

A visitor from elsewhere—if such a thing were possible—might call it strange. The small ones would not understand the word. What is there to be strange? The stone is wet. The wet stone is warm. The warm wet stone makes more stone, makes bodies, makes light, makes sound. This is simply how the world is. This is simply what the world does. It has been doing it for a very, very long time, and it is in no hurry to stop.
