The first thing that arrives is the sound. It is not a single noise but a pressure, a physical pushing against the body from all directions. The air itself seems to be tearing, a constant low-frequency roar overlaid with a million finer cracklings, like cellophane being crumpled by enormous hands, like the groan of heated metal, like wet ripping. To stand here is to have your own thoughts shouted down, your heartbeat stolen and replaced by the place’s own rhythm. You feel the sound in your teeth, in the hollow of your chest.

The light is dusky, warm-toned, filtered through layers of membrane. It takes a long moment to understand you are below the surface. Not in a cave, exactly, but in a vast, open volume that has been roofed over by the living world above. The sky—what should be sky—is a tangled canopy of root and tendril so thick it becomes a solid ceiling, hundreds of feet overhead. From this ceiling, great cables of vegetation descend, some as thick as redwood trunks, others fine as hair, all of them pulsing faintly, slowly, with a deep internal peristalsis.

There is no ground. Not in any stable sense.

The floor of this place—the surface you must walk upon—is not earth or stone but a massive, continuous, interwoven mat of plant bodies. They are warm underfoot, yielding slightly, like walking on a living hide stretched tight over something deeper. The vegetation here is not a collection of individual plants. It is a single, unified tissue, a continent of flesh that has filled the available space entirely, pressing against the walls of this buried chasm, climbing them, roofing itself over. The parameter is not "covered in vegetation." The place *is* the vegetation. The architecture of the world is botanic anatomy.

And it is in the process of making itself.

This is what the sound is. Growth.

All around, new structures are pushing up from the living floor, emerging and collapsing in a turbulent cycle. Diaphanous sheets of pale green tissue rise like sails, stretching upward until they tear under their own tension with that terrible wet ripping sound. Thick bladders inflate slowly, their translucent walls revealing swirling gases and dark fluids within, only to burst minutes or hours later, showering the area with a warm, slightly acidic mist. Stems uncoil with violent speed, whip against the canopy above, and then stiffen into place, becoming part of the architecture. The air is thick with the smell of sap and something sharper, electric—the scent of ozone from the friction of constant movement, the exhalation of rapid metabolism.

There is no water here as you understand it. No pools, no streams. The moisture is locked entirely within the tissues of the biome. The environment is chemically dry at the surface, a sealed system where liquid exists only inside cells and vessels. When a bladder bursts, the fluid it releases is immediately absorbed by the thirsty membrane underfoot, sucked away with an audible hiss. You realize, with a jolt, that you are walking through a desert made of flesh.

And you are not alone.

The fauna are overwhelming, not in size but in sheer, dense multiplicity. They are part of the plant, emerging from it. Creatures bud from the living floor like fruit, not born but peeled off, extruded. They have no time. A thing that might be described as a six-legged lizard, its skin still glossy and unformed, pulls itself from a pore in the ground, scurries with desperate speed across the undulating surface, and then, within a few meters, its run falters. Its legs stiffen. It stops, and from its own back, a new sprout of green tissue pushes through its cracking skin. It is being reclaimed before it has lived an hour. It is a mobile bud, a delivery system, its animal form a temporary phase of the plant's expression.

There are thousands of these ephemeral creatures. They fill the air with their own sounds—chittering, buzzing, an endless percussive rustle as they scurry, fly, crawl, and die. Some are insect-small, clouds of winged motes that swarm in dense, shifting columns, pollinating nothing, simply mixing the internal atmosphere. Others are larger, soft-bodied things that haul themselves across the mat, eating the dying faunal forms, only to be absorbed themselves, their biomass melting back into the substrate from which they came. The density is dizzying; every square meter holds dozens of visible life-details, a near-touching saturation of forms in constant, chaotic flux. It is a bazaar of body plans, an anthill energy, every component moving, consuming, expiring, and being replaced.

Scattered across this buried world are the marks of something else. Not of the plant.

Here and there, rising from the living floor, are low walls of stacked stone, unmistakably built. They are primitive—simple hand-tools, no mortar, stones fitted with the patience of early craft. They form partial enclosures, the foundations of small structures that once stood in this chasm before it was filled. In one, you see the curve of a hearth, the stones blackened by ancient fire. In another, a single shaped pillar stands to waist height, its top carved into a shallow bowl. It holds nothing now but a tangle of fine white rootlets that have grown up through it and spread across the rim like frozen lace.

These remnants are being consumed, slowly, inevitably. The mat has grown over some walls entirely, digesting them. The pillar is leaning, undermined by the constant, subtle movement of the floor. There are no bodies, no bones. If the builders died here, their remains were claimed long ago by the system, their minerals folded back into the cycle. You sense that these structures were not abandoned so much as overwhelmed, their makers simply unable to keep pace with a world that decided, at some point in its deep history, to wake up and build itself over everything they knew.

The air is warm, a steady, oppressive warmth that has no source point—it radiates from everywhere. The plant itself is exothermic, its ceaseless growth generating the heat of a compost pile scaled to a planet. In some areas, where the tissue is thickest and the growth most furious, the ground is actively hot to the touch, and the air shimmers with heat-haze despite the high humidity inside the closed system. You find yourself sweating, the moisture evaporating almost instantly, pulled from your skin by the thirsty atmosphere that tolerates no free water.

Strange is not an adequate word for the color palette. The light, filtered through layers of living canopy, is amber and green and occasionally, in patches where some bioluminescence contributes, a soft, pulsing violet. Shadows are not black but deep maroon. The constant motion gives everything a hallucinatory edge—you see a shape moving in your peripheral vision, turn to look, and find it has already resolved back into the mat, or burst, or been replaced by a different shape entirely. The laws here are not the laws of rock and air and water. They are the laws of a tissue that dreams in flesh, constantly proposing and discarding forms. It is an embryonic world, forever emerging, never arriving at a stable state.

And over it all, through it all, is the sound. Deafening. Unrelenting. You realize after a time that your own breathing has synchronized to the pulse in the great descending cables. Your voice, if you were to speak, would be lost instantly. The place does not permit interruption. It is speaking its own becoming, and it has been doing so for a very long time, and it will never, ever stop.

You stand on the living floor of a buried, dry ocean of flesh, in the roaring, creative dark, surrounded by a trillion temporary selves, and you understand that you are standing inside a god that is building itself, and it does not know you are there, and if it did, it would not care.
