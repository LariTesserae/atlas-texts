It’s low land here—not a basin, exactly, but drawn downward, as if something heavy passed overhead and pressed the ground into a long, shallow declivity. You might call it a plain, except plains don’t hum with this kind of intention. The whole place is only half-emerged from its own genesis, still wet with it. Water doesn’t pool in the ordinary way, though there’s water everywhere: beading on every surface, seeping from pores you can’t quite locate, condensing out of air that tastes of hot metal and damp clay and something faintly salt-sharp, like the inside of a shell. The temperature sits around that point where your skin can’t decide if the wetness is sweat or airborne dew or simply the atmosphere pressing its own fluids into you. There’s no rain exactly, but the air is a slow, warm rain in waiting, saturated to trembling.

And yet the ground isn’t mud. It’s built—all of it, every particle. You can see that now, the longer you let your eyes adjust to the dimness. The light here is a low, diffuse amber, as if the sky itself is a great sheet of translucent horn lit from behind by a guttering flame. No sun, no point source, just a directionless glow that barely reaches the ground and leaves long, soft-edged shadows that don’t quite correspond to any object. In that half-glow, the ground resolves into a vast fitted surface: hand-worked, or something like hands. Flat stones, mostly, but also plates of what might be fired clay, and strips of beaten metal—copper maybe, or a greenish bronze—all set edge to edge without mortar, wedged and interlocked so tightly that the seams are just hairlines. The whole floor of the world, as far as you can sense, is a mosaic. Not decorative: functional. Like the bottom of a pan, or the inner surface of something that’s meant to hold a great weight, or a great heat.

The scale of it is hard to grasp, because there are no landmarks to measure by. The mosaic stretches flat and then begins, very gradually, to rise in places into low, broad hummocks and swales—but even the swells are built, you realize: they’re not hills of earth but heaped structures, cairns of the same fitted stone, or rough pagodas of stacked slate, or domes assembled from overlapping ceramic scales. All of it hand-made, primitive in technique but vast in ambition. This is a world that someone, something, is building from the ground up, and it isn’t finished. That’s the eeriest part: it’s under construction. The land is emerging, but emergently made, fabricated as it goes. And the builders are gone, or not yet arrived, or you can’t see them.

You walk, and your footsteps ring slightly, a dull ceramic chime, then mute as you cross a patch of coppery plates. The sound is mostly quiet—your breathing, the wet scuff of your feet, an intermittent drip-drip-drip that you finally trace to a joint in the ground where warm water is oozing up from below and flowing sluggishly across the stone, filling the low grooves between plates with shallow, steaming runnels. Steam, too, rises here and there, but not from vents; it wreathes up from gaps between the stones themselves, a ground-fog of hot exhalation, as if the earth’s lungs are just beneath the crust and breathing out. In the dimness, the steam looks solid, like slow-moving sculptures that lean and dissolve.

There’s no vegetation. Not a scrap. No moss, no lichen, no algal smear. The surfaces are aggressively bare—if anything, they seem hostile to life, the way a hot griddle is hostile. What grows here instead are patterns. You notice them after a while: not on the stones, but in the spaces between. Where the steam curls at a certain angle, for a moment you see a latticework in the air, faint as afterimage, a structure of interlocking lines that might be scaffolding, might be a diagram, might be the ghost of something still being thought into shape. Then it flicks and is gone. These aren’t mirages—they’re too systematic, repeating in different places with small variations. They’re blueprints. The world is thinking its own next stage, and your presence is incidental to that long cogitation.

The heat and the slow drip and the wide, dim silence would be oppressive if they weren’t also so unstable. Because the place is turbulent, genuinely so: not in the sense of wind or weather—there’s no sky-weather—but in the ground itself. The mosaic shifts. You’ll be standing on what seems solid, and a plate underfoot will tip slightly, rumbling deep, and hot water will gush up and sluice away across a dozen yards of stone before subsiding. Occasionally, a cairn unbuilds itself, stones slumping outward one by one with a clatter that echoes flat and long across the empty expanse, and by the time you look back, a different structure is already pushing up from the same spot, stones fitting together in a new configuration, glistening and wet-born. It’s not alive in any organic sense, but it is restless, a landscape in a state of permanent creation/destruction, like the surface of a star made miniature and slowed to the speed of tectonic patience.

There are no animals. Nothing moves but the water, the steam, the slow grind of stone on stone. But you begin to sense that the patterns themselves are the fauna. That flashing blueprint-lattice in the steam is, in some sense, the local equivalent of a bird or an insect—a transient, self-organizing form that feeds on the heat differential and dies when the steam dissipates, leaving only a faint, cooling residue of order. Most are silent. But you hear, once, very far away, a sound like a bell struck weakly, and you realize a dome has collapsed somewhere, and the ringing is the ceramics settling into their new arrangement.

The weirdness creeps up on you slowly, as it should. Because it’s not just a constructed world of hot stone and seeping water; it’s that the physics of it feel half-set, like an underbaked clay. You notice that your own shadow sometimes lags a beat behind your movement, or stretches in a direction the amber light shouldn’t permit. When you touch a stone, it’s warm and wet, but it also gives slightly, not like flesh, more like a thing that hasn’t yet decided to be fully solid. And once, you see your own footprint on a copper plate fill in from below with a fresh blister of metal, erasing your presence, and you understand that the world is not interested in you. It’s interested in completing itself.

So you stand there, in the enormous dim hot quiet of a vast, low, handmade world only half-born, with water weeping up from its seams and steam sketching its future shapes in the air, and the only constant is the low tectonic shudder of things being not-quite-finished, forever.

That’s the place.
