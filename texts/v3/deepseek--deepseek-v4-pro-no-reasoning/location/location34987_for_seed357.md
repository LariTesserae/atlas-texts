The air is a held breath that never releases, thick as water and twice as warm. It condenses on every surface—not as droplets, but as a continual wetness that beads and slides in slow, iridescent rivulets down walls that are not stone, not wood, but *grown*. The vertical planes of this place are living tissue: immense pads of modified leaf-structure, each the size of a door, stacked and fused in Fibonacci spirals that rise toward a sky visible only in fragments far above. The pads are the architecture, their edges curling to form shelves, buttresses, archways that drip with the constant exudate of the world's respiration. Water is not a separate feature but a medium of exchange, seeping from pores in the living walls, trickling down channels etched by enzymatic secretions, pooling in the cupped palms of lower growths before overflowing in warm, silent cascades.

You stand at an elevation that would be called a high plateau if this world had bedrock. There is no bedrock. The "ground" beneath your feet is a tensile mat of rhizomatic cables, pale and smooth as tendon, woven into a springy mesh that spans a vast, open drop. The feel of it gives slightly with each shift of weight, like walking on a trampoline of woven muscle. Through gaps in the mesh—scattered, deliberate, the sparse architecture of a net—you can see down into the levels below: other layers of the spanning canopy, other condensation-drenched chambers glowing faintly with their own bioluminescence, descending into a warm, misty abyss. The drop is not empty. It is filled with the loose, dangling tendrils of epiphytic growths that hang like silent windchimes, each one a pale green or blush-pink filament, swaying in currents of saturated air that move with the deliberate, peristaltic rhythm of the whole structure breathing.

The heat is a constant pressure, a weight on the skin that loosens the boundaries of things. It is the heat of a compost pile's core, the fever of vigorous metabolism, radiating from every surface. The walls are warm to the touch, not with the borrowed warmth of a sun, but with an internal, generated heat—the waste product of a trillion microscopic processes in the living tissue. This heat cooks volatile oils from the leaf-pads, filling the air with a complex scent of camphor, wet cinnamon, and the clean, sharp tang of ozone. The atmosphere itself seems on the verge of lightning.

But for all the heat, the wetness, the overwhelming life-mass, the space itself is open. The living walls rise and curve, but they do not enclose. They define a volume that feels like the nave of a cathedral designed by a mind that knew only branching and unfurling. Vast windows exist, not as holes, but as transparent membranes stretched taut between structural ribs, looking out onto a vista of pure chaos. The landscape beyond is a turbulence of green cloud, a rolling, shifting mass of vapor thick as whipped egg white, from which new peaks of the same kind of structure occasionally erupt. You watch a spire of interlocking leaf-pads, glistening and volcanic, push upward from the cloud-sea, its new edges sharp, angular, unweathered by any erosion but its own dissolution. It grows for a time—minutes, an hour—then, with a sound you feel in your sternum before you hear it, it begins to unravel. The pads separate, not shattering but unzipping at the seams, spiraling outward in a slow-motion detonation of organic form, dissolving back into the churning source-cloud. This is the youngness, the constant making and unmaking. Nothing is old here because nothing persists long enough to be eroded. Sharp features are simply the default state of the newly formed.

There is no animal life. The sound is immense, but every decibel of it is plant-sound. It is the loudness of pure creation. The wet sigh and creak of expanding tissue. The hydraulic pop of vesicles bursting. The deep, subsonic groan of the great spanning cables under tension. The high-frequency crackle of the bioluminescent membranes, a sound like bacon frying turned into a musical tone. And over it all, a turbulent, bazaar-like symphony of fluid movement—the constant drip, flow, gurgle, and sudden, surging rush of water being pumped, transpired, and re-condensed. The space is alive with a sort of plant-anthill energy, a hyperactive commerce of fluids and gases, an economy of pure chemistry. The scattered, sparse nature of the structural elements only emphasizes the energetic turbulence; each pillar and platform is a nexus of frantic, invisible activity, an island of furious life in an ocean of mist.

This place is a pure, constructed ecosystem with a technology level of zero. There are no devices, yet there is no natural substrate. The very atmosphere is a made thing, a product of the respiration of the vast being that is the landscape. The rules of this world are a closed loop of wet, hot, high-energy biology. The structure tears itself down and builds itself up with chaotic abandon. It is a young, loud, turbulent, and utterly strange forest that has become its own architecture and its own weather, extending past the immediate vista, a whole regional entity of sparse, purposeful growth, endlessly, sweatily, remaking itself from the water and the heat and the light.
