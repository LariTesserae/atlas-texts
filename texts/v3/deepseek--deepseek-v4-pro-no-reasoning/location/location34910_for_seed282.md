The air hits you first—cold enough to feel wet, though no water drips from the riveted iron ceiling low overhead. The temperature lives in that narrow band just above freezing where condensation beads on every surface but never quite becomes frost. You breathe shallowly; the cold wants to steal the warmth from your lungs.

You are in a seam. That is the only word for it. A space between two vast machined plates of dark alloy that slope toward each other, meeting in a V twenty feet above your head, narrowing as they descend to a floor of extruded metal grating. The seam is perhaps eight feet wide where you stand, but it tapers as it rises, and in the dim red glow of indicator lights strung along the walls like a dashed line, you can see that the convergence is not quite perfect. There are gaps. Fissures where the plates don't meet flush, and from these, a faint steam escapes in slow, rhythmic pulses—*huff, huff, huff*—like the breathing of something enormous on the other side.

The walls are not empty. This is what makes the place feel dense despite its narrowness. Pipes, conduits, cable bundles, and mounting brackets crowd every square inch of the alloy plates. They press against each other, layered three and four deep in places, held off the walls by rust-caked standoffs and corroded clamps. You count at least seven distinct pipe diameters just in arm's reach, some insulated with fraying asbestos wrap, others bare copper gone green-black with time. The space between them is filled with smaller things: junction boxes, unlabeled valve wheels, pressure gauges with cracked faces, and everywhere, a fine copper mesh that seems to serve no purpose except to be there, woven between the larger elements like metallic cobwebs.

There is no floor beneath the grating. You realize this when you drop a pebble of fallen scale through the diamond-pattern openings and hear it strike something far below—a long, hollow *ping* that echoes up through the metal, followed by a deeper, answering thrum that you feel in your chest more than hear. Below the grating is a drop, and below the drop, something that resonates.

The grating itself flexes slightly under your weight. It was not built for walking. No catwalk, no handrail, no access ladder—just this interstitial space between pieces of a larger machine, never intended for human presence, colonized anyway by the maintenance needs of a century. Someone has been here before you, though. A tool roll lies abandoned against a pipe, the canvas stiff with cold and speckled with something dark. A length of chain hangs from an overhead bracket, its last link fused to the bracket itself by a thick blob of ancient solder. A handprint in grease on a valve wheel, small enough to be a child's, or a woman's in heavy gloves.

The moisture comes and goes. You notice it when the rhythm of the steam pulses changes—every few minutes, a deeper vibration runs through the floor grating, and a fine mist sprays from a series of nozzles embedded in the ceiling plates. The mist hangs in the cold air, catching the red indicator lights and turning the seam into a tunnel of luminous crimson fog. It settles on your face, tastes faintly of iron and something alkaline, like concrete dust. Then the ventilation kicks in—you hear the fans start up somewhere distant, a low drone that builds—and the mist is pulled away through gaps in the grating, leaving the surfaces just damp again, glistening.

There is no vegetation here. Not in any biological sense. But the copper mesh, you realize, is growing. That's the word that comes to mind. The mesh is not manufactured and installed; it is precipitating. You can see it at the edges, where new filaments of copper are extending from existing strands, delicate as frost, branching and rebranching in patterns that mimic lichen or the mycelial networks of fungi. It follows the moisture. Where the steam nozzles spray most heavily, the copper growth is thickest, forming sponge-like accretions that bridge between pipes and engulf small fittings. It grows in real time—or close to it. As you watch, a filament no thicker than a hair extends maybe a millimeter toward a water droplet beading on a valve stem. You are not sure you saw it move, but you are not sure you didn't.

This is what passes for ecology here. The copper mesh is the primary producer, precipitating metal from the mineral-rich condensate. And something eats it. You see the evidence in patches where the mesh has been scraped away, leaving bright scratch marks in the patina. Something mechanical, you think, noting the parallel grooves. A drone, perhaps, or an automated scraper, harvesting the copper for reasons you can only guess. The scratch marks follow paths through the pipework, and at the end of one such path, you find a small panel, open, with a hopper inside still half-full of copper dust. The panel has a label, etched into the alloy: NUTRIENT RECLAMATION—SUBSYSTEM 47.

There are no animals. No rats, no insects, nothing that breathes or moves of its own volition. The only motion is mechanical: the pulse of steam, the drip of condensate, the slow creep of copper filaments, the distant grind of gears from somewhere beyond the walls. The place is utterly without biology, yet it feels alive in a way that unsettles you. The breathing steam. The growing copper. The deep resonant thrum from below that answers when you make a sound. Everything is built, everything is industrial, and yet the boundaries have blurred. The machine is not maintained so much as gardened.

And this seam, you understand, is new. That is the strangest realization. The edges of the alloy plates are sharp in places, unweathered, the mill scale still clinging in blue-black patches where the moisture hasn't reached. The gap is widening. Almost imperceptibly, but the evidence is there: fresh stress fractures at the bolt holes, bright metal where the plates have shifted against their mounts. The seam is being born. It is an embryonic space, a fault that is becoming a room, a crack that is developing its own climate and its own strange, metallic life. The machine is breaking—or restructuring—and this seam is one of its new organs, still raw, still forming its rules. You are standing inside a wound that doesn't know it's a wound yet. Or maybe a birth canal. It's hard to tell which.

You listen. Mostly quiet. The ventilation cycles off, and the seam falls into near-silence—a silence populated only by the slow dripping of water, the occasional tick of cooling metal, and that deep, low-resolution hum from below, so constant and so deep that it almost disappears into your own heartbeat. Then, from somewhere far above and to the left, a sequence of thuds, rhythmic, like a punch press or a drop hammer. Three beats. Pause. Three beats. The machine goes about its work, indifferent to the seam, and the seam goes about its own work, growing copper, breathing steam, widening imperceptibly in the cold and the dark.
