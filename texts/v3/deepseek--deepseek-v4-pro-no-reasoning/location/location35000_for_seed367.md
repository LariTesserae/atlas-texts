It is not a room. It is not quite a world, either. It is a cleft, a pocket of living heat caught high in something vastly older and utterly dry.

The walls rise sheer and close enough that the span across could be crossed in ten strides, maybe twelve. They are not rock, exactly—not in any wet-world sense. They are a single mineral mass, something like pale, flesh-toned chalcedony, or a fine-grained quartzite worn to the texture of old soap. The surface is not cracked so much as rumpled; it has the look of skin that has been creased for a very long time and will never be smoothed again. The erosion here is not the kind that comes from scouring wind or patient water. There is no water. The wearing-down came from something else, a process so slow and complete that only the final, stable form remains: a place polished by its own long stillness, by infinitesimal settling, by the weight of millennia pressing the walls into their final, intimate curve. This cleft is ancient not because it is ruined, but because it has finished becoming itself.

The floor is a dense mat of vegetation, and this is the paradox at the heart of the place. The plants are profuse, layered, leaning into one another—a single continuous tangle of stems and broad leaves and pale, reaching shoots that fill the space from wall to wall. There is no soil underfoot in the usual sense, only a deep thatch of the plants' own dead, generations of growth compressed into a spongy, springy mass that gives slightly as you move across it. The air is thick and warm, a constant steady heat that does not fluctuate; it clings to the skin like a second atmosphere, carrying a smell that is sweet without being floral, something closer to the scent of warm grain or rising dough, the breath of slow decomposition and rapid growth locked in a closed cycle.

This warmth has a source. It rises from below, from the deep seam where the cleft's floor meets the mineral embrace of the walls. There are no vents, no visible fissures, but the heat is unmistakable and localized. The plants nearest the floor's center are darker, broader, their leaves mottled with deep crimson and purple, as if flushed with the gift of that rising warmth. Toward the walls, where the heat fades slightly and the dimness is more profound, the vegetation grows paler and taller, straining upward.

The light is dim, diffuse, sourceless. It does not fall from above; the cleft is roofed, sealed entirely within the greater mineral body. The illumination seems to seep from the walls themselves, a faint phosphorescence in the chalcedony, just enough to see by, to catch the sheen on a leaf or the slow pulse of something moving beneath the mat. It is a light that makes distances uncertain and turns every shape into a soft-edged suggestion.

And the place is loud, but not with noise. With life.

The fauna is overwhelming. From the moment you become aware of it, it is impossible to ignore: a ceaseless, layered chittering, a rustling that is not the rustle of leaves in a breeze—there is no breeze—but the sound of innumerable small bodies moving through the thatch, over it, under it. The air itself seems thick with motion. Things flit at the edge of vision, small and quick, their movements stitching the dimness. The predominant form is something like a beetle, but long and segmented, with too many legs, each one tipped with a fine golden bristle. They pour over the vegetation in twitching streams, their carapaces clacking softly against one another. Larger things hunt them. Occasionally the mat heaves in one spot, a frantic scrabbling pulse, and then stills. A brief, high-pitched whine cuts through the general noise and then stops. The soundscape is one of constant, rhythmic predation—a world compressed to a single, dense point of eating and being eaten.

And it is urban. This is the strangest layer, the one that takes longest to resolve. Built into the walls, carved into the very mineral, are structures. They are not separate from the cleft; they are part of it, hollowed out of the pale chalcedony in stacked tiers that climb from just above the vegetation mat to the dim ceiling high above. Dwellings. They are small, rounded openings, each large enough for a single curled body, their edges worn soft with age. Ladders and narrow foot-ledges connect the tiers, also carved, a web of vertical pathways that speak of a people who moved on two legs and lived in the vertical. The craft is simple, precise, made with hand tools of surprising delicacy—chisel-work that follows the natural grain of the mineral, enhancing its glow.

The city is not empty. The dwellers are there, among the tiers and in the ladders, moving through their own dense and layered community. They are small, human-shaped but slight, with skin that has taken on the pale, luminous tone of the walls. Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the dim. They wear garments woven from the long, pale fibers of the wall-climbing plants, beaten soft. They carry tools of polished bone and chipped stone, and they move with the unhurried rhythm of people who have lived in this single, hidden pocket for more generations than the mineral walls can count. Their voices are part of the sound: a soft, melodic clicking speech, a patter of syllables that weaves through the chittering of the beetles and the rustling of the mat. They tend the vegetation. They harvest the beetle-grubs from the thatch. They carry small oil-lamps made from hollowed nodules, which add the faint, warm smell of burning fat to the air.

Somewhere in the middle tiers, a child leans out from a rounded doorway and calls down to another on a lower ledge. Below, in the mat, something larger surges and is pulled down again. The beetles stream on, unheeding. The walls glow their patient, eternal glow. The warmth rises. The place contains itself, a sealed and living city folded into an ancient, waterless stone, hidden from any world that might still exist outside, and dense with life that has long since forgotten it was ever anywhere else.
