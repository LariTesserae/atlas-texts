The city of Ember-Moss does not rest on the mountainside; it *is* the mountainside, a vertical urban sprawl built into a slope so high that clouds split around it like river water around a stone. The elevation is a constant presence in your lungs and in the air’s sharp coolness—not freezing, but a thin, damp chill that clings to everything. Moisture is sporadic here: morning mists coil up from the valley below and condense on every surface before burning off by midday, leaving beads of water trembling on leaves and pipes and struts. Then, without warning, a pocket of rain will sweep sideways through the city for twenty minutes and vanish, or a low cloud will settle in for days, soaking everything in fine drizzle. The dampness never fully leaves. Rust blooms in fractal patterns; wood is slowly digested and replaced.

The substrate of this entire world—the architecture, the infrastructure, the bones of the city—is vegetation. Not plants growing *on* things, but plants *as* things. The buildings are titanic, long-dead tree-trunks the color of tarnished bronze, their bark fossilized into structural stone but their internal grain still visible in the walls. From these ancient trunks sprout younger growth: living vines as thick as industrial cables that weave the walls and ceilings, moss that carpets floors in deep, sound-absorbing green, ferns that unfurl from windowsills to form awnings. The streets are not paved but *knitted*—thick mats of intertwined root-fiber, springy underfoot, that the inhabitants trim and train like topiary. There is no bare earth, no exposed mineral. Every surface is plant-derived, plant-saturated, living or once-living. The city breathes.

And yet, for all this green, the place is sparse. The spaces between presences are vast. Walkways and chambers open unexpectedly into cathedral-sized voids where a single spiral staircase of twisted liana ascends through dappled light, and nothing else. The scale is open, regional: you can look down a vertical shaft and see a neighborhood dangling two hundred meters below, small as a diorama, then look up and see another neighborhood above, and another, the city receding into mist. Between these centers of activity stretch long stretches of silent, root-bound galleries with only the sound of water dripping and the occasional hum of a distant engine. The population is urban in organization—there are districts, infrastructure, a logic to the layout—but the inhabitants are scattered across this enormous volume, their homes and workshops separated by acres of living wall and damp, empty air.

Industry is woven through the city like a second circulatory system. The tech level is unmistakably industrial: there are engines here, and factories. You hear them before you see them. The soundscape is loud but not chaotic—a deep, layered drone of mechanized production that echoes through the wooden canyons. Somewhere to the east, a processing mill runs twenty hours a day, its thrum a constant physical presence felt through the root-mats underfoot. Closer, in a chamber walled with thick, sound-deadening pads of moss, a gear-works clanks and hisses with steam-powered lathes. Pipes run everywhere, not metal but hollowed-out vines reinforced with resin, carrying water, steam, and occasionally a viscous green fluid that pulses with its own internal pressure. The engines burn processed plant oil, and their exhaust smells faintly of roasted nuts and chlorophyll. This is a scaled production, a city that makes things—tools, cloth, machine parts—but the factories are never crowded. They tend to sit in isolated grottoes or hang from the undersides of overhanging root-structures, attended by a single operator or left to run autonomously for hours.

The light here is normal, almost mundane, which makes everything else stranger. Sunlight filters down through layer upon layer of translucent leaves and woven canopy, shifting from gold to green to a diffuse, aquarium-like glow. In the deeper interiors, bioluminescent fungi have been cultivated into ceiling panels that emit a steady, cool radiance indistinguishable from overcast daylight. The light reveals everything clearly, and what it reveals is deeply wrong.

Fauna is rare. You can go days in Ember-Moss without seeing anything that moves of its own volition, and that absence is a pressure. Occasionally, a small, pale creature—something between a lizard and a silk moth—will scuttle across a wall and vanish into a crack in the bark. Colony insects, perhaps, but solitary and scarce. The only reliable animal presence is the spiders: tiny, translucent things that spin webs across the gaps in the root-mats, their silk catching the light like spun glass. They are so few and so motionless that you might mistake them for part of the architecture. The ecosystem is almost entirely plant-based, a closed loop of growth, decay, and regrowth that needs no animal intermediaries. But the lack of movement, of eyes watching from the shadows, of birds or rodents or even flies—it leaves a void in the mind, a signal that something is missing.

And that is where the weirdness begins.

The reality breakdown here is not a flashy thing. It’s a slow, pervasive wrongness that has settled into the city like the damp, until no one notices it anymore except strangers. The plants do not obey the usual rules. A vine will grow into a perfect spiral and then stop, as if it had decided on that shape before it began. The moss on the floor will sometimes reconfigure overnight, the patterns shifting from random speckle to geometric grids, then back by morning. There are chambers where the leaves are all the same size, exactly, down to the millimeter, on every branch of every tree, and it feels like walking into a repeating texture in a simulation. The ancient trunks that form the buildings are not from any tree that exists now; their wood grain spirals in patterns that suggest muscle and tendon more than cellulose, and their growth rings record years that don’t match any planetary cycle. Occasionally, in the deepest, oldest parts of the city, you’ll find a wall that is simultaneously living bark and rusted iron, the two substances merging so seamlessly that you can’t tell where one ends and the other begins. The industrial machinery itself is suspect. A piston might pump in a rhythm that syncs with your heartbeat, then stop the moment you notice. A pipe carrying that green fluid will branch into a shape that spells out a word in a script you don't recognize. The city feels not haunted but *overwritten*—a place where the rules of biology, physics, and time have been gently but thoroughly eroded, rubbed away by a process too slow and too ancient to see.

The erosion is, after all, ancient. This is the oldest-stable-state, the final form. The mountains have been worn down to rounded summits, the valleys filled and refilled until the slopes are smooth. Ember-Moss was not built in a century or a millennium. It grew, was inhabited, was absorbed back into the plant-web, was re-inhabited, was re-grown, countless times, until what remains is a palimpsest so deep that the original intention—if there was one—is beyond recovery. The city sits in its landscape like a creature that has exhaled and will never inhale again, utterly settled, utterly final. And yet, within that stillness, there is motion.

The city is active. It is not a static ruin or a museum. It has weather cycles of its own: the morning condensation, the daily fog-drain, the interior rain that falls from accumulated moisture on high leaves, the weekly venting of steam from the factory hubs. The plants grow visibly, some vines extending a few centimeters a day, and the inhabitants prune and guide them constantly, a perpetual negotiation between human intent and vegetative will. There are predator-prey dynamics here, but not of the animal kind—certain fungi hunt other fungi, spreading in slow, visible waves across a wall over weeks, consuming and being consumed. You can watch a patch of phosphorescent shelf fungus be overtaken by a dark, creeping mycelium that leaves behind only blackened, sterile bark, before that in turn is colonized by a new, more aggressive moss. Time-lapse ecology, visible in real time. And the industrial heart of the city beats like a metabolism: production cycles, maintenance cycles, the movement of workers along the root-pathways, the shifting of loads, the opening and closing of steam valves. The city is a body. It breathes, it feeds, it digests.

Stand on a high balcony of woven fiber, looking out across the open drop. The air is cool and wet on your face. Below you, a neighborhood of hanging dwellings sways almost imperceptibly, suspended from the overhang by cables of braided vine. Across the gap, a kilometer away, a factory platform hums, its smokestack—a hollowed trunk—venting pale steam into the mist. Water drips from somewhere above and hits a broad leaf with a steady, resonant plink. The drone of machinery fills the space, a low, omnipresent chord that you feel in your sternum. Everything is green, bronze, gray-green, and the pale white of spider silk catching the normal, steady light. Nothing moves except the steam, the dripping water, the slow curl of a young tendril reaching for the rail. Nothing looks at you. The silence where animals should be is a sound in itself, a gap in the spectrum. And the wall beside you, the living bark of the ancient trunk, is warm to the touch, pulsing at a rate that is almost but not quite your resting heartbeat.
