The place is called the Bight.

It occupies a drowned valley between two ridges of dark, water-smoothed stone that rise just enough to cup the space against the surrounding deep. The ridges do not quite surface—their crests sit a meter below the waterline, visible only as long smears of still-darker shadow in the already lightless water, or as places where the current shears and doubles back.

The Bight is not large. A swimmer could cross its full diameter in twenty strokes, though no swimmer has ever entered it. Its scale is that of a flooded cathedral nave, defined less by floor and walls than by a quality of enclosure—the sense that the water here is inside something, that the pressure distributes differently than in the open sea beyond. The floor lies at eighty meters, the ridgelines at twenty. The space between is the territory.

The water temperature holds steady at just above freezing, a fraction of a degree from the point where salt water crystallizes. This is not seasonal cold—the Bight lives in a permanent winter that has nothing to do with surface weather. The chill comes from below, from groundwater seepage through the valley’s ancient rock, freshwater that has spent ten thousand years filtering through the bones of the continent and emerges here as invisible, near-freezing threads that layer along the bottom like a second, heavier sea.

Everything worth describing happens in the middle column of water.

The Bight’s defining feature is a slow engine of precipitation that has no surface analogue. Dissolved minerals well up through fissures in the valley floor—manganese, iron, magnesium, silica—and encounter the colder seep-water, which forces them out of solution. They precipitate not as particles but as a continuous, microscopic sleet: fine grey-white motes that descend through the column at a rate of centimeters per hour, so slow that the water appears still, yet so dense with falling material that visibility ends at arm’s length. The motes are sharp-edged, crystalline in structure but irregular in form, each one a tiny fang of chalcedony or rhodochrosite that catches whatever faint light exists and throws it sideways before extinguishing it. To be in the Bight is to be in a silent blizzard of stone.

This precipitation has been running for a long time. The valley walls are cased in a rind of accumulated precipitate, layer upon layer like cave formations grown in liquid. The rind is not uniform. It thickens into flanges and buttresses where eddy currents concentrate the falling motes, and in places it has grown into baroque, coral-like latticeworks that reach out from the walls toward the center of the space, never quite meeting. Some of these structures are hollow, perforated by thousands of small-bore tunnels. Others are solid, their growth rings visible in cross-section as bands of alternating grey and rose. The oldest sections, near the bottom, have begun to metamorphose under their own accumulated weight, the earliest layers recrystallizing into something harder and darker. The valley is slowly filling itself in, becoming geology.

The built element of the Bight is distributed throughout this growing stone.

It takes a moment to recognize it as construction rather than formation, because the same precipitation process has coated everything in a skin of new mineral. But the forms beneath are too regular, too orthogonal in their underpinnings. There are passageways bored through the rind in straight lines, their walls showing the original tooling before the precipitate smoothed them. There are chambers cut into the living rock of the valley walls behind the accretion, some of them sealed with metal doors that have not been opened in a long time. The doors are steel, heavily alloyed, but they bloom with white and orange oxides where the cold water has worked on them for decades. Gaskets bulge and fray. Handwheels frozen in place.

These chambers connect horizontally in a loose network, a honeycomb of dry spaces that were once pressurized against the outside water. Some have failed. Their doors stand open or ajar, and the interiors are now part of the Bight’s water-column, filled with the same slow sleet, furniture and equipment half-dissolved into mineral efflorescence. Intact chambers still hold breathable atmosphere—nitrogen, oxygen, a trace of something else that reads wrong on sensors—maintained by systems that draw power from cables running along the valley floor, buried now under half a meter of precipitate.

The cables converge on a structure at the Bight’s deepest point: a cylinder four meters in diameter, eight tall, lying on its side and partly embedded in the valley floor. It is the source of the Bight’s only deliberate sound. Inside the cylinder, at intervals of approximately forty seconds, something actuates. A piston, probably, or a diaphragm. The sound it makes propagates through the water as a low, brief pulse, more felt than heard, a thud that arrives in the chest before the ears. The pulse does not echo—the precipitate rind absorbs reflections—but it travels, and after each pulse there is a waiting. The cylinder is waiting for an answering signal that does not come. Then the forty seconds pass, and it sends again.

The vegetation of the Bight is sparse by any measure, but it finds purchase. On the upper ridges, in the zone where attenuated surface light dies completely into black, there are patches of something that is not quite bacterial mat and not quite lichen. It grows in rosettes the size of spread hands, a pale grey that reads almost luminous in the darkness, though it emits no light of its own—it simply reflects whatever faint bioluminescence drifts past. The rosettes feed chemosynthetically on the manganese in the upwelling water, fixing it into their tissues, and when they die they become part of the precipitate, adding their own microscopic shells to the sleet. They grow slowly. A single rosette can be forty or fifty years old, its age measurable in concentric rings that are nearly identical to those of the mineral formations around it, and it is easy to mistake one for the other, the living for the merely grown.

Fauna in the Bight is rare but not absent. The creatures that pass through are mostly visitors from the surrounding deep: a species of small, blind squid that drifts in on irregular currents, its chromatophores flickering through patterns that mean nothing here, its body language a conversation with partners that are not present. The squid do not stay long. Something about the mineral sleet confuses their lateral lines; they swim in small, disoriented circles and then leave, ascending toward pressures their tissues prefer. Occasionally a grenadier fish noses along the valley floor, attracted by the electrical field of the buried cables, and departs. The only permanent residents are copepods—minute crustaceans that have adapted to filter the precipitate itself, their gut tracts lined with silicate-hardened ridges that can crack the crystalline motes. They are abundant, teeming in the middle water, so small and transparent that they are invisible until the pulse from the cylinder makes them twitch in unison, and for an instant the entire water column shimmers with their presence.

The strange quality of the Bight resides in its patterns, which do not quite fit. The forty-second pulse is the most obvious: whoever built the dry chambers and laid the cable and installed the cylinder chose that interval for a reason. But there are other rhythms. The upwelling of mineral-rich water follows a cycle of roughly eleven hours, surging and ebbing in a slow tide that has no correspondence to any lunar period. The precipitate sleet thickens and thins in response, so that the Bight breathes, its visibility shrinking and expanding on a schedule that reset long before the chambers were cut. And there is a third cycle, a colder one, that operates on the scale of seasons or longer. Every seventeen months, approximately, the groundwater seep drops in temperature by another fraction of a degree, and something at the base of the mineral rind cracks—a sharp, single report that propagates through the stone and the water and the flesh of anything present. Then stillness. Then the pulse again, forty seconds later, as if nothing had happened.

The Bight is a place where several different clocks are running, none of them set to the same time, and the resulting sense is of a machinery that has drifted out of synchronization with itself. The built infrastructure, the geological processes, the sparse living things—all active, all moving at their own pace, none of them quite intersecting. The cylinder calls. The stone grows. The copepods twitch. The rosettes fix manganese and die and become the stone. The whole system is in motion, dense with overlapping processes, dense with material falling and forming and accumulating, and yet the net impression is of something suspended, watching itself happen, waiting for a signal that is overdue.
