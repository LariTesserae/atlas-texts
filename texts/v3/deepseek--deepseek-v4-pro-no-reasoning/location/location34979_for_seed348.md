The place has no name, or if it does, no one remembers it. The iron footbridges that stitch its upper reaches have plaques, but the script has worn to shallow ripples, illegible as the whorls on a fingertip. This district — a knot of galleries and ductwork pressed into a declivity in the greater urban mass — never sees true sunlight. What reaches it is a secondhand glow, a diffuse amber that filters down through the mesh of overhead walkways and the collapsed roof of some older structure now serving as a ceiling. The light is steady, seasonless, the same at all hours. It gives the air a brown-gold thickness, like weak tea in a glass.

The air is cool and heavy with moisture. It isn't raining, but it has been, or will be soon, or perhaps it simply breathes damp. The distinction is irrelevant. Water travels in strange paths here. It condenses in sheets on the vertical surfaces of old heat exchangers, sluices down fluted iron pillars into channels cut in the flooring plates. There are pockets where it pools — shallow, clear, perfectly still — in the concavities of buckled sheet metal. The water never deepens, never evaporates entirely. It just occupies its hollows, skinning over with a faint iridescent film that is probably bacterial, probably mineral, probably both. Sometimes a droplet gathers on a pipe overhead and falls. The interval is always the same. Eight seconds. The sound it makes when it hits a particular copper flange is the only punctuation in a continuous roar.

That roar is the place's voice. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, a deep mechanical churning that has homogenized into a single tone over the decades. There are engines here, buried in the walls and beneath the floor plates, their original purpose lost. They run. They have always run. Their sound is not the chaotic clatter of machinery but a settled drone, a bass note that has worn smooth all the sharper frequencies. It passes through your bones more than your ears. There are no echoes because the sound has no beginning and no end. It is the baseline of silence.

And through that drone, threading it in restless streams, move the birds. Or what might be called birds if you didn't look too closely.

They are a presence before they are a shape — a shifting, boiling movement in the upper galleries, a collective rustle like a million pages turning at once. The flyways follow the old ductwork, the hanging cable trays, the gaps where floor plates have rusted through. When a flock passes, the amber light dims in waves. Their bodies are small, sparrow-sized, but there is nothing soft about them. Their wings are articulated lattices of dark metal, feather-like vanes of stamped alloy that click and rasp as they turn. Their heads are nodules of ceramic and wire, featureless except for the tiny aperture of a speaker grille. They do not sing. They emit a constant, low-volume chatter of static, overlapping signals, fragments of code, bursts of white noise that almost resolve into voices before fragmenting again. The aggregate of their calls is a seething upper layer of the soundscape, a restless treble laid over the engine drone.

They never stop moving. Their flight paths are loops, endless circuits through the rafters and down the long, corkscrewing ramps. They don't eat, or if they do, their prey is invisible — motes in the mineral water, ions in the air. Their numbers are, for all practical purposes, infinite. At any moment, several thousand are visible from any single vantage. The floor beneath their main thoroughfares is carpeted not with guano but with a fine, dark grit: abraded metal particles, microscopic shavings of their own bodies flaking away over what must be centuries of ceaseless flight. Nothing decays here in the organic sense. Things just erode.

The architecture is a low, compressed maze. Cathedral scale, but a cathedral for something hunched and crawling. The ceiling — that interleaved mass of fallen beams and floor plates — presses down in places to just above head height, then opens unexpectedly into narrow vertical shafts where the birds spiral upward into darkness. Walkways of expanded metal mesh hang from chains. Their surfaces are slick with condensation, and they sway almost imperceptibly under a weight that isn't there — just settling, just the memory of footsteps. Pipework runs in bundles along the walls, every surface blooming with the white and pale green of oxidation. There are no straight lines of sight. Every view terminates in a filigree of ironwork, a hanging curtain of cables, a dense cloud of the mechanical birds.

There are signs of human intention, but they have been overwritten. A stairwell descends through the floor in a square-cut shaft, its treads worn to slivers. It ends not in another level but in a smooth wall of what appears to be slag, a vitreous black stone with bubbles frozen in it, as though the lower city was simply sealed off at some point by a flow of impossible heat. Elsewhere, a console is embedded in a wall, its dials and toggles fused into a single blob. What was once a room has become a cistern, filled to the brim with that perpetually still, amber-tinged water, its surface so calm and dust-covered that it registers as a solid floor until a bird's wingtip dimples it.

Nothing grows. The biological concept of vegetation is absent. In its place is a slow, inorganic efflorescence. Copper pipes have blossomed into blue-green crystal gardens where slow leaks have persisted. Iron surfaces extrude delicate, rust-red curls like frozen smoke. The wall of slag has its own ecology of minerals, tiny brittle stalactites of some black glass that chime faintly when a bird brushes them. The world here is post-life but not dead. It has a mineral vitality, a tectonic metabolism measured in microns per decade.

The weirdness of the place is not in any single anomalous feature but in its total equilibrium. It is a closed system. The engines run, the birds fly, the water drips every eight seconds. The parameters are fixed. You could leave and return a century later to find the same flock turning the same corner, the same droplet forming on the same pipe. This is not stasis imposed from without; it is an exhaustion of all gradients. Every potential has been spent, every reaction reached its end state. The place has simply settled into the deepest possible groove of its own existence, and now it hums there, changeless and loud and strange, a pocket of eternal afternoon in the body of the dying city.
