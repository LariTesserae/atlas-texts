The place is called Underbloom, though only a few hundred people would know that name and most would not speak it aloud.

Picture a bowl the size of a cathedral nave set deep in cold ground: a basin of black, glossy rock too fresh to have weathered smooth, its walls still bearing the tear-marks of whatever opened it. The floor lies only a few feet above the water table, and water does not pool so much as breathe out of the stone—slick films, weeping seams, a constant cold condensation that beads on every surface and drips from every overhang in a slow, irregular percussion. The air sits at just above freezing, dense with wet, and the only light is the faint blue-grey glow of certain fungi and the occasional soft blink of worm-larvae in the moss.

But you must understand the moss. It is not moss over stone. It is moss as stone, moss as architecture, moss as the entire structural fabric of the undercity. Generations of the people here—if people is the right word—have coaxed peat-moss and cold-loving sphagnum into walls, arches, lintels, staircases, nests, partitions, vaults. They wove living wicker frames and trained the moss to swallow them, then carved rooms out of the resulting vegetable mass. The “buildings” are therefore not built so much as continued: walls still growing at the edges, floors springing faintly underfoot, ceilings beaded with moisture and releasing a vegetable musk so thick it feels like a second atmosphere. This is a city in the way a coral reef is a city. The construction is layered—old chambers compressed into dark peat below, newer growth bulging outward above—and the whole thing thrums with a slow botanical tempo: roots shifting in the dark, leaves unfurling over doorways, water moving by capillary action through living beams.

And yet, for all that vegetation, the population is sparse. The tunnels and chambers of Underbloom could hold thousands, but perhaps two hundred souls live here, scattered through the walls like larvae in a comb. You can walk for long, dripping minutes between lit doorways—lit only by glow-caps planted in wall niches—and hear nothing but the water and your own breath. The city is too big for its inhabitants, or the inhabitants are too few for their city, and the emptiness has a presence of its own: the sense of immense, vegetable patience.

The fauna is overwhelming, if you know how to look. Not in the sense of herds or flocks, but in saturation, in numberless small forms occupying every niche. The moss-walls are alive with springtails and blind isopods; pale newts move through the drainage channels in slow abundance; on the ceilings, colonies of hibernating moths hang in folded grey sheets, so many that they look like fur. There are larger things too—the moss-pigs, pale and bristled, that root through peat walls with methodical hunger, and the blind cave-lampreys in the deeper sumps, and the whisper-eels that hunt the dark water channels by sensing the electrical tremble of a heartbeat. The people here have a saying: “Count what you can see, then know that for every one you see there are a hundred you don’t, and for every hundred, a thousand too small to see.” The ecology is in constant motion: larvae drifting on seep-water films, moths waking in synchrony to the temperature changes of the outside world, moss-pigs tearing open old walls to eat the compressed peat beneath, and the people themselves weeding, culling, harvesting, tending—all of it slow and methodical in the near-dark.

Underbloom is strange in a way that is hard to name. Partly it is the light—or near-lightlessness—and partly the queerness of all this life in a place so cold and so waterlogged. But mostly it is the sense that the city is not fully *for* the people. They maintain it, yes; they carve rooms and plant glow-caps and reinforce sagging archways. But the moss grows over their doorways if they sleep too long. The walls seal up abandoned chambers with the placid industry of a healing wound. The city is a living thing that tolerates them, not a thing they control, and its true allegiance is to the water and the dark. The people here do not build. They negotiate.

And beneath all of it, this: there is no sun, and so no day, no season in the usual sense. Time is marked by water—by the slow rise and fall of the seep, by the rhythm of drip and flow, by the way certain mosses bloom white only after a cold rain above makes the walls run. The city breathes in condensation and breathes out mist. And the sound, mostly quiet, is not silence: it is a low, steady music of dripping, trickling, soft animal movement, the wet creak of settling moss, the faint high chirp of a springtail swarm, and, very occasionally, the long descending note of a whisper-eel in the deep water, like a bell rung under ice.
