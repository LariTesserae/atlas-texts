The air here does not dry. It cannot. It beads on every surface, runs in rivulets down stone that was born yesterday, and hangs in a perpetual woolen fog that the wind never troubles. The sky—if there is one beyond the mist—has not been seen since the place came into being.

You are standing on a high shelf of basalt, one of dozens in a cluster that erupts from the wet earth like the broken teeth of some buried machine. The rock is black, glassy, and sharp-edged, its fractures so fresh they gleam with the rainbow slick of unoxidized iron. No lichen yet. No moss has found a purchase in the microscopic crevices. This is stone that has never known weathering, that still holds the memory of its own formation—which was not volcanic. There are no cones, no vents, no ash. The columns simply *rose*, extruded from the ground in hexagonal prisms that terminate in razor spires, as if the planet's deeper chemistry decided to express itself in architecture and then stopped, mid-thought.

Between the spires, water moves. Not in streams—the grade is wrong for that, the shelves too flat and too elevated—but as a constant sheet-flow, a millimeter-deep skin of cold liquid that glides over the stone with no apparent source. It wells up from the rock itself, weeping from the smooth vertical faces of the columns, condensing out of the fog faster than gravity can claim it. The ground underfoot is not soil but a thin black grit, sharp as crushed glass, and the water makes it shimmer with a frictionless gleam. Every step sounds like walking on wet slate and every step is amplified, sharp and close, then swallowed by the fog. The acoustics of the place are strange: sounds carry ten feet and then vanish, as if the mist is not water but a heavier medium, something that drinks frequencies.

The vegetation is a contradiction. At 2.3 on the scale, the place should be lush—and it is—but the life here does not rise from roots in earth. There is no earth. Instead, plants grow directly from the weeping stone, their anchor-points invisible, as if they are not anchored at all but balanced, held in place by the density of their own damp leaves. They are cool-weather things, dark greens tending toward purple and near-black, with broad fleshy structures like rhubarb crossed with kelp. Their stems are thick with stored water, their leaves unfurl in spirals that cup the mist, and they are never still. Not from wind—the air does not move—but from the weight of the water they collect. A leaf will fill, bow, spill a silver cascade into the sheet-flow below, and then spring back with a wet slap that echoes in the muffled way of all sounds here. The rhythm of these slaps is the only meter the place knows, and it is constant, unchanging. The plants do not grow. They do not wilt. They have found their equilibrium and frozen it.

The fauna are abundant and entirely wrong.

They are small, most of them, and they occupy the spaces between the plants and the stone columns, moving with the mechanical purpose of things that were never born. Some resemble amphibians—slick black bodies with too many legs, or legs that end in too many toes, or toes that are themselves miniature versions of the legs that bear them, recursive and fractal. They do not move so much as they *iterate* across the stone, a wave of identical body parts translating a central mass from one position to another. The sound they make is a faint clicking, like the valves of a well-tuned engine, and it never varies. The same creatures in the same places making the same sounds, looped with a fidelity that suggests they are not living things but recordings of living things, played back by a medium that cannot tell the difference.

Then there are the larger ones.

They are visible through the mist, at the edges of the small cathedral space formed by a ring of the tallest columns. Shapes, taller than a person, that stand on two legs or four or a number that changes depending on the angle of view. They are industrial in their anatomy—joints like polished pistons, body segments that resemble boilerplate but flex with organic give, heads that are clusters of intake manifolds breathing the fog. They are furred and they are machined and they are neither, a collapse of categories that the eye cannot resolve. Steam vents from seams in their bodies in steady, rhythmic puffs, and the steam adds to the fog that feeds the plants that drip the water that weeps from the stone. A closed cycle. Perfect. Unchanging.

One of them turns its head—its manifold—toward you, and the sound it makes is not a call but a chuff, the exhausted release of a pneumatic cylinder. It has seen you. It has always seen you. It will always be seeing you, in this moment, because the moment does not pass. The dynamic scale is 0.2 for a reason. This is a place that has achieved stasis, a single frame of reality that the projector has jammed on. The water never stops flowing but the flow never changes. The plants never stop moving but the movement is a loop. The creatures never stop their clicking, their chuffing, their iterative locomotion, but they also never *arrive*. They are caught in the act of being, severed from sequence, held in a now that has no before or after.

The built scale is 0.1, and that one-tenth is the hardest thing to look at. Not a structure—there are no walls, no roofs, no made things—but a pattern. On one of the basalt columns, the hexagonal face has been worn smooth in the shape of a gear. Not carved. Worn, as if by the passage of countless mechanical teeth over eons. But this stone is young. The erosion parameter is 0.8; nothing here has had time to erode. The wear-pattern is a fossil from a future that will never arrive, a signature of machinery that has not yet been invented and now cannot be, because time has stopped.

The light is dim, gray, sourceless. It does not cast shadows because it does not come from anywhere. It is simply the ambient glow of the mist itself, a phosphorescence too faint to read by but enough to see the gleam on the stone, the wet black of the plants, the slow piston-motion of the things at the edge of the clearing. The temperature is cool without being cold, the chill of a root cellar, of a cave entrance in spring. Your breath does not fog because the air is already saturated; your skin is wet within minutes, and you cannot tell what is your sweat, what is condensation, what is the water that weeps from the stone and runs over your boots in its endless, static sheet.

The sound is loud the way a factory floor is loud: a composite of many small precise noises that add up to a blanket of white sound. The clicking of the recursive amphibians. The wet slap of leaves. The chuff of venting steam from living machines. The constant low hiss of water on rock. And beneath it all, a deeper sound, felt more than heard—a thrumming, a vibration that comes up through the soles of your feet, through the basalt columns, through the water itself. It is the sound of a motor. It is the sound of whatever lies beneath this shelf of stone, beneath the thin crust of unreality, churning in steady state. An engine. A pump. The thing that lifts the water, that extrudes the columns, that generates the mist. It has been running forever. It will run forever. It constitutes the place.

You realize, standing in the circle of dripping fronds and black glass spires, that you are not in a landscape at all. You are inside a machine. The place is a component. The water is coolant. The plants are filtration. The creatures are maintenance, or monitoring, or simply a byproduct—bioluminescent indicators on a circuit board, glowing to signal normal operation. The weirdness is 2.9, nearly absolute, because the breakdown is not in the place but in the category. "Landscape" and "engine" have collapsed into each other. The fog is exhaust and weather. The stone is structure and geology. The sound is ecosystem and industry. The boundaries are gone.

You do not leave. The dynamic scale is 0.2; leaving is a change, and change is not permitted. You are here now, in the frame, part of the loop. The manifold-head turns toward you again, or still. The leaf slaps. The water runs. The engine thrums.

And nothing, not one single thing, ever alters from this arrangement.
