The climb takes you through a city that has forgotten it was built.

What you first take for stepped agricultural terraces—those green shelves stairstepping up the volcanic slope—reveal themselves, on closer approach, as the slumped floors of collapsed buildings. The angle of the climb is relentless. You are ascending the inner wall of a caldera so vast that its far rim is a blue-gray suggestion twenty miles distant, a ring of peaks wearing clouds like loosened scarves. The elevation here is extreme, the air thin enough to make the lungs work, thick enough to carry the weight of water. And water is everywhere. It drips from every surface, condenses on skin, runs in sheets down the moss-furred faces of broken walls. The temperature never drops below sweltering; the air is a steam bath held at blood-warmth, and the distinction between breathing and drinking begins to blur.

The city was built into this slope—or perhaps *grown* from it, in some older, stranger way—so long ago that the jungle has not merely overgrown it but incorporated it. The architecture and the vegetation are now a single composite substance. Walls of fitted black basalt have become trellises for a kind of dense, fleshy creeper that does not so much cling as fuse, its roots entering the stone along hairline cracks and emerging again ten feet later as woody cables thick as a thigh. The canopy is multilayered: towering tree-ferns with trunks like wet brown feathers give way to a midstory of broad-leaved things—philodendrons, perhaps, or something that has evolved beyond that name—whose leaves are the size of tabletops and droop under the constant weight of condensate. Below that, a ground layer of mosses and liverworts in greens so deep they are nearly black, punctuated by the bright red fruiting bodies of fungi that glow faintly in the perpetual overcast light.

There are no animals. Not one. No birdcall, no insect whine, no scuttling in the leaf litter. The absence is not peaceful. It is a pressure, a held breath. In a place this wet and hot and lush, there should be a riot of animal noise—frogs, cicadas, howler monkeys, something. Their absence makes the place feel like a stage set waiting for a cue that will never come. The only sound is water: the hiss of mist, the gurgle of a thousand tiny rills cutting channels through the moss, the deeper roar that rises and falls as clouds shift against the upper slopes, amplifying and muffling the waterfalls in slow, unpredictable rhythms. And this sound is not gentle. The roar is continuous and immense, a physical force that presses against the eardrums until the inner ear begins to invent phantom tones—a high, crystalline ringing that might be the voice of the place itself or might just be the body's protest against the relentless acoustic assault. You cannot hear your own breathing unless you stop and hold it, and even then, the sound of the valley fills the silence completely.

The weirdness settles in slowly. At first it is just the absence of animals, which you can rationalize. Then you notice that the water is flowing uphill.

Not everywhere. But in certain channels, carved into the ancient stone, the water runs against the slope of the caldera as if the local gravity has been rotated five degrees off true. A rivulet will climb a broken stair, pause at the lip as if gathering courage, and then sheet sideways across a vertical wall. Mist rises from the valley floor below in ropes and braids that twist against each other in geometries that look intentional, almost script-like, before dissolving. The steam does not behave as steam should. It pools in the lee of ruined archways, condenses into shapes that hold their form for a heartbeat too long before slumping back into vapor.

The city itself speaks to the breakdown. The stonework is basalt, hexagonal columns and fitted blocks, and the carvings that cover every surface are eroded to a soft blur by millennia of wet and warmth. But what remains visible makes no sense. There are friezes that appear to show the city *before it was built*, the caldera empty and forested, and beside them, friezes of the city in its prime, and beside those, friezes of the city as it is now, ruins and moss, rendered in identical style and identical weathering. As if all three states were carved at once. As if time here has always been a landscape rather than a line.

The streets—you can still trace them, long level grades winding across the slope—are thick with a black mud that is not mud. It is a mass of decomposed vegetation so old and so saturated that it has become a kind of liquid peat, and it quivers faintly underfoot, not from the impact of your step but from some internal process, a slow fermentation that generates heat. The ground here is feverish. In low spots, where the black muck pools deeper, the surface is broken by bubbles that rise with the viscosity of lava and pop with a wet exhale of methane and rot. The smell is sweet and foul together: gardenias and gangrene, nectar and decay.

The structures grow denser as you climb. The lower slopes were the outskirts, neighborhoods that sprawled and bled into the jungle. Here, nearing the rim, the buildings shoulder together, their walls sharing party lines, their upper stories long since collapsed into the narrow canyons between them. You are walking through rooms now, not streets. A doorway opens into a chamber whose ceiling is the underside of a strangler fig’s root mat, dangling hundreds of pale aerial roots like a frozen rain. Another chamber—this one with the remains of a staircase spiraling up into nothing, the upper floors having peeled away and slid down the mountain centuries ago—has become a cistern. The water in it is perfectly clear and perfectly still, and it reflects the gray sky and the dark green canopy with a clarity that is unnerving because the reflection is slightly *delayed*. A cloud passes overhead; the reflection follows it a full second later. You do not look into that water again.

At the rim, the world opens. The caldera drops away into a bowl of cloud, a lake of white mist that stretches to the far peaks, and the sound of the waterfalls is now so immense that it becomes indistinguishable from silence. The heat up here is no less, but the wind—the first moving air you have felt—carries the mist across your skin in a constant, warm caress. The ruins at the rim are the largest yet: a temple, a palace, a thing of collapsed domes and standing pillars, all of it the same black basalt, all of it furred in green. And here, carved into a lintel block that has fallen face-up across the threshold, is a figure. A human figure, arms outspread. It has no face—eroded, or never carved?—but the posture is unmistakable. It is the posture of someone listening.

You realize you have been hearing the city's name in the waterfall's roar for the last hour. You cannot quite make out the syllables, but the rhythm is there, repeating, a word spoken over and over by the mist and the stone. The place is not empty. It is waiting. It has been waiting, without change, without gradient, in a perfect equilibrium of wet and green and heat and ruin, for a length of time that has no meaning here. The city forgot it was a city. The jungle forgot it was a jungle. They became a single thing, and that thing has a name, and you are standing in its mouth.
