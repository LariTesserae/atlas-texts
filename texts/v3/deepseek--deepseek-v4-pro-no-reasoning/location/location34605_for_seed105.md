Let yourself sink into it.

You are in the high country, but this is not the high country you know. The world here is inverted. You are not walking on land beneath a sky full of rain—you are inside the rain. Inside a vertical ocean that falls upward, or sideways, or simply hangs in a darkness so complete that direction loses meaning.

The water is everywhere, but it is not still. It moves in slow, muscular currents that you feel as pressure against your skin, as a constant low hum transmitted through your bones. The temperature sits just above freezing, cold enough to leach warmth steadily from anything warm-blooded, but not enough to form ice. There is no ice here. There has never been ice here. The water has been liquid for as long as this place has existed, and that is very long indeed.

You are not swimming. You are standing on something solid—a slope, a ridge, the curve of what might once have been a mountain before the flood that was not a flood but the normal state of things. The ground beneath you is stone, but it is not bare stone. It is thickly covered with growth.

This is the strange part, the part that tells you this place is not simply a drowned world but something else entirely. The vegetation is lush, as lush as any jungle, but it is a jungle made for the dark and the cold and the all-surrounding water. There are no leaves. There are fronds, filaments, things like kelp but not kelp—thicker, fleshier, colored in deep blues and purples and blacks that read as pure black in the lightless water. They grow in great profusion from every surface, swaying in the slow currents, and they are tall. Some rise three times your height before they feather out into plumes of sensory filaments that wave like hands feeling the water for something that never comes.

The spacing of things is generous. The growth, though lush in its own way, does not form a dense thicket. Each major organism occupies its own zone, its own radius of influence. Between them, the stone is bare except for a fine crust of something that might be lichen or might be bacterial mat, black on black, faintly phosphorescent when disturbed. The light from that disturbance is the only light there is—brief patches of dim blue-green glow where you step, fading within seconds. The darkness otherwise is absolute, the kind of darkness that presses against the eyes, that makes your visual cortex fire random phantom shapes in desperation.

Sounds come through the water clearly but strangely. The currents themselves have a voice—a deep continuous rush, layered with overtones from the way the water moves through the different densities of the plant growth. The fronds vibrate. Some of them, the tall ones, are hollow and tuned; as water passes over their openings, they produce low tones, like the bass pipes of an organ breathing. These tones shift as the currents shift, and the currents shift often. This is an active place, governed by cycles you cannot see the origin of—perhaps tidal, perhaps thermal, perhaps something else. The water pulses. The soundscape pulses with it, rising and falling in slow waves, occasionally building to a roar that makes the stone vibrate underfoot, then subsiding to a mutter.

There are no animals. None. The water is full of movement, but all of it is water moving, plants moving, loose stones tumbling in the current. Nothing breathes. Nothing flees. Nothing hunts. The absence is so total that after a while you begin to feel it as a presence in itself—a kind of ecological silence under the physical loudness, a vacancy where something should be and isn't. The plants do not seem to need animals. They feed on the water itself, or on the minerals dissolved in it, or on the faint chemical traces that rise from the stone below. Their reproduction must be passive, spores released into the currents, trusting to chance.

And there are structures. They are scattered, easy to miss. At first you think they are natural rock formations, but then you see the regularity—stones stacked, stones cut, the faint remainders of walls. They are low, never more than knee-height now, worn down to nubs by the endless passage of water. The material is the same dark stone as the mountain itself, but shaped. Here a line that was once straight. There a curve that was once an arch, collapsed, its fragments colonized by the blue-black fronds. The craftsmanship is simple, hand-tool level, the marks of chipping and grinding still visible under the patina of bacterial crust. Whoever built these things used stone tools and patience, and they built small—shelters perhaps, or markers, or something ceremonial. There are no bones. No artifacts beyond the stone itself. If they left anything of wood or fiber, it has long since been consumed by the water, or by the same biological processes that feed the plants.

You walk along the ridge. The ground slopes away on either side into deeper darkness. The sense of elevation is strong—this is a high place, a ridgeline or a plateau edge, and the water falls away into something deeper below. You can feel it in the current, which quickens near the edges, and in the way the tall fronds lean outward, straining toward the void. The scale of this place is larger than it first seems. The ridge is not a hilltop; it is a regional feature, miles long, part of a drowned mountain range. You could follow it for days and the character would shift slowly—the growth thinning here, thickening there, the stone changing color, the current-organ tones rising in pitch as the channels narrow.

The cycles press on. The current strengthens, weakens, changes direction slightly. The cold is a constant, but within it there are micro-gradients—places where water wells up from below slightly warmer, carrying different minerals, marked by different growth. The whole system is alive in a slow, geological way, processing the energy of the moving water into form, into sound, into the quiet accumulation of stone structures that no one builds anymore.

And the water itself—3 out of 3—is not just the medium. It is the world. It is the atmosphere, the weather, the geography. The ridge you walk on is an island in reverse, a place where solid matter briefly approaches the surface of the water-column. Below you, the mountain falls away into the true depths, the abyssal plain of this drowned world. Above you, the water goes on and up, and somewhere impossibly far overhead there might be a surface, a boundary between liquid and something else—but you suspect not. You suspect the water goes on forever in all directions, that the mountain is an idea floating in a universe of cold dark liquid, and the plants and the scattered stones and the slow majestic currents are all that has ever been.
