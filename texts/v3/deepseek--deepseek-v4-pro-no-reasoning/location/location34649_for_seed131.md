The place is a room, or what was once called a room—a chamber nestled deep inside a larger structure so vast and old that its boundaries have been forgotten. The walls are not walls in any natural sense; they are constructed entirely of fitted plates, gray-black metal stained with condensate, every surface beaded wet. The plates are immense, each one larger than a person, their seams sealed with some darker compound that has blistered and cracked in places, weeping slow threads of moisture down onto the floor. Nothing here is stone. Nothing here is earth. The substrate beneath everything is manufactured, fabricated, assembled by intent so long ago that the intent itself has evaporated from memory.

The floor is a grating, a lattice of thick bars with wide gaps between them, and below the grating there is water—not a pool exactly, but a body of it, dark and still and filling the void beneath the room to an unknown depth. The water surface sits perhaps a hand's breadth below the grating, so close that the metal is perpetually slick, so close that the air itself is saturated. Every breath comes thick. The temperature is warm, steady, the kind of warmth that no longer registers as comfort but simply as condition. It has been this warm for longer than warmth has had meaning.

And the sound. The sound is everything.

It comes from everywhere and nowhere, a continuous deep roar that presses against the eardrums with physical weight. It is the sound of engines—great machines sealed away behind the walls and beneath the floor, their function forgotten, their operation uninterrupted across spans of time that have no name. It is a churning thrum, layered with higher harmonics, the whine of turbines or pumps or something that was never given a name in human language. The sound does not vary. It has never varied. It is the baseline of existence here, so constant that silence would be a shock, a wound, an impossibility.

Vegetation grows here despite everything.

It is lush—the word is almost too gentle for what has happened. The plants are not plants in any surface-world sense. They are pale, fleshy things, ivory-white and translucent in places, their tissues thick with stored water. They do not root in soil because there is no soil. Instead they anchor themselves to the metal itself, tendrils worming into seams and cracks, drawing sustenance from the condensate, from the ambient humidity, from something in the metal's gradual dissolution. They drape the walls in sheets, layer upon layer of broad rubbery leaves that overlap like scales. Some hang in great dripping curtains from the ceiling, their tips brushing the grating. There are forms like tubes, like bladders, like flattened ribbons that pulse almost imperceptibly, slowly, in time with some rhythm unrelated to the engines. The vegetation is everywhere, and yet the space feels empty. The gaps between the hanging curtains are vast. The room itself is large enough that the far walls are lost in a haze of mist and dimness, and the plants, for all their profusion, occupy only surfaces. The volume of the air between them is enormous and still and hollow. One could stand in the center of the grated floor and feel utterly alone, surrounded by growth but suspended in near-void.

The light is the one thing that is simply normal—a diffuse illumination that seems to emanate from the ceiling plates themselves, a steady white glow without source points, without flicker, without warmth beyond the ambient. It is enough to see by, enough to read by if there were anything to read. It does not change. There is no cycle, no dimming, no night.

The fauna is rare, but it exists. Occasionally, from the corner of vision, something moves among the hanging vegetation—a slow, pale, many-legged thing the size of a hand, gliding from one rubbery leaf to another. It leaves no trace. It makes no sound that can be detected above the engines. There might be dozens of them in the whole chamber. There might be three. They do not seem to interact, do not call, do not gather. They simply are, and then are somewhere else, and then are gone.

This place is low—submerged in the sense of being buried, pressed down into the deepest levels of the constructed world above it. The elevation is near the water table of this entire built labyrinth, the point where the sum of all downward seepage gathers and rests. It is the sump, the basin, the place where everything settles. And it is ancient, eroded not by wind or rain but by time and moisture and the patient work of the vegetation's slow acids. The metal plates are worn thin in places, edges rounded, surfaces pitted. The grating beneath one's feet is scalloped along the tops of the bars where countless droplets have fallen from countless leaves across durations that mock the word "century." This is the end state of erosion in a place where erosion has nothing left to do but whisper at surfaces that have already yielded everything they can.

And yet it never changes. The water never rises and never falls. The engines never pause. The temperature never fluctuates. The plants grow so slowly that a single leaf might take a thousand years to reach its full span, and when it finally withers, it does so over another thousand, dissolving back into the general wet without drama. The place is frozen in dynamic equilibrium, a stable state that has persisted so long that time itself has become a meaningless parameter. Change would require an outside force, and outside forces have not reached this deep in aeons.

Everything here is built. The metal, the grating, the sealed seams, the light-panels, the engines whose sound is the world's heartbeat. There is no natural substrate. The water below is not a spring or an aquifer; it is condensate from the great machines' cooling cycles, collected over time beyond measure, filtered by the vegetation's roots, dropping back down as transpiration from the fleshy leaves to begin the cycle again. Even the ecology is an artifact, an unintended consequence of construction, a secondary system that booted itself up in the margins of the primary one and has been running ever since.

The room holds a single feature at its center, and this is the pocket-scale heart of the place, the hiddenness, the intimacy that makes it a landscape rather than merely a location. It is a pool—or what could be called a pool—a place where the grating has been removed or perhaps never installed, a rectangular opening perhaps three paces long and two wide, giving direct access to the dark water below. The edges are smooth, worn, almost soft to the touch. The water here is clearer than elsewhere, or perhaps it is simply that the light falls differently, penetrating a small distance before being swallowed. The surface is utterly still, mirror-like, disturbed only by the faintest vibration from the omnipresent sound. This pool is the focus, the reason for the room's existence or at least the reason it is remembered. The rest of the chamber is vast and empty, the hanging vegetation mere drapery around this single opening, this one point of access to what lies below.

A place like this is not meant to be found. It is embedded within layers of machinery and forgotten corridors, a hidden chamber that exists only because some ancient designer included it in the blueprints for reasons now indecipherable. Perhaps it was a maintenance access. Perhaps it was a cistern. Perhaps it was something else entirely, something that the word "ritual" might approximate if ritual could be imagined as a purely functional act performed by people who no longer exist in a context that no longer applies.

The atmosphere is thick, heavy, pressing in on the skin. The condensation beads on eyelashes. The roar of the engines is so constant that it becomes a kind of silence after a while, the mind adapting, learning to ignore it, until a sudden awareness snaps back and the volume is staggering again. The plants breathe out moisture. The water below gives back nothing but darkness and the faint reflection of the ceiling's glow. The creature—if it can be called a creature—drifts along a hanging curtain of leaves and disappears into a fold of white flesh and is gone. The place waits. It has been waiting for a very long time. It will continue to wait, warm and wet and loud and still, until something changes.

Nothing changes.
