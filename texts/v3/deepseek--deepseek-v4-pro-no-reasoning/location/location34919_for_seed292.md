The air is never still here. Even the word "air" feels approximate—what moves around you is a cool suspension, a breathable white vapor thick with tiny droplets that never quite become rain and never quite become fog. It streams sideways at a walking pace, carrying the sound with it. The sound is the first thing anyone notices: a continuous low-frequency thrum that you feel in the sternum, overlaid with a ceaseless metallic ringing, like ten thousand hammers striking ten thousand anvils at slightly different moments. This is the voice of the Ringing Deeps.

You are standing on what might be called land, if land were a lattice. Underfoot is a substance like cold coral—bone-white, porous, wet—that forms a webwork of bridges and platforms suspended over a blackness that goes down and down. The ocean is there, far below, but you cannot see its surface. What you see instead are the Pillars.

The Pillars rise through the mist, hundreds of them within your field of view, each one a perfect cylinder of dark basalt perhaps forty feet across. They do not end below in any visible foundation, and they do not end above in any ceiling you can discern. They simply continue, vertical and absolute, into the upper white and the lower black. Their surfaces are not smooth. Every inch is carved with grooves, flutes, spiraling channels that run the full visible length. Water courses down these channels in continuous sheets, not gently but with a driven, urgent velocity, as if pumped. The water is cool but not cold—you could survive immersion, though the current would take you.

The water falls and falls and falls, and where it strikes the lattice-bridges, it splits and scatters into the vapor that fills the space. The impact creates the ringing. Each droplet strikes the resonant coral-stuff and sets it singing. Multiply this by a billion droplets per second across a region the size of a mountain range, and the sound becomes a physical presence, a white-out of noise through which you must shout to be heard at arm's length.

This place is ancient. The erosion parameter does not do it justice. The Pillars have been here long enough that the water has carved the basalt not into jagged forms but into flowing, polished, settled shapes. There are no sharp edges left anywhere. Every surface has been touched and retouched by liquid until it has found its final form, the shape it will hold for the rest of geological time. The coral-lattice, too, is a finished thing. It does not grow. It does not crumble. It is the stable-state skeleton of something that lived and died and mineralized so long ago that the concept of "ago" begins to fray.

And yet the place is lush. The vegetation parameter did not lie, but it demands a redefinition of "vegetation."

From every wet surface, from the grooves of the Pillars and the undersides of the bridges and the walls of the chasms, something grows. It is not plant life in any photosynthetic sense—there is no sun here, only the ambient light that seems to emanate from the vapor itself, a gray-white glow of uniform intensity. The growths are fungal, or perhaps bacterial, forming mats and curtains and pendulous bladders of tissue in deep reds and purples and bruise-blacks. They breathe the vapor. They filter the water. Some of them are massive—single bladders the size of bathyspheres, translucent, with darker nuclei visible within. Some of them are filigree, lace-fine networks that tremble continuously in the wind. They coat every surface that is not actively scoured by the fastest water, and even there, they send exploratory tendrils.

They are the base of everything.

Moving through the mist and the noise, visible in glimpses between the Pillars, are the fauna. Abundant, yes. They fill every niche the fungal forests create. Most are airborne, or semi-airborne. Creatures like manta rays made of translucent cartilage ride the vapor currents, their bodies undulating with a motion part swimming, part flying. They are filter feeders, mouths agape, straining the living spores from the air. Smaller things dart among them—spindle-shaped, many-finned, quick as hummingbirds. They navigate by sound, you realize. The ringing of the coral is not random to them. They read it, the way a bat reads the silence between its cries. Some of them sing back, adding their own percussive notes to the cacophony.

There are larger things. You catch the shadow of one now, a shape moving behind a Pillar two hundred feet away, bloated and slow, trailing long feathery appendages. It pays you no attention. Nothing pays you attention. You are a warm spot in a cool world, but you are not part of the food chain. The food chain is vapor-spore-filterer-predator, and it hums with the steady-state intensity of a system that has been running, unchanged, for epochs.

And then there are the marks of industry.

Scattered is the word. The structures you find—and you have to look, because the fungal growth reclaims everything eventually—are small, isolated, singular. A platform of black iron, riveted, bolted to the coral with fasteners the size of your fist. A ladder descending into the mist, its rungs slick with condensation, ending in nothing, or in a trapdoor sealed by rust and time. A single combustion engine, massive, house-sized, crouching on a bridge-platform. It is not running. None of them are running. But they are not ruined, either. The pistons are oiled. The flywheels are free of corrosion. Someone maintains them. Or something does.

You find a gauge, its glass face beaded with moisture, its needle trembling at the low end of a green band. What it measures is unclear. Pressure, perhaps. Steam. The deep heat of the world below. Whoever built these machines was tapping something. Geothermal, maybe. The Pillars go down far enough that the water should be scalding at the base. But here, at the level you occupy, the temperature is cool—a steady, clammy cool that never fluctuates, the kind that seeps into your clothes and your bones and stays there.

You walk for what might be a mile, crossing bridges that sway almost imperceptibly under your weight. The coral is strong, stronger than bone. The mist opens briefly and you see the true scale of the place. The Pillars do not form a grid; they form a spiral. The entire structure—cylinders, bridges, chasms—rotates around a central void that is even darker than the rest of the darkness. Looking into it, you feel the first genuine vertigo. That is the true deep. That is where the water goes, eventually, after its long fall. That is the drain of the world.

The turbulence, the dynamic chaos of the place, is not in the structures. The structures are eternal. The turbulence is in the water, the vapor, the life, the noise. It is a system in constant churn, a bazaar of biological and hydrological exchange running at full throttle, forever. The water cycle here is not a cycle; it is a single continuous downward rush, driven by some mechanism you cannot see. The biology feeds on the rush. The machines, wherever they are and whatever they do, are patient within it.

And then you see it: a single figure, humanoid, on a bridge two spans over. It is not moving toward you. It is not moving away. It is maintaining one of the engines, its hands moving with the steady, unsleeping efficiency of something that has been doing this work for a very, very long time. It does not look up.

You call out. The ringing eats your voice.

The figure finishes its task, wipes its hands on a rag, and walks into the mist. Faintly, through the soles of your feet, you feel a new vibration join the chorus. The engine has started. A low, rhythmic chuffing. What it powers, what it regulates, what catastrophe it prevents—you do not know. But the place continues. It has always continued. It will continue until the last Pillar erodes to a filament and snaps, and that eventuality is so far in the future that the fungi will have evolved into philosophers by then.

You find a place to sit, your back against a warm pipe that emerges from the coral like a thought. The vapor streams past. The mantas feed. The Pillars ring. The deep drain turns. And the world, this small pocket of normal weirdness, this industrial-organic-ancient-lush-turbulent submerged cathedral, keeps its steady, deafening, alive equilibrium, asking nothing of you, offering nothing, simply being, fully and finally itself.
