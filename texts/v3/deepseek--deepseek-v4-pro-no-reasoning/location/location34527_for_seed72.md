The air hits you first—thick as wet wool, hot enough to feel like a second skin you never asked for. It carries a mineral tang, something metallic and alive, and underneath that, the sweet-rot smell of water that never quite drains away.

You are below. That's the first certainty. The ceiling presses down at maybe thirty feet, rough-hewn but visibly worked—the marks of drills and cutters still fresh in the stone, no time-worn smoothing, no patina of ages. This place is *new*. The walls still weep with the violence of their own making, dark streaks of groundwater tracing hieroglyphs down gray rock that glistens in the low light. Puddles gather in the uneven floor, shallow but everywhere, reflecting the dim glow of sodium lamps strung along a main conduit. The water table lives just beneath your feet; with every step, the stone squelches, and small pools ripple outward, disturbed by your weight. In some corners, water doesn't just seep—it drips in a steady, percussive rhythm, a thousand tiny hammers working in the near-dark, condensing on warm pipes overhead and falling back to the floor in a cycle too patient to measure.

This is a settlement in the making—or perhaps a settlement that will never quite finish becoming. The "urban" character is there in the bones of it: a grid of main tunnels and side passages, the skeleton of a town laid out by an engineer's logic. But it is sparse, cavernous, a city where every structure has too much breathing room. A machine shop occupies one broad alcove, its lathes and presses bolted to the still-rough floor, their shapes hulking and mammalian in the dimness. Further down, a row of prefabricated housing units sits silent, only two of them glowing from within. Between these islands of intention, there is just space—raw, wet, cathedral-scale emptiness where the ceiling drips and the walls run with moisture. The infrastructure is industrial but not yet dense; power cables and ventilation ducts run exposed along the ceiling, branching at junctions with a logic you can almost read, a circuitry that promises future growth but currently leads mostly to shadow.

The heat pours from a geothermal plant at the settlement's heart—a squat, thrumming thing of pipe and valve and heat-exchanger, its metal skin too hot to touch, painted in warning stripes that are already peeling from the humidity. It vents excess steam through a series of louvers high in the ceiling, where it condenses instantly and falls back as a localized rain over the central junction. This is the loudest place: the plant's turbines whine at a frequency that lives in your teeth, a constant industrial hymn backed by the syncopated drips and the distant, rhythmic clang of a pump somewhere in the branching dark.

And everywhere, the *residents*.

They arrived before the walls were dry—or perhaps the construction disturbed them, unearthed them, called them up from some deeper, hotter dark. They are small, beetle-like things, no larger than a thumbnail, but their numbers are a biblical plague rendered in chitin. Their shells are a dark, iridescent brown that shifts to oil-slick green when they pass under a lamp. They carpet the floors in drifts that part around your boots like slow, viscous water, then close again behind you. They scale the weeping walls in columns, layer upon layer, their combined weight too great for some, creating tiny avalanches of bodies that patter down into the puddles with a sound like rain on a tin roof. The air is thick with a low, constant susurrus, the sound of billions of tiny legs scraping against stone, billions of wing-cases rubbing together in a ceaseless, dry shiver that somehow registers *above* the turbine whine. They are drawn to the heat, to the moisture, to the faint electrical hum of the lamps. They cluster thickest on the geothermal plant's housing, forming a living, moving insulation that must be swept clear every few hours by a worker with a push-broom, only to reform within minutes. This is a slow war, a patient tide against which the human presence is a temporary and largely ignored obstacle. They do not bite, they do not sting, they simply *are*, in numbers that make the notion of the individual absurd. They are the ecology. They are the atmosphere given form.

A tram-line runs the length of the main concourse, its rails slick with condensation and crawling with the beetles, their bodies crunching softly under the wheels of a flatbed car that moves at a walking pace. It carries a load of fresh-cut stone from the face of the excavation, a slow, patient delivery system that requires no great speed because the distance is small and the cycle is unending. The workers you see are few, their movements unhurried, their faces sheened with sweat and the ever-present damp. They speak in low voices that do not carry, as if the dense air swallows sound. Nothing here is frantic. The place is coming into being at the pace of the drip, the creep of the beetles, the slow, mechanical breath of the ventilator shafts. The gradients are weak—heat bleeding from the core, moisture pushing in from the surrounding rock, life pressing up against the new void.

This is a landscape beneath the world, a humid, industrial, half-finished sanctuary where the walls have not yet stopped shedding the trauma of their birth, where the machinery is new but already glazed with condensation, and where life—single-minded, overwhelming, indifferent—has already moved in and made itself at home. It is embryonic. It is under construction. And the slow, loud, wet darkness is already being colonized.
