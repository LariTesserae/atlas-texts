It is not a city the way a living person would say the word, but it is a city. It has the shape of one: the held breath of streets, the leaning memory of walls, the way space organizes itself around absent movement. It stretches across a low country that drinks more water than the sky can carry, and it has been here long enough that the ground has forgotten how to be anything else.

You come upon it from the wet side, where the land gives up its edge and becomes a marsh without ceremony. The water is not a lake or a flood; it is a condition. It moves in sheets no thicker than a hand, then gathers into channels, then spreads again. The surface is warm. In the dark, it reads more by touch than by sight — your feet meet it, then your shins, then the hem of your awareness. It never gets much deeper than the knee, but it never ends either. The water is blood-warm, silty, and entirely still, except where it isn’t. Small currents turn over themselves for reasons you cannot see. The air above it is dense with wet; it clings to skin like a second weather. There is no rain falling, but everything is post-rain. The world has the glassy, saturated feel of a place that has just finished weeping and might start again without announcement.

The city begins as a low ridge of stone, almost buried in the flatness. At first, you think it is a natural outcrop, a long spine of sedimentary rock, its strata facing the marsh like the pages of a closed book. But then you see the joins. The blocks are huge — too precise for quake or river to have made — each one the size of a house, laid without mortar, their faces worn to the same softness as the bedrock beneath them. They have been cut, once, but the cutting has been weather-beaten into suggestion. Edges have rounded. Corners have dissolved. The city is not ruined so much as it is exhausted into shape. It has the look of a place that has been erased by its own persistence.

Low, single-story structures spread from the ridge in a loose grid. Their walls are quarried from the same dark stone, and in the starless heat they hold the day’s warmth like breath in a closed mouth. Roofs are long gone, if there ever were roofs. Some buildings are no more than knee-high outlines; others stand shoulder-deep, corridors of unit walls marching out into the marsh and vanishing. The streets between them are not paved but have been compacted into a hard clay, now submerged in places, now glossy and exposed. The water runs through the city in a slow, deliberate way, as if it too remembers the old channel patterns. Where it pools, the buildings seem to float, their reflections so soft and black they double the world without repeating it cleanly. In the dark you can mistake reflection for depth, or depth for reflected sky. The line keeps shifting.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. It keeps to the edges, to the places where mineral and water meet without competition: filaments of rust-colored algae tracing the waterline on the stone; a few thin, heat-shriveled reeds growing in cracks where silt has gathered; a muscular, low-sprawling vine with leaves the color of dried blood, spreading across the base of a collapsed colonnade. Nothing grows in the open. The heat sees to that. It is the kind of heat that does not press down from the sun — there is no sun here, not now — but rises from the earth and water as if the land itself were running a fever. Steam, no, not steam exactly: a vapor too thin to see but too present to ignore, a permanent exhalation from every wet surface. Your own breathing becomes part of it.

The air smells of mineral and soil, of slow organic decay held in warm suspension, of water that has been moving through stone for a very long time. But not of life. There is no animal life. No birds disturb the surfaces of the pools. No insects stitch the dark with sound. No frogs. No fish stirring the silt. The absence is absolute and very old. This place was never quiet because something stopped; it is quiet the way a sealed room is quiet, the way a bell without a clapper is quiet. The only sound is your own movement, and even that seems to be absorbed, swallowed by the water and the thick air. When you stop, the silence is complete. Not restful. Complete.

Then, after a while, you notice that the city is not as still as it first seemed.

It moves, but not like anything alive. It is more that the space is having difficulty holding a single shape. The walls, when you look at them directly, are walls. When you let your attention slide, they are sometimes at a slightly different angle. A street that opened to the right now opens to the left, or perhaps it never did, or perhaps both are true in the way that a word can mean two things. The water surface does not obey a single horizon. In one direction it mirrors the low sky; in another it reflects a sky with a lighter patch, a pale, diffuse glow that is not present when you look up. Step, look down, look up, look down. The water remembers a sky the sky does not have.

There are places where the city has decayed not into ruin but into something less categorical. A cornerstone that is half stone, half dark air. A set of steps that ascends one, two, then stops, though your calf muscles feel the third and fourth as you lift your foot. A row of walls whose shadows point away from every possible light source, not in a manner that would be missed as a trick, but as a blunt, physical fact. You hold your hand near one wall and feel no heat, no cold, not even the pressure of nearness. A few inches to the left, the wall is warm. You do not investigate further. The city has entered its own unreality; it has been here so long that it has started to forget itself, and its forgetting is contagious.

And yet, the town is not hostile. It does not feel like a trap or a warning. It has none of the malevolence of a place that wants something from you. It is simply no longer loyal to the conditions of a single world. The grid of streets extends past the limits of the marsh and into the low dark in all directions, and some of those streets exist in a way that is more remembered than present. They are there, but their thereness is thin, as if they persist mainly because the stones once agreed to and have not formally been released. When you walk through one of these streets, the splash of your steps comes half a second late, or from a few feet away, or not at all. The silence does not object. It simply does not include you.

At the edge of the inhabited center of the city, where the ridge opens into a broad, flat courtyard, the water is deeper — hip-high in the middle — and hot. Not warm: hot, like a bath forgotten on a stove. It steams now, visibly, in ribbons that uncoil from the surface. The stone platform beneath is carved with channels too regular for drainage; they form spirals, concentric rings, lines that intersect at angles that do not quite sum. Not writing, unless writing can be wholly illegible and still be writing. Here the reflection problem is worst. The water is too still and too black to see into, and the stars are not above, there are no stars, but they are below, tiny white points scattered in the water like a field of drowned lights. They do not twinkle. They are not moving. A few of them align into shapes. If you look at them for too long, the shapes align into the ghost of the city behind you. You turn. The city behind you is exactly where it was. But the reflection showed it closer.

The heat is beginning to condense on your skin. You cannot tell if you are sweating or if the air is simply handing the marsh back to you, drop by drop. The water is no longer a thing you are in; it is a thing that has accepted you as an extension of itself. There is no sound except your pulse, which is perhaps not yours, but the slow, tidal pulse of the warm channels moving through the city’s stone. It might be the city’s pulse. It might be the marsh’s. It might be nothing. The place does not care to distinguish.

In the far distance, beyond the running streets, there is a low ripple of hills, barely higher than the town, folded into the dark like the knees of a sleeping animal. They are the only horizon. Everything else is flat, wet, dark, warm, built, and quietly undone. A city that has outlived its own rules. A swamp that has risen to meet it. A darkness that behaves less like the absence of light and more like a material, a warm black sludge of atmosphere that pools in the low places and leaks into the corners of the eye. There are no animals, no machines, no fire, no wind. The only dynamic is the long evolutionary pulse of stone and water, reshaping each other with the patience of a process that has forgotten its beginning and does not expect an end.

You do not understand it. But understanding does not seem to be a currency here. The place accepts bewilderment the way dry earth accepts rain. It soaks it in. It spreads it thin. It lets it sit on the surface for a moment, then takes it down into the dark, ancient channels below.

You have been standing in one place for a very long time. The water has not moved. The city has. Slowly. Almost not at all. But enough. When you look down, the reflection is no longer of you. It is of a different person, standing in a slightly different posture. They are also looking down. They are seeing, perhaps, nothing at all. Or you. The light is still gone. The heat is still rising. The place persists, active and silent and wet and endlessly itself, at the very edge of what a world is allowed to be.
