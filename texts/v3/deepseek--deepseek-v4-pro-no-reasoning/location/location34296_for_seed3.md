The city is only half-built, and already it is erasing the mountain.

You come upon it from below, climbing through the thinning fog, and the first thing you notice is how the road changes underfoot—from packed scree to something harder, something laid in deliberate slabs that tilt and crack where the slope beneath them has shifted. The mountain does not want this place. The mountain is still raw, still arriving, limestone spines pushing upward through thin soil at all angles, as if the ridgeline is a thought still forming. The elevation makes itself known in your lungs and in the slow, patient ache behind your eyes. This high up, the air is a constant cool pressure, not cold enough to sting but thin enough to remind you that you are borrowing it.

Water. It is never quite raining and never quite dry. The world exists in that in-between state where moisture condenses on every surface without ever falling as distinct drops. Cobblestones glisten. Iron railings weep. The fur of the animals—and there are so many animals—holds a permanent dampness, beading on guard hairs, darkening the ruffs around their necks. The city has built gutters and channels, elaborate stone troughs that run beside the streets, but they carry only a trickle, and sometimes nothing at all. The water here is incipient, like everything else. It hasn’t decided whether to be a river or a mist. It lingers in hollows and niches, collects in the cupped palms of unfinished statues, drips from the eaves of buildings that have not yet acquired roofs.

The architecture is dense where it exists—a compressed warren of brick tenements and factory shells climbing over each other in the narrow defiles and ledges of the mountainside—but the settlement has not filled in its own boundaries. There are gaps. Whole blocks laid out in foundation stones and then abandoned, the grid of streets running suddenly into scree slopes where saplings have taken hold. You can walk from a working brass foundry, its windows pulsing orange, its steam whistle blowing shift changes, directly into a meadow of alpine flowers, their colors almost violently bright against the gray stone. The city is sparse in this way: not empty, but scattered, pockets of intense construction separated by the raw and waiting mountain. The scale is open. From any high street you can see the valley floor below, still half-lost in cloud, and the next ridgeline beyond it, and sometimes the distant glint of another settlement being assembled on another peak.

The factories came first. That is what the iron plaques at the city gates claim, though the dates on them shimmer and change when you look too long. They are not factories in the lowland sense—these are vertical industries, their floors connected by external elevators that climb the cliff faces in cages of riveted steel. Smokestacks rise not from horizontal ground but from terraces cut into the rock, and their plumes join the fog so that you cannot tell what is exhaust and what is weather. The sound is a perpetual industrial hum, a low-frequency vibration that travels through the stone and up through the soles of your feet. Piston-driven pumps extract something from deep inside the mountain—not ore, exactly, or if it is ore it is a substance that does not behave like matter should. Occasionally one of the pumps seizes, and the whole mountainside shudders, and then the sound changes pitch and becomes, for a few seconds, something that almost resembles speech.

The animals.

They are the first true shock of the place. The mountain sheep do not flee from you. They stand on the half-built parapets and watch you pass with their strange horizontal pupils, their breath pluming in the cool air. Their wool is thicker than it should be, matted with the constant damp, and some of them have grown fleece in colors that sheep should not produce—a deep rust-red, a pale blue-gray like the sky before a storm. They have learned the factory whistles. When the foundry shift changes, they move in a slow, deliberate migration from the upper slopes down to the workers' canteen, where the cooks throw out vegetable scraps. They are not pets. They are not quite wild. They are something that has adapted to the halfway state of this place, citizens of a city that does not yet fully exist.

There are birds, too, nesting in the girders of the half-finished cathedral, their songs incorporating fragments of machinery noise—the ratchet of a winch, the hiss of steam release. And something else moves in the fog, larger than a sheep, too tall for a bear. You do not see it clearly, only the shifting of its shape against the paler gray of the mist, but the streetlamps flicker when it passes, and the cobbles under your feet grow warm for a moment, then cold again.

The weirdness here is not hidden. It is not a secret.

The city is being built according to plans that change themselves. You can see it in the buildings: a wall that begins as brick and becomes, halfway along its length, a seamless extrusion of the native limestone, as if the mountain simply decided to continue what the masons had started. Windows that open onto views that cannot be from this ridgeline—a sea, a desert, a sky with two moons. The street signs are in a language you almost recognize, and if you stare at them long enough the letters rearrange themselves into words you understand, but the meaning shifts each time you look away and back. The reality breakdown is not violent. It is slow, gradual, as patient as the fog. The mountain is dreaming the city, and the city is dreaming itself, and the two dreams do not quite agree on the details.

There are people. Of course there are people. They walk the streets in oil-stained overalls and thick wool coats, their faces lined by the high-altitude sun on the rare days when the clouds part. They do not seem alarmed by the animals, or by the buildings that grow without builders, or by the way the cobblestones sometimes ripple underfoot like the surface of a slow-moving river. They are building this place, or they believe they are building it, and perhaps both things are true at once. They tip their caps to you as you pass. They say good morning or good evening regardless of the hour, because the light here is always dim, always the same diffuse gray glow, and time is one of the things that has not fully arrived.

The light. It comes from everywhere and nowhere—the cloud layer diffuses it into a sourceless luminescence that casts no shadows. This is not darkness. It is something else, a permanent twilight that lives in the middle distance, a light that makes everything look like a photograph developing slowly in a chemical bath. The factory windows glow their own orange. The streetlamps hum with electric filaments, but their light seems to stop at the edge of their glass globes, unable to push further into the pervasive gray.

And the sound. Beneath the industrial hum and the steam whistles and the bleating of the sheep, there is a deeper sound, a subsonic thrum that might be the mountain's own resonance. The pumps are pulling something up from below, and the something is vast, and it does not want to be pulled. Or it does want to be pulled, and it is singing, and the song registers not as music but as the slow bending of physical law. When the wind blows from the east, the sound grows louder, and the workers pause in their labor and tilt their heads, listening, and do not speak until it passes.

The vegetation is a surprise. At this elevation, with this much industry, you would expect lichen and not much else. But the mountain is in a mood to be generous. Mosses in a dozen shades of green carpet every north-facing wall and every damp crevice. Ferns uncurl from cracks in the factory foundations. In the gaps between buildings, where the city has not yet decided what it will become, alpine meadows flourish with a density that seems impossible—flowers packing their blooms together, root systems tangling in the thin soil, a profusion of life that treats the cold and the fog as a gift rather than a hardship. The lushness is not tropical; it is the lushness of a place that has learned to concentrate its growing into the brief, cool summer with almost desperate intensity. Every leaf is glazed with moisture. Every stem bends under the weight of condensation. The greenery is so vivid it hurts the eyes, a color that feels like a sound.

The erosion, what there is of it, is not the patient wearing-away of water and wind. The mountain is too young for that. The rocks are sharp-edged, freshly fractured, their surfaces not yet rounded by the passage of time. When a cliff face collapses—and they do collapse, sometimes, a deep shuddering rumble that sets all the animals to lowing—the freshly exposed stone gleams with crystal facets that have never seen light before. The city's builders have learned to work with this, or around it. Some of the buildings are cabled directly into the rock face, suspended in a way that allows the mountain to adjust itself without taking the structures down. Other buildings have been rebuilt so many times that they are now hybrid things, stone and steel and something organic that might be root or might be mineral deposit, a slow-grown architecture that blurs the line between construction and geology.

The dynamic of this place is not static, but its change is almost imperceptible from moment to moment. The fog thickens over the course of a morning, not minute by minute but with a gradual accumulation that you only notice when you realize you can no longer see the next ridgeline. The factories increase their output over weeks and months, the hum deepening, the steam plumes thickening. The city is growing, but slowly, stone by quarried stone, beam by forged beam. The mountain is rising—imperceptibly, a fraction of a millimeter per year, but rising nonetheless, pushing the whole half-built metropolis toward a sky that receives it with the same gray patience.

A place between states. That is what it feels like, standing on a half-finished street, breathing air that is half mist and half smoke, watching animals that are half wild and half civic. The city is emerging from the mountain the way the mountain is emerging from the earth, a slow extrusion of intention and material, and the boundary between the two is not a line but a gradient. Here, a brick wall. Here, the same wall becoming limestone. Here, the limestone opening into a cave that contains a working furnace. Here, the furnace casting shadows that move independently of the flames that should produce them.

The mountain is dreaming. The city is building. Neither has finished yet. And somewhere in the fog, a sheep with indigo fleece stands on the balustrade of an incomplete bridge and watches a procession of workers descend into the mine, their helmet lamps bobbing in the dimness, swallowed one by one by the mountain's ongoing and unfinished mouth.
