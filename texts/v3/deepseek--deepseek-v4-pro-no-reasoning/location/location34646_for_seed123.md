The crawlspace beneath the pin factory is barely four feet high, and you have to crouch with your back pressed against the warm concrete slab overhead, your knees nearly touching the damp earth below. The warmth comes from above—from the great steam-driven camshafts that run the length of the factory floor, their vibrations passing down through the concrete like a low, continuous heartbeat. It is never cold here. Even in winter, when the river outside slushes with grey ice, this pocket of earth holds steady at the temperature of a living body.

The light is not what you would expect. It is normal light, the same quality of daylight that falls through a window on an overcast afternoon, but it has no single source. It seems to emanate from the packed soil itself, a diffuse gray-gold glow that leaves no shadows. You can see everything, and everything you see is close. The space is dense with objects, saturated, every reach of ground occupied by something that hums or clicks or slowly rotates.

The moisture comes in cycles. Three times a day, a valve opens somewhere in the piping overhead and a fine mist descends—condensation from the steam system, released in a soft sigh. It settles on everything: the brass casings, the copper wire, the fungal blooms, the carapaces. Then the warmth bakes it off again, leaving a faint mineral crust on the surfaces. The air smells of hot metal and wet wool and something organic, something like the smell of a freshly split log.

The structures are scattered but deliberate. Here, a miniature railway track curves around a support pillar, its gauge no wider than your thumb. There, a low wall of soldered tin cans forms a semicircular enclosure, within which something glitters and rearranges itself. A single ceramic insulator—the kind that once capped a telegraph pole—has been repurposed as a water tower, and it fills and empties on a schedule you haven't yet deciphered. These are not ruins; they are maintained. Worn components are replaced. Joints are re-soldered. The marks of small tools are visible everywhere, and the tools themselves hang from hooks driven into the concrete above.

The fauna is abundant and strange, and it is the fauna that maintains the machines.

You saw your first pin-beetle within minutes of lowering yourself through the service hatch. It was the size of a fist, its carapace the color of tarnished silver, and it was delicately tapping a tiny hammer against a misaligned gear tooth. It paused when your shadow fell across it, antennae waving in your direction, and then it resumed its work with the focused indifference of a creature that has never known a predator. There are hundreds of them down here. Thousands. They fill the crawlspace like a living floor, packed so densely that you cannot take a step without them parting around your boots and flowing back together behind you. The sound of their industry is a constant soft tick-tick-ticking, a quiet chorus of tiny impacts, and beneath that, a deeper hum: the vibration of the factory above, yes, but also the hum of beetles, their wing cases vibrating at a frequency you feel in your teeth.

They are not the only things that live here.

Flat, pale creatures like living circuit boards—you've taken to calling them skimmerworms—glide across the damp soil on undulating cilia, their bodies etched with conductive traces that spark faintly when they pass over the copper rails. They seem to be the information carriers. When one of them touches a machine, the machine's rhythm changes. A pin-beetle will stop its work, wait for the skimmerworm to move on, and then adjust its tapping to a new cadence.

There are wetter things, too. In the low spots where the mist collects and doesn't fully dry, a kind of slime mold grows in branching, dendritic patterns. It is the color of verdigris, and it pulses very slowly, about once every three minutes, sending a wave of contraction from its center to its outermost tips. The pin-beetles harvest it with their mandibles, carrying globs of it to machines that seem to run on some chemical reaction you don't understand. The slime mold regrows, fed by the condensation and the mineral dust and the bodies of dead beetles, which are never left to decay on their own.

Death here is a process, not an end.

When a pin-beetle ceases to function, its body is dismantled with extraordinary speed. Other beetles converge on it, each one taking a specific part—a leg joint, an antenna, a segment of carapace—and carrying it away to a different location. Nothing is wasted. The soft tissues go to the slime mold. The hard parts are sorted by material and stored in caches you've found tucked behind the support pillars. The beetles build their machines from the bodies of their dead.

You've been here for three hours now, crouched in this warm, damp, packed earth beneath the factory floor, and you are beginning to understand that this is not an infestation. The beetles were here first. The factory was built above them, and they simply... continued. They wove the factory's vibrations into their own rhythms. They tapped into the steam pipes for warmth and moisture. They learned to use the discarded industrial waste—the bent pins, the tin scraps, the broken insulators—as raw material for their own constructions. The line between ecosystem and factory is not blurred here; it was never drawn.

A skimmerworm glides over your boot. You feel a faint tingle through the leather. It pauses, as if reading you, and then continues on toward a cluster of machines that are clicking in a rhythm you now recognize as a kind of counting. The pin-beetles around you have long since accepted your presence. One is sitting on your knee, methodically cleaning its antennae with its forelegs, and you find that you do not want to disturb it.

The light has not changed. It will not change. There is no day or night here, only the cycles of mist and warmth, industry and decay, birth and dismantling. A world in a crawlspace. A pocket of dense, warm, damp, living machinery, hidden beneath a building whose workers have no idea what ticks beneath their feet.
