The first thing that comes is the sound of your own breathing. Not loud, but present in a way it never is elsewhere, because here there is nothing to compete with it. The air is thick with humidity, warm as a mouth, and it swallows every other noise—the drip of water, the creak of fiber, the distant, felt-more-than-heard thrum of wings.

You are standing on a bridge that is not a bridge, but a root. It spans a gap you cannot see the bottom of, though you know the bottom is there because sometimes, when the mists shift, you glimpse the distant glint of water, miles down. The root is wide enough for ten people to walk abreast, its surface worn smooth and slightly concave by the passage of countless feet over countless years. It is a made thing, shaped and braided from thousands of smaller fibers, and it joins the city behind you to the city ahead, both of which are also the plant.

This is not a forest that has been built in. This is a single organism, or perhaps a single system so densely interwoven that the distinction has ceased to matter. The world-tree, the great weave, the breathing scaffold—its name changes from terrace to terrace, generation to generation. It is a vertical continent. The main stems, some of them a mile in diameter, rise from the abyssal wet into the dim gold of the upper atmosphere. The canopy, if it exists, is an abstraction, a ceiling of light-scattered cloud so far above that no one living has seen it. You were born somewhere in the middle strata, in the warm belt where respiration is easiest, and where the constant exudation of water from the leaves keeps everything slick and gleaming.

All around you, the structure of the plant is also the architecture of the world. The outer bark has been peeled and re-layered into sloping walls. Chambers have been encouraged to grow as hollows, then lined with beaten fiber-felt to make them watertight. Windows are not cut; they are trained, living apertures ringed with contractile tissue that open and close with the humidity cycle. Light comes from the plant itself—not the harsh, actinic light of a sun, but a diffuse, internal glow. The vascular fluids that carry warmth and sugar from the depths also carry phosphorescent symbionts, so that the main thoroughfares pulse with a faint, sap-green luminescence. In the dwellings, hand-pressed cultures of these symbionts are smeared onto walls in patterns, giving a dim, domestic light, enough to read the knots in a message cord, enough to see a face by.

It is warm here, always. The plant generates its own heat, a metabolic furnace that keeps the inner stems at a constant, sweat-beading temperature, and the air is so saturated that every surface weeps. The path you walk is cushioned by a living mat of secondary growth, a napped surface of tiny, water-absorbing filaments that release a clean, fungal scent when you step on them. All of it is tended. There is no natural substrate in this world, only the made and the grown, and the distinction is one of intent, not material. The dwellers work with hand-spun cords, with flaked obsidian and ground-bone chisels, with the patient, generational craft of guiding a living thing into the shape of a home. A ladder is a sequence of trained vines, strong as hemp, each rung a grafted join. A reservoir is a modified bladder-leaf, capable of holding a family's water for a month, its lip hardened with a paste of mineral dust and sap.

And yet, for all this construction, the overwhelming sensation is one of emptiness. The scale is planetary, and the population is not. You can travel a day along a spiraling gallery and see no one, only the silent, slow-moving shapes of the browsers. The space between presences is vast. A family compound is a cluster of chambers in a stem wall, and the next such compound is a morning's walk away, across bridges like the one you stand on now, suspended in the humid void. The air is not empty—it is thick with spores, with drifting seedlings, with the silken lines of larval caterpillars—but it feels empty because it is so profoundly quiet. The sound of the place is a near-silence composed of water sounds: the drip, the seep, the occasional, startlingly loud *plink* of a water-drop falling from a high leaf onto a taut-stretched fiber-sheet far below.

The weirdness is not something you notice much anymore, though the old stories call it the time of the breakdown, when the boundaries between things grew thin. It is simply the way the world is. Sometimes, the galleries do not lead where they led the day before. Sometimes, the faint, green light in the vascular tissue forms itself into patterns that are almost letters, or almost faces, before dissolving. The browsers—creatures of leaf and chitin, herd animals the size of dogs that feed on the constant yeasts—move with a purpose that feels directed, though no herder walks with them. And the whispers, the almost-sounds on the edge of hearing, are to you simply the voice of the plant, a natural phenomenon. You are a child of the reality breakdown. It is your normal.

The air cools almost imperceptibly as you move toward the outer edge of the stem. This is a node, a place where several major limbs diverge, creating a cavernous, domed space large enough to have its own weather. A perpetual mist hangs in the center, a cloud that rains softly onto a managed garden of food-ferns below. You see the figures of the tenders moving among the fronds, small and dark, their movements slow and rhythmic. Above them, on a high gallery, a spinner works at her frame, twisting fibers from the constant fall of airborne silk into a new cord. The *whirr-click* of her weighted spindle is the loudest sound in this entire vast chamber, and it does not even carry to where you stand. It is a private sound, for her alone.

You breathe in. The air tastes of rain, of growth, of the faint, copper tang of the plant’s deeper fluids. You breathe out, and the sound of it is swallowed by the great, soft, waiting silence of the high, warm, wet, empty, constructed, and endlessly growing world. You continue across the root-bridge, toward the small, dim glow of a doorway, home.
