The air here never really dries. It hangs cold and close, a constant seep of moisture that beads on every surface without ever gathering the will to fall as proper rain. You are high—impossibly, geologically freshly high—on a plateau so young the stone still remembers the violence of its birth.

This is not a single rock but a congregation. The plateau is a shattered mosaic of basalt columns, each one a dark, angular presence, packed so tightly together that they appear to be a single continuous surface from a distance, but up close you can slide a hand, a forearm, maybe your whole body into the narrow gaps between them. These are the "streets." The city is woven into the cracks.

Vertical shafts plunge down between the columns, some only an arm's span wide, others broad enough for a thin person to descend. The walls are not smooth; they are faceted, hexagonal in cross-section, the natural jointing of cooling lava now serving as the load-bearing skeleton for a human termite mound. Hand- and foot-holds have been patiently pecked into the damp stone over generations, each notch polished dark by the grip of countless climbers. The columns themselves, each a tower block, are perforated with windows and doorways carved into their vertical faces, rooms scooped out of the dense, fine-grained rock. Bridges of braided rawhide and lengths of black, twisted driftwood span the chasms at various levels, sagging under their own weight and the ever-present condensation, dripping a slow, metronomic water into the depths below.

Below. That is where the river is. You cannot see it from most points on the surface, but you can always sense it. The sound is not a roar but a pervasive, low-frequency hum, a vibration that travels up through the soles of your feet and the palm you press to the stone. It is the world’s bass note. In the deepest crevasses, where the light never reaches, a fine, cold mist rises from the unseen current, carrying the smell of wet minerals and a faint, fishy musk. This is the water's true domain, a hidden, riverine world that exists in negative space.

Up on the plateau’s top, it is a different plane. The surface of the columns, roughly level overall, is a vast, dimly lit expanse. The sky is a heavy lid of stratus, featureless and silver-grey, the kind of light that flattens shadows and makes distance ambiguous. It is a landscape of intimate objects in a colossal room. Vegetation is sparse but deliberate—every plant is a presence. Tussocks of wiry, dark-adapted grass sprout from cracks, their roots finding purchase in the accumulated dust of lichen and stone. There are shrubs that look like twisted, miniature trees, their bark a scaly, graphite grey, their leaves reduced to needles or fleshy scales the color of jade, each one beaded with a tiny, perfect sphere of water. They don’t form a canopy, just a scattering of individual, patient entities, islands in a stone ocean.

And the fauna. It is abundant, a slow churn of life in the cold.

On the surfaces, dark, lichen-like growths are not lichen at all but colonies of something animal. They are broad, flat mats of interlocked bodies, a finger’s length across, each individual a soft, chitinous oval fringed with cilia. They move with the speed of a growing shadow, a near-invisible creep, rasping moisture and algal film from the wet stone. When you step too close, the cilia freeze in a rippling, silent wave of alarm that propagates across the whole colony, turning a seemingly dead crust into a momentarily bristling field of alertness.

The spaces between columns are highways. Small, feathered things—more fur than feather, really, with four wings and a serpentine tail—dart and glide in the permanent twilight of the crevasses, never emerging onto the brighter plateau. Their cries are clicks, not songs, a steady, quiet sonar-chatter that echoes up from the dark. They hunt the mist moths, pale, silent insects that rise in spiraling columns from the spray zone, their wings leaving ghostly, phosphorescent trails in the absolute dark near the water. That is the only light from below, a faint, swirling bioluminescence too dim to see unless you are down in it.

The humans here are part of the density. The city—and it is a city, a vast, layered settlement organized across miles of this stone field—operates on a logic of proximity. Walls between dwellings are mere hands-breadths of basalt. A family’s home is a series of connected chambers, one per column, linked by those precarious hide bridges. Their industry is quiet and precise. You might see a woman sitting in a doorway carved thirty feet up a column, her legs dangling over the void, chipping patiently at a blade of volcanic glass using a deer-antler pressure flaker. The sound is a sharp, rhythmic tick-tick-tick that dies instantly in the damp air. Elsewhere, a group might be pulling a net up from a deep fissure, a communal effort using braided lines, the net bulging with pale, blind fish. Their tools are stone, bone, wood, and sinew. Their only metal is the occasional nugget of meteoric iron, cold-forged over years into a chisel or knife, an heirloom worth more than a life.

The world is one of patient cycles. The river rises and falls with a season governed not by the sun, which is a mere suggestion behind the perpetual cloud, but by the slow freezing and melting of a distant headwall glacier. Fog, dense and damp, is a weather system unto itself, forming a localized cloud layer that can blanket the plateau for days, reducing visibility to an arm's length and saturating every surface with runnels of condensation. This is the harvestable moisture, channeled by carved grooves into rock-cut cisterns. The soundscape is mostly quiet—the low thrum of the river, the tick-tick of knapping, the occasional call of a child echoing across the chasm, the whisper of wings. But sometimes, the vast scale of the plateau reminds itself to you. A cold wind will suddenly funnel down the long, straight corridors between the columns, a river of air that sets the bridges swaying and pulls a deep, resonant organ note from the rims of the deeper fissures. The sound is the voice of the stone itself, a thing so low and vast you feel it in your chest before you hear it with your ears, a sound that belongs to a geography of planetary scale, a single, slow breath of a landscape that has only just begun.
