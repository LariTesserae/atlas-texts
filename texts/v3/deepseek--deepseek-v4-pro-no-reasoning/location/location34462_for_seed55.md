The place knows no surface.

There is only depth, and the slow, cold drift of water that has been moving for longer than any mountain has stood. No wave-crash, no current-rip — only a vast, internal circulation so gradual that a single particle might take a thousand years to complete a circuit. The water itself is old, recycled through a billion quiet deaths, and it presses downward with the patient weight of a world entirely given over to liquid.

And yet this is not an ocean. It is a construct.

You perceive this in the regularity beneath you: a floor of close-fitted stone blocks, each the size of a house, laid in a hexagonal pattern that extends beyond the reach of any light. Their surfaces are not polished but roughened, pitted with age, every sharp edge worn to a soft roundness by water that has flowed over them since before the concept of erosion had a name. The joints between them are fine, barely a finger’s width, and from these seams rises the only vegetation this place has ever known.

It is not plant life in any sun-hungry sense. Long, ribbon-like growths — pale as old bone, some translucent, some faintly phosphorescent with a blue-green chill — stream upward from the seams in dense, slow-waving forests. Each strand is narrow, fingernail-wide, but they grow in thick tufts, thousands per square pace, so that the floor is furred with them. They drift in the weak internal currents, not reaching for light but for something else: the suspended particles of mineral and memory that sift down through the water column. They are filter-feeders, or perhaps depositors, or perhaps something closer to the breathing apparatus of the structure itself. They never tangle. They part and rejoin with a slowness that makes a single motion look like a held breath.

Above the forest, the water rises. And rises.

The scale is difficult to hold in the mind because there are no reference points — no surface above, no seabed below that is not fabricated. The floor is there, yes, but it extends sideways without boundary, and the water above it climbs into a darkness so complete that depth becomes theoretical. The only way to know distance is by the sound, which is a constant, low, almost sub-audible hum — the voice of water pressing against its own containment, or the groan of ancient seams settling further into their final equilibrium. Occasionally, a deeper tone sounds, a note so low it is felt rather than heard, a frequency that brings the cold into the bones. It passes, and the near-silence returns, layered with the faint, susurrous slide of the pale strands against one another.

There are no animals. No fish, no drifting zooplankton, no scuttling benthic things. The water is chemically simple, mineral-rich but sterile of the living complexity that would mark an ocean. The strands are not food for anything. They grow, they reach their length, they detach at the tip in tiny, flake-like fragments that drift upward and dissolve into the dark. The place feeds on itself, or on time, or on nothing at all.

And it is cold — not the biting cold of ice melt, but a settled, metabolic chill, as though the water has long since surrendered all its warmth to the stone and the stone to the void beyond. Temperature creeps in from the edges of the body, leaching heat without urgency. It is the cold of a place where no metabolism quickens, where no muscle has ever twitched. The water is a few degrees above freezing, held there by the sheer mass of itself and the faint, geothermal whisper of the deep blocks, which are — you sense, though you cannot see — fitted not onto planetary crust but onto something else entirely.

The built nature of this world is total. The hexagonal paving is not a floor laid over a natural basin; it *is* the basin, the substrate, the only solid thing. The walls, if they exist, are beyond perception, lost in a horizonless curve that suggests a sphere of incomprehensible diameter. Everything here was made, shaped, set in place by hands or tools or processes that belonged to a time so remote that the concept of "primitive" is an insult to their patience. The making is not crude — the hexagonal blocks are uniform, the seams precise — but the tools that made them were simple: stone on stone, abrasive mud, time as the primary shaping force. There is no metal here, no refined alloy, no trace of forge or furnace. Only the cold working of stone by stone, a craft that took an epoch to finish and has spent a hundred epochs since settling into its final, quiet self.

Light is not quite absent. The phosphorescence from the strands is minimal, a ghost-light that reveals only the nearest motions — a few arm's-lengths of slow-waving ribbons, the faint glint of a seam line. Beyond that, darkness. Not the darkness of a closed room, but the darkness of a volume so vast that light has no surface to return from. It is a darkness that has texture and pressure, a medium in itself, and it fills the eyes and the mind with the same patient, heavy emptiness.

The cycles here are so long that they barely register as change. The strands grow and release their tips in a rhythm tied to the hum-tone, which itself pulses at intervals that might mark years or centuries. The water temperature varies by fractions of a degree over millennia. The only true dynamic is the drift — that vast, circular migration of the water mass — and it is so slow that it appears as stillness. This is a place that has reached its oldest stable state, an equilibrium so deep that "erosion" has become a kind of perfection: every surface worn exactly as far as it will ever wear, no further shaping possible, no remaining energy to drive change. It is a landscape at the end of its becoming, and the end is long and quiet and forever.

The dense forest of pale strands sways. The low tone vibrates through the blocks, through the water, through the body. The darkness presses in, absolute and tender.

And the place continues. It has been doing so for longer than life has existed elsewhere, and it will continue until the blocks themselves dissolve into a finer arrangement, a softer order, a silence even deeper than this one.
