From below, it is a ceiling of roots and riveted iron.

The Hollowmarket occupies a single drowned cavern, bell-shaped and broad at its base, narrowing as it rises toward the surface some eighty feet overhead. No diver ever sees that surface from within—the water is too thick with silt and oil and living matter, a perpetual amber murk that drinks what little light exists. The cavern walls are limestone, ancient reef stone worn smooth by currents that no longer move, pocked with the boreholes of mollusks long extinct. But the stone is only the substrate. What has been built upon it, through it, over centuries, is the market itself.

Structures descend from the ceiling in a kind of inverted urbanism. Habitation pods, storage bladders, workshop spheres—each encased in stitched membrane or beaten copper or salvaged hull plate—hang from the roof by braided cables of gut and wire, by chains, by the descending trunks of cultivated whalefall trees. These trees are the architecture's skeleton. Their roots anchor in the limestone vault above, drawing unknown chemistries from cracks that reach toward a surface no one here remembers visiting, while their pale, blind branches drape downward, each one as thick as a body, bifurcating and bifurcating until the finest tendrils brush the cavern floor. The trees grow slowly. A new branch might take a generation to thicken enough to hang a dwelling from. The oldest ones, near the cavern's center, have girths woven into rope ladders and balconies, their bark scarred with generations of cleats and eye-bolts. In the dimness, they seem less like plants than the architecture of a body—some vast creature's circulatory system inverted and left hanging.

The water is warm. Not bath-warm, but blood-warm, a steady 1.7 heat that has no seasons. It rises from geothermal seeps in the cavern floor, mineral-rich exhalations that coil upward in visible shimmering columns where the temperatures briefly misalign. The warmth is what makes the market possible. It feeds the whalefall trees. It feeds the phosphorescent bacterial mats that paint every surface in a palette of blue-greens and rotting-amber, the primary light source here. The dimness—that 0.6—is not absence of light but its slow, particulate distribution. Nothing casts a shadow. Everything glows a little, from some angle, if you wait long enough for your eyes to adjust.

The density is almost unbearable. Every cubic foot of water is occupied by something—a hanging workshop, a net of cultured pearl-flesh, a tangle of pipes moving unknown fluids between one suspended structure and the next, a drifting vendor with bioluminescent lures dangling from their mask, a school of blind finger-fish that navigate by pressure-sense and weave through the market in slow, synchronized pulses. There is no open water, no plaza. The spaces between things are not empty but filled with the lesser things—minor commerce, decay, repair. The market is saturated. You move by pulling yourself along guide-ropes strung between the hanging pods, careful not to disturb the delicate symbioses that encrust every surface. Brushing against the wrong bacterial mat will stain your suit with a phosphorescence that takes days to fade. Brushing against the wrong vendor will cost more.

Sound is strange here. The water carries vibration faithfully, but the market's inhabitants have learned to be quiet. Sound propagates too well, and too much noise disturbs the cultivation—pearl-flesh is sensitive to pressure waves, the whalefall trees respond to resonance with defensive secretions, and some of the deeper-dwelling things that visit the market's lower edges are attracted by vibrations. So the dominant soundscape is a near-silence of small clicks, the whisper of ropes under tension, the faint chime of metal on metal from the workshops, the distant, subsonic hum of a geothermal vent cycling through its decade-long exhalation rhythm. Voices, when they come, are modulated through throat-mics and bone-conductors, directed and private. The market is quiet as a held breath. The silence is industrial—the hush of a factory floor between shifts, machines idling but not stopped, processes running their slow courses.

And it is industrial. Not in the sense of smokestacks and furnaces—fire has no purchase here—but in the sense of organized production. The workshops are small manufactories, each dedicated to a single process in a chain of production that spans the entire cavern. One pod cultures the pearl-flesh. Another extrudes the copper wire that another weaves into mesh. A fourth brews the nutrient slurries that feed the trees, using offal from the market's food stalls and the bodies of the dead—nothing is wasted. The largest structure, a bulbous copper sphere near the cavern's center, houses a slow-turning turbine that draws power from the thermal gradient between the vent-warmed deep water and the slightly cooler water near the ceiling. It turns once every forty seconds. It has been turning for two hundred years. Its current feeds the electrolysis tanks that crack water into breathable gasses, which bubble up through the market in silver streams, caught by inverted funnels and piped to habitation pods.

Fauna is present but sparse. This is not a reef; it is a machine, and most animal life has been excluded for efficiency. The finger-fish are tolerated because they eat the fungal blooms that might otherwise overwhelm the bacterial mats. A few domesticated forms of sea-slug are cultivated for their secretions, kept in mesh cages. Occasionally a visitor arrives in a hardened suit, carrying trade goods from the surface or from deeper, stranger places—they are rare, 1.3, and their presence causes subtle shifts in the market's pressure-dynamics as inhabitants adjust their schedules to accommodate the disturbance. The real fauna, if it can be called that, is the market itself. The whalefall trees are cultivated but not domesticated, and they have their own slow agendas. Their roots occasionally find new cracks, new chemistries, and the market's chemists spend generations decoding what the trees have begun to metabolize.

The strangeness—that 1.7—is not in any single element but in the logic of the whole. There is a cathedral scale to the cavern, but the packed density reduces it to a series of intimate chambers, a warren in the vast. The urban and the organic are not merely interwoven but have become a single category: the trees are load-bearing infrastructure, the copper conduits are trellises for bacterial agriculture, the dead feed the living in closed loops that have cycled for centuries. The slow dynamic means that change is barely perceptible within a single human lifetime. A new branch. A shifting vent. The gradual, inevitable drift of the turbine's bearings toward a failure that someone will notice, eventually, and correct. The technology is industrial but adapted to the liquid medium in ways that feel wrong to a surface mind—no combustion, no rapid motion, only chemistry and electricity and slow, patient mechanics.

Below the hanging structures, the cavern floor is not inhabited but worked. Mining machines, automatic and rust-caked, crawl along the sediment with a patience that matches the market's pulse. They extract the mineral-rich concretions that form around the vent mouths, grinding them to slurry that rises in pipes to feed the refineries above. They operate on pre-programmed circuits laid down centuries ago by someone now dead, someone whose great-great-grandchildren might still live in a copper pod thirty feet above. The machines make almost no sound—just a low, grinding vibration that is felt more than heard, a continuous presence like the hum of the turbine.

This is the Hollowmarket: a cathedral-space compressed to a thicket, a factory growing on trees that die slower than empires, a city breathing its own recycled exhalations in the warm, permanent dusk of a flooded cave. It has no boundaries except the cavern walls, but it knows its edges intimately. Every rope has a terminus. Every chain supports exactly what it was rated for, and the rating was calculated in a generation that is now myth. The silence holds. The water moves, but so slowly that a particle might take a month to cross from one side to the other. It is a place in its own state, weathered into an equilibrium that has forgotten any other configuration.

You could drift here for a lifetime and never touch bottom. You would not need to. There is always something to hold, something hung, something older than your grandparents, glowing faintly in the dark.
