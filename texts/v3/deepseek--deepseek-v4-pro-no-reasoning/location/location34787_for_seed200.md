The air here does not behave as air should. It is less a gas than a weight, a saturated fleece that settles against the skin and stays there. Breathing is a deliberate act—the lungs must push against the atmosphere to make room for themselves, and what they push into is only partially oxygen. The rest is water so finely divided it never quite becomes mist, and something else, something organic that tastes green on the tongue and coats the throat like warm oil.

The ground is low. Not low in the sense of a valley between hills, but low as though the entire region has been pressed gently downward by the thickness of the sky, a shallow bowl that extends in all directions without visible boundary. There are no ridges on the horizon, no uplands to walk toward. The land is simply here, and it stretches outward until the haze erases any distinction between earth and air. What passes for a horizon is a band of trembling obscurity, a zone where the visual field dissolves into particulate moisture and the suggestion of distance becomes theoretical.

Underfoot, the world is new.

This is a place still deciding what it will be. The ground surface has the texture of something recently exposed—not mud, not soil, but a mineral flesh that glistens with its own internal wetness. It is dark, the color of basalt that has never seen sun, but it yields slightly under weight, not with the softness of organic accumulation but with the give of something not yet fully compressed, something that still remembers being fluid. In places, the surface has begun to differentiate. Shallow depressions hold water that is warmer than the air, and these pools are lined with a pale film that might be the first stirring of mineral deposition or might be the earliest form of life organizing itself at the edge of chemistry. Nowhere is there a sharp edge. Nowhere is there a broken stone or a cliff face or any evidence that time has been at work. Erosion hasn't found this place yet. It is land before land, a geological infancy suspended in the moment before forces begin to shape it.

But life has already arrived.

The vegetation is not what the word usually summons. There are no trees, no ferns, no grasses swaying in wind—there is no wind. Instead, there is a profusion of forms that seem to have grown from the ground without passing through any intermediate evolutionary stage. They rise directly from the mineral substrate, and they are many. The density is such that from any position, the eye cannot travel more than an arm's length without encountering living tissue. Some stand upright, columns of translucent flesh that taper to blind, softly pulsing tips. Others spread laterally, covering the ground in overlapping lobes that shade from deep purple at their centers to a bruised pink at their advancing edges. Still others form tangled masses, not quite vines, not quite roots, but something that braids itself into knots and then, having knotted, slowly reabsorbs the knot over hours in a rhythm too slow to watch but impossible not to sense.

Everything touches. The upright forms lean against each other, their surfaces conforming where they meet. The ground-huggers overlap in layers, and where one lobe covers another, the one beneath does not die—it changes, adapting its pigmentation to capture whatever filtered light makes it through the tissue above. The braided masses weave through all of it, connecting column to lobe to ground in a continuous living fabric. There are no gaps. There is no path. To move through this place would be to push through flesh, and the flesh would close behind.

The fauna makes movement impossible.

They are not animals in any recognizable sense, but they are present, and their presence is overwhelming. The air is thick with them. They are small, most no larger than a thumb joint, and they move in clouds so dense that the space between individuals can be measured in body-lengths—two or three lengths at most, sometimes less where the clouds converge. They navigate by touch and chemical gradient, colliding gently with each other and with the vegetation, and the sound of their collisions is a constant, low-frequency rustle that never stops, never changes tempo, never shifts in pitch. It is the sound of a billion soft bodies brushing against a billion other soft bodies, and it fills the air like a fog of noise.

Larger forms move among the vegetation, slower, their bodies conforming to the spaces between the upright columns. They have no obvious front or back, no head, no limbs—just undulating sacs of translucent tissue that flow around obstacles rather than over them. Their surfaces are colonized by the smaller airborne creatures, and the relationship appears symbiotic or perhaps simply inevitable; the distinction between one organism and another blurs when everything is touching everything else.

The light is dim. The sky, if it can be called sky, is a ceiling of vapor that glows with a diffuse amber luminescence, the color of candle flame seen through horn. It does not change. The brightness does not wax or wane; there is no sun moving across a dome, no diurnal rhythm. The light simply is, and it has always been this particular quality of muted warmth, and it will always be. Shadows do not exist. The illumination comes from everywhere and nowhere, filling the space evenly so that objects cast no darker selves onto their neighbors. This absence of shadow adds to the sense that the world is not fully formed—shadow requires a light source, a direction, a narrative of illumination and occlusion, and this place has none of those things.

The heat is constant. It comes from below as much as from the air, radiating up through the mineral ground, and it comes from the bodies of the living things, and it comes from the water that stands in the pools at a temperature just below the threshold of discomfort. The heat is wet, and it carries scent—not the decay of a swamp, not the crisp oils of a forest, but something richer and stranger: the smell of complex organic molecules being assembled, the smell of biosynthesis running at full speed in an atmosphere saturated with its own precursors. It is the smell of a place making itself.

The sounds never change. Beneath the constant rustle of the airborne fauna, there is a deeper note—a subsonic thrum that might be the ground vibrating with its own slow mineral transformations, or might be the collective metabolism of the vegetation, or might be the sound of the world thinking about what it will become. The pools make no sound. The upright columns make no sound. The braided masses absorb vibration. The only other voice is a periodic wet exhalation that seems to come from everywhere at once, as though the whole landscape takes a breath in unison, holds it, releases it, and the rhythm of this breathing is so slow that only after long attention does the pattern emerge: in, hold, out, pause. In, hold, out, pause. Never varying.

There is nothing made here. No stone has been stacked on stone. No branch has been broken and sharpened. No mark has been scratched into any surface. The concept of making does not apply. This is a world before intention, before the division of the given into useful and useless. The living things do not build because building implies a separation between self and environment, and here that separation has not yet evolved. Everything is continuous with everything else. The columns merge with the ground. The ground merges with the water. The water merges with the air. The air is full of bodies. The bodies merge with each other.

Strange, but not alien. The weirdness here is subtle—it is the weirdness of something almost recognizable, a landscape that feels like a memory of a place that never existed. The upright columns resemble plants, but they pulse. The airborne creatures resemble insects, but they have no legs, no wings, no segments. The ground resembles soil, but it is warm and alive in a way soil should not be. This is a place that took a different path, or perhaps the same path but much earlier, before the branches diverged. It is a sketch of a world, a first draft, and the author has not yet decided what to keep and what to revise.

It is open. Despite the density of life, despite the walls of flesh that surround any theoretical observer on all sides, the place extends. It is not enclosed. There is no roof, no cliff, no boundary. The amber haze stretches outward in every direction, and the vegetation continues, and the airborne clouds continue, and the sounds continue, and if a mind capable of boredom could be here long enough, the uniformity might become oppressive, but also comforting—the certainty that this is all there is, that nothing will change, that the breathing will continue forever, that the light will never fade, that the heat will never break.

A place before erosion. Before the wearing-down. The ground is still arriving. The pools are still forming. The minerals are still differentiating. The whole landscape is embryonic, caught in the moment of its own emergence, and it has been caught there for a span of time that has no measure because measure requires change. Nothing erodes because nothing is finished enough to erode. Nothing accumulates because accumulation implies a past, and this place has no past. It is always just now, just coming into being, the first second of creation stretched into an eternity.

And it is loud, and it is dense, and it is wet, and it is hot, and it is full.

The water never falls as rain but rises as exhalation from the ground and the bodies and the pools, condenses in the amber ceiling, drips back down not as drops but as a continuous settling of moisture that coats every surface with a film that never dries. The rocks—there are no rocks, not yet—weep without having learned what weeping means. The vegetation is lush beyond the meaning of lush, a riot of forms that have not yet discovered the economy of leaves and stems and roots, that spend their energy on proliferation without pattern, on growth as pure expression.

And everywhere, the touching. The near-touching, the actual touching. The airborne bodies brushing against each other, the lobes overlapping, the columns leaning into their neighbors, the braids weaving through gaps that close as soon as they open. A density not of crowding but of intimacy, as though the world has not yet invented the concept of personal space, as though separation is an idea that will come later, much later, when some organism finally evolves a skin thick enough to know where it ends and the world begins.

Here, nothing ends. Everything flows into everything else, a continuous flesh, a continuous warmth, a continuous sound, a continuous dim amber light, and the slow, slow breath of a world that is still being born and will always be still being born, forever emerging, never arrived, dense and wet and hot and loud and full and open and never, never changing.
