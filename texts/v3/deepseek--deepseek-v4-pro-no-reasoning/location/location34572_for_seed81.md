The first thing you notice is the heat—not the blunt, sun-pounded heat of a desert, but a saturated, lapping warmth that seems to breathe against your skin. The air is so thick with water you could almost cup it in your hands, yet nothing grows here. Nothing green, anyway. The ground underfoot is not soil but a continuous, dark paving of something between ceramic and slag, its surface matte and finely pocked, warm as a living flank. It slopes gently upward in broad terraces, each one the height of three storeys, their edges too precise to be natural. This is a city, or what a city becomes when it has been abandoned for long enough that the word "abandoned" has lost its meaning.

The terraces rise into the fog. Not a low-hanging cloud but a permanent exhalation that sits at what you estimate is mid-elevation, perhaps two hundred metres above you, obscuring the upper reaches of the structures. The fog is the source of the wetness: it condenses on every surface, runs in constant thin films down the faces of walls and stanchions, drips from overheads you cannot see. The drip is the primary sound. It is not the random patter of a forest after rain but something methodical, a near-regular beat of water falling from engineered lips and channels, as if the city’s drainage system long ago forgot to stop working and now simply recirculates the same water in an endless devotional cycle. The drops fall into shallow depressions worn into the paving, into collecting basins that overflow silently into lower basins, into grates that swallow the water into the city’s interior plumbing with a low, distant gurgle. The sound is quiet, but it fills the space completely—a soft, pervasive percussion that seems to emanate from every direction at once, so omnipresent it becomes a kind of silence.

What little light there is comes from the fog itself. It glows faintly, a bruised amber, as if somewhere above the overcast a sun is burning and the mist has captured and diluted its light into a uniform, directionless luminescence. There are no shadows, only a gradual recession of objects into the haze. Visibility extends maybe sixty metres before details smear into grey. Beyond that, the city becomes a ghost of itself, the suggestion of towers and buttresses and arched spans that might be bridges or might be aqueducts or might be neither, their forms half-imagined, half-seen.

And it is vast. The terraces stretch away on either side until the curve of the slope takes them out of sight, and each terrace is itself a landscape, wide enough that walking from one edge to the other is a journey of minutes. The space between significant structures is enormous—not because they are spaced apart, but because the structures themselves are titanic and the thoroughfares between them are broad ceremonial avenues, now empty. The word "empty" here is absolute. There is no debris, no scattered artefacts, no trace of the inhabitants except the architecture itself. The city has been scoured clean, whether by time or intent or the constant washing of the fog. The surfaces are bare. The density of objects per cubic metre of space approaches zero; the city is a cathedral of vacancy, its volumes defined by absence.

What makes it strange is the combination of the industrial and the biological, or perhaps the post-biological. The walls are not brick or concrete in any familiar sense. They are striated like sedimentary stone but the strata are too regular, alternating bands of a lustrous black material and a chalky, porous grey that seeps water like a sponge. In places, the grey bands have eroded—the established erosion parameter made manifest—leaving the black bands standing proud as fins and ribs, so that the buildings appear to have skeletons emerging from their skins. This is the settled form of the place, the way it has been for longer than any human calendar could measure. It has found its equilibrium: the fog wets the porous stone, the stone slowly dissolves, the water carries the minerals down to some unseen sump, and the process has repeated for epochs until every surface has been etched into its final, patient shape.

The heat has a source, and finding it explains much. At the heart of each broad terrace, there is a low, circular structure like a kiosk or a wellhead, and from its opening rises a column of shimmering air. The city is built on a geothermal gradient—the high elevation is not a mountain but a plateau over a deep magmatic sill—and the original builders, whoever or whatever they were, designed their settlement to capture and circulate that heat. You can feel it through the paving: a steady warmth that drives the condensation cycle, evaporating the collected water back into the air so that the fog is continuously replenished. The city is not just urban. It is a machine. A vast, passive, still-running engine for moving heat and water through its own body. The terraces are radiators. The channels are capillaries. The grates are mouths. The whole edifice breathes, slowly, with a rhythm measured in the fall of drops.

You walk for an hour. Nothing moves but the water and the heat-shimmer. Then, at the edge of perception, a flicker. Not fauna in any sense you prepared for. A brief, localized dimming in the fog’s glow, a coherent shape of darker amber that slides along the flank of a distant buttress and is gone. A convection wraith, maybe—a pocket of cooler air given temporary form by the city’s thermal gradients. Or something else. Something the city still generates, a by-product of its ancient metabolism, a predator without prey, a presence without purpose. You see another one twenty minutes later, and then nothing for the rest of the day. They are rare, these manifestations. They do not seem to notice you.

The soundscape remains constant: the drips, the gurgles, the faint hiss of evaporation, and beneath it all, almost subsonic, the hum of the earth’s heat pushing upward through the foundations. No wind. The fog is too heavy to move laterally. No birdcall. No insect whine. The quiet is the quiet of a system running in its steady state, needing no intervention, acknowledging no observer. You are a transient anomaly in a world that has long since solved the problem of its own existence.

Ahead, the next terrace rises as a sheer wall of stratified black and grey, its face weeping a continuous sheet of water that falls into a receiving trough worn ten metres deep by the patience of erosion. A stairway—more a ramp, too broad, its risers too tall for human legs—angles up into the fog. You begin to climb, and the city continues to unfold around you, as empty at this new level as it was below, and as alive with its silent, industrious, water-driven heartbeat.
