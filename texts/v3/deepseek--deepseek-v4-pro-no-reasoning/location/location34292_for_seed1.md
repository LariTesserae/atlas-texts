The first thing that strikes you is the sound. Not a single noise you can name, but a dense, saturated roar, like standing inside a broken machine the size of a city. It thrums through the soles of your feet before it reaches your ears, a vibration that lives in the wet ground permanently. This is not a place that knows silence.

You are in a low basin, a vast depression stretching in all directions until a curtain of mist and steam swallows the view. The horizon is a suggestion, an open expanse that feels regional in scale—big enough to hold a town, a valley, several distinct terrains all pressed together without borders. And pressed is the word. There is no empty space. Every surface is occupied, every cubic foot of air thick with something moving.

The ground beneath you is not soil. It is a mineral landscape, ancient beyond measure, worn down by eons of water and chemistry until it lies almost flat. Imagine a world that has been eroding for so long it has settled into its final, tired form. The bedrock is exposed everywhere, but it is not solid. It is riddled. Wormed. Fenestrated into a labyrinth of channels, pockmarks, and weeping holes. The stone itself has the texture of old bone, pale gray-white, slick with perpetual condensation. It is cold. The cold is not the sharp bite of winter air but the deep, patient cold of something that has been leaching heat into a damp atmosphere for geological ages. Touch the rock and it will pull the warmth from your palm like a hungry mouth.

Water is everywhere. It does not fall as rain—not exactly. The air is so saturated that moisture condenses on every surface, beads up, runs in rivulets, drips from every overhang. The ground seeps. The stone weeps. Shallow sheets of water, never more than ankle-deep, flow sluggishly across the worn bedrock, following the ancient, pre-ordained paths of the channels. There is no vegetation. None. Not a blade of grass, not a smear of lichen, not a single cell of photosynthesizing life. The world here is post-plant, or perhaps pre-plant, or perhaps plant was never an option. The lack of green is total.

And yet the landscape teems.

The fauna are industrial.

You see them now, resolving out of the mist as your eyes adjust to the dim, metallic twilight. They are abundant beyond counting, packed into the basin so densely that they seem to be the terrain. Creatures of iron and brass and unknown alloys, their forms ranging from the size of beetles to hulking masses the size of factory buildings. They move with the sound of pistons and the hiss of steam, and they are engaged in processes you can only guess at.

Here, a cluster of jointed, centipede-like machines with rotary grinding mandibles slowly, methodically scrape the mineral crust from the bedrock, ingesting it into glowing furnace bellies. Their exhaust vents pour thick, moist air back into the atmosphere. There, towering tripod structures with fluted backs like radiator fins stand motionless, condensing water vapor from the air, channeling it down their legs into the shallow channels, drinking it through some internal mechanism that clicks and hums. The air is filled with small flying things—not birds, but delicate, dragonfly-like constructs of tin and copper wire, their wings a blur of perforated metal, their bodies emitting high-frequency whines. They stitch through the mist in swarms so thick they cast moving shadows on the ground.

This is an ecosystem. A predator-prey dynamic plays out in slow, inevitable motion. A massive, tank-like creature with a cowled, furnace-mouth slowly pins a smaller scraping-machine against a ridge of pitted stone. The smaller machine shrieks—a literal mechanical shriek, gears grinding—and then goes still as the larger one begins to methodically dismantle it, plucking off components with eerie delicacy and feeding them into its own body. The ground around it is littered with the husks of previous meals, slowly rusting in the pervasive wet, new channels already forming around them as the water flow adapts.

There is no biology here. Or rather, biology and industry have become the same thing, or perhaps never were separate. The creatures are not operated by anything; there are no drivers, no pilots, no tiny engineers inside. They *are* the animals. They reproduce—you see a hulking boiler-beast split open along riveted seams, releasing a cascade of wet, mewling metal young that immediately scatter to begin scraping and condensing. They respire—steam and smoke and waste heat. They have territory and calls and behaviors honed by some impossible natural selection.

And yet the parameters of reality feel stretched thin. This is the weirdness at the core of the place. Look at a machine-creature for too long and its surface seems to flicker between states: iron one moment, chitin the next, then something like bismuth crystal with impossible geometries that hurt the eye. The sound, that constant overwhelming roar, sometimes resolves into patterns that are almost language, almost music, almost a distress call. The mist does not just obscure—it occasionally seems to thicken in geometric shapes, cubes and fractals of fog that hold their form against the breeze for a moment too long before dissolving.

There are no built structures because the concept of "built" is meaningless here. The creatures are the structures and the builders and the built, all in a closed loop. The entire basin is a machine, or a body, or both, running processes laid down in its bedrock bones since before anything like soil existed.

The light is dim and sourceless, a pervasive gray glow that seems to emanate from the mist itself. It casts no strong shadows, just a flat, even illumination that makes depth hard to judge. The temperature is barely above freezing, just warm enough to keep the water liquid, and the sound—that packed, saturated, industrial roar—never stops. It is the sound of a world that has been running for so long it has forgotten why, a factory abandoned by its builders and inherited by its products, which then forgot they were products at all.
