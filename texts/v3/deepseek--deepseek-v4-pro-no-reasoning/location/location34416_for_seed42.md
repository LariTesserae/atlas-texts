This is a place called the Tangle-Kept City.

You are suspended in cool water, though you do not drown. The liquid is clear as pressed glass, faintly sweet on the tongue, and it holds you at a depth where the light — a diffuse, directionless radiance the color of pale honey — seems to come from everywhere and nowhere at once. The temperature is the cool of a shaded spring, steady and unchanging, a temperature that has forgotten the sun. Your movements are slow, deliberate; the water resists gently, as if it has grown accustomed to stillness and only grudgingly yields.

Above, below, and on all sides, the city rises through the water column.

But you must understand: the city is not *in* the water. The city *is* the root.

All of it — the towers, the archways, the winding passages, the chambers large and small — is one continuous organism, a vast submerged tangle of pale, woody growth that fills the volume of this high, drowned place. You are inside a plant that is also a settlement, a plant that has been coaxed and shaped across spans of time so long they have smoothed the distinction between grown and built into something obsolete.

Look up. The great central trunk — if it can be called a trunk — ascends through the middle of the space, a column thicker than the widest old-growth tree, its surface a braided cable of thousands of individual stems that fused together in the city's youth and have long since become indistinguishable. From this central mass, structural limbs radiate outward at graceful angles, spanning the watery distance to anchor points you cannot see, creating a three-dimensional latticework of vaulted space. Between them, finer branches weave into floors, walls, roofs: a cubic, organic tapestry, hollowed into rooms and corridors and staircases that spiral around the main axes.

This is the architecture. There is no stone here, no metal beyond the simplest hammered copper fittings. No cut timber — because nothing was ever cut. The Tangle-Kept City was not constructed; it was directed, over centuries, by hands that understood how to ask the plant to grow a lintel, to thicken here into a wall, to leave this space open as a room. The surfaces are smooth and pale, a greyish-tan like water-bleached driftwood, worn to a soft polish by the constant, gentle abrasion of the water and the passage of bodies. Where a wall meets a floor, the transition is a seamless curve, the grain flowing without break. The rooms are irregular, organic, each one a unique cavity in the great tangle, connected by passages that are sometimes tubular tunnels and sometimes mere gaps in a lattice of living beams.

You drift through one such room now. It is perhaps the size of a chapel, defined by six major limbs curving upward to form a vaulted, ribbed ceiling. The space is sparse — here is the low density of the place. It is not a crowded city. The rooms are many, but they are separated by thick structural growth, sound-muffling layers of intertwined fiber, and the water itself, which turns distance into a blue-green haze. You can sense other presences, other dwellings, but they are scattered through the volume like seeds in a fruit, each one its own held sphere of clarity. The emptiness between is not void; it is filled with the body of the city itself, living and silent.

And it is not silent. The parameter said "loud," but not in the way of a market or a factory. The loudness here is a constant, pervasive hum, a deep thrumming that travels through the water and into your bones. It is the sound of the plant breathing. Somewhere in the great central trunk, vast diaphragms of porous tissue expand and contract in a rhythm too slow to count, pushing water through the city's internal channels, aerating the liquid, circulating warmth. The sound is felt as much as heard — a subsonic *woom-woom-woom* that sets up sympathetic vibrations in the walls, the floors, even the hollow spaces of your own chest. Layered over this are the creaks, the long, slow groans of lignified tissue adjusting to a weight it has borne for millennia. The city is established. It has settled into itself. Nothing moves quickly here. The creaks are a conversation held over geological time.

There is fauna. It is abundant, but you must recalibrate your definition of abundance.

You see them now, as your eyes adjust to the honey-light. In the chapel-room, they cling to the walls like living fretwork: colonies of something that is not quite coral and not quite insect, tiny pale polyps with feathery feeding arms that extend into the gentle current, plucking invisible something from the water. They are everywhere, a dusting of animate lace over the structural wood, and their collective, microscopic movements create a faint, continuous rustling, like the sound of very distant rain. Small, translucent creatures — shrimp-like, with too many legs and eyes on stalks — pick their way through the polyps with careful, deliberate steps, grazing. A shoal of flattened, silver fish, each no bigger than your palm, ripples through the open center of the room, turns as a single entity, and vanishes through a circular passage into the blue-green haze.

These are the citizens, or some of them. The fauna is the city's true populace. The original builders — the hands that directed the growth — are here too, or their descendants are. You catch a glimpse of movement in a passageway overhead: a slow, drifting shape, roughly humanoid, its body sheathed in a pale, almost translucent integument. It moves by gently fanning the water with webbed appendages. It is carrying something — a cluster of the polyps, carefully detached and cradled in its arms — from one part of the city to another, a farmer transplanting its livestock. This is the technology of the Tangle-Kept City: a slow, manual aquaculture practiced by beings who have adapted so completely to the submerged, vegetal world that it is hard to say where their domestication of the plant ends and the plant's domestication of them begins. They craft simple tools from shell and dense, oil-rich seedpods. They use fire nowhere — could not, even if they wished to. Their material culture is a thing of patient weaving, of fiber twisted into rope and net, of living polyps arranged in gardens that are also walls.

The weirdness here is not a piercing strangeness, but a pervasive, sideways tilt to the expected. It is in the way the water breathes. It is in the slow realization that the city is not just a structure but a metabolism, that the faint sweetness on your tongue is the taste of its exhalations, that the light itself might be a byproduct of some symbiotic bioluminescence cycling through the plant's tissues in a rhythm matched to the great pumping diaphragms. It is the knowledge that the city has been here, balanced in this state, for a span of time that makes the word "ancient" feel inadequate. The erosion is not decay but a kind of polishing, a slow wearing-into-perfection. Everything rough has been smoothed. Everything sharp has been rounded. The city is a thought so old it has become a fact of nature.

The cool water carries the deep thrum of the city's heart. The polyps rustle. A fish shoal turns like a mirrored wheel in the distance. The Tangle-Kept City drifts in its high, flooded hollow, settled into the slow rhythm of its own existence, and waits, as it has waited for ages, for nothing to change.
