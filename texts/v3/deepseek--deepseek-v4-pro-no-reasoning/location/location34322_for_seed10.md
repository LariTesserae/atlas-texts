It is not a place that welcomes the idea of *ground*.

You step—if "step" is the word—onto a surface that gives slightly, then gives more, a moss that is not quite moss, a mat of interwoven plant bodies so dense and so deep that the notion of soil beneath it is a geological abstraction. They are not individual plants. Or rather, individuality has long since ceased to mean anything here. What was once a forest, perhaps, or a meadow, or some cold vast tundra, has spent uncountable millennia folding into itself, roots growing through roots growing through the decay of roots, until the living and the dead became a single continuous tissue. It is a peatland raised to metaphysics. The vegetation score of 2.7—*total*—is not an invasion. It is the medium. The architecture of the world is cellulose and respiration. What you walk on is the roof of a continent-spanning organism exhaling slowly.

You are high up. The elevation presses on the air, thinning it, and the cold has a sharpened edge. Thermal springs don't bubble here in the usual way. Instead, the near-boiling water—a gift of deep geothermal restlessness—weeps continuously from the plant-mass itself. Not from the ground. The *plants* bleed hot water. Thick, sponge-like structures, pale as frozen butter and ridged like brain coral, swell up from the mat in hummocks the size of houses. Their pores bead with condensation, then overflow in rivulets that run warm down their flanks, steam curling into the frigid air. The steam is the loudest sound. Not the roar of geysers, but a vast, continuous, industrial-grade hissing, as if the whole landscape were a leaky boiler room. Layer under that is a deeper, wetter sound: the constant, gulping percolation of water through a trillion capillary spaces in the living mat, a sound like a million tiny mouths drinking.

The water on the ground—or what serves as ground—is ankle-deep and hot enough to scald, kept liquid by the heat bleeding from below. It sheets across the surface in a film, gathering in the depressions between hummocks. These pools are strange. The vegetation doesn't stop at their edges; it simply changes state. Submerged, the plant-stuff becomes gelatinous, a clear or faintly amber jelly shot through with filaments that twitch slowly in convection currents. The water is 2.4, *wet*, a constant condensing-atmosphere wet, a weeping-rocks wet, but the rocks here are living and the tears are nearly blood-warm. Drop a stone, if you could find one, and it would not splash. It would dimple the jelly, sink a little way, and then be absorbed by slow, ameboid embrace.

Look up, or rather, look out. The scale is *open*. You are on a high plateau, or perhaps a mountain range worn down to its nubbins by the *ancient*, 3.0-grade erosion. The horizon is impossibly far, ringed by what might once have been peaks but are now only gentle swells, like the backs of napping whales. Nothing is sharp. Everything has been rounded, softened, settled into its final, flattest form. There are no cliffs, no talus slopes, no exposed bedrock. The plant-mass has consumed all that, digested the entire mineral skeleton of the mountains over eons. The openness is punctuated by the hummocks, which rise and fall in a topology of swelling and subsidence, but they are spaced apart. The density is sparse—0.6. There is *much space between presences*. Each pale, steaming, brainlike monolith stands in isolation, separated from its neighbors by acres of flat, hissing, water-sheened mat. It gives the place the feel of a drowned city seen from above, its towers reduced to mounds, its streets to shallow, steaming lagoons.

And yet, the silence is not silence. The hissing is punctuated by calls. Fauna, 2.4 *abundant*. But fauna born of this logic. Things move across the mat on many-jointed legs, skimming the water film with hydrophobic feet. They are all exoskeleton and breathing-tube, looking like the offspring of a diving bell spider and a mantis, sheathed in a fur of white, waxy filaments that repel the constant wet. They are loud. Their calls are not bird-song but mechanical rasps and chitters, the sound of badly oiled hinges and the clicking of relays. They swarm over the hummocks, picking at the steaming flesh with delicate, surgical mouthparts. Some are as large as dogs. Others, smaller ones, cloud the air in glittering swarms, their wings beating fast enough to create a low, industrial hum, an electric-motor whine layered over the steam-hiss.

This is the wrongness. The reality breakdown, the 2.7 weirdness. It is not in the landscape's strangeness alone. It is in the *impossible* logic of the place. The hummocks, for instance. Watch one for an hour, a day, and it will change. The hissing pores might close, all at once, like a thousand eyes blinking. The rivulets of hot water will stop. The pale flesh will begin to darken, bruising into shades of liver and iron, and then it will begin to peel. Not like dead bark. It will peel in perfect, machined strips, curling away with the precision of a lathe-cut spiral, revealing a new, slick, translucent surface beneath. The spirals will fall and float on the water film, and before they dissolve, you can see they are stamped with a pattern, a repeating hexagon like a heat exchanger’s matrix. A blueprint shuddering out from the living tissue.

The most disturbing sounds are the ones that don’t belong to the animals. They come from under the mat. Deep, resonant groans, the sound of metal cooling, or of a great ship’s hull adjusting to pressure. Sometimes, the ground ripples. A wave will pass through the plant-mass, a slow, muscular undulation that lifts the water film and sends the spider-things skittering. In the wake of the wave, for just a moment, something surfaces. Not an animal. A structure. A vast, curved plate of something that is not plant, not bone, dark with a luster that suggests alloy, its edge machined, its surface studded with what might be rivets or sensors. It breaches the mat like a whale, dripping jelly and trailing severed root-fibers that spark and arc with a dying, internal light. It groans, a sound of immense mechanical distress, and then the plant-mass swallows it again, fibers knitting, closing the wound. The turbulence is constant. Dynamic 2.8, a storm-state of creation and destruction, but the storm is between two systems, two orders of being that have been at war so long—or have been one thing so long—that the distinction is meaningless. The plant is industrial. The industrial is plant. The factory is the forest.

The light is always dim, a perpetual twilight under a cloud deck that is partly meteorological and partly the sheer volume of steam this one living, dying, building, un-making thing exhales. It is cold, 0.5, a damp, penetrating cold that the hot, wet ground fights against, so that you feel both at once: your feet and ankles scalded, your face and lungs frozen, trapped in a gradient of contradictory sensations.

No human hand is here. The built score is 0.5, *none*, but that feels like a lie, because the entire place feels constructed, feels machined, feels like a blueprint for a process that has lost its designer and its purpose and has simply continued, iterating and iterating into this cold, wet, noisy, empty-punctuated-by-monoliths eternity. There is no artifact, unless the entire world has become one. A landscape that is not a place but a function. The last factory on Earth, and the Earth has learned to love its chains so much it has grown a skin around them, and forgotten where the metal ends and the flesh begins.
