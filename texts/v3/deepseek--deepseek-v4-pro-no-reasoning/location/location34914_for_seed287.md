The place is a buried hall of weeping stone.

Imagine coming down through a crack in the world — a narrow slot canyon that breathes wet heat like a mouth. The descent is not long, maybe fifteen of your body-lengths, but the angle is steep and the walls are greased with a slick of dark algae and mineral ooze. You do not climb so much as lower yourself hand over hand, and every surface you touch is warm, the way skin is warm when fever has just begun to show. The air gets heavier as you go, not just with heat but with sound: a pervasive, machinic thrum that you feel in your teeth before you can name it as noise.

Then the slot opens, and you are in the room.

Not a cave exactly. Too regular. The floor is smooth and hot, a single slab of something black and faintly resilient underfoot, shedding crumbs of rust at the edges where it meets the walls. The walls are old brick — not the pale handmade brick of ruins, but heavy industrial brick, glazed in places, scorched in others — and they rise in a slow ring to a ceiling you can touch if you stand on your toes. The space is no larger than a small chapel. A cathedral in miniature. One central chamber, perhaps twelve strides across, plus alcoves where the brick has bowed outward or collapsed into rounded hollows.

It is very loud in the room. Not with voices. With water and machinery.

From the ceiling, water arrives in a constant, patient sequence: not rain but a schedule of drips, each one fat as a thumb, landing in the same places they have landed for so long that the floor beneath them has grown cups — shallow, rimmed depressions where the black material has dissolved and re-precipitated into rings of rust-colored chert. Some of the cups are full to brimming. Some are dry, waiting their turn in the slow cycle. The water is hot. When a drip hits your shoulder, it leaves a brief, startling heat that fades into the general swelter.

The sound is deepened by what stands in the center of the room: a machine. Or the remains of a machine. Or the still-working ghost of one. It is built mostly of copper and iron, green in the joints, welded here and there with seams of crude glass. It resembles an organ with its back to you — a hunch of pipes and pistons and narrow, cabinet-like chambers — and it is breathing. Not continuously. On a cycle. Every few minutes, something inside it exhales: a long gurgling sigh as water is pulled through hidden tubes, then a sharp percussive click, then a brief, deafening rush of steam that blooms upward and slicks every surface in the chamber with another layer of moisture. Then silence, or near-silence, for a while. Then the gurgle begins again.

The machine does not appear to do anything else. If it was built to pump, to crush, to generate, to call something up from below, that purpose has long since worn down to this single, unkillable ritual: draw in the dripping water, hold it, boil it, release it as vapor. The walls have softened because of it. The bricks wear rounded shoulders. The glass seams in the machine have gone milky with mineral scale. Everything in the room is in equilibrium with the machine’s patience.

And yet the place is not barren.

Because of the heat, because of the water, because there is no wind and no season down here, the room is full of vegetation. Not plants in pots. Not orderly growth. Vegetation as a climate.

Ferns grow from the collapsed alcoves, their fronds pressed flat against the brick like green hands trying to hold the wall together. Moss covers the lower half of every vertical surface in a dense, dripping pelt — not only green but rust-red, amber, a soft silver-gray where the light barely reaches. Air-plants hang in tangled curtains from the steam pipes overhead, their leaves thin as blades, beaded with condensation, trembling slightly with every machine-exhalation. In the corners, where the floor meets the walls, there are mats of something between fungus and liverwort, wet and faintly luminous, spreading in slow licks across the rusted metal seams.

The light itself is dim, but it is not absent. It comes from no lamp. The glass seams of the machine glow a low orange, like embers seen through smoked glass, and when the steam releases, the whole room brightens for a moment as if the light were carried in the vapor. There are also patches of the wall-moss that glow very faintly, blue-green, only visible when you look away from them. It is enough to see by, if you are patient. It is not enough to read by.

And there are signs of people, though not recent ones. Scattered, as the parameters say. At the far end of the room, a narrow bunk has been built into the brick, its wooden frame now soft as sponge and hosting its own crop of pale, heat-loving fungi. Above it, on a rusted nail, hangs a single steel helmet, its interior full of moss. Near the machine, someone long ago arranged a row of small, irregular stones — perhaps to mark maintenance intervals, perhaps to count something that could not otherwise be counted. The stones are worn nearly smooth by the constant steam.

There are no insects. No birds, no rodents, no fish in the dark water cups. The silence between machine-breaths is completely empty of animal sound. It is one of the reasons the place feels so much like a held breath.

Yet the vegetation itself seems to make up for that absence. It is so thick in places that it has its own tiny weather. Condensation runs down the fronds in slow, deliberate rivulets. Droplets gather on the tips of the air-plants and hang there, trembling with the vibration of the machine, until they fall. The moss has a scent: hot metal, wet clay, and a sweetness like overripe melon. When you move through the room, your passage disturbs not animals but microclimates — you feel the air cool slightly where your body shades a patch of wall, you hear the minute creak of a fern frond unloading a bead of water onto the floor.

The erosion is everywhere, but it is not ruinous. It is an old place. It has had time to become exactly what it is. The bricks no longer look like human masonry; they look like a natural formation that happens to be rectangular. The pipes no longer look like engineering; they look like roots, or veins, or the slow exhalations of the earth shape itself into metal. The machine has no maker’s mark anymore. Whatever name was once stamped on its casing has been eaten away into a fine, illegible lace of green oxidation.

And below all of it — beneath the drip and the hiss and the thrum — there is another sound, very low, almost more a pressure in the chest than a noise. It is the sound of water moving far underneath the floor. Not a stream. A body of water. Slow, vast, patient. The room is not the bottom of the world. It is only a drumhead stretched over something deeper, and the machine, with every pulse of steam, is keeping time with it.

You might sit down on the warm black floor and realize the floor has a slight give, as if it were not solid rock but a skin of mineralized rubber over a hollow. You might notice, after a while, that the drip pattern has shifted slightly, that the machine’s breathing has become a little faster, that the moss near the ceiling has begun, very slowly, to grow toward the new source of heat that is your body. But these changes are too slow to watch. You would have to leave and come back years later to see them. The place would not miss you. It is too busy being itself: a low, hot, loud, living room below the surface of the earth, where water and steam and green things and one old machine have been negotiating the same small territory for so long that the negotiation has become a form of devotion.
