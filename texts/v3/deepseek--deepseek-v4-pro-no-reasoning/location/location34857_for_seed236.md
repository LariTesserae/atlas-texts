The settlement occupies the top of a basalt fist — a plug of dark, glassy rock that pushed up through the plateau a few thousand years ago and never finished cooling. The rock is young, geologically speaking. Its edges are still sharp. Where lava flowed and froze mid-drip, the stone keeps the memory of motion: ridges like tendons, lips of once-molten rock curled over themselves, cracks propagating through the mass in clean geometric lines. Nothing has had time to round these edges. Sand hasn’t found its way here. The wind carries no grit. The air is dry enough that even the idea of rust feels foreign.

The settlement is built directly into this stone. The people here — or whatever passes for people — carve their rooms as voids within the basalt, tunneling into the rock rather than building atop it. The openings face the sun, and the sun is generous. The air is warm, not hot, a steady warmth that seems to rise from the rock itself as much as from the sky. The basalt drinks heat all day and gives it back all night. There is no water anywhere. No wells, no springs, no rain. The vegetation that grows here does so through some pact with the stone.

And it is lush. Dense. Overwhelming.

The plants are low, matted, layered over each other so completely that no bare ground remains visible between them. They grow in the fissures of the basalt, following the cracks, spilling from every ledge and shelf. Their leaves are small and closely packed, waxy and pale gray-green, some tinged orange at the edges from the heat. They grow in the shape of the stone — cascading down vertical faces in flat rosettes, filling the gaps between rock layers, building green terraces where the lava once pooled. From above, the settlement looks like a dark island drowning in a pale green sea that spreads across the plateau in every direction. The growth is not tall — nothing exceeds a meter — but it is dense beyond belief, each plant pressing against its neighbors, roots knitting the cracks together.

The plants do not need water. Their chemistry is different. When you break a leaf, it doesn’t weep; it crumbles like dry paper, releasing a faint mineral smell, like hot flint. The growth cycle is slow. Leaves emerge over weeks, not days. The green deepens gradually. You could watch for hours and see almost nothing, but the change is there, moving at the pace of the sun across the year.

The fauna is everywhere in this green. Most of it is small — the size of a thumb, a fist, at most a forearm. They live in the spaces between leaves, in the tunnels of the stone, in the thin shadows cast by the basalt ridges. They are quiet. Their feet are soft. Their bodies are low to the ground. Many have flattened, leaf-like bodies, green with pale veins, nearly invisible until they move. They feed on each other and on the mineral sap of the plants, and they are abundant beyond counting. At any moment, if you look closely, you see movement: a ripple through the leaves, a tiny shape darting into a crack, the slow unfurling of something that might be a creature or might be a growing thing.

The settlement itself is quiet. No machines. No sound of water. No wind. The main sound is a kind of soft rasp — the sound of many small bodies moving through vegetation, the rustle of hard leaves against each other, the faint click of animal feet on basalt. It is a sound so ubiquitous that after a while it becomes silence. The people do not speak much. When they do, their voices are low and brief, absorbed immediately by the green. There are no tools beyond carved stone and woven plant fiber. The architecture is all negative space — rooms carved, corridors bored, stairs cut into the face of the rock. The settlement is small, no larger than a cathedral or a grove, but dense with rooms. Every square meter of the basalt plug has been carved, several rooms deep in places, connected by narrow passages that follow the natural cracks.

The odd thing — the thing that makes a visitor pause — is the pattern of the carving. The rooms are not arranged for human convenience. Many are too small to enter, no larger than a skull, carved deep into the rock with no apparent opening large enough for anything bigger than a hand. Others are tall and thin, chimney-like, rising through the stone with no floor except the basalt below. The people seem to build not for themselves alone. They carve rooms for the small fauna, for the plants, for the stone itself. The settlement is not a city so much as a tenement shared by everything that lives here. The walls between human rooms and animal dens are thin, sometimes only a few centimeters of basalt, and the people live in close, constant proximity to the rustling green things. There are chambers where plants are cultivated in dense, hanging layers, their roots gripping carved niches in the walls. There are chambers where the small fauna are deliberately encouraged, fed on crushed leaves and stone dust, their quiet bodies moving in thick carpets over the floor.

The light is ordinary — a warm, clear light, neither harsh nor dim. It comes from a sun that seems close, but this is an illusion of the high plateau and the clear air. There is no humidity to scatter the light, no haze. Shadows are sharp, cast by the basalt ridges and the carved openings. The light changes slowly. The shadows rotate through the day with a patient, stately movement. You can mark time by which carved niches are in shadow and which are full of light. The plants respond to this, their leaves tilting imperceptibly, following the light at a pace that is invisible until the season is half gone.

The rock is young, and so is everything built into it. Nothing has had time to erode. The carving marks are still visible — fine parallel grooves where stone tools bit into the basalt, the rough edges of abandoned chambers, the sharp angles of doorways that have not yet been worn smooth by passing bodies. The people here seem aware of this. They treat the stone as recent. They keep the carvings clean. They sweep the floors with bundles of dry leaves. They do not repair, because nothing has broken. The whole place has the feeling of something recently made, still settling into itself, still testing its own weight.

The plateau stretches in all directions — flat, high, covered with the same pale green growth. The settlement is one of many, presumably, though from here you can see no other. The green continues to the horizon, uninterrupted except for the occasional dark outcropping of rock. There are no paths. The growth is too dense to allow movement without crushing it, and the people here do not leave the settlement often. When they do, they move slowly, stepping on the stone ridges, disturbing the plants as little as possible. The distance to the edge of the plateau is unknown. No one here has walked it. The world is this: the basalt fist, the green sea, the warm light, the quiet rustle of small lives.

There is no water. There is no wind. There is no tool beyond stone. And yet the place is full — dense with life, dense with structure, dense with presence. The air smells of dust and warm stone and something like sage, though nothing here is sage. The temperature never changes by more than a few degrees. The growth is slow but relentless. The carving is fresh but complete. The silence is not empty; it is full, as if the whole plateau is holding its breath.

And in the deepest rooms of the basalt, where the light does not reach, the people tend small chambers that no one has ever explained. The rooms are smooth, polished by hand, and in each one a single plant grows, pale and leafless, fed by nothing but the warmth of the stone and the breath of the person who tends it. These rooms are visited rarely. They are not spoken of. They are simply part of the place, like the green outside, like the quiet, like the slow turning of the sun.
