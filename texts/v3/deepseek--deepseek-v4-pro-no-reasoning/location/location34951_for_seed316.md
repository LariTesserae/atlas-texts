The air hits you first—a warm, wet weight that settles in the lungs like something alive. And the sound. The sound is a physical presence, a pressure against the eardrums that never relents, a continuous roar that isn't white noise but something structured, something with teeth. It's the sound of a world being unmade and remade simultaneously, too vast to locate, coming from everywhere and nowhere.

You stand at the edge of the Sink.

The terrain underfoot is low and soft, a saturated mat of something that isn't quite soil and isn't quite flesh. It gives slightly with each step, then slowly, slowly rebounds, as if remembering your weight after you've gone. Water doesn't pool here so much as it permeates—the ground exhales moisture with every compression, a fine warm mist that never dries. The whole plain breathes.

Ahead, stretching beyond the curvature of the world, the Sink extends. A flatness so complete it hurts to look at. This is what three billion years of erosion does when given time enough to finish the job. There are no hills, no valleys, no features in the conventional sense—topography has been erased, worn down to a single unbroken plane that continues until it meets a horizon blurred by its own haze. The rock here, where rock still exists, is exposed in low scabrous patches, stone that has been leached and leached again until it crumbles at a touch, weeping a thin mineral slurry from pores you'd need a lens to see. The weeping never stops.

What vegetation exists is sparse and strange—not plants in any botanical sense but stubborn holdouts, slow things that have adapted to a world with no soil to grip. They present as wiry tangles of translucent fiber, spread wide and flat against the saturated ground like nervous systems pinned to a slide. Some pulse faintly, a slow circadian throb visible only if you watch for minutes at a time. They don't photosynthesize in the normal spectrum; the light here is too dim for that, a perpetual overcast that turns the sky the color of tarnished silver. Instead they seem to feed on the condensation itself, on the boundary layer where water meets air, siphoning some chemical gradient we have no name for.

The sky is not empty.

This is where the scale asserts itself. The atmosphere above you is packed—not with clouds, not with weather as you know it, but with *occurrences*. Vast ribbons of vapor twist and knot and unknot at speeds that shouldn't be possible, forming structures that hold their shape for minutes or hours before collapsing into new configurations. There's no room between them. The sky is occupied, saturated with phenomena, a three-dimensional tapestry of turbulence that extends upward until it fades into a darkness that isn't night, isn't space, but some third thing. The speed of these formations creates the noise—the deafening, omnipresent roar of air tearing and reforming, of moisture flash-condensing and flash-boiling in the chaotic pressure differentials that rip across the dome of the world.

A structure stands to your left. It wouldn't catch your eye if you weren't looking for signs of prior presence. Low walls, barely knee-high, arranged in a geometry that might have been a rectangle once, now slumped and softened until the corners have melted away. The material is the same as the ground, indistinguishable, as if the structure is being reabsorbed. There are more of these scattered across the plain, hundreds of them visible if you turn slowly, each one isolated by vast distances, no paths connecting them. They weren't built here. They were left here, and the world has been digesting them ever since.

The fauna, when it appears, is rare enough that you might go hours without seeing anything and then catch movement from the corner of your eye that's already gone when you turn. What lives here is small and quick and deeply strange—forms that seem to be all locomotion and no body, flashes of iridescence that skim the surface of the saturated ground, leaving trails that heal over in seconds. Some burrow. Some simply *pass through*, moving in and out of the ground like it's a suggestion rather than a barrier. None make sound you can distinguish from the roar. None seem to notice you at all.

But the weirdness. The weirdness is the hardest thing to hold in your mind.

The Sink is old. Not old like mountains, not old like continents. Old like the last stage before heat death, the final configuration that matter settles into when all the interesting processes have run their course. But it hasn't gone quiet. Something has inverted, flipped causality or locality or something more fundamental, and now the place vibrates with a creative destruction that has no source and no end. The roar isn't just sound—it's the noise of reality straining against itself, of physics running backward and forward in the same instant.

Watch long enough and you'll see it. A patch of ground will ripple and *un-erode*, roughness emerging from smoothness, a hillock pushing up from the plane only to be worn flat again in the space of a breath. A ribbon of sky-vapor will freeze into crystalline precision, hold for three heartbeats as a lattice of impossible geometry, then dissolve into chaos. The scattered structures—sometimes they're more whole than they were an hour ago, sometimes less. The process has no direction. Time here doesn't flow so much as it seethes.

The temperature never changes. Warm. Always the same warmth, the warmth of a fever that's been running so long the body has forgotten what health felt like. Humidity at the threshold of condensation, perpetually on the verge of rain that never falls because the sky is too busy tearing itself apart.

The sound is beginning to differentiate as your ears adjust. Beneath the roar, rhythms. Beneath the rhythms, something almost like voices—not speaking, not singing, but resonating in frequencies that feel like language if you don't listen too closely. The ground transmits them. You can feel it through your feet, a bone-deep hum that syncs up with the slow pulse of the wiry not-plants, with the flicker of the skimming things, with the rise and fall of those distant dissolving rectangles.

This is a world at the end of erosion, when everything that can be worn down has been worn down, and what's left has learned to thrive in the wearing. It is packed with activity, saturated with presence, a maximum-density state where no gap exists between one phenomenon and the next. It is vast enough to contain weather systems that would swallow planets. It is turbulent beyond any metaphor for turbulence.

And it is, recognizably, a place where something lived once, scattered a few stones into rectangles, and then departed—or was absorbed—leaving behind a landscape that has continued, in their absence, to invent new ways of being ancient.
