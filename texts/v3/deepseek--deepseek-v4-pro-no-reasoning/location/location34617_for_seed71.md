The city does not have a name, because there is no one left to speak it.

It clings to the inner wall of a rift so deep that clouds form in its middle reaches, far below the city's foundations, and the valley floor itself is a rumor, lost in permanent blue shadow. The rift cuts across a high plateau like an old scar, a continent’s worth of level ground that simply opens. The plateau is cold in a way that feels geological, a cold that has been settling into the stone since the world was young. Thin, dry wind pours across the tableland above, but here, inside the cleft, the air is still and damp, saturated by condensation that never quite becomes rain, just a constant cold seep that beads on every surface. There are no seasons to speak of. The temperature barely varies. The water has only two states: this suspended mist, and the thin films of ice that form in the deepest shadows and never melt.

The city is built in terraces, carved back into the cliff face itself, and also forward, outward, in structures that lean precariously over the abyss. The construction is stone upon stone, fitted without mortar, a dry masonry of astonishing precision. Wood is rare—no trees grow at this elevation, nothing large anyway—so everything is stone, or something like stone but lighter, a compressed moss-aggregate that was once cultivated in great hanging gardens and then baked in the thin sun. The architecture repeats: rectangular windows, flat lintels, steep narrow stairs that switchback up the cliff face, connecting the terraces. Bridges of braided rope and planks of the compressed moss-material span the wider gaps, though many of them have rotted through and hang in tattered threads, swaying in the slow upwellings of valley air.

At first glance the city seems densely built, a vertical warren climbing for hundreds of meters up the cliff. But that is an illusion of perspective. The structures are scattered across the immense face of the rock, separated by broad sweeps of bare stone, by vertical gaps that swallow whole plazas. A single building might stand alone on its terrace, three stories of empty windows, with the next habitable structure a ten-minute climb away. The city is a loose constellation of dwellings and workshops and gathering halls spaced across a canvas of damp, dark rock. The population was never large. It just spread itself thin, as if each inhabitant needed a certain volume of silence around them.

Now there are no inhabitants at all. The fauna parameter reads zero, and that zero includes humans, includes anything that moves with its own will. No birds ride the thermals in the rift. No insects tick in the crevices. No rodents have nested in the abandoned storerooms. The city is not dead in the way a corpse is dead; it is dead in the way a word is dead when no one remembers its meaning.

Yet it is not still.

The city processes itself. That is the only way to understand what happens here. The stone, over eons, has learned to remember.

The condensation never stops. Water slides down the cliff face in continuous micro-films, and that water carries dissolved minerals, and those minerals, over an immensity of time, have accreted into forms that are no longer entirely random. The dripstone that hangs from the lintels does not form simple stalactites. It grows in curled, recursive patterns, in frills and folds that look, from a distance, like writing. Up close, the resemblance dissolves into abstraction, but step back, and the script resolves. The same forms appear on the plaza floors, where mineral deposits have built up in fine raised ridges. On the walls of the gathering halls, the damp has bloomed in dendritic patterns that spiral inward, concentrically, like a fingerprint the size of a room.

The city is in conversation with its own past, but the conversation has outlived the participants, and it has begun to drift. The patterns are increasingly baroque. In one terrace, the mineral deposits have begun to form free-standing sculptures, columns of ridged stone that twist as they rise, and they hum, very faintly, when the wind moves through the rift at a certain angle. The sound is almost sub-audible, a pressure more than a note. In another terrace, the moss-aggregate blocks of a collapsed wall have begun to move. Over centuries, the expansion and contraction of ice films has nudged them, one infinitesimal shift per freeze-thaw cycle, and they have arranged themselves into a spiral on the plaza floor, a spiral that mirrors the mineral deposits on the wall above. It is not random. It cannot be random. And yet there is no mind directing it. The city itself has become the mind, a distributed cognition of water and mineral and thermal flux, thinking at the speed of geology.

The reality breakdown is subtle at first. You notice that the stairs do not always lead to the same place twice. Not in a dramatic way—you climb from the fourth terrace to the fifth, and the hall you find there is not the hall you remember, but it is clearly the same hall, the same dimensions, the same moss-aggregate blocks, only the erosion patterns on the wall are different. Older. Or younger. The light is wrong for the time of day. The damp has a smell, faintly, of ozone, of something discharged.
