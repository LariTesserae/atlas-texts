The place has no name, or if it does, no voice speaks it aloud.

---

You stand in the dome-light. It comes from everywhere at once: diffused through the upper membranes, caught and scattered by ten thousand waxy panes of translucent plant-structure, reflected upward again from the pale floors. Not sunlight exactly — something whiter, cleaner, more even. The quality of light in a cleanroom, in an operating theater, in a place where shadow has been designed out of existence.

The floor underfoot is not stone. It is root. A weave of lignified fibers pressed into seamless terracing, still alive in its deeper strata — if you kneel and place your palm flat, you can feel the faint, slow peristalsis of nutrient transport, a rhythm on the scale of hours. The temperature of the surface is cool against skin, perhaps twelve or thirteen degrees, and perfectly uniform. This coolness is deliberate. The whole structure maintains it, a thermal set-point held across continental distances by the metabolic balance of the living architecture.

Everything is made. Nothing here evolved in the sense you understand. The walls, the vaults, the descending shafts, the arching buttresses that sweep up into the bright haze — all of it grew, but grew to specification. This is a built world of cellulose and lignin, of engineered meristem and directed xylem. A plant-industrial complex spanning what must be thousands of kilometers in every direction.

You are inside an organ.

The space around you is vast in a way that plays tricks with your sense of scale. The ceiling — if that word applies — vaults upward three hundred meters, but it is not a single span. It is a branching, recursive vaulting of interwoven trunks and bracing strands, all of them alive, all of them glossy with the same pale integument. The light emanates from specialized bladders in the upper layers: translucent blisters the size of houses, filled with a bioluminescent medium that glows steady and cold. When one of them reaches end-of-life, it dims to amber over a period of days, then detaches silently and drifts down through the still air like a dead jellyfish, slowly, slowly, to be absorbed by the floor-tissue below. You might see one falling, if you watch long enough. You might not. The turnover rate across a structure this size means one falls somewhere every few seconds, but the distances are so great that you could wait an hour and see nothing move.

Silence. Not absolute — there is sound, but it is the sound of a body's interior. A low, nearly subsonic hum that you feel in your sternum before you hear it: the vascular pulse of millions of tons of living infrastructure moving fluid through its hierarchical pipe-systems. Occasionally a sharper sound, a fibrous creak, as some distant bough settles or adjusts its load. The place ticks and breathes. No voices. No wind. The air is still and cool and faintly sweet with some volatile you cannot name, an exudate from the walls, a metabolic byproduct of the plant-machinery.

Everything is layered. Looking horizontally, your eye travels through space after space, chamber after chamber, connected by archways that are not built but grown — the trunks of the structural pillars parting to form passages, then meeting again above and below. These spaces extend into a blue-white distance that eventually fades not to darkness but to a kind of perceptual static, a fuzzing of detail as the density of intervening membranes and glowing bladders accumulates across kilometers of depth. You understand, in your bones, that the landscape continues like this for a long way. Not infinite — nothing is infinite — but beyond the horizon of a human lifetime's walking.

And yet. And yet the place is not empty.

The fauna is rare. Very rare. But it exists. You may never see it; you may only sense it as a disturbance in the stillness, a pattern of marks left on the waxy integument of a structural column. Something has passed this way. Something with sharp mandibles, or gripping claspers, or a scouring radula — the marks are precise, geometric, not the random abrasion of settling architecture. They follow a path. They turn at intersections. They seem purposeful. You get the sense of a population density measured in single digits per cubic kilometer, solitary things moving on slow metabolisms through the unchanging cool, following maintenance routes laid down in their instinct or their programming. They do not sing or call. They leave no spoor you can detect. But sometimes, if you stand very still in a chamber for long enough, you will hear a new sound join the vascular hum: a faint, dry skittering, far away, moving through the walls.

The place is dry. Not parched — the living tissues hold their moisture internally, in closed-loop systems that lose nothing. But the surfaces are dry to the touch, sealed with a waxy cuticle that feels almost ceramic. There is no free water anywhere. No pools, no dew, no drip. This is a world that has optimized water out of its visible vocabulary, that treats it as a precious internal currency never to be exposed to evaporation. The atmosphere in the great chambers is not humid. It is cool and clean and smells faintly of ozone and something like crushed stems.

And here is where the reality breakdown begins.

Because the place is alive, and entirely constructed, and industrial in its function — you can see the factories, if that is the word. Organelles. Nodes where the plant-tissue differentiates into intricate machinery: arrays of spinnerets extruding fibers of what might be silk or might be carbon-chain polymer into the waiting grasp of other, differently specialized tissues that weave them into complex membranes. Banks of photosynthetic bladders that are not using sunlight, you realize, but something else — a radiance generated deeper in the structure, fed upward through light-pipes of clear xylem from a source you cannot locate. The whole edifice is manufacturing something. You cannot tell what. The process is vast and recursive and seems to produce, among its intermediate outputs, more of itself.

And then you notice the space.

The space is wrong. You have been walking — or you imagine you have been walking — along the same broad root-floor, through chamber after chamber, and you have not yet found an exterior wall. But more: the distances do not add. You walk a hundred meters, and you look back, and the chamber you came from seems both closer and farther than it should be, as though the intervening tissue compresses or expands when you are not attending to it. The perspective doesn't converge the way your visual cortex expects; the lines of pillars and archways bend slightly, everywhere, a fractional curvature that seems to hint at non-Euclidean geometries but never quite commits to them. You look up. The ceiling seems to recede faster than it should. You look down. The floor has a subtle convexity that shifts when you try to stare at it.

The rules of space in here are biological, not mathematical. The structure grows according to algorithms that optimize for something other than flatness. Maybe the topology is a manifold. Maybe the entire edifice is folded through itself like the cristae of a mitochondrion, so that what seems like a straight walk down a corridor is actually a traversal across nested, interpenetrating volumes.

You pass a wall that is not a wall. It is a membrane, translucent, several centimeters thick, stretched between two structural trunks. Through it you see — something. A shape, motionless, suspended in a matrix of clearer tissue, vaguely animal in its organization. Large. Several meters long. Its surface is covered in the same waxy integument as the architecture, and from its ventral side hundreds of threadlike rootlets extend into the surrounding matrix, fusing it to the living structure. Sleeping? Being digested? Being assembled? You cannot tell. You feel no impulse to breach the membrane and find out. Fifty meters farther on, there is another. Then another. All different. None recognizable. The volume is dense with things embedded in the walls, and you understand that every surface, every structural element, might contain more of them. Thousands. Millions. A library. A manufactory. A mausoleum. You do not know which.

The place is in balance. It has been this way for a long time. The erosion parameter said it best: established. Settled-form. Long-existed. In-its-own-state. The wear patterns on the floor-roots, the faint ambering of older tissues, the way the glowing bladders dim in some sections and brighten in others in a slow, cyclic wave that takes decades to complete one oscillation — all of it speaks of a system that reached its steady state long ago, before your species learned to walk upright, and has been humming along ever since, self-maintaining, self-renewing, self-absorbed.

You imagine the sound of it from a distance. If you could step outside this world — if there is an outside — and listen to it from space, you would hear nothing. The silence would be perfect. But inside, the vascular hum continues, the skittering comes and goes, the occasional creak of settling tissue marks the passage of a structural minute that might be a century long.

And the light continues, steady and bright and white, from everywhere, from nowhere.

You stop walking. You stand in the cool, root-woven floor, surrounded by the breathing walls, under the vaulted living machinery, and you realize you have no idea whether you are in a building or a body or a product or a world, and whether the distinction means anything here at all.

Something far away blinks — a bladder going amber. It detaches. It begins its long drift downward through the still air. You watch it fall. It takes a very long time.

There is nowhere to go. There is no hazard. There is only the landscape, vast and dense and entirely alive and entirely constructed, continuing its quiet, industrial, photosynthetic, mammalian work, in the bright silence, forever.
