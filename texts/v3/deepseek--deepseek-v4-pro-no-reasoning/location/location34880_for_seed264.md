The air is warm and thick, almost gelatinous with moisture that never fully commits to rain. It beads on every surface—not dew exactly, but something closer to the sweat of the world, a perpetual exudation that glazes the mineral expanses with a dull shine. The light is steady, diffuse, a flat white-gray that seems to emanate from everywhere and nowhere, casting no shadows worth mentioning. This light has existed in exactly this quality for longer than any measurement could capture.

The ground is low and open, a broad basin that extends outward to a horizon softened by humid haze. What you stand on is not soil, not in any biological sense. It is a continuous surface of what might be mistaken for dark, weathered metal, but is in fact something stranger—a mineral formation that grows in slow, unchanging sheets, like the scales of some impossibly vast creature turned to stone. The texture is uniform: gently undulating, pocked with shallow depressions that cup the ambient moisture into still, warm pools. Each pool is perfectly circular, as if drilled, and each holds the same depth of water, and each has held that same depth for epochs. There are no ripples. There is no inflow or outflow. The pools simply are, and have been, and will be.

And the sound. It is the defining feature of this place, the thing that makes it what it is. A low, rhythmic thrumming rises from everywhere beneath you, a deep mechanical pulse that you feel in your sternum before you properly hear it. Thump-thump. Thump-thump. Thump-thump. It is perfectly regular, unwavering, a bass note that has not varied in tempo or volume in uncountable cycles. The sound is industrial—there is no other word for it—the deep heartbeat of engines buried in the world's crust. But there are no structures here, nothing built, no vents or stacks or intake grilles to explain the source. The ground itself seems to be the engine, or the engine is so far below that it has become the ground, or the ground has grown over it in the mineral-equivalent of scar tissue. The sound is loud enough to be oppressive, yet it does not echo. The open expanse swallows reverberation, leaving only the pure, direct pressure of the beat.

The air vibrates with it. You can see the effect at the edges of the pools: a faint, standing interference pattern in the surface tension, a geometric shimmer that never resolves into ripples, never breaks, never changes. The pools do not evaporate in the warmth because the thrumming somehow holds them, feeds them with condensation drawn up from the deeper strata. This is the water cycle here—a closed loop, an equilibrium, an engine-driven hydrology that has reached perfect stasis.

Living things, or what might be considered living, appear rarely and keep to the pools. They are not fauna in any familiar sense but something more like stray thoughts given temporary material form. Occasionally, at long and unpredictable intervals, a pool will extrude a shape: a wobbling column of the mineral-water, a slow-moving pseudopod that rises, tastes the air with a blind, groping motion, and then subsides back into its basin. These "things" are made of the same substance as the pools—dark, slick, faintly translucent—and they leave no trace of their brief existence. They do not eat, do not respire in any observable way, do not interact with one another even when two pools happen to extrude in proximity. They are rare, and their appearances follow no pattern that patience has yet discerned.

There is no vegetation because there is no need for it, no niche for photosynthesis in a world lit by unchanging grey and fed by internal engines. There is no decay because nothing truly grows. The mineral sheets do not spread or recede; they are complete. The pools do not deepen or shrink; they are in balance. Erosion has done all its work long ago, wearing every sharp edge down to the gentle curves of settled form, and now it has nothing left to erode. The place is established in the deepest sense: not old, precisely, because "old" implies a trajectory from young to aged, and this place has simply always been as it is. Time here is not a river but a steady state, a hum, a holding pattern.

Yet for all its industrial sound, there is nothing technological in view. No pipes, no conduits, no control panels. The technology—and it is technology, you are certain of that, the sound is unmistakably machined—has become geology. Or geology has become it. The boundary between engine and environment dissolved so completely that the category no longer applies. This is a landscape that runs on some vast, maintained process, but the maintenance is intrinsic, automatic, and the process has no output, no product, no purpose that you can fathom. It is a factory that manufactures only its own continuation.

The heat is pleasant, actually. Warm without being hot, constant, the kind of temperature that would be comfortable on bare skin. And the moisture, though heavy, is clean—it tastes of nothing when you breathe it in, no salt, no minerals, no organic compounds. Just water and air and the endless thump-thump-thump of the world's deep machinery.

You could walk for days in any direction and find more of the same: more mineral sheets, more circular pools, more of that steady thrumming. The landscape is open, sparse in its features, but not empty. Each element is deliberate, each depression spaced with a regularity that feels intentional. The pools are never close enough to merge, never far enough apart to leave a sense of barrenness. They are distributed like notes in a composition that has been playing so long it no longer needs a composer.

This is a place that forgot it was made but never stopped running.
