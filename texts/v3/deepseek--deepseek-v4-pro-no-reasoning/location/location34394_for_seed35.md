The air is thick as wool and twice as warm, pressing in from all sides with the steady weight of something that has forgotten what weather means. You are underground, though the ceiling is lost somewhere above in a darkness that the scattered lights cannot reach. The floor beneath your feet is black glass, rippled like cooling lava but polished smooth by something other than water—perhaps feet, perhaps time, perhaps a chemistry that does not involve friction at all. It reflects the lights in long smears of amber and dirty orange, pulling each point source into threads that weave across the undulating surface.

The walls—when you can find them—are not walls but slopes of compacted mineral, salt-white and iron-red in layers too straight to be natural, too irregular to be built. They lean inward as they rise, giving the whole chamber the feel of a throat. Nodules of darker stone stud the surfaces like glands, and from some of them seep thin exhalations of vapor too heavy to rise, gases that curl downward and pool in the low places between the glass swells. Where these vapors gather, the air shimmers with a heat that is not fire but something closer to decomposition, a slow unraveling of molecules that releases warmth as a byproduct. The temperature is constant, a humid 35 degrees that never fluctuates, never breathes.

And the chamber is loud.

Not with any single sound you can name, but with a layered drone that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere—a composite of vibrations transmitted through the floor, the walls, the dense air itself. Somewhere in that drone is the sub-bass thrum of engines, great machines buried in the mineral flesh of this place and running on logics that predate your arrival by centuries or millennia. They have never stopped. They will never stop. Their rhythm is the rhythm of the place, a heartbeat of pistons and inductance coils that sets the glass humming at frequencies you feel in your teeth.

Higher in the register are the clicks. These are sharper, more directional, and after a few minutes you begin to trace them to their sources. The fauna here is abundant—startlingly so, once your eyes adjust to the dimness. They are small things, most of them, built on body plans that seem to have started with an insect and then made different choices. Some are flat and many-legged, skittering across the glass with a sound like fingernails on slate, their carapaces the color of tarnished copper. Others are spherical and covered in fine metallic hairs that bristle and lie flat in waves, as if stroked by invisible hands. These roll rather than walk, gathering speed across the slopes and banking off the mineral nodules with soft chiming impacts. The air itself is thick with fliers—tiny, silent-winged things that navigate by the heat plumes and leave faint trails of bioluminescence in their wake, brief blue-green afterimages that fade as you turn your head.

The rules here are not the rules of a living ecology. Nothing eats anything else, at least not in the ways you recognize. The sphere-creatures cluster around the gas-seeping nodules and seem to absorb something directly through their bristles, swelling slightly, their metallic coloring brightening. The flat crawlers press themselves against the warmest patches of the glass floor and grow still, vibrating in sympathy with the engine-drone until they, too, have absorbed what they need. The fliers sip from the vapor pools and excrete a fine crystalline dust that coats everything, settling like a constant slow snow of mica, catching the dim light and sending it sideways in tiny prismatic flashes.

Then there are the structures. Scattered, as the old survey said, but each one a punctuation mark in the dimness. Most are no larger than a shipping crate, built of the same black glass as the floor, sealed and windowless and humming at a slightly different pitch than the background drone. Their surfaces are studded with gauges—brass-rimmed, glass-faced, bearing markings in a script that seems to flicker and rearrange itself when you look too long. Needles quiver in the yellow-green half-light. Some of the structures have doors, heavy and slightly ajar, and from within comes a warmth more intense than the general heat, a smell of ozone and oil, and sometimes a deeper vibration that makes the sphere-creatures roll away in sudden unison.

The largest structure—if you can call it that—is not a building but a machine that has become architecture. It rises from the lowest point of the chamber floor, a column of nested rings and reciprocating arms that pump in slow, perpetual motion, never quite completing a cycle, never quite starting one. It is perhaps the source of the deepest drone, or perhaps one source among many. The creatures here cluster thickest around its base, layered upon each other two and three deep, their small bodies vibrating in unison with the great slow rhythm. They do not move. They have not moved, you suspect, for a very long time. They are not dead. They are in equilibrium—a state the whole chamber has achieved, a perfectly balanced arrangement of heat and sound and chemical presence that has locked itself into form and will not, cannot, change until some outside force breaks the seal.

There is no water here. There may never have been water here. The chemistry of the place is based on something else—silicon, perhaps, or sulfur, or metals in states that surface chemistry has no names for. The vegetation, such as it is, takes forms you almost missed: thin pale filaments growing in the cracks between glass flows, not plants but mineral extrusions that branch and bifurcate according to electric fields, slow crystalline ferns that shatter if you touch them and regrow in the same patterns, once the disturbance fades.

The light sources, when you finally locate them, are not bulbs or flames but captive reactions suspended in glass globes along the leaning walls—chemical glows of deep orange and amber that never brighten and never dim, steady as everything here is steady. They cast the only shadows in a place where time has stopped meaning what it means elsewhere. The shadows are sharp-edged and still, as if painted onto the glass floor, and the creatures move through them without disturbing them.

You could stay here for hours, days, years, and nothing would be different when you left. The vibration would continue. The slow snow of mica-dust would continue. The creatures would cluster and absorb and never hunger, never grow, never die. The gauges would register the same pressures, the same temperatures, the same flow rates, their needles trembling but never moving. This place has found its answer and it is keeping it, sealed beneath the weight of earth and time, a closed loop of heat and sound and strange life that asks nothing of the world above and receives nothing in return.

Your footsteps are the only new sound, and when you stop walking, even that stops. The place reabsorbs the silence you disturbed, or rather the equilibrium you briefly interrupted, and the drone fills in the space where your presence had been. As if you were never here. As if the equation had a term for visitors and its value was always zero.
