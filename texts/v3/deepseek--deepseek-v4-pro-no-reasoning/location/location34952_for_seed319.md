The city is called Cinder-Sheet, and it is built across a high, tilted plateau of weeping stone. The rock is a dark, porous basalt, fractured into countless vertical fissures over geological ages of cooling and settling. Water, never absent, condenses on every surface and seeps from the mouths of these cracks, not in dramatic falls but in a constant, glistening exudation. It is a post-rain landscape that has no memory of the rain itself, only the lingering aftermath. The ground underfoot, where it can be found between structures, is a thin, rust-coloured grit, the decayed remnant of the plateau’s skin. This is not a place of mud, but of slick, hard surfaces, perpetually wet and shining under a sky the colour of worn lead.

The sound is the first thing a stranger notices, a thing so pervasive it becomes the medium of the world. It is a deafening, omnidirectional drone, a composite of many sources. The deepest layer is a sub-bass thrum from the Geothermal Siphons, vast corkscrewed pipes that plunge into the plateau’s heart, pulling up heat and a sulfurous breath. Above that hums the constant, high-frequency whine of the Moisture Scavengers, great banks of charged wire mesh that line the rooftops and pedestrian bridges, hissing as they coax drinkable water from the saturated air. The chorus is punctuated by the percussive, arrhythmic clanking of the Cable-Trams, iron carriages swinging on a slack-chain web strung between the factory spires. There are no voices loud enough to be heard over this; communication is a thing of hand signals, light-flashes, and written notes left on grease-paper. In this city, to be deafened is to be a native; one learns to feel sound through the soles of the feet and the vibrations in the handrails.

This is an industrial city, but its industry is a slow, patient metabolism. The Spire-Factories are not blocky, brick-built monoliths but tall, skeletal frameworks of black iron and rust-streaked steel, layered one atop the other in a palimpsest of architectural ambition and decay. The oldest levels, near the ground, are massive and gothic, their riveted beams corroded into lacy, organic patterns by the eternal wet. The newest additions are sharp, unweathered cages bolted onto the sides or thrusting up through the centers of the old, a parasitic growth reaching for a clearer wind. These factories do not roar with the frantic energy of a startup; they process in slow, continuous cycles. One might perform a single, ponderous stamping operation every ten minutes. Another distills a rare earth salt from the deep water over the course of a month, its product pipe releasing a single, viscous drop every hour into a waiting ceramic vat. The entire city operates on this rhythm of minimal flux, a machine civilization in a state of near-equilibrium, where a shift in a primary pressure gauge is an event noted in a logbook over a cup of chicory.

Vegetation is sparse and tactical, a matter of survival rather than spectacle. There are no parks. Instead, a specific, grey-green lichen—Wiremoss—is cultivated in careful, latticed beds on the leeward sides of the taller structures. It is the source of a crucial enzyme for one of the chemical processes. Occasionally, a pioneer puffball, bone-white and brain-sized, will erupt from a neglected corner of grit overnight, feeding on some industrial leakage. It is harvested quickly, before its spores can corrode copper wiring. The only other life is rarer still: the Rust-Skimming Bees. They are not social; each is a solitary, thumb-sized creature of slick, black chitin, its wings a blur of iridescent oil. They build no hives but instead mold solitary, metallic nests from iron oxide. You will see one, maybe, drifting across a beam of steam-filtered light, a solitary and purposeless-seeming presence whose purpose the citizens have long stopped questioning.

The city itself, the built environment, is an interconnected web of overhead walkways, enclosed bridges of steamed-up glass, and wide, open staircases that cascade down the wet rock faces. The "streets" are often just the natural, sloping ledge of the plateau, worn smooth by generations of boot-leather. The space between presences is what defines the city’s interior. The Cable-Tram station plazas are vast, open platforms of grated metal where one can stand and look down through the grid to a hundred-foot drop into a mist-filled chasm, a canyon between factory foundations where the original fissures have widened beyond use. The city, for all its density, is built *around* these voids, respecting them. A residential block of stacked, single-occupancy iron cells will wrap around a sheer, empty rock face where water eternally slides, the rock itself a central, silent resident in the communal space.

At night, the city-light is normal, a steady amber glow from shielded arc-sodium lamps that line the main transit ways. It reflects in a million points on the wet iron and on the gathered, trembling beads of water on every unheated surface. The farthest edges of the city simply dissolve into darkness on the plateau, an open landscape stretching past the last flickering lamp. The air carries a taste of hot metal and clean, cold stone. Cinder-Sheet does not sprawl; it accretes, growing denser and taller upon its own skeleton, a three-dimensional territory staked out on a high, weeping, and profoundly patient world.
