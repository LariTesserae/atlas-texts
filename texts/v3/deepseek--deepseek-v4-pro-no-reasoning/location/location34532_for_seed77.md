The first thing that strikes you is the silence. Not an empty silence—it has texture, a faint continuous hiss that seems to emanate from everywhere and nowhere, like the sound of steam escaping from countless tiny fissures. There’s no wind, no birdsong, no insect hum. Just that whisper, and beneath it, a deeper sub-audible throb you feel in your sternum more than hear.

You stand on a plain that stretches to every horizon, so vast that the curvature of the world is faintly visible—the ground ahead of you seems to lift almost imperceptibly before dropping away. The surface underfoot is not rock, not soil, not anything geology ever produced. It’s smooth and slightly warm, a continuous skin the color of tarnished copper, mottled with blooms of darker patina—verdigris greens, manganese purples, the deep brown-black of oxidized silver. The material has give to it, like walking on cured leather stretched over a drum. In places it dips into shallow basins, some no wider than a footprint, others broad as dry lake beds. Most are empty, but not all.

Water is here, but it doesn’t behave as water should in a hot world. The temperature is palpable—a dry, pressing heat that makes the air shimmer in slow waves above the darker patches of ground—and yet there’s moisture everywhere, just never free. The basins collect it as condensation. You kneel beside one the size of a cupped hand and see beads of water trembling on the surface, too small to gather, too reluctant to evaporate. They quiver with that deep thrumming vibration that comes up through your knees. Elsewhere, seams in the copper-skin ground weep a thin, warm exudate that smells faintly metallic, faintly organic, like licking a battery. Nothing flows. Nothing pools deeply enough to drink. The moisture is a presence, not a resource—a sweat the plain cannot stop producing.

Across this immense skin, vegetation clings to life with the tenacity of lichen on a boulder. Sparse doesn’t mean absent; it means every living thing is an event. There are growths that look like wiry black moss, following the seam-lines in the ground in long, branching threads, as if tracing a vascular system. In the larger basins, where moisture persists a few hours longer each cycle, you find structures that might be plants: low rosettes of rigid, fibrous leaves the color of flint, their edges serrated not for defense but to catch the slightest drift of mist. They grow alone, each one a meter or more from its nearest neighbor, each one exactly the same size. When you look at the spaces between them, you notice shallow root-traces under the translucent upper layer of the ground-skin, connecting them in a radial network. They’re not individuals. They’re a single organism, thinly spread, waiting.

There are no animals. You’ve walked for what feels like hours and haven’t seen so much as a mite, a gnat, a worm-casting. The quiet is that absolute. And yet the place is intensely active. The ground throbs. The seams weep and seal and weep again. The condensation follows a rhythm you begin to sense: a slow inhalation over minutes, the beads swelling, then an almost imperceptible relaxation, the moisture reabsorbing into the skin. A breathing. You time it without meaning to. Thirty-seven of your heartbeats for the in-breath. Forty-one for the out.

This is when you understand: the vast copper plain is not a landscape with a built thing upon it. The plain itself is the built thing. The ground you walk on is a construct of incomprehensible scale—a continuous membrane, kilometers thick perhaps, laid over whatever the original planetary crust might have been. The skin is the only surface. Nothing natural protrudes through it. No bedrock outcrops, no scree fields, no sand dunes shaped by wind. The hot wind, when it does come, is brief and sterile and stirs nothing because there is nothing loose to carry. Everything is fixed, stretched, sealed. A total fabrication, but a fabrication that feels like a body.

In the distance, the monotony breaks. Heat-haze shapes resolve slowly into something vertical. As you approach over the drum-skin ground, you see it’s a ridge—but not a tectonic ridge. It rises too smoothly, too symmetrically, a long welt in the copper membrane standing three or four meters high and running straight as a meridian to both vanishing points of the horizon. The flanks are steeper than they looked from afar, and on the crest, the moss-threads are denser, almost fur-like. The seams here are wider, and from them rises actual steam—faint, translucent columns of vapor that twist briefly in the still air before diffusing. The hissing is louder here. This is where the moisture leaves the system, you realize. The ridge is an exhaust vent, an exhalation point in a planetary cycle.

You climb it. The surface is rougher under your hands, textured with nodules the size of knuckles, each one warm, each one vibrating slightly at its own frequency. From the top, the view is simultaneously too much and not enough. The copper plain extends without landmark in all directions. But now you can see other ridges, parallel to this one at intervals that suggest regular spacing—kilometers apart, a ribcage of the world. Between them, the shallow basins glitter faintly with their trembling beads of water. In one basin directly below you, the fibrous rosettes have synchronized their cycle with the ground’s breathing, their leaves opening as moisture gathers, closing as it wanes. A slow, vegetal pulse. Butterflyless, birdless, bacterially silent except for that pervasive hiss and thrum.

The sky is pale, washed out by the heat, a white-gold dome that bears no clouds because the moisture never rises high enough to form them. The light is ordinary daylight—familiar, uncanny in its normalcy given everything below it. You could almost believe you were on Earth, in some desert, if you didn’t look down.

You stay on the ridge until the light begins to shift. Not sunset—there’s no sun visible, just an omnidirectional brightening and dimming that passes for a diurnal cycle. As it dims toward what you assume is night, the plain changes. The beads of water stop trembling and begin to glow, faintly, a bioluminescent blue-green that traces the seam-lines like a circulatory map lit from beneath. The rosettes fold tight. The hissing drops to a nearly inaudible level. And the thrumming slows, the great deep pulse of the ground stretching its intervals, as if the entire construct were entering a different metabolic state—rest, or something like it, for a body that was never alive in any way you were taught to recognize.

No animals. No tools. No natural substrate. Just this: a world being built by processes you can only glimpse, vast and patient and actively, silently, breathingly underway.
