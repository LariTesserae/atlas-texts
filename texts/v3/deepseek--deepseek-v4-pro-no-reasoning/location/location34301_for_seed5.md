It is not a place you find by looking for it. You must be lost, or very small, or very patient.

The first thing is the light. It arrives from everywhere and nowhere, a steady, sourceless radiance the color of skimmed milk, and it presses against the walls of the world with a brightness that is somehow without heat. The walls themselves are not walls but the smooth, dark bellies of enormous stones, boulders the size of overturned carts, their surfaces worn to a soft gleam by centuries of patient work. They are packed so tightly together—density 2.6—that the spaces between them are not cracks but rooms. The city is a solid puzzle of granite, a three-dimensional mosaic fitted by no human hand, though human hands have certainly been here.

The air is cold, a deep and settled cold that lives in the stone and never leaves. It is the cold of permanence, of a place that has never known the sun. With it comes the damp, a moisture that does not fall as rain but condenses in a billion tiny beads on every surface, a perpetual dew that slicks the rock and feeds the green. And the green is everywhere. It is a riot of vegetation, mosses and ferns and things with no name, each one finding its purchase in the microscopic porosity of the granite. They are packed together, too, a dense fur of emerald and olive that pads the stone like upholstery, so thick you could press your hand into it up to the wrist and still not feel the rock beneath. It is a cold, wet, bright, and breathless jungle in miniature.

This is a cleft, a single submerged chamber in the bedrock of a vast and ancient city. Above, the buildings of men—low, stone-built, primitive by any starfaring measure—huddle against the wind. But this place is below, a forgotten basement, a pocket of static equilibrium. The sound is not silence, not exactly. It is a sound so quiet it has become a texture. The combined, near-subsonic exhale of a million leaves transpiring. The wet tick of a single drop of water, forming on a stone tooth over the course of an hour, falling with a sound like a tiny kiss into a pool the size of a dinner plate. The sound repeats, or rather, it simply is, a metronome set to a geological tempo. It is a place that has forgotten time, or has decided to stop participating in it.

And yet, it is not uninhabited. The fauna is abundant, if you know how to look. They are not animals of fur and blood, but of chitin and slow intent. Pale, blind isopods, the size of rice grains, navigate the condensation trails in the moss. A millipede, an amber bracelet of a creature, works its way through the root-hairs of a fern with a devotion that is architectural. Everything here is a builder, a layer, a contributor to the saturated, packed density of the world.

There are signs of the builders above, too. In one corner, where two boulders meet to form a shallow arch, a pile of smooth, black, river-rounded stones has been carefully stacked into a small, purposeful pyramid. It is wet with dew, and a single, perfect fern has rooted in the tiny space between its apex stone and the one below. An offering? A marker? A child's forgotten game from a century ago? It doesn't matter. The city of moss has already claimed it, weaving it into its own structure. The damp, the cold, the bright, the green, the packed and silent life of the place—it is all one thing now, an unusual, self-contained world, a universe the size of a room, stable and never-changing, held in the sculpted palm of an old, old stone.
