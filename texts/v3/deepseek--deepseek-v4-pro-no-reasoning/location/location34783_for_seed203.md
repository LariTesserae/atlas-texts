Here is the landscape.

---

You descend, and the first thing to register is the light. It is not the light of a sun, but a pervasive, sourceless, pearlescent glow, as if the atmosphere itself is a cold, fluorescent gas. It makes your skin look bloodless, cadaverous, and it casts no shadow in the usual sense—instead, where a shadow should be, there is simply a more intense deep-blue stain, a concentration of the ambient cold.

This is a world below the surface, but not a cave. You call it the Rind. It is a space so vast it has its own faint, chaotic weather, a scale of its own physics. The ceiling, if it can be called that, is lost in a luminous haze a kilometer or more overhead, a distant, ribbed vault of interlocking roots. These are the undersides of the true world above—a single, continent-spanning organism. The vegetation parameter here is total. The world is the plant. The plant is the world.

You are standing on a shelf of matter that resembles pale, fibrous cartilage. It is structurally part of the root-vault above, a descending buttress that has grown down and then curled out horizontally into a thin, broad platform no thicker than your hand. It feels impossibly strong, yet when you shift your weight, it vibrates with a high, clean frequency you can feel in your teeth. Gazing out, you see thousands of these structures—cable-roots and buttress-fins—hanging like frozen rain from the hazy vault. They crisscross and intersect, creating a three-dimensional labyrinth of structural emptiness. The density here is a near-void; the space between these plant architectures is colossal. You could fly a small airship through the open rooms of the Rind and never come near a wall.

Between the great vertical cables, smaller structures are stretched like vellum. Diaphanous, veined membranes, young growths with the sharp, fresh-edged features of recent emergence. They are cool to the touch, like fresh linen, and a slight, constant breeze makes them ripple. This breeze is the breath of the Rind, and it carries the sound—a sound that is mostly quiet, yet has a character. It is not silence. It’s an infrasonic hum, a deep-bass thrumming that oscillates slowly, the sound of sap the density of tar moving through pipes the size of sequoias miles away. Layered over this is a chaotic, high-frequency rustle, a hiss of crystalline friction as new growths, sharp and juvenile, grate against the older, more leathery substrates. The place is in a state of turbulent creation; you can actually see a new membrane unfurl like a time-lapse, its edge as sharp as a scalpel, rending itself from a pulpy parent-strand.

And it is cold. The cold here is a damp, penetrating thing that clings to everything. There is no standing water, but the air hovers at the saturation point. Condensation pearls on every surface in a glittering, pea-soup fog of micro-droplets. High above, where thermal gradients clash in the vast, empty spaces, miniature weather systems form and dissipate in real-time. You watch a patch of cloud—a misty, glowing silver—coalesce around a cluster of hanging threads, precipitate a fine, needle-like drizzle that falls for exactly thirty seconds into the abyssal space below, and then vanish, its energy spent.

This is not an untouched wilderness. The built parameter is scattered, a faint archaeological signature on a planetary organ. As you move along the elastic shelf, your hand brushes a strange artifact. It is a hand-tool, or a religious object—a perfect triangle of polished grey slate, its edges sharpened to a molecular degree. It is lashed with sinew to a femur-shaped strut of petrified wood. The craftsmanship is primitive, early-tool-use extreme, but it has a stark, formal elegance. It rests in a natural niche as if placed there yesterday.

Then, the fauna. It is rare, and utterly unusual. A long, protracted silence, and then a sound like a single piano note struck with a soft mallet. You see it floating between two cable-roots a hundred meters away. It is a thing of thin, aerogel-bone and transparent skin, a lattice-sphere creature that looks like a wandering soap bubble imbued with intent. It’s not an animal in the classic sense; it’s a detached spore-intelligence, a biomechanical sporangium of the world-plant itself. It undulates, not flying, but refracting its internal gas bladders to alter its buoyancy. It is the only moving thing in this vastness, and its singular, chime-like call repeats once, then fades, leaving you alone again in the bright, cold, turbulent immensity. The Rind continues to grow, to crystallize, to breathe, its scale making you a passing thought in the mind of a god.
