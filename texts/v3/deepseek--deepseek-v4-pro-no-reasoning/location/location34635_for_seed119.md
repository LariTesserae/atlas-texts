The air is the first thing you notice — it has a weight, a clammy presence that settles on your skin like a second membrane. It is not quite raining, and it is not quite not raining. Water moves here in a thousand small, patient ways: it beads on every surface, drips from every edge, seeps up from below, and hangs suspended in a haze that never fully clears. The temperature sits at that precise point where cool becomes a physical texture, where the difference between wet stone and wet air is almost imperceptible. You inhale dampness. You taste minerals.

The place is small. You could walk its length in forty strides, its width in twenty, and you know this because the boundaries are absolute. Walls rise on all sides — not natural walls, not canyon or cave, but built things. Stone fitted to stone, block upon block, the joins so ancient and so colonized by moisture that they read now as geology rather than architecture. This is a room, or was a room, or has been a room for so long that the concept of "room" has become a law of physics here. The floor is flagstone, uneven, each slab cupped slightly in its center where uncountable feet — or something — have worn shallow basins. And every basin is full of water. Not deep water; a finger's depth, a palm's breadth. Still water, but not stagnant. It moves, somehow. It replenishes.

Look up. The ceiling is lost in the dimness, but you can sense its presence as a kind of pressure, a lid on the world. The light that reaches this place comes from no visible source. It is a general luminescence, grey and diffuse, the light of heavy cloud cover translated into stone. It reveals everything as shades of charcoal, wet slate, the deep green-black of old bronze. Shadows do not fall so much as gather, pooling in corners and beneath ledges like the water itself.

Sparse vegetation. But what vegetation. It grows in the seams. Wherever two stones meet imperfectly, wherever a crack has widened over centuries of freeze and thaw that never quite freezes, something pushes through. Lichens, mostly — but lichens here do not behave as they should. They are not crusts; they are structures. Pale, finger-like growths, the color of old bone, rising in clusters from the mortar lines. Some are knee-high, branching clumsily like antlers carved from chalk. They are wet to the touch, and they are warm — just barely, just detectably warmer than the ambient air, as if they metabolize the dampness itself. At the tips of the tallest ones, a slow droplet of water forms and hangs, and every few seconds one falls, and the sound is a note in the larger music of the place.

The sound. It is loud, but not in the way of a city or a machine. It is loud in the way a dripping cave is loud, an echo chamber where every small noise multiplies. The dominant note is water. Drops falling from the bone-lichen into the floor-pools, a steady arrhythmic percussion — *plink, plonk, plip* — each drop striking a slightly different surface, a different depth, a different resonance. There is a low, constant trickle somewhere behind the north wall, a sound like a buried stream working its way through a fissure. And beneath that, a hum. No, not a hum. A thrum. A vibration you feel in your sternum before you hear it with your ears.

That thrum is them.

The fauna. Abundant. The word does not capture it. The air is thick with movement, the walls are alive, the pools are crawling. The dominant life form is small — the size of your thumb, or smaller — and it is everywhere. They are difficult to see clearly in the dimness, but as your eyes adjust, they resolve: segmented bodies, many legs, carapaces that gleam with the same wet sheen as the stone. They are some kind of isopod, perhaps, or something older than taxonomy. Their shells are not grey but a translucent amber, as if they have been stained by the mineral water, and through that translucence you can sometimes see the slow pulse of something internal. They do not scurry. They move with a considered, deliberate pace, like citizens in a city they have inherited. They flow over the stone, around the lichen-stalks, across the surface of the water pools — and here is the strangeness, the first of the true strangeness — they do not break the surface tension. They walk on the water as if it were simply another grade of stone. You watch one cross a pool, its tiny legs dimpling the water but never penetrating, and at the center of the pool it stops. It lowers its head. It drinks, or seems to, and then it continues on.

Every surface is in motion with them. The walls are a mosaic that shifts. The floor is a slow river of amber shells. There are larger forms too, though fewer. In the deepest shadow of the far corner, something the size of a cat is hunched, its shape indistinct but clearly living, clearly breathing. You watch it, and it watches you — not with eyes you can see, but with an attention you can feel. It makes a sound: a soft, wet click, like a stone tapping stone. From elsewhere in the room, faint in the dripping din, a answering click. Then nothing.

Everything here is built. The parameters said *total*, and they meant it. There is no exposed earth, no raw rock face, no natural substrate. The walls are dressed stone. The floor is laid. The ceiling, you realize as your eyes finally find its limits, is not stone at all but a vault of fitted timbers, black with age and dripping with condensation, each beam carved along its length with grooves and channels that seem to guide the water in deliberate paths, feeding it down the walls in thin, distributed sheets that keep the mortar eternally damp. This is not a ruin. This is a machine. A primitive machine, yes — no metal, no power beyond gravity and capillary action and the slow metabolisms of the things that live here — but a machine nonetheless. A water-harvesting system. A habitat. An ecology in a box.

You notice the pools on the floor are not randomly placed. The worn hollows in the flagstones form lines, radiating from the center of the room where a slightly raised platform — a stone table, an altar, a bed? — stands unoccupied. The bone-lichen grows thickest where the grooved channels in the walls deliver the most water. The isopods are thickest where the lichen grows. The larger predator in the corner is positioned down-flow of the densest isopod trails. Every element is in relationship. Every element is placed.

The small scale of the place becomes intimate rather than confining. This is a world, entire. It has its own weather: the constant downward precipitation from the timber ceiling, the microclimate of humidity and coolth, the gentle air currents you can track by watching the sway of the tallest lichen-stalks. It has its own cycles: the gradual filling and emptying of the floor pools as evaporation and condensation trade mass back and forth. It has its own dramas: you see a larger isopod — a patriarch, a mutant, something — seize a smaller one in its mandibles, a brief quiet violence that is over before you fully register it, the victor moving on with its deliberate gait, the victim's shell already being dismantled by three other passing travelers.

The sound shifts. Some subtle pressure changes, some door opened far away that you cannot perceive directly but only through its effect on the air currents. The thrum of the isopods changes pitch. Thousands of tiny legs pause, resume, pause again. The drip-drumming continues, indifferent. You realize you have been holding still for a long time, and that this place has no need of you, and that this is not unsettling but reassuring. The machine continues. The room breathes its wet breath. The amber multitudes go about their slow, certain business. Somewhere in the walls, the hidden stream shifts a pebble in its channel, and the note of the trickle changes slightly, and this is an event, and it is recorded by the lichen, by the isopods, by the dark watcher in the corner, by the stone itself, and you are there to witness it, but you are not required. The place was here before you. It will be here after. It is, in its own strange way, complete.
