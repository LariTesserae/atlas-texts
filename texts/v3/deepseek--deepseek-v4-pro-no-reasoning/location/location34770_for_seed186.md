The first thing that registers is the cold, and then the quiet, and then the fact that both are alive.

You stand on a high, wandering ridge of bare stone, one of many that braid across the plateau like the exposed vertebrae of a buried giant. The elevation is immense—not the jagged, youthful spires of a new mountain range, but the settled, extreme height of an ancient tableland, worn into its final, patient form. The air has that thin, sharp clarity that makes distances deceptive; what looks like a short walk would take half a day. This is open country, regional in scale, a whole world of ridgeline and trough, vale and scarp, extending in all directions until it smudges into a horizon of perpetual mist.

And it is dark. Not the darkness of night—though true night here must be an abyss—but a deep, crepuscular dimness that seems baked into the very sky. A heavy, slow-moving overcast, thick as felted wool, smothers the whole basin from ridge to ridge. The cloud deck is so low and dense it feels less like weather and more like a ceiling, the underside of a great lid set upon the world. Yet it never fully commits to rain. Instead, the moisture lives in the air itself, a pervasive damp that beads on every surface, condenses on stone, and drips with a kind of vegetative patience. The place is not wet, but it is never dry; it weeps slowly.

And from this weeping, life explodes.

The vegetation is lush to the point of delirium, but it is a lushness shaped by the cold, the dark, and the immense empty spaces. There is no soil to speak of—only the ancient, frost-shattered rock—so the plants have become the soil. Thick, sponge-like carpets of cryptogamic crust in deep burgundy and charcoal black drape every horizontal surface, slowly digesting the stone itself. From this living mat, forests rise, but they are not forests as you know them. The trees are vast, solitary things, spaced far apart across the open terrain, each one a world unto itself. Their trunks, thick as houses, are sheathed in wet, furred moss that hangs in dripping beards, and their branches, high overhead in the gloom, spread into flattened, tiered canopies like layered umbrellas, each tier a garden of ferns and lichens and pale, pendulous flowers that glow with a faint bioluminescence.

It is one of the few lights in the world. The other is the industry.

Between the vast, dark trees, in the troughs of the ridgelines where the condensed moisture collects into shallow, cold-black pools, stand remnants of an industrial presence that defies the "untouched" parameter, yet somehow obeys it. You see them now: tall, slender derricks of corroded metal, not built but *grown*, or perhaps grown-over, so thoroughly encrusted with the same dark moss and shelf fungi that they read as geological, not technological. They are no longer separate from the ecology; they are a substrate. From the top of one, a slow, rhythmic piston still moves, a weary, perfect ellipse, pumping not oil but water—drawing the frigid seep from below and releasing it as a fine, constant mist that feeds the hanging gardens of the nearest tree-giant. There is no sound from it, only the motion. The silence is profound, a dense, physical emptiness that swallows the faint creak of the piston, the slow drip of water, the distant, muted rush of air.

That rush is the sky-sifters. In the active, open air above the ridge, huge forms wheel. They are not birds—at least, not entirely. Their bodies are leather-grey bladders and ribbed air-sacs, but their long, sweeping wings are studded with the faintest pinpricks of light, like running lights on an airship. They are grazers of the mist, filtering the clouds for aeroplankton with slow, majestic beats, utterly silent. A pod of them, seven or eight, drifts across the middle distance, their passage a slow, industrial ballet. They are fauna, and they are machine, and the distinction has lost all meaning.

Below them, in the shallow, peaty pools, other things move. You catch ripples in the dark water, the slick gleam of a back, pale and fleshy, breaking the surface. A low, huffing breath, a single cough that echoes strangely in the thin air. Something is hunting, or being hunted. The ecosystem is active, a steady current of life and consumption flowing through the dark and the cold. You see a flash of light from the water, a brief, bioluminescent pulse that mimics the glow of the canopy flowers—a lure, a warning, a language.

The cold sinks deeper into you. You notice, then, the fine white dust that coats everything, a rime not of frost but of something else. You touch it. It is dry, almost gritty. It sifts down constantly from the cloud-lid, a slow, silent snow of desiccated, processed matter, the indigestible remnants of whatever the sky-sifters filter from the air. It settles on the moss, on the water, on the ancient, working piston, and it is the only soil this place will ever know, delivered molecule by molecule from the sky. The land receives it in silence, the quiet of a world that has been running this same cycle for epochs, long past the hum of its original engines, deep into its settled, weathered, living afterlife.
