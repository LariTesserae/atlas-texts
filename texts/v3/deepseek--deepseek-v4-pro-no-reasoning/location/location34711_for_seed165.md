The first thing that reaches you is the sound. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, a deep, grinding roar that lives in the bones of the place, so constant and unwavering that it ceases to be noise and becomes the baseline of existence, the auditory shape of silence. It is the sound of stone singing to itself in a key too low for human ears to properly catch, a vibration that shakes the moisture from the air into brief, glittering curtains before they fall.

You stand on a slope of dark, water-slicked rock, a mountainside so vast it bends the concept of scale. This is not a peak among peaks but a single, monstrous rampart of the world's foundation, rising into a dimness that never clarifies into sky. There is no true sky here, only a direction of light that fades upward into a charcoal nothing, a perpetual twilight cast by no visible sun. The elevation is not measured from sea level—there is no sea—but from some primordial baseline long forgotten, and you are very, very high upon it.

The rock is wet. Not streaming, not flowing, but glistening with an ever-present condensate that beads on every surface. The air is cold, a few degrees above freezing, and so saturated that each breath feels like inhaling a thin, chilly soup. Pockets of actual water exist: shallow, mirror-still basins cupped in the stone's undulations, their surfaces perfectly unbroken save for the occasional, inexplicable ripple that propagates from no visible cause. Every few minutes, without rhythm or warning, a section of the air simply reaches its limit and releases its moisture in a fine, slow drizzle that falls for exactly seventeen seconds and then stops dead, the droplets hanging for an extra heartbeat in defiance before they complete their descent.

There are no plants. That word implies a category that has no anchor here. Instead, across the vastness of the slope, there are *presences*. Sparse, widely scattered things that are not quite lichen and not quite flesh. They are patches of deep, bruise-colored growth, flat against the stone, that seem to absorb the dim light and give back a faint, internal luminescence in the indigo range. They mark the places where the rules are thinnest.

And the rules *are* thin. This is the heart of the reality breakdown. You can feel it as a wrongness in your inner ear, a persistent sense that the world around you is a held breath, a paused gesture. The parameters of the landscape—the damp, the cold, the stone, the dark—are not just conditions but active, eternal participants in an equilibrium so profound it has become a law. Nothing changes. The temperature does not fluctuate. The sound does not waver in pitch or volume. The drips fall on a schedule older than time. The whole system is frozen in a dynamic tension, a perfect, self-maintaining stasis. Erosion hasn't happened here for eons; the rock has weathered to its final form and will hold it forever.

The scattering of the built only emphasizes the emptiness. You are not the first to witness this place, but those who came before are now almost entirely reduced to memory made manifest. Here, a single, low wall of dry-stacked stone, each rock carefully chosen and fitted, runs for ten meters and then stops, ending not in ruin but in *completion*, as if that stretch of wall was all that was ever meant to be. There, a flight of three steps is carved directly into the mountainside, leading up to a flat platform that holds nothing. The steps are worn from use, but no foot has touched them in a duration that makes "century" an infant measurement. In a shallow depression where water has pooled, a series of small, smooth bone-white pebbles has been arranged in a spiral that never blurs, never scatters, preserved by the changeless air.

These remnants suggest no single culture, no unified architecture. They are artifacts of passage, left by things that were not quite human, or perhaps human in a way that has since become impossible. The materials are only local stone, unmortared, unworked beyond their shaping. There is no metal here, no wood, no tech that rises above the cunning of hands and patience. It is a place where only intention leaves a mark, and even then, the mark must align with the stasis to survive.

Fauna is rare and seems to phase in and out of the perceptible world. Occasionally, a long, many-jointed leg, pale and hairless, will extend with infinite slowness from a crevice, taste the air with a feathered antenna, and then retract with a motion that is less a withdrawal and more a *deletion*. Once, far across the open slope of glistening rock, you see a herd of something moving. They are large, translucent boulders of gelatinous flesh, their bodies lit from within by a faint, rosy glow, each one larger than a house. They slide across the stone, leaving a trail of briefly increased dampness, and their passage is utterly silent beneath the grinding roar. They take an hour to cross a hundred meters, and they will likely take a thousand years to reach the next patch of bruise-colored not-lichen they are grazing toward.

The dim light has no source, but it reveals the true scale of the place. You can see for miles across the tilted plain of rock. Other mountains exist, but they are so distant they are only suggestions of deeper darkness against the charcoal void, their peaks lost above, their bases lost below. The air between here and there is an almost perfect emptiness, a volume of cold, wet nothing bounded by stone. The density is so low that your own heartbeat feels like an intrusion, a cluttered, busy sound against the single-note drone of the world.

And yet, the sound is loud. Unignorable. When you stand still, you feel your own thoughts begin to entrain with it, your consciousness wanting to stretch into that eternal chord, to become one more strand in the glacially vibrating stone. The place offers a deal, a profound and terrifying temptation: dissolve, slow down, become part of the equilibrium. The scattered steps and walls are testaments to those who chose to stay just a little too long, who left a mark and then walked into the dimness to join the stone. The weirdness is not an active hostility, but a gentle, insistent gravity of being, a reality that has settled into a single, unwavering state and makes every other state feel, by contrast, like a fever dream.

You breathe the cold soup-air. A seven-second drizzle begins. The sound roars on. The spiral of pebbles in the pool holds its shape perfectly. Nothing has changed. Nothing ever will.
