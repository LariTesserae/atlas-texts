You stand in the sag-room of a dead hive-city, where the walls are not walls but the stacked and folded laminae of something that once breathed.

The space is small by the measure of built things — perhaps thirty paces from the nearer curvature to the farther — but it feels vast because it is mostly hollow. The ceiling vaults upward in a ribbed dome that was never carved but grown, ridge after ridge of pale grey-brown material rising like the palate of a vast mouth frozen mid-syllable. Between the ribs, the gaps are deep and dark, receding into a fretwork of hexagonal cells, each one empty, each one a perfect six-sided absence. The walls are not uniform; they bulge and taper according to some logic of structural flow that predates architecture, the way bone grows denser along lines of stress. Everything here has the look of something that secreted itself into being, layer upon chitinous layer, and then stopped.

The floor is not flat. It sags in a shallow depression toward the center, where a low mound rises — a hump of the same material, but smoother, eroded not by wind or water but by the slow polishing of bodies that are no longer here. Around the mound, the floor is textured with faint radial grooves, as if something dragged itself in circles for centuries. The lowest point of the depression holds a scatter of objects that catch the dim light in a way the walls do not: small, hard, smooth, vaguely oblong, pale amber and cloudy grey. They rest in a shallow drift, the way seeds collect in the lee of a dune. There might be forty of them. There might be sixty.

Nothing grows here — not in the sense of green things. The “vegetation” of this place, if the word can be stretched that far, consists of thin pale filaments that lace across the ceiling ribs like the mycelium of a fungus that has gone dormant. They are the color of old ivory, and so sparse that you can count the strands without losing track. They do not move. They do not seem alive, though they are not obviously dead either. In a few places, they have detached and hang down like broken harp strings, trembling almost imperceptibly, though the air is dead-still.

That air is cool — not cold enough to see breath, but cool enough that the skin on your arms wakes up and stays awake. The temperature is steady, unvarying, the kind of cool that speaks of enormous thermal mass and no sun. There has never been sun here. The only light is a dim grey-green phosphorescence that emanates from patches on the ceiling ribs in slow, arrhythmic pulses — a faint glow that waxes and wanes over the course of a minute or two, never bright enough to cast a shadow, never dark enough to leave the space fully blind. When it strengthens, you can see that the walls are mottled with darker whorls, like fingerprints on a massive scale. When it fades, the room becomes a diagram of itself in near-blackness, with only the pale filaments and those amber oblongs holding onto the light a moment longer.

The silence is not empty. It is a positive presence, a pressure against the ears. No water drips. No wind moves. No insect scratches. But there are sounds so quiet they might be your own blood moving: a very low, very slow thrum that comes and goes, felt more in the soles of the feet than heard, as if something far below still pulses. And occasionally — once every few minutes, if you stand long enough — a faint dry click, like a tendon snapping, from somewhere deep in the walls. Then nothing again.

The place is young, in geological terms. The surfaces are sharp-edged; the hexagonal cells still have crisp rims, not yet blunted by any erosive force. No dust has accumulated beyond the fine powder that grinding bodies left behind. The whole structure has the raw, fresh look of something recently completed — or recently abandoned moments after completion. And yet it feels ancient because it is so utterly still, because nothing alive remains in a space designed for teeming life.

What was alive here is suggested by the architecture itself. The sag-room is one chamber in what must be a larger complex — you can see openings high in the walls, dark tunnels angling upward at steep pitches, their entrances rimmed with the same smooth polish as the central mound. The scale of these tunnels is wrong for humans: too narrow for an adult to crawl through upright, but too wide for comfort, as if made by things that moved on many legs or no legs at all, things that flowed rather than walked. The whole room is a junction, a confluence, a place where paths met and something happened — something cyclical, something that left those radial grooves and that polished hump and that scatter of amber seeds.

The strange thing — the truly strange thing, the detail that takes the mind a moment to register and then refuses to let go — is that the walls are not solid. They are hollow. You can tell because some of the hexagonal cells are missing their inner faces, revealing the space between the inner and outer layers of the dome: a narrow gap, no wider than a hand's breadth, extending back into absolute darkness. And in that darkness, something shifts when the light pulses. It is not a creature. It is the same grey-brown material as the walls, but it is folded differently — convoluted, pleated, packed into the interstitial space like a brain into a skull. And it is moving. Not breathing. Not twitching. Growing. So slowly that standing here for an hour you would see nothing change, but the pattern of it is different every time the light swells, the convolutions slightly rearranged, as if the whole structure is still thinking, still processing, still building itself cell by cell out of the hollow places.

The city is not dead. It is waiting.
