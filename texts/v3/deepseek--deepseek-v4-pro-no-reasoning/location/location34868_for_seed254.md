The entrance is a sphincter of woven root, a mouth in the hillside that breathes. You descend because the path goes down, because the stone stairs—cut long ago, now bearded with pale, searching root-hairs—leave no other direction. The steps are wet. Not with standing water, but with a constant seep, a sweat that beads on the stone and slicks the ancient treads until they shine like polished liver. Each footfall makes a sound you first mistake for a squelch in mud, but it’s more precise than that: a wet, adhesive parting, a thousand tiny root-membranes releasing their grip on your sole. The air itself is a weight, warm and close. It doesn't just sit on your skin; it settles into your lungs with the intimacy of a living thing, dense with spores and the exhalations of a biome that has not known open sky for centuries.

You’ve entered the Quieting Vats.

This is a place built, not grown—or perhaps grown so long ago that the distinction has lost all meaning. The "room" (though it feels inaccurate, a word for something with corners and a floor) is roughly dome-shaped, the size of a small chapel or a large family tomb. Every surface is plant. Not covered in it, but comprised of it, as if the structural materials—brick, iron, concrete—were eaten and replaced cell by cell, leaving only the shape of human intention behind a facade of living tissue. The walls are a solid, seamless mass of rhizome, a tight-packed conglomerate of buff-coloured tubers, each the size of a child's fist, pressed together with such force they have deformed into polyhedrons, their junctures sealed with a dark, resinous exudate. This is the substrate. This is the 'what everything else is made of.' From this tuber-masonry, the secondary growth erupts.

Fungi. Not the fauna you might expect; there are no insects here, no skittering things, no worms. The Quieting Vats are aggressively, absolutely botanical. The fungi are a city of their own. Bracket fungi, wide as dinner plates and thin as parchment, tier down the curving walls in overlapping shingles, their upper surfaces a dusky, velvety brown, their pale undersides dropping a perpetual, microscopic rain of spores that sifts through the warm air like a slow-motion mist. They build the space, articulating the walls into shelves, buttresses, apses. From the ceiling, a hundred fibrous, dangling structures descend—the aerial roots of something above, or the mycelial tapestries of something local. They are thick as hawsers, braided, dripping with condensation that gathers and falls with the steady, rhythmic plink of a leaking roof after a storm. The sound is a constant, a low, percussive thrum made of a thousand of these individual droplets hitting puddled stone, soaking into spongy moss, pattering on the leathery caps of the bracket fungi. It is loud. Not the loudness of a factory, but the loudness of a waterfall heard from inside a cave, a pervasive white noise that fills the skull and erodes thought.

The industrial soul of the place lingers in its geometry and its purpose. In the center of the chamber, a procession of forms stand like mute engines. They are vats. Perhaps they were once steel; now they are cradled in a lattice of lignified vine, a woven wickerwork that has embraced and immobilized them. Fat, pale pipes, their original galvanized surface mottled with a leprosy of lichen, erupt from the tuber-wall, enter the vats, and emerge again to plunge back into the living masonry. Valves, their iron wheels now hubs for radiating shelf fungi, sit in a state of eternal stasis. The process is slow now, a ghost in the machine. The vats themselves are full. The liquid within—if it is a liquid and not a clear, heavy gas—is the source of the dim light. It is a low, amber luminescence, the colour of weak tea held to a candle, and it pulses. Not rhythmically, but in a kind of lazy, meandering flux, a slow brightening and dimming that takes a minute or more for a single cycle, a patient, metabolic breathing. This is the only light, and it does not illuminate so much as it saturates, a stain of visibility that leaves the corners in absolute, velvety black.

The air moves. You feel it as a weak, warm, wet pressure against your cheek, pushing from a low archway on the far side of the chamber. It carries a smell that completes the image: not the rot of death, but the deep, sweet, cloying scent of fermentation. It is a bakery abandoned to a swamp; it is yeast and wet hay and the bruised, peppery perfume of a thousand obscure, sappy herbs. There is a sense of a process completing itself across a span of time that has no regard for you whatsoever. The loudness is not a warning or a welcome. It is simply the sound of a system in balance, a miniature, subterranean urban infrastructure that has been fully reclaimed, its bones repurposed, its purpose subsumed into the slow, moist breathing of a world made entirely of water and plants. The stones weep. The fungi spore. The great braided roots drip. The vats, in their dim, amber silence, continue their patient work, a production line with no product but the steady state of its own existence. You are in the marsh's engine room, the industrial heart of a bog, and it is, in its own slow way, perfectly normal. There is nowhere to sit but the wet stone, and nothing to hear but the water, falling and falling.
