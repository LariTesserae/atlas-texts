The room is a sphere fifteen feet across, and it is old.

Not old in the way of abandoned factories or rusted machinery, though those things are here too. Old in the way of a canyon worn by a river that no longer remembers its name. The walls—and there is no true floor or ceiling, only a continuous inward-curving surface—are built of brass and riveted iron, every inch worked by hands or tools long since dissolved into the same deep time that has smoothed the edges of every bolt head and softened the angles of every seam. The metal is dark with patina, streaked green and black and a deep, oxblood brown where copper leaches through. You could press your thumb into the surface and feel how the material has grown porous, almost spongy, like bone that has lain in the sea.

Suspended at the center of the sphere, somehow held without visible support, is a tangle of roots.

They are thick as thighs and slender as hair, braiding around themselves in a continuous slow writhe, and from their surface spring leaves—thousands of them, glossy and dark green, heart-shaped and palm-sized and each one trembling faintly with a motion that is not from any wind. The roots descend from a puckered opening at the top of the sphere and plunge downward into a pool at the bottom, a body of water that fills the lower third of the room. The surface of the water is perfectly still, black as oil, and it reflects the roots and leaves in a way that doubles the mass of vegetation, makes it seem infinite, a forest folded into a globe of brass.

The air is warm, close to body temperature, and it carries water so heavily that breathing feels like drinking. Moisture beads on every surface—on the walls, on the leaves, on the delicate iron catwalks that ring the interior in three narrow bands, their railings reduced by rust to lace, their grates sagging in the middle. Condensation drips constantly, not a rain but a ceaseless weeping, drops falling from leaf-tip to leaf-tip, from catwalk to water, from water back into water, a soundscape of small percussive notes layered so thickly it becomes a texture rather than a rhythm: *plik-plik-tok-plit-tok-tok-plik*.

This is the loudest thing. But there is more.

The roots themselves make sound. A low, sub-audible creaking, like the hull of a ship under pressure, issues from the heart of the tangle, and occasionally a sharper crack splits the air—the sound of woody fiber tearing and re-knitting as the roots adjust their grip on whatever they are holding. And beneath everything, a steady thrum, a mechanical pulse that you feel in your sternum before you hear it, the heartbeat of whatever engine still operates somewhere behind the walls or below the pool.

The light is ordinary daylight, or near enough—a soft, diffuse glow that seems to emanate from the walls themselves, with no visible source, the color of sun through a thin overcast. It catches the water droplets and makes the whole sphere glitter, turns the leaves translucent at their edges, picks out the ghostly shapes of things that live here.

Because this place is not empty. The fauna is abundant, strange, and wholly adapted.

Pale, translucent frogs, no larger than a thumbnail, cling to the undersides of leaves. Their bodies are so clear you can see the slow pulse of their internal organs, the dark bead of an eye turning to watch you. They do not croak; they make a sound like two pebbles tapped together, a ceramic *click-click-click* that echoes from all sides. Larger creatures move through the root mass—something long and sinuous, dark against dark, that you glimpse only as a shift in the pattern of shadows. Perhaps an eel, or a snake with too many joints, or something that has no name in a world outside this sphere.

On the catwalks, colonies of small brass-colored beetles with iridescent shells move in slow, coordinated streams, their bodies chiming faintly as they brush against the railings. They are cleaning something—a pale lichen that grows in patches on the metal—and as they feed they secrete a substance that hardens into new metal, repairing the corrosion as fast as it forms, a million-year cycle of decay and renewal that has reached a perfect, trembling equilibrium.

The water, when it ripples—and it ripples now, a ring expanding outward from where a frog has dropped from a leaf—reveals what lies beneath. Submerged machinery, vast and incomprehensible, gears the size of cartwheels frozen mid-mesh, pipes that coil like sleeping serpents, a control panel with dials whose needles still quiver at the edge of some unreadable threshold. The pool is not deep, perhaps three feet, but the darkness of the water and the complexity of the machinery give it the vertiginous depth of an ocean trench. Vents in the submerged brass exhale a constant stream of bubbles, each one rising with a wobbling slowness through the heavy water, each one carrying a faint smell of ozone and hot oil.

This sphere is not a natural place. Every square inch has been made, designed, assembled with an intention so remote it has become ecology. The roots were planted here, or they seeded themselves through some crack in the world and were simply allowed to remain, and now they are as much part of the machine as the gears below. The frogs were introduced, perhaps, to control some insect that once threatened the lichen that feeds the beetles that repair the metal. Or perhaps no one introduced anything; perhaps the sphere simply called out into the world and the world answered, filling the space with whatever could survive the warmth and the water and the endless deep vibration.

The smell is green and metallic at once—wet leaves, copper, a trace of animal musk, the clean-sharp bite of ozone. When you breathe deep, you taste the age of the place, a flavor like old stone and older rain.

There is no door visible. If you are here, you have always been here, or you arrived by some means the sphere declines to reveal. The catwalks offer nowhere to go, no ladder, no hatch. They simply circle, observation decks for a process that has been observing itself for millennia. You are not the first visitor; on one of the iron bands, scratched into the corrosion with a tool or a fingernail, there is a date in a script you do not recognize, and beside it a single word that might be a name, or a prayer, or a warning.

The roots continue to writhe. A frog clicks. A bubble rises and bursts. The engine thrum never wavers. This small, dense, saturated world spins on in its hidden pocket of existence, entirely indifferent to you, entirely complete, a terrarium built by hands that turned to soil before the first human ever struck flint to steel.
