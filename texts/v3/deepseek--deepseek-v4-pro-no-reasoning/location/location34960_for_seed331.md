The air itself seems to sweat.

You stand in a shallow basin, perhaps a hundred meters across—no larger than a cathedral nave, bounded by low, water-slicked ridges of dark stone that rise barely above head height. The stone is what remains of something older than mountains, worn to nubs by an erosion so patient and complete that the ridges no longer have edges. They are smooth as soap, glistening in the bright, directionless light, and everywhere they weep.

Water isn't flowing so much as condensing, continuously. It beads on every surface. It runs in thin, silent sheets down the rounded walls. The ground underfoot is not mud exactly, but a kind of living sponge—a mat of vegetation that has given up on structure, a single contiguous organism that carpets the basin floor in a layer perhaps ankle-deep. It is the color of oxidized copper, blue-green and glistening, and it exhales moisture with a slow, almost imperceptible rhythm, as if breathing. Step on it and it depresses with a wet, sucking sound, then slowly rebounds. It is warm. Everything is warm—fever-warm, mammalian-warm—the stone, the water, the thick, humid air that wraps around you like a second skin.

The light has no obvious source. It is simply bright, a luminous saturation that fills the basin evenly, casting no shadows on the wet stone, but glinting off every droplet as if the whole place were jeweled. The sky—if there is a sky—is not visible. Above the basin, the light diffuses into a haze of suspended water droplets, a perpetual, low-hanging mist that caps the space like a vaulted ceiling. The effect is of being inside a room, a single enclosed feature, a grotto without a roof.

And you are not alone.

The fauna is not loud—the sound parameter says mostly quiet, and it is—but it is *overwhelming* in its density, in its sheer presence. They are everywhere, but they do not crowd. There is a sparseness to their distribution, a respect for negative space, a scattering. Most are small, beetle-like things with carapaces that shift through the metallic spectrum, blues and purples and oil-slick rainbows. They cling to the weeping walls, motionless for long minutes, then detach and tumble slowly through the air, not flying but somehow falling sideways, navigating with minute adjustments of transparent, filigreed vanes. They make no sound. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, a silent constellation moving at the pace of drifting pollen.

Larger things move through the mat on the floor. They are difficult to see at first—the same copper-green as the vegetation, long and low and segmented, with too many legs. They undulate rather than walk, waves of motion passing through their bodies as they part the breathing carpet. One surfaces near your foot, a flat, eyeless head rising just above the mat, dripping, and then it is gone again, submerged in the living weave. The mat ripples where it passed, then stills.

The sparse built elements are what gives the strangeness its edge. Scattered around the basin, half-swallowed by the vegetation mat, are objects. At first they seem like fallen rocks, but their shapes resolve into something else. A curved section of what might have been a housing emerges, its surface not stone but a material that seems to shift under your gaze, not actively, but as if it were caught between two states, solid and something else, and could not decide. It is warm, warmer than the stone, and touching it sends a faint, deep hum through your bones, a frequency you feel rather than hear. Another object nearby is smaller, a perfect torus of the same material, and from its center, filaments of water are being drawn upward in a continuous, silent thread, defying gravity in a thin, unwavering column that disappears into the mist above. You watch it for a full minute. It does not waver. It does not drip. It simply pulls water from the air and sends it somewhere else.

There are other artifacts, all partial, all of a technology that operates by rules adjacent to but not described by current physics. They are not machines in any recognizable sense. They are more like… concentrations. Places where the local laws of matter have been given a different instruction set. One such concentration, a low, circular platform of something that looks like dark glass but flexes slightly under weight, is entirely free of the vegetation mat. The mat grows up to its edge and stops, forming a perfect perimeter, as if the platform had simply told it to stop, and it had.

The air smells of metal and life, of wet copper and something sweeter, like ozone and nectar. The temperature never varies. The light never varies. The weeping never stops.

The processes here are multiple but glacially slow. The water cycle is internal, a closed loop of condensation and transpiration and the silent, impossible threads lifting moisture into the mist. The beetle-things drift, and occasionally one will intersect with the path of another, and they will pause, touching vanes, exchanging something—data, nutrients, intent—before separating. Predators there must be, because sometimes one of the long, segmented things will surface with a beetle struggling in a grip of fine, hairlike appendages, but the struggle is soundless, and the beetle is pulled under, and the mat seals over the spot without a mark. An ecosystem in motion, but a quiet one, a dense one, a cycle turning within its small, bounded cathedral.

You become aware, after a time, of a sound. It is the only sound, and it is so low, so intermittent, that it barely registers. It is a chime. Not a bell, but the sound of water dropping onto something resonant, far away, perhaps on the other side of the basin, muffled by the mist. It happens irregularly, a single clear note, and then silence, for a minute, or five, or ten. Then another. The intervals are not random—there is a pattern there, a long, slow code being spelled out in the language of droplets and the deep, patient listening of whatever receives them. The sound is the one thing that seems to belong to the scale of these artifacts, a remnant of their purpose still being enacted, slowly, in the bright and weeping stillness.
