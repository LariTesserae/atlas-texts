The first thing that reaches you is the sound, or the shape of it—a low, constant hum that is almost not a sound at all, felt more in the chest and the soles of the feet than heard. It is the voice of the Spillways, though no one here has ever called them that. No one has ever called them anything. No one is here.

You are standing on a surface of what seems at first like bare rock, but is not. Underfoot is a substance dark as graphite, faintly warm to the touch, with a grain so fine it feels like polished soapstone. Look closer and you see the striations: flow-lines frozen in place, layer upon layer, a palimpsest of motion. This is deposition stone, left behind by water that no longer flows but still, somehow, marks the land with its memory. The rock is riddled with hairline cracks that weep no moisture, only air—air that arrives already breathed, tasting faintly of rust and salt and something militant, something that speaks of combustion.

The terrain rises around you in long, gentle swells, not hills so much as the ghost-shapes of sediment deltas from a river system that operated at a scale you have no frame for. The swells are hundreds of feet high and miles long, furred with a low-lying scrub that has the look of lichen but the internal architecture of something more complex. Each plant—if plant is the word—is a dense rosette of charcoal-colored fronds, no two exactly alike, their surfaces micropitted like the skin of a shark. They grow in distinct, widely spaced clumps, each clump ringed by a bare zone of paler stone where nothing else takes root. Between clumps the ground is empty for twenty, thirty, forty feet, a negative space as intentional as the plants themselves. The spacing gives the landscape a deliberate, cursive quality, as if some vast intelligence had written a sentence across the plateau and then forgotten it halfway through.

It is warm. The warmth does not come from a sun—there is no sun here, or else the sky itself is a kind of perpetual twilight in which a sun would be an intrusion. The darkness overhead is not the darkness of night but of depth: a sealed, sovereign dark that has never known interruption. And yet there is light, of a kind. It rises from the ground, a faint bioluminescence that pulses in slow, asynchronous waves across the stone, traveling from one clump of vegetation to the next like a slow conversation. The fronds themselves hold a dim emberglow at their centers, and when they are touched by one of these traveling pulses they flare briefly brighter before subsiding. The overall effect is of a landscape breathing light.

Water is here, but it is a trickster. You can smell it—the mineral tang of wet stone, the green-sweet reek of something metabolizing dampness—but you cannot see it. The air is thick with humidity, condensing in tiny iridescent beads on the fronds and on your skin. Every few minutes, without pattern or warning, a fine, warm mist descends from nowhere, a fog so light it feels like breath on the back of your neck. It lasts seconds, then vanishes, leaving the stone briefly slick. Then the slickness evaporates with unnatural speed, drawn back into the stone, into the air, into the vegetation, cycling through a hundred small and temporary reservoirs that never quite become standing water. The ecology here has learned to drink without a cup.

And the ecology is busy.

The fauna moves through the scattered clumps like punctuation through a sentence—always with purpose, always with distance. You see them first as motion in the corner of your eye: small, dark, angular bodies navigating the negative space between plants with the liquid precision of fish in a current. Up close they resolve into creatures built along the lines of insects, but their scale is wrong, their joint-count too high, their movements too deliberate. They are the size of small dogs, with segmented bodies sheathed in the same dark deposition-stone material as the ground, as if they had been carved from the landscape itself and then set in motion. Their legs—six, eight, sometimes more—end not in feet but in fine, hair-like filaments that brush the stone so lightly they leave no sound. They are scavengers, or maybe farmers; you watch one pause at the edge of a vegetation clump, extend a long, articulated proboscis, and delicately tap the stone-ring boundary before withdrawing. The fronds at the center of the clump flare brighter. The creature moves on.

Larger things move in the deeper dark between the rises. You cannot see them but you can track them by the way the ground-light dims under their passage, by the sudden silence of the smaller creatures when a shadow passes, by the faint, subsonic rumble that reaches you through the stone a heartbeat before their arrival. They are big enough that they displace air, and you feel them as a change in pressure, a disturbance in the humid warmth. Whatever they are, they are predator-shaped. The whole landscape knows it. The pulse-light quickens when they pass. The mist falls more often. The smaller fauna freeze or scatter, and in their scattering you see the pattern of the place reveal itself: they retreat toward the vegetation clumps, but only to the ring-boundaries. They will not cross into the center. Something about the plants defends them—chemical, electrical, something—and the predators will not approach. The clumps are islands of truces in a landscape of slow, patient hunger.

Rising from the far distance, half-lost in the perpetual gloom, is the source of the hum. It is not a building—there are no buildings here, nothing constructed by any hand you would recognize as a hand—but it is infrastructure nonetheless, a formation of the deposition stone that has been shaped or grown or eroded into a configuration that serves a purpose. It is a ridgeline two miles long and half a mile high, its face worked into a series of vertical flutes and chambers, a natural pipe organ blown by wind that does not exist. The wind is coming from inside it. The hum is its exhalation, a steady, industrial note that speaks of engines and furnaces and the vast, slow machinery of a world that processes itself. The mist, you realize, is its breath too, and the warmth, and maybe even the dark. The whole landscape is an exhaust system for something beneath.

The plateau you stand on is the high shoulder of a much larger form, a continental mass that slopes away to invisible distances. Far off, the ground-light reveals the outlines of other ridges, other swells, other patterns of clumped vegetation stretching to a horizon that swallows itself. The scale is such that you cannot tell if you are on a landmass or inside a chamber. The sky could be a mile high or a thousand; the dark gives no clues, only the steady, patient weight of a space that has been itself for a very long time.

This is not a place that was made. It is a place that happened, a steady-state accident, an equilibrium so old it has forgotten the forces that set it in motion. The water that shaped the stone is gone. The vegetation has settled into its sparse, deliberate spacing, each clump a small sovereignty. The creatures move in their inherited patterns, predator and prey and something in between, and the great ridgeline-organ hums its one-chord song, and the mist comes and goes like the breath of something sleeping. Nothing is built, but everything is manufactured. Nothing is artificial, but everything is a machine.

You stand in the space between clumps, in the negative space that is the real subject of the composition, and you feel the hum through your bones. It sounds like industry. It sounds like a factory running at full tilt. It sounds like the turning of the world.

And no one hears it but you.
