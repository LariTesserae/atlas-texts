The city of Keld hangs in the throat of a canyon so deep and old that its walls have forgotten they were ever cleft by water. What remains of the river that carved this place now travels as a perpetual cold sweat down the rock faces, a billion capillary threads of moisture that never quite coalesce into streams. The damp is interstitial — it lives in the space between dry stone and wet air, condensing on iron railings, beading on window glass, seeping into the mortar of buildings that have learned to breathe it.

And against all odds, this moisture has birthed a riot of green.

The vegetation here does not root in soil. There is no soil. Instead, it clings to every surface the way breath fogs a mirror. Mosses in fifty-seven catalogued shades of verdigris and emerald blanket the oldest walls, their fronds so fine they read as fur. Liverworts spread in liver-colored rosettes across concrete. Ferns uncurl from drainpipes, from the gaps between riveted steel plates, from the mouths of decommissioned vent shafts. Hanging gardens descend tier after tier down the canyon walls — not planted, not tended, but simply occurring, as though the city were a terrarium that had been sealed for centuries and allowed to find its own equilibrium. The dominant species is something the locals call "wirevine," a tough, almost cartilaginous creeper with leaves the size of dinner plates that follows the path of electrical conduits, wrapping them in spiraling green sleeves that drip condensation in steady, metronomic beats.

The cold is not a biting cold. It’s a settled cold, the kind that has been here so long it no longer feels like weather. It’s the thermal inertia of billions of tons of stone that haven’t seen direct sunlight since before the last ice age. The canyon runs deep enough that the sun, when it penetrates at all, comes as a brief blade of pale light that sweeps down the eastern wall for perhaps forty minutes each morning, then withdraws. The rest of the day is dim — a high, silver-gray luminescence reflected and re-reflected between walls of damp stone and wet foliage and the ever-present mist that rises from the canyon floor.

Temperature: cool enough that breath mists year-round. Cool enough that rust never quite dries, that leather grows pale blooms of mold within a week, that the inhabitants wear oiled wool and waxed cotton as a matter of course. Their skin has adapted a particular pallor, their lungs a particular capacity for air that is 90% saturated.

The city itself climbs. This is the meaning of "extreme elevation" here — not that it sits atop a mountain, but that it occupies a vertical slash in the world, and its inhabitants live stacked. Buildings are not built on the ground; they are built *into* the walls, cantilevered from ledges, suspended from cables, connected by a webwork of catwalks and stairways and the occasional funicular railway whose cars sway alarmingly when the canyon winds gust. The oldest structures, at the deepest levels, are carved directly into the rock — chambers and passages that predate the city's formal founding, smoothed by millennia of damp and touch until their walls feel almost organic, like the interior of some vast petrified creature.

Above them, the industrial layers: the brick mills and iron foundries of the city’s second age, now mostly converted to other uses but still exuding their original purpose in the black patina of soot and grease that stains their walls. Higher still, the residential towers of the third age, tall narrow tenements of riveted steel and wired glass, their balconies overgrown with moss, their roofs dripping with cultivated fern gardens. And highest of all, near the rim, the power station and the vent stacks of the deep factories, their emissions mingling with the natural mist to create a perpetual atmosphere of diffused particulate.

The factories. They are what keep Keld alive, though "alive" is a relative term. They are not the hellish, smoke-belching mills of the lowlands; these are precision works, machining shops and instrument foundries that produce the fine tolerances and specialized alloys that the world above values precisely because they cannot be made in drier, warmer climes. The city’s damp, its cold, its stable barometric pressure — these are not liabilities but conditions, and industry has adapted to them. Engines run on sealed steam loops. Electrical systems are triple-insulated against the moisture. The characteristic sound of the city is not the roar of furnaces but a low, pervasive hum: the sound of dehumidifiers, operating everywhere, at all scales, from the industrial units that keep the machine halls merely damp rather than flooded, to the domestic units that are each household’s most prized possession. Their collective drone is the city’s baseline, a sixty-cycle om that vibrates in the teeth and the sternum.

Above this hum, other sounds emerge if you listen. The drip of water, everywhere, in every register from the high ping of droplets on metal to the low thud of heavier falls on moss. The distant, arrhythmic clatter of the funicular. The soft hiss of mist moving through the canyon. The wet rustle of wind in ten thousand hanging gardens. Footfalls on catwalks, always muted by the slick of moisture. Human voices, similarly muffled, as though the air itself absorbs speech. The city is mostly quiet because sound does not carry far here; it is absorbed by moss and mist and the vast, indifferent bulk of the walls.

The canyon is not a single channel but a networked labyrinth, the work of a river system that once braided through these highlands before the land rose and the water cut deeper. Side canyons branch off the main gorge, and these too are inhabited, connected by bridges whose spans disappear into fog. Looking out from a mid-level balcony, you see not a single wall but a layered complexity of rock faces and structures, some near enough to touch, others receding into silver-gray distance. The city's population is perhaps forty thousand, but they are distributed across this three-dimensional maze rather than concentrated in any one quarter. This creates the paradox of density: the built environment is crowded, structures nearly touching, stacked five or six deep in some places, but the overall impression is not claustrophobic. The canyon is simply too vast. You can look out across half a kilometer of mist-shrouded void to the far wall, where lights burn in windows like stars in a nebula.

The fauna are rare but not absent, and they have adapted in ways that seem improbable until you see them. There are lizards that never descend to the ground, living their entire lives on vertical rock faces, their toe pads evolved into sucker-discs that can grip wet stone. They are pale, almost translucent, and they hunt the small insects that breed in the moss mats. There are birds — a species of swift that nests in the drainpipes and funnels, its call a thin high chitter that echoes in the narrow spaces. There are larger things too, though they are rarely seen. Something lives in the deepest, oldest tunnels, the ones that flood intermittently when the water table shifts. The locals call them "silt-dogs," and describe them as blind, pale, furred with moss. A few carcasses have been recovered over the years, washed up during particularly heavy flows, but no living specimen has ever been clearly observed. They are a rumor that occasionally leaves a body.

The rules of this place are the rules of equilibrium. Nothing dries, nothing warms, nothing grows fast. The wirevine that covers the conduits might advance a centimeter a day. Rust proceeds at its own deliberate pace, and so maintenance is a constant, low-grade requirement of existence — every surface painted and repainted, every seal checked, every dehumidifier serviced. The inhabitants of Keld have a particular relationship with time. They do not rush. They do not expect rapid change. They inhabit a world that has been settling into its final form for eons, and they understand themselves as a temporary perturbation, a brief bloom of activity in a place that will continue its slow, damp entropy long after they are gone.

The built environment reflects this. It is layered, accreted, full of repurposed spaces and abandoned corners. A factory floor becomes a hydroponic garden becomes a school becomes a storage depot for dehumidifier parts. A carved stone chamber from the first age becomes a cistern becomes a shrine becomes a music hall, its acoustics perfect, its walls sweating a slow, sacred dew. The city is a palimpsest, and its inhabitants read the layers like a text: here the original masons of the deep age, here the brick-builders of the furnace age, here the steel-erectors of the modern age, here the moss, here the rust, here the damp eternal.

The light changes as you descend. Near the rim, the mist thins enough during the day that you can see a general brightness, a sense of sky, though the sun itself remains hidden. The factories here are more traditional, their stacks venting into open air. But as you go deeper, the light becomes entirely artificial: electric lamps in blue-white and sodium-orange, their light reflecting from wet surfaces in a thousand glittering points. At the very bottom, where the river still runs as a thin, cold thread through channels cut in the bedrock, the only illumination comes from bioluminescent fungi that glow a faint green-blue on the walls, and from the headlamps of the occasional maintenance worker checking the flood pumps.

This is Keld: a vertical city in a wound in the world, cold and damp and dense with green life, humming with the sound of machines that hold the wet at bay, settled into its rhythms for so long that it has become less a place of human habitation and more a collaboration between industry, vegetation, and the ancient patience of stone. The mist rises. The wirevine grows. The dehumidifiers hum. Somewhere in the deep tunnels, a silt-dog opens its blind eyes in the dark and tastes the water for changes.
