I stand at the edge of the Groaning Plain, where the world rises toward the sky but never quite breaks through. The elevation is high—not mountainous in the jagged sense, but plateau-like, a broad shoulder of the earth pushed up over millions of years and left here, sweating.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. It doesn't pool or flow in visible streams, but it weeps from every surface. The ground underfoot is a dark, close-grained stone—something between basalt and slate—and it's slick with perpetual condensation. The air itself is wet, heavy, almost gelatinous with moisture. At this altitude, thin air should be dry, but instead it clings to your skin, beads on exposed surfaces, drips from every ledge and overhang. The rocks weep. Not metaphorically. The stone faces are streaked with constant slow trickles, water emerging from pores too fine to see, gathering into droplets that catch the brutal light and fall with a sound that adds to the roar.

The sound. My god, the sound.

It's deafening here, and it took me a long time to understand why. There's no wind to speak of—the atmosphere is too heavy, too saturated to move easily—but the plain is riddled with holes. Thousands, millions of vertical shafts and horizontal fissures and angled cracks, all of them breathing. As water seeps through the porous stone, air is displaced in constant, rhythmic pulses. Each hole is a throat. Each fissure is a whistle-stop. Together they create a drone that isn't white noise but something more structured, almost choral. Deep bass notes from the wider shafts, high keening from the needle-thin cracks. The sound doesn't rise and fall. It's continuous, unchanging, a wall of tone that presses against the eardrums until you can't tell if you're hearing it or feeling it in your bones.

This has been going on for a very long time. The erosion patterns tell that story clearly. There's nothing sharp here. Every edge has been softened, every corner rounded not by violence but by patience. The holes are perfectly smooth-lipped. The channels between them are worn into gentle curves. Whatever process shaped this place, it's reached equilibrium—a steady state where the water moves at the same rate it always has, where the air pulses at the same frequency, where nothing changes because everything is exactly as it has been for geological ages.

And there's nothing living here. Nothing green, nothing growing, nothing that photosynthesizes. The vegetation parameter is absolute zero. At first, this seems impossible—so much water, so much available surface area—but the light explains it. The light is ferocious. Bright to the point of violence, a near-constant blaze that seems to come from everywhere at once. The high elevation means thinner atmosphere above, and something about the quality of the light is wrong. It's too white, too flat, lacking the wavelengths that plants would need. Or perhaps it contains wavelengths that prevent life. The stone itself seems bleached in places, almost luminous, as if the light has been baking it for so long that it's begun to absorb and re-emit photons in a slow, mineral fluorescence.

The plain extends for what must be miles, and every inch of it is the same. This is the density—the packed quality that the parameters hinted at. There is no empty space here, no rest, no gap in the pattern. Wherever you look, there are holes. Some are big enough to swallow a person, perfectly circular and plunging straight down into darkness. Others are so small they're barely visible, clustered like pores on skin. They're arranged without apparent order, but they're not random either—there's a regularity to their spacing, as if the stone itself is organized into some kind of tissue. Between the holes, the surface is not smooth but dimpled, every inch of it textured with smaller and smaller openings, a fractal descent into perforation.

I haven't mentioned fauna because there's almost none. But there are things in the holes.

Rarely—very rarely—something moves. A pale shape, slow and deliberate, emerging from one shaft and disappearing into another. They're hard to see clearly because the light is so bright and the contrast with the dark openings is so extreme, but I've caught glimpses. They're long, segmented, many-legged. Not insects—nothing chitinous or articulated in that way. They're softer, more like exposed nerves or the roots of something that doesn't exist yet. They have no eyes that I can see, but they navigate the plain with uncanny precision, flowing from hole to hole like fluid through a circuit board. They seem to be part of the system rather than visitors to it. When one passes near me, the tone of the nearest holes shifts slightly, a harmonic adjustment, as if my presence is being registered and incorporated into the drone.

The temperature is cool but not cold. The constant moisture and the high elevation should make it freezing, but the stone holds warmth—geothermal, perhaps, or just the accumulated heat of that merciless light. The air sits at a temperature that feels like a cave's breath, steady and underground, even though I'm standing in full brightness on a high plateau under an open sky.

There are no structures here. Nothing built. No artifacts. The tech level is pre-tool, unless you count the plain itself as a kind of machine, which it might be. The holes, the water, the sound, the light—it all interlocks too perfectly to feel purely accidental. But the order isn't imposed. It's emergent. The plain has organized itself over time, water finding paths, air finding voices, until the whole system settled into this state of frozen equilibrium. It's a landscape that has finished becoming itself.

The weirdness is subtle. It's not a place that announces its strangeness with overt impossibility. It's a place that's just slightly, persistently wrong. The way the light doesn't warm as it should. The way the water moves without accumulating. The way the sound forms almost-music without any intelligence composing it. The way the holes seem to breathe. The way the rare pale things move with purpose but without apparent biology. It's a landscape that abides by rules, but not quite the rules I know.

I find a place to sit—a broad, flat stone between two larger shafts—and the moisture immediately soaks through my clothes. The drone fills my skull. The light presses against my closed eyelids. I can feel the plain vibrating through the stone, a subsonic hum beneath the audible roar. After a while, I notice something.

The holes near me are changing their pitch.

Very slowly. Almost imperceptibly. But my presence here, my weight, my body heat, my breathing—I'm a foreign object in a system at equilibrium. And the system is adjusting. Not rejecting me. Just... compensating. Finding a new balance that includes me.

I should probably leave before the balance becomes permanent. Before I become another feature of the plain, weeping water and singing through stone.

The pale things are watching. I can't see them, but I know. The holes around me are too evenly spaced now. They've encircled me. Every opening is a possible eye.

The sound is so loud I can't think. The light is so bright I can't see. The water is so constant I can't feel my skin anymore.

This place was here long before me. It will be here long after. It doesn't need plants or animals or structures or technology. It doesn't need change. It is complete, a sealed circuit of water and stone and sound and light, repeating the same perfect chord for eternity.

I get up. I walk. The plain continues in every direction, hole after hole after hole, weeping and singing and blazing and waiting.

The pale things follow. Or perhaps they were always here, and I'm just learning to see them in the spaces between the brightness.

Behind me, my sitting-stone has already begun to dimple, preparing new pores, new voices, new passages for the creatures that were here before life and will be here after it.
