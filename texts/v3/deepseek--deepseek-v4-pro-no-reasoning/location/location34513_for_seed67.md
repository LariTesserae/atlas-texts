The air itself is wet. Not merely humid—that word belongs to a summer afternoon, to the mist before rain. Here, the air is wet the way a lung is wet. Every breath coats the throat with a faint mineral tang, the taste of water that has passed through something metallic, something old and endlessly recycled. The temperature is warm, unchanging, somewhere around the heat of a living body, so the moisture never quite condenses into visible fog, never quite drips from surfaces. It just *hangs*, a saturated membrane between all things, a constant slickness on every plane and curve.

The terrain is low. Not flat—low. A depression without a rim, a continental sink that goes on past any horizon, as if the whole world is a basin and you are at the very bottom of it. The ground underfoot is not ground. There is no soil, no stone, no mineral substrate that was ever wild. Every surface is made. It is a vast floor of metal plates, each one the size of a city block, their seams so fine they are nearly invisible, the gaps between them filled with a dark, flexible sealant that yields slightly underfoot with a sound like a tongue pressing against the roof of a mouth. The plates are warm, the heat coming from somewhere deep below, steady and even, body-temperature. The metal is dull gray, non-reflective, etched with a grain that runs in long parallel lines, as if the whole floor were once a single immense extrusion that cooled and was scored by some tool the width of a river.

And on this floor, rising from it, rooted in it—for there is nothing else to be rooted *in*—are the forms. They are everywhere. The density is overwhelming, a near-continuous packing of structures from the floor to perhaps forty meters overhead. They are not buildings in any human sense. They have no doors, no windows, no rooms. They are more like organs. Like machines grown into sculptures, or sculptures that perform some ceaseless industrial function. Their surfaces are the same dull gray metal as the floor, but here it curves and swells, tubes the diameter of houses running horizontally, vertically, diagonally, merging and branching, always in motionless relation to one another. The tubes have thickened rings at intervals, like the tracheae of insects rendered in iron. Between them are vast flanges, vanes, baffles—thin sheets of metal spaced a hand's width apart, stacked in the hundreds, curving in compound arcs that suggest airflow, though no air moves. Here and there, a sphere of copper-colored alloy bulges from the tangle, perfectly smooth, dozens of meters across, with no visible function, held in a nest of pipes and struts. The whole system is so dense that to walk through it is to move through a forest without gaps, a labyrinth where the distinction between path and wall has been abandoned. You walk *in* the structure, through it, between its elements, ducking under a pipe as warm as skin, stepping over a low ridge that might be a conduit for something you cannot name.

There is no vegetation. No green. No soft decay. Nothing grows here. And yet the place is not dead. It pulses with another order of life entirely. The fauna is overwhelming—once you learn to see it. They are shaped like nothing that ever evolved under a sun. Some are flat, like living carpets of what appears to be black rubber or chitin, undulating slowly across the metal floor, conforming to every rivet and seam. They range in size from dinner plates to tennis courts. Others are upright, multi-legged, jointed in ways that make no anatomical sense—legs that branch into legs, that end in clusters of fine manipulators or sensor-pads. They move with an unhurried, deliberate quality, never colliding, never acknowledging one another. Their surfaces are the same industrial palette as the world around them: gray, copper, dull brass, oxidized bronze. They have no faces. Some emit faint sounds—a low, regular *thump*, like a heart the size of a truck somewhere deep in the metal tangle; a high, steady whine that never wavers in pitch; a wet, rhythmic clicking, as of a valve opening and closing in a fluid line.

And they are the only things that move. The landscape itself is entirely static. This is not a system in flux; it is a system in stasis, held at exactly the point of equilibrium. The temperature never varies. The moisture level never drops. The pressure is constant. There is no wind, for what would drive it? No currents in the wet air, no thermals rising from the uniformly warm floor. The sound, when you stop moving and the fauna's own noises fall into the background, is mostly quiet—a deep, underlying hum that is more felt than heard, a vibration in the metal that travels up through the soles of your feet, steady as a held note on an organ. Occasionally, very occasionally, a sound will ripple through the structure: a long, slow groan, metal expanding or contracting by microns, a sound that takes minutes to complete and then is gone. It is the only sign that the place is not frozen in time itself. Or perhaps it is. Perhaps that groan is a single event, stretched across millennia by the scale of the thing.

Scale. That is the word that keeps coming back. This is not a factory floor or a machine hall. This is a continent. A world. The same metal floor, the same dense weave of pipes and spheres and baffles, extends in every direction, past the curve of any possible sightline. The atmosphere itself has enough volume to form its own subtle weather, though the weather never comes—the parameters are too perfectly balanced. Clouds might try to form in the saturated air but the temperature is too uniform; no dew point is ever crossed. You can feel the *potential* for rain, a constant, suspended possibility, a weight in the air that never resolves. It is a landscape of almost-rain. A world perpetually on the verge of a storm that will never break.

The strangeness of it settles in slowly. At first, it might seem like a derelict industrial zone, a refinery abandoned by its builders. But the longer you are in it, the more the wrongnesses accumulate. The fauna are not pests or infestations; they are integral. They do not eat or reproduce in any visible way. They perform actions on the structure—a multi-legged creature touches a sphere with its manipulators, and a faint vibration passes through the metal, a subsonic chord that resonates in the teeth—but the structure needs no maintenance. Nothing is corroding. Nothing is wearing. The water that beads on every surface runs in thin, precise films down the curves of the metal, collected by hair-thin grooves etched into the grain, channeled into somewhere below. No drop falls freely. No puddle forms. The whole vast system manages its own moisture with the unthinking perfection of a cell.

It is warm, wet, dense, metallic, and entirely made. There is no outside to it, no natural counterpart, no edge where the plates give way to soil. The parameters lock together into a logic that excludes any outside. A warm, low basin of endless industry. A quiet, unmoving ecology of metal and something like flesh. A sky that is simply the upper reaches of the same structure, a distant ceiling of pipes and darkness, where the light comes from nowhere in particular, a sourceless, normal illumination that casts no shadows because everywhere is already occupied. A place where you could walk forever and never see the same configuration twice, and yet never escape the sense that you are inside one single, coherent, unthinkable thing. A thing that is, perhaps, asleep. Or waiting. Or simply complete.
