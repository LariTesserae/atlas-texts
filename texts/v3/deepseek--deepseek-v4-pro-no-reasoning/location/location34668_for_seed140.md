The first thing that comes, before any shape or boundary, is the sound. It is a continuous, low-frequency thrum that does not travel through air in any normal way but arrives instead through the soles of the feet, through the palms if pressed against any surface, a bass note felt in the long bones. It is the sound of water working, patient and inexhaustible, and it has been working for a very long time.

The place is a single chamber, roughly oblong, perhaps eighty feet at its widest and twice that in length, though the walls are so heavily draped with vegetation that the true dimensions are largely a matter of inference. The ceiling vaults away overhead, lost in wet darkness beyond the reach of any light. It is not cold here — the air sits at a steady, drowsy warmth, heavy with saturation — but the darkness gives it an initial impression of chill that the skin quickly corrects. The darkness is not merely an absence; it is a presence, a humid velvet weight that fills the space completely, a darkness that has been here for epochs, undisturbed and self-renewing. Light enters only in the form of a diffuse, milky bioluminescence emanating from certain of the plant forms, a glaucous blue-green glow just sufficient to etch the nearest shapes out of the black.

Those shapes are vegetation, but vegetation that seems to have reached its final, most patient expression. There are no sharp edges, no thorns, no bright blossoms. Everything is broad, rubbery, and profoundly old. Enormous fronds, each the size of a man's torso, unfurl in extreme slow motion from a central thicket that occupies the lowest point of the chamber's uneven floor. Their surfaces are beaded perpetually with condensation, great trembling droplets that fall with maddening slowness, one every several minutes, to plash into a shallow pool below. The pool is the chamber's floor, or most of it — black, still water of unknown depth, mirroring the bioluminescent glow in broken smears. It is this water that makes the air so wet, a saturation so complete that the distinction between submerged and emerged seems arbitrary. Mosses and liverworts, swollen to grotesque proportions, coat every surface that is not actively running with moisture. They spill over the lips of what must be rock, though the rock itself has not been seen in millennia. It is buried under a sebaceous, living skin of emerald and jade so deep that the shape beneath is a memory.

And then there are the remnants. They are not ruins in the sense of fallen columns or fractured arches, but rather individual, irreducible artifacts, scattered with an almost deliberate vastness of space between them. There is one directly ahead, protruding from the shallow end of the dark pool. It is a rod or pylon of a material that is neither metal nor ceramic nor glass but seems to remember being all three, perhaps a crystalline phase of matter that has no name. It is perfectly smooth, a pale, non-reflective silver, and it stands about ten feet high, its tip vanishing into the overarching fronds. Its surface is broken by a single, hairline seam that pulses with a deep violet light, a light so slow in its cycle that the eye cannot catch it moving, only perceiving after many minutes that the color has shifted. It is a component, clearly, of a larger machine, a machine of which all other parts are missing. It does nothing. It waits.

Across the chamber, half a dozen such artifacts are distributed with the logic of a sentence from which most words have been erased. A flat, hexagonal plate, perhaps two paces across, hovers silently a hand's-breadth above the water, its underside throwing a faint, humming diffraction pattern onto the black surface. A cluster of filaments, finer than spider-silk and glowing with the same patient violet, spreads out from a crevice in the living wall, each strand terminating in a tiny, faceted dot that might be a sensor or an emitter. They sway in a current of air too slow to feel. The space between these objects is immense relative to their size. They are lost in the bioluminescent murk, isolated points of a vanished grammar. They have been here long enough that the moss has begun, with infinitesimal patience, to accept them. The hexagonal plate is edged in a delicate fur of green.

Life beyond the plants is abundant, but specialized for this one room, this one slow rhythm. The pool is not empty — pale, blind arthropods, all antennae and filamentous legs, drift through the water with a motion that uses the viscosity as much as muscle. They are large, some the size of a hand, and they navigate by the faint pressure-wakes of the falling droplets. In the thickest mats of vegetation live creatures that are all soft, wet lung and adhesive foot, slug-like but segmented, grazing on the algal film that coats the fronds. They move with the speed of the minute-hand of a clock, and their passage leaves a faint, phosphorescent trail that persists for hours before fading, writing a slow, glowing palimpsest on the living walls. There are no winged things. There is no need for speed, no threat to flee. The entire ecology is a closed loop of decay and reconstitution, a living distillation apparatus powered by the slow, ventral glow of the artifacts and the ancient, geothermal warmth rising from somewhere deep below.

The air does not smell of rot, despite the saturation and the endless growth. It smells mineral and slightly electric, like the air after lightning, though there has never been lightning here. The sound — that bone-conducted thrum — is joined now and then by a liquid, musical drip, and by the faint, crackling susurrus of the slow slugs grazing. There is no other sound. The walls of the chamber are not silent, but they absorb and return the sound in a way that makes the space feel even smaller, more enclosed, a velvet-lined box at the top of the world. For it is high, this place. It perches on the shoulder of an ancient range so old that the peaks have been worn to gentle domes, mountains that have been settling into their final form for a hundred million years. Outside, unseen, the wind may blow across those worn-down summits, but in here, the saturation buffers all, and the only wind is the exhalation of the plants.

The slow processes are visible once you settle into the dark. The droplets fall. The violet pulse in the pylon cycles from deep purple to near-ultraviolet and back over the course of what feels like an hour. The phosphorescent trails fade. And the moss creeps, molecule by molecule, over the ancient, scattered components of a technology that perhaps once made this chamber something else entirely — a station, a generator, a temple — but which now serves only as the skeleton upon which this slow, wet, dark world has grown its flesh. It is a place that has been finished with for so long that it has become something else entirely, a small, complete, loud-quiet universe where almost nothing happens, and everything is happening, very, very slowly.
