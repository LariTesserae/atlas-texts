The place is an eyrie, a rookery, but not for birds. Not for any flying thing, in fact—not in the usual sense. It is a vertical seam of habitation, a pocket-city slotted into a geological wound.

The walls are obsidian, but not the smooth, vitreous kind. This is *young* obsidian, barely a century out of the cooling front. Its surfaces are still sharp as shattered syntax, untouched by rain because there is no rain, unscoured by wind because the local atmosphere is nearly stagnant, a cool, sluggish, high-CO2 breath that pools in the gorge and barely moves. The stone is black, faintly iridescent in the dim light, with a texture like snapped toffee—bladed, brittle, full of micro-conchoidal fractures that catch what little glow there is and throw it sideways in arcs of bruised purple and oil-slick green.

The light comes from the city itself. It's a dense-woven thing, this settlement. Structures are not so much built as *affixed*. They clamp to the rock faces, spanning the narrow fissure in layered bridges, gantries, and nested platforms that look like the cross-section of a mechanical wasp nest. Every surface is occupied. Homes, workshops, conduit-runs, and hatcheries are packed so tightly that the walls themselves seem to be exhaling architecture. The elevation is extreme—the fissure plunges at least three hundred meters straight down and rises another two hundred to a pinched, slivered sky the color of a fading bruise. The city occupies the middle third, a dense belt of industry and life suspended over the abyss.

No water flows here in liquid form. The rock is anhydrous, the air lethally dry. What moisture exists is bound up in the sealed atmospheres of the settlement's countless small domes, or locked in the gelid tissues of the fauna—the *abundant* fauna. The air is cool, perhaps ten degrees Celsius, and it hums with a constant, low-frequency vibration: the subsonic rumble of thousands of heat-engines, the deep-tissue noise of a place running on geothermal tap and steam-piston logic.

And everywhere, the clickers.

They are the fauna, the reason for the rookery. Pale, segmented things the size of a human thumb, with too many legs and a cluster of dry, chitinous manipulators at the head. They are not insects. Their biology is non-aqueous in a way that makes their carapaces feel like cold ceramic. They feed on the radiant heat and the mineral exudates of the obsidian itself—perhaps on the disordered silica chains, some chemotrophic miracle still being reverse-engineered by the city's bio-tinkers. They are farmed. But "farmed" is too pastoral a word for what happens here. They are *harvested* in a continuous, turbulent cycle that makes the whole settlement feel like a single seething organism.

The sound is mostly quiet, because the clickers speak in ultrasonic snaps and because the human and post-human inhabitants here have long since learned to move softly, to pad their boots, to oil their machinery into a whisper. The dominant noise is a kind of dry, running susurrus—the sound of a billion tiny claws on resin-treated mesh. It's a noise like rain, if rain were made of static electricity and chitin. Occasionally, a sharper note: a new outcropping of rock shears off under its own internal stresses and tumbles, clattering down through the layered city, bouncing from one gantry to another, triggering brief flurries of activity from the clicker colonies that cascade in waves of defensive bioluminescence—sudden, cold-blue starbursts that ripple across the walls and die.

The place is turbulent in a way that belies its quiet. It is a society built on chaotic generation, a kind of industrial-Solaris. New volcanic dykes push up overnight from the planet's restless mantle, splitting streets and forcing frantic architectural adaptation. The clickers undergo rapid, unpredictable life-cycle blooms in response to the shifting heat-plumes. A wall that was stable yesterday might be fissured today, pouring out a fresh carbon-dioxide seep that kills a whole sub-district's livestock with acidosis unless the gas-scrubbers can be redeployed in time. The city is perpetually *becoming*, rebuilding itself in the cracks of a cooling substrate that hasn't finished its own violent adolescence. There are no elders here who remember a settled time. The place is only a few generations old, and it feels like it—a frontier that never stops being a frontier, a dense, hidden, vertical factory-world in a dry, dark wound in the planet.
