The air here is never truly dry. It holds a constant, fine-grained dampness that settles on every surface — not rain, exactly, though it does rain, in short, casual bursts that come without warning and stop just as suddenly. The moisture arrives more often as condensate: beads of water that form on the undersides of leaves, on the metal pipes, on the stone, drawn out of the air by the coolness radiating from the ground itself. This is a high place, not mountainous but elevated enough that the temperature hovers in that narrow band between brisk and chill, where breath barely mists and the skin feels perpetually cool to the touch. The dampness amplifies this, carrying the cold into fabric, into hair, into the gaps between knuckles.

The vegetation has responded to these conditions with exuberance. It is lush to the point of obsession — a layered, three-dimensional tapestry of green that occupies every possible niche. Mosses and liverworts form a continuous plush carpet over the ground, but they do not stop at the horizontal; they climb the vertical faces of rock with the same thick enthusiasm, blanketing declivities and ledges in a fur of emerald and olive. Ferns uncurl in dense thickets, their fronds overlapping like nested spoons, each one beaded with water droplets that catch and scatter the light. Above them, broad-leaved plants with leaves the size of torsos spread themselves wide, and above those, taller growths — not quite trees in the traditional sense, but woody-stemmed perennials with twisting, muscular trunks — form a canopy so interwoven that looking up is like peering into a green lattice ceiling. Every branch supports its own ecosystem of epiphytes; every crook in the bark holds a tuft of something growing. The density here is near-touching. Plants grow into one another’s spaces, roots entwining, leaves pressing against leaves, creating a fabric of vegetation so continuous that the ground beneath is more a concept than a visible surface.

This is a young landscape, geologically speaking, and it wears its newness in its sharp edges. The rock formations — and there is much exposed rock, despite the vegetation’s best efforts — are angular, fractured along clean planes, unrounded by the patient abrasion of wind or water. Basalt, mostly, dark and fine-grained, which has cracked into hexagonal columns in places, forming natural organ-pipe cliffs that rise thirty or forty feet before the moss claims them. In other places the stone has shattered into fields of talus, each fragment still crisp-edged, clattering underfoot with a ceramic sharpness of sound when dislodged.
