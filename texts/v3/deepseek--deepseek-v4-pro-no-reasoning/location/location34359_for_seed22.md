The air hits you first—thick as wool, warm enough to prickle sweat along your hairline, saturated with a moisture that never quite commits to rain but never dries either. Your clothes cling immediately, and breathing feels like drinking through a damp cloth. This is a place of constant, delicate wetness, where water moves by capillary action and condensation rather than by falling.

And sound. Sound drowns everything. Not the roar of engines or the clatter of machinery, but a layered, biological shrieking that seems baked into the atmosphere itself. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, a chorus of trills and whistles and low percussive booms that overlap into a solid wall of noise. You feel it in your sternum. You can't hear your own footsteps. You can barely hear yourself think.

As your eyes adjust—and they take a long time, because the light is wrong—you begin to make sense of the space. You're standing on a broad, slightly concave plain of what looks like polished obsidian, but it gives slightly underfoot with an almost imperceptible resilience. The surface is tracked with fine, branching channels, like the vein network of a leaf, but scaled up to the width of your hand, cut fresh and sharp as if carved yesterday. In the bottom of each channel, a thin film of water slides silently, drawn by some micro-gravity or surface-tension trick, glinting faintly in the low amber glow that seems to emanate from the ground itself rather than any sky.

There is no sky, not in the conventional sense. Above you, perhaps two hundred meters up, the world doesn't end so much as transition into a kind of structured fog. Vast, translucent folds and curtains hang in the dim air, kilometer-long membranes of what might be polymer or might be grown tissue, draped between spires that rise at impossible intervals across the plain. The spires are the only vertical elements, spaced so far apart that each one is a landmark on the horizon, a slender column that tapers as it rises until it vanishes into the membrane-layer. Their surfaces are not smooth; from this distance they seem latticed, honeycombed, crawling with smaller structures. One nearby shows growth rings, or something like them—concentric layers peeling outward slightly, fresh and sharp-edged, as though the spire has recently expanded and hasn't yet weathered.

The emptiness of the place is profound. The plain stretches for kilometers in all directions, and between the spires, there is almost nothing. Just the black resilient floor, the glowing channels, the hanging membranes above. No debris. No loose rocks. No accumulated dust. The space feels curated, cleaned, or simply too young to have collected clutter. The sheer volume of air between you and the nearest spire is staggering; you could walk for an hour and still be in the middle of open ground, small as a field mouse under an enormous ceiling.

But it would not be quiet walking, and you would not be alone.

They move through the air. Not flying exactly—more like undulating, swimming through the thick atmosphere with muscular ripples of their bodies. The creatures are everywhere, visible once you learn how to look, slender dark shapes against the amber glow, moving in loose schools that shift direction in synchronized waves. They range from the size of your forearm to some that must be thirty meters long, translucent along their flanks so the faint light shines orange through their tissues. They have no heads you can recognize, just anterior clusters of what might be cilia or might be manipulators or might be mouths, constantly in motion, tasting the air. And they are loud. The shrieking, the booming, the percussive chatter—it's all coming from them. They never stop vocalizing. Each one produces a complex signature of clicks and tones, and the aggregate is this wall of sound that forms the baseline of the world.

Damped ground, *shrieking chorus beats in your chest*, warm heavy air.

The creatures are feeding, you realize. The membrane-curtains above are not inert; they weep a fine nutrient mist that hangs in the air, invisible until a school of the long translucent things passes through a cloud of it, and then the fluid clings to their flanks, pearls up, and is slowly absorbed. The channels in the floor collect the excess—some elaborate circulatory system that returns unabsorbed nutrients back to the spires, back up to the membranes, to be released again. Young erosion. The channels are knife-sharp because they're constantly renewed, the black material actively re-forming itself along the flow paths. Nothing here is old enough to have rounded edges.

Dotted among the bases of the distant spires, you can make out clusters of more geometric structures—low domes and stacked terraces and what might be towers or might be heat exchangers, their surfaces bristling with fins and vanes. This is the urban element, the 1.5 on some scale you're now forced to reconsider. It's a city, but a city built in the gaps, a low sprawl that occupies maybe five percent of the plain's total area, clinging to the anchor points of the spires. The rest is this open, empty, food-production flyway—a vast ranch for sky-creatures, engineered at a scale that makes the settled areas feel like afterthoughts. The tech is baked into the biology, or the biology is the tech; the membranes are living filters, the spires are grown architectures that also house whatever inhabitants this place has, the channels are a circulatory system for a facility-organism that might be semi-sentient.

A shift in the light. The amber glow from the floor intensifies briefly, a slow pulse like a long, deep breath, and the membranes above ripple in response. The temperature rises half a degree. The creature chorus changes pitch—some new information rippling through the schools, a collective decision being made. A vast school peels off from the others and descends, hundreds of them spiraling down toward a section of the plain a kilometer to your left, where the channels suddenly widen and the ground opens into a series of dark pools. The noise redlines. You feel it in your teeth.

The water parameter, 0.6 out of 3. Now you understand. This place is damp, perpetually condensing, crisscrossed by film-flow and mist, but it has no true bodies of water except these harvesting pools that open and close on some cycle. The creatures are coming down to drink, or to spawn, or to deliver some biochemical payload they've synthesized in their long bodies. The pools appear and disappear, opened and sealed by the living substrate. Intermittent moisture. Pockets of water in a larger system that runs mostly on vapor.

As your hearing begins to fatigue and adapt, you pick out other sounds underneath the creature din: a low hum from the spires, almost subsonic, the vibration of vast volumes of fluid moving through internal plumbing. A faint crackling, like ice melting, which might be the sound of the black floor growing. From the direction of the nearest settlement cluster, a rhythmic tapping, metallic and deliberate, someone or something at work. The city is inhabited. Somewhere in those low domes and finned towers, there are beings with intentions, with tools, with a relationship to this roaring biological machine they've built or partnered with. The built environment is 1.5—not the dominant feature, but present, woven in, layered under and around the engineered biology.

The ground beneath you shivers. Not an earthquake—something more local and deliberate. A section of the black floor a few meters away begins to iris open with a wet, adhesive sound, revealing a descending ramp lit from below by that same amber glow. A door. An entrance to the under-levels. The channels re-route around the opening automatically, water-film detouring with a flicker of redirected flow. The sharp edge of the new aperture is perfectly defined; it looks like it was manufactured minutes ago.

You could go down. You could stay here on the plain and watch the sky-creatures spiral and feed. You could walk to the nearest spire-city, a journey of hours through warm damp air and deafening noise, a small figure crossing an engineered savannah designed for creatures much larger than yourself. The scale is open but not infinite—the plain has edges, you can see where the membrane-ceiling comes down to meet the floor in a hazy distance—but it's open enough to hold entire weather systems of mist and sound, open enough to feel like a world rather than a room.

The dim light pulses again. The sky-creatures scream their ongoing song. The warm heavy air clings to your skin, and the sharp new doorway waits at your feet, vanishing down into the amber dark beneath the plain.
