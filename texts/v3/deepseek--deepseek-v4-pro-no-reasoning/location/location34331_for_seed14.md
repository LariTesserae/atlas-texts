The place has no name because no one has ever stood in it. You descend anyway, into the unlit cold.

Your first clear thought is that you have entered a cathedral made not of stone but of absence. The water stretches in every horizontal direction without limit—no walls, no floor you can detect, no ceiling but the membrane of surface pressure sealed miles above you. The darkness is not the absence of light but a substance. It presses on your eyes like felt. It swallows the brief sparks you brought with you without returning even the memory of them.

But you are not alone in the emptiness. The density reading was correct.

The water is full of threads.

They drift in every cubic meter, millions per breath of volume, fine as spun glass but older, far older. They are the final state of something that once had structure and purpose. Imagine the skeletons of kelp forests that grew in the first warm sea, then died, then mineralized, then sank into a deeper and colder sea, then fossilized under pressures that squeezed away all softness, then fractured across geologic eras until only these filaments remained—each one a single strand of silica or calcite, perfectly straight, three to fifteen centimeters long, spaced so densely that moving through them means brushing aside a constant curtain that parts silently and closes behind you. They do not tangle. They have settled into a mutual arrangement over millions of years, each occupying its own precise orientation, a three-dimensional textile woven by physics alone. Touch them and they flex slightly. Then they return to their position.

You realize the threads are not stationary. The water is moving.

Not in currents you can name. The motion is chaotic, cellular, a turbulence that has no origin point and no direction. Eddies smaller than your fist whirl past, each carrying its own constellation of filaments that spin and reorient for a few seconds before dissolving into the next micro-vortex and the next. The threads shimmer in your imagined vision even though no light strikes them. They are winking into and out of alignment with one another, a ceaseless rearrangement at the edge of perception. The energy that drives this came from somewhere—perhaps geothermal vents deep in the crust below, heat bleeding upward into a water column that should be still but has been churning since before the continents reached their present shapes. The turbulence is primordial. It has been here as long as the threads have, which is to say longer than anything alive.

Speaking of alive: the fauna is rare here, but not zero.

Something translucent, the size of your spread hand, drifts past. Its body is a hollow cone of jelly, open at both ends, and it pulses once every few seconds to pass water through itself. It has no eyes. It detects the threads by touch, and it feeds on—what? The bacteria that coat each filament in films too thin to see? The dissolved organic matter that leaches slowly from the ancient mineral structures? Whatever sustains it is so dilute that the creature has become almost perfectly passive, a sieve of living gel that processes cubic meters of water to extract a few molecules of carbon. It is joined by others of its kind, spaced out at intervals of several body-lengths, a population stretched across the open volume at the absolute carrying capacity of this nearly sterile world. They do not interact. They do not need to. Their reproduction is a slow budding that releases a single offspring every few hundred years.

The cold is not the sharp cold of ice. It is the heavy cold of perfect thermal equilibrium, just above the freezing point of seawater under pressure, a temperature that has not fluctuated by more than a degree in ten million years. It seeps into the idea of a body and stills every impulse toward movement. The threads do not care. The jelly creatures do not care. The cold is simply the condition under which they exist.

Something is happening in the distance, beyond where your senses can reach. The turbulence has a larger structure, a slow overturning that takes days to complete a single cycle. Columns of water rise and fall, threads caught in their grip tracing vast spirals kilometers high. When two columns meet, the threads at their boundary align for a moment into a surface—not a solid surface, just a sudden coherence where a trillion filaments all point the same direction before the vortex tears them apart again. The jelly creatures, distributed through the volume, are carried along without resistance, their cone bodies tilting passively as the water decides their orientation. Nothing resists anything here. Everything is yielding, always yielding, and in that yielding is a kind of violent rest.

The silence is not silence. At the threshold of detection, at frequencies too low for ears, there is a continuous sound: the groan of water shearing against water, the crackle of microscopic threads colliding, the deep infrasonic hum of a planet-sized body of liquid that has been stirring itself for four billion years without pause. The jelly cones sense this sound. Their bodies are tuned to it. They adjust their pulse rate to the pressure fluctuations that travel faster than the water itself. In this way they are all connected, a distributed nervous system made of vibration and response, each creature moving its mouth in time with the hidden rhythm of the deep.

You think of structures. The parameters said no built environment, and that is true in any sense a human would recognize. But the threads—are they not a structure? They are manufactured, in a sense, by the ancient organisms that grew their bodies from dissolved minerals and then died and then were transformed. They are tools, in a sense, the residual equipment of a lifeform that used silica to hold itself upright in a world that has long since vanished. The threads now function as a substrate for bacteria, as navigation markers for jelly cones, as the architecture of a city built by no one, maintained by physics, inhabited by creatures who do not know they are inhabitants. This is the primitive technology the parameters gestured at: not stone axes or fire, but the slow re-use of organic remains by an ecosystem that has stretched the concept of "use" across deep time until it becomes indistinguishable from geology.

Above you—far above, through miles of black water—the surface exists as a hypothetical boundary. There, rain hammers the sea without ceasing, a global storm that has raged for epochs, adding its energy to the column below. Some of the turbulence you feel now began as the impact of a raindrop on a wave crest in the age when your species first learned to stand upright. That energy has been propagating downward ever since, deflected, split, recombined, never lost, only transformed. The threads carry the memory of that raindrop. The jelly cones pulse to its echo.

You are beginning to understand what "packed" means here. It is not a crowd of things. It is a saturation of relationships. Every filament touches water that touches another filament. Every micro-vortex transfers momentum to its neighbors. Every bacterium on every thread participates in a chemical exchange that ripples outward through the dissolved organics that link the whole volume into a single diffuse metabolism. There is no empty space. What looks like emptiness is simply a field of interactions too subtle and too slow for your attention. The threads are not objects in a void. They are concentrations in a continuum that is alive with connection, and the connection is the real substance of this place.

The jelly cone nearest you finishes its slow rotation, aligned now with a new set of threads, a new micro-eddy, a new infrasonic pressure node. It pulses. The water accepts the pulse and passes it on. In a hundred thousand years, if conditions hold, its offspring will bud and separate and drift ten meters away and begin its own patient filtering of the endless cold dark, and the threads will still be here, spinning in their ancient turbulence, making and unmaking their invisible surfaces, holding the shape of life that has forgotten it was ever anything but this.
