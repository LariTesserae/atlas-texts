The air hits you first—thick, hot, a wet blanket laced with something acrid and metallic. It condenses on every surface, on your skin, running in rivulets down the fluted concrete walls. This is the Sump-Husk, a city that grew in the lowlands after the old factories died, and it never really dried out.

You stand on a high catwalk, one of thousands stitching the open space between the massive, windowless blocks of the processing stacks. The stacks themselves are monuments to a lost industry, each one a terraced ziggurat of stained, sweating concrete, their surfaces etched and softened by a century of condensation and chemical rain. There is no paint, no cladding—just the raw, grey-brown material, weeping a constant, greasy dew. This is the "vegetation" here: a mineral forest of stalactite-like drips and calcified flowstone that oozes from every pipe-joint and shattered vent, building its own slow, silent geology over the industrial bones.

The city is dense, a vertical labyrinth of gantries, ladder-shafts, and hanging conduits, but its density is one of framework. The space between things is vast and hollow. You look down a "street"—a canyon between two stacks—and see the opposite wall half a kilometer away, a dim tapestry of countless lit portholes and flickering neon, the detail lost in the humid haze. The volume is enormous, almost planetary, and nearly empty. A few distant figures move on the web of walkways, tiny specks. The dominant sound is not their noise, but a low, encompassing hum, the baseline drone of immense ventilation fans somewhere deeper in the stacks, a sound so constant it becomes a form of silence. It’s a quiet punctuated by the echoing drip of water, a sudden hiss of steam from a pressure valve, the distant, muted clang of metal settling.

This is a hot world, a dynamic one built on cycles of moisture and heat. The deep geothermal pumps, the city’s thrumming heart, push superheated brine up from below. It flashes to steam in the old pipework, driving turbines before being released in great, sighing plumes from the lowest levels. The steam rises, hits the cooler concrete of the upper spires, and condenses, a constant artificial rain that trickles back down, gathering in murky pools on every sagging platform and broken terrace. This is the active process, the weather of the Sump-Husk: a vertical, closed-loop monsoon.

And it is this perpetual, mineral-rich film of water that feeds the overwhelming fauna, the only life that has no need for soil or sun. The walls are not just stained; they are encrusted. Vast, leathery sheets of rust-membrane undulate slowly in the wet breeze from a vent, their surfaces a galaxy of silver, purple, and ochre. They are chemotrophic, breathing the hydrogen sulfide and iron oxide in the weeping water as they grow, millimeter by millimeter, across the concrete. The real presence, though, is in the swarms. From a thousand cracks and sunken cisterns, clouds of filamentous filter-feeders erupt in synchronized pulses, driven by the pressure cycles. They look like drifting smoke, a grey, particulate mist of fine, thread-like bodies that writhe through the humid air, sieving bacteria and metal salts. They are drawn to the heat signatures and the carbon dioxide of larger creatures, a fact you know all too well. A shimmering, silvery blanket of them drifts down the canyon now, not a single organism but a colonial intelligence, its billions of strands communicating by touch, parting around the struts of a gantry like a single, thought-filled fluid. The air is alive with their quiet, hissing respiration.

Below, in the permanent twilight of the lower levels where the water is knee-deep and scalding, the true giants move. You can’t see them, but you feel them: a deep, subsonic thrumming that vibrates up through the catwalk’s metal grating. A low, cetacean moan echoes from the flooded sump-chambers—a Leviathan-Slug, a blind, hundred-meter-long mass of blubber and cilia that grazes the rust-membranes and is, in turn, followed by its own retinue of smaller scavengers. Their movements create currents in the floodwater, the only tides this city knows.

There is nothing green. The idea of a leaf, a root, a flower, is a forgotten abstraction here. This is a place that has moved beyond that. The industrial substrate did not destroy the ecology; it was the bedrock for a new one. The city is a reef in a humid abyss, an established, weathered, and balanced system where the living and the constructed have become a single, continuous membrane. It is aggressively, overwhelmingly alive, a testament to a different, inevitable lineage of being, built on iron, steam, and silence.
