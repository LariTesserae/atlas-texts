You emerge from a maintenance shaft into the throat of the world.

The first thing that strikes you is the *wetness* of the heat. It doesn’t rain here—not exactly—but the air is a saturated,活着 thing, heavy with condensation that beads on every surface and drips in slow, fat drops from the twisted geometries overhead. The moisture comes in cycles you can almost set a clock by, if clocks still mattered: a long, dry, searing exhalation, then a gathering of steam from the deep vents, a pearlescent fog that thickens until it can no longer hold itself, releasing a slick, warm drizzle that coats everything in a mineral glaze before the cycle begins again. Pockets of standing water collect in the folds of the terrain, shimmering with an oily, metallic sheen, too hot to touch, but evaporating and re-condensing in an endless, torpid loop.

There is no soil. No leaf, no root, no humus. The landscape is raw, embryonic—a geology caught in the act of being born. You are standing on a high, precarious shelf in a vast, open space, but the scale is dizzying. It’s not a canyon, not a valley; it’s more like the interior of a colossal, half-assembled machine the size of a town. The “ground” is a dense, near-touching accumulation of forms: hexagonal cooling columns of basalt, twisted into spirals; plates of obsidian glass, still tacky with the memory of flow; a latticework of metallic pipes and ducts, some as wide as tunnels, encrusted with a furry riot of mineral deposits—copper-green, sulfur-yellow, iron-rust-red. These are the bones of the city.

Because this is a city. It was built, or is being built, or is building itself. The settlement is vertical and dense, a three-dimensional maze of factories and refineries that clings to the sheer, hot walls of an immense upwelling. The structures are not separate buildings but a continuous, layered crust: a lower stratum of heavy, riveted boilers and blast furnaces, their massive forms softened by the constant precipitate; above them, a webwork of walkways, cable trays, and smaller pipe-runs; higher still, the skeletal frames of new construction, climbing toward a ceiling lost in the steam. It’s an industrial cubism, a Piranesi of production, all rendered in black, grey, and the sudden, shocking verdigris of copper oxidation.

The weirdness of it settles on your skin like the damp. The light is strangely normal—a pervasive, sourceless grey-white glow that seems to emanate from the fog itself, casting everything in a flat, shadowless pallor. It reveals the texture of every surface in exhausting detail. And in this light, you see the architecture is wrong. Pipes terminate in blank walls. Stairways lead to sheer drops. Vast, riveted tanks are suspended from the ceiling by chains that hum with a low, almost inaudible vibration, their purpose inscrutable. The entire place hums with patient processes, the slow, gradual cycles of some vast metabolism. The changes are imperceptible moment-to-moment, but you can feel them: the deep, throbbing *whump* of a piston cycling once an hour, the subtle change in the pitch of a venting pipe, the barely-detectable shift of a suspended gantry as something cools and contracts over the course of a day.

The sound is mostly quiet, but it’s a dense quiet, a pressure on the eardrums. It’s the near-silence of a thousand tiny flows—the hiss of steam from a pinhole leak, the viscous *glop* of a condensate drop falling into a pool, the faint, high-frequency ringing of cooling metal. No voices. No wind, for this place has no atmosphere of its own, only its own exhalations.

And then, the fauna. They are rare, these creatures, but you know them when you see them, because they are not part of the static machinery. They are patrons of the heat and the damp. There—a slow, deliberate flicker in a pool of scalding, copper-sulfate-blue water. A colony of something like blind, white salamanders, but their flesh is translucent, shot through with a network of silver capillaries that pulse with a mercury-slow beat. They don't swim; they undulate, absorbing heat directly through their skin, their bodies the moving part of a thermodynamic circuit. And over on a vertiginous span of pipe, a clattering, jointed thing the size of a large cat picks its way with exquisite care. It’s a maintenance drone, perhaps, or something that evolved to mimic one. Its body is a collection of mis-matched, heat-stained metal plates, and it probes the pipe’s surface with delicate, whisker-like sensors, searching for the perfect thermal gradient to recharge its internal battery. It pays you no mind. Its world is heat, and condensation, and the slow, slow erosion of mineral scale.

This is a landscape under construction, not from a blueprint, but from a principle. The hot, mineral-rich water is the architect, the builder, and the inhabitant. It precipitates the pipes into being. It carves the basins. It powers the sluggish, silver-blooded life. It is the slow, patient engine of a world deciding what it wants to become, one drop at a time.
