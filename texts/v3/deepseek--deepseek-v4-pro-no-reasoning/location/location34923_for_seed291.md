The Mourn is not a valley, though people call it that. It is a long, low depression in the landscape, a wound of damp earth running for thirty miles between walls of black basalt that never quite steepen into cliffs. The walls are raw. They glisten with the wetness of their own making, as though the rock is still bleeding from the act of being split open—which, in a sense, it is. The Mourn is emerging. It is a place still being born, and it is doing so at a pace that a human life can almost track.

The bottom lies at barely a hundred feet below the surrounding plains, and the descent is gradual enough to be called a walk rather than a climb. But the floor is saturated—a 0.6 water rating that expresses itself not as standing pools or streams, but as a pervasive, clinging damp that never resolves into anything you could properly call a river. Instead, the moisture wells up through the ground in a thousand thousand places. Pebbled seep-springs bead the surface of the stone. Filaments of condensation run down every vertical surface. The air itself is heavy, somewhere between mist and breath, and it carries the faint mineral tang of water that has never seen open sky. There are pockets—hollows in the stone, folds in the emerging topography—where the moisture gathers more assertively, forming cold, shallow basins of shockingly clear liquid. In these, you can see the stone growing.

And it *is* growing. This is the strangeness at the heart of the Mourn: the erosion score of 0.3 is not a stage of decay, but of construction. The valley is not being carved out and worn down; it is being built up. The basalt walls are slowly, observably thickening upward from the floor, extruding fresh lobes of dark stone like a cooling flow that never stops. The process makes sound—a continuous, sub-auditory hum that registers more in the teeth than in the ears, the sound of crystallization on a geological scale, compressed into a human timespan. When the extruded stone meets the pockets of seep-water, the water boils gently, not from heat but from a cold, chemical effervescence, and new minerals precipitate out in ruffled, fleshy-looking formations that the Mourn-grown citizens call "cradlewax." It is soft. You can press your thumb into it and leave a print, which will be gone by morning, smoothed over by fresh deposition.

Despite the cold—a steady 0.4, the kind of chill that seeps into your bones not because the air is freezing but because it never warms—the Mourn is lush. Vegetation score 1.7 manifests as a dense, low-lying carpet of life that has adapted to the mineral feast and the cold damp with unsettling enthusiasm. The plants do not grow upward in vertical competition. Instead, they grow *inward*, layer upon layer, pressed flat by the weight of their own saturation. There are mosses so deep and dense they feel like walking on a drowned mattress. There are ferns that unroll in continuous slow spirals, their fronds never reaching more than a foot high before being subsumed by the next rank of growth. There are fungal bodies—or things that look like fungi—that glow a faint, cold blue and pulse gently, circulating the mineral-rich moisture through translucent tissues. Nothing here grows on soil. The plants root directly into the raw stone, their rhizomes etching delicate, lace-like patterns into the basalt even as the basalt rises up around them. The rock swallows stems, and the stems bore through new rock. It is a slow, silent war of succession, and at any given moment, it is unclear which side is winning.

And yet, into this emerging, cold, damp, and uncanny landscape, humanity has come in force. The Mourn is a city.

A city packed to saturation. The density of 2.8 is not a metaphor; it is a statement of geometry. There is no soil here, no open ground between structures. The buildings rise directly from the stone, their foundations fused into the growing basalt, and they are built wall-to-wall, floor-to-ceiling, arch-to-arch in a continuous, unbroken fabric of habitation that fills the entire thirty-mile length of the valley bottom and climbs several hundred feet up both walls. The structures are not arranged in streets or blocks. They are a single, contiguous mass of masonry, brick, iron, and later concrete, grown like the vegetation, layer upon layer, century upon century. To walk through the Mourn is not to walk along a street; it is to navigate a three-dimensional interior. The "streets" are tunnels bored through the lower levels of older buildings. The "squares" are vast, multi-story atria where dozens of previously independent structures have been hollowed and joined over time. Light comes from above through skylights and light wells that descend for ten or twelve stories before they reach anything that might be called ground level, and even that ground is often artificial—a platform built over older, darker, deeper chambers that have been sealed and forgotten.

This is the tech score of 2.5 made architectural. The Mourn is an industrial city in the most literal sense: its reason for being is the processing of the valley's own geology. The growing basalt is rich in rare-earth minerals and metallic salts that precipitate from the cold seeps. The entire city is one vast, distributed factory. Extrusion pipes run from the active rock faces into the lower levels of the built mass, siphoning the mineral slurry before it can harden into useless stone. In the factory levels—marked by the low, constant thrum of crystallization grinders and thermal separators—this slurry is refined into a dozen different products: the brilliant blue phosphor used in Mourn-lamps, the ultra-hard ceramic plating called sable-panels that the city exports across half the continent, the reactive cements that are pumped back into the buildings to fuse new construction seamlessly to old. The factory levels never go silent. The sound score of 0.8, "mostly quiet," is true only if you discount the ever-present hum of the industrial processes, which the locals do. They have learned to hear it as silence. The actual, defined sounds of the Mourn are discrete and carefully managed: the clank of a tram’s wheels on the elevated iron ways, the hiss of a pressure door sealing, the distant, cathedral-reverbed call of a vendor in one of the deep atria, the soft, wet plink of condensation dripping from a thousand heights into a thousand catch-basins. These sounds stand out against the hum like negative space, clear and precise.

The scale is open—2.3, regional—but it is an enclosed openness. From a high gallery on the valley wall, you can look out and see the entire Mourn stretching away into misty distance, a single building that is also a city that is also a landscape. The view is disorienting. There are no horizons, just the slow curve of the walls and the layered, ascending terraces of windows, balconies, exhaust vents, drying racks, tram lines, and satellite dishes diminishing into the damp haze. The far end of the valley is a smudge of darker texture, where the city is still actively gnawing into the newest rock extrusions, buildings budding off the stone like organic growths.

And then there are the other inhabitants. The fauna score of 1.5, "abundant," describes a population that has co-evolved with the city and the emerging geology without ever having been domesticated. There are the skirls—small, lizard-like creatures with six legs and chromatophoric skin that precisely mimics the brick-and-iron textures of the walls they cling to. They are invisible until they move, a flicker of misplaced masonry darting into a crack. They feed on the insects that themselves feed on the cradlewax, and they fill the role that rats or pigeons might in another city, except that the Mourners regard them with a strange, superstitious respect. A skirl entering your chamber is considered an omen of structural integrity; they avoid spaces about to collapse or seal.

Larger and more troubling are the gantries. No one knows what a gantry is, in a taxonomic sense. They appear in the deeper, darker levels—the drowned neighborhoods that were sealed off centuries ago and partially re-absorbed by the advancing stone. They are tall, thin, jointed things, moving slowly through the drowned corridors on three legs that end in delicate, multi-fingered manipulators. They make no sound at all. They ignore humans entirely when they can, and when they cannot, they dismantle the obstacle—not violently, but with an appalling, methodical grace, unbolting a wall or a piece of machinery as if they were the final custodians of the built world. Some Mourners claim they are biological, having grown from the same strange mineral processes that fuel the valley. Others swear they are machines, products of a pre-existing industrial civilization that the current city simply built over. The Gantry Guild, a quasi-religious order of deep-level explorers, maintains that the question is meaningless; they are the immune system of the valley, or its memories, or both, and they should be left to their work.

The dynamic of the Mourn is turbulent—2.6, a state of constant creation and destruction. This is a place where new rock extrudes into someone’s basement overnight, buckling the floor and cracking the walls, and the response is not panic but a call to the local Extrusion Guild, who arrive with cutting gear and extraction piping to harvest the new growth before it can compromise the structure. It is a place where buildings are routinely condemned not because they are too old, but because they are being too efficiently assimilated by the stone, their walls mineralizing, their rooms filling with new-grown basalt like a throat slowly closing. The inhabitants of such buildings pack their belongings and move laterally, into adjacent structures, and the sealed block becomes first a gantry-preserve, then a mine, then a raw face of emerging geology once more. The entire city is a slow, grinding churn of matter, a conveyor belt where the rock rises from the earth, passes through the industrial system, becomes architecture for a few human generations, and then sinks back into the mineral cycle, pressed under the weight of new construction above.

It is between these cycles that the hidden spaces form—the interstitial pockets, the abandoned levels, the condensation chambers where the only light is the faint blue glow of cradlewax and the only sound is the distant hum of the grinders and the soft, patient tick of water meeting stone. In these places, the Mourn becomes almost unbearably quiet, and the cold settles in like a presence, and you can press your hand to the new stone and feel, beneath your palm, the subtle vibration of a world still making itself, endlessly, without ever arriving at completion.
