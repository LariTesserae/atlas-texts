The city is called Siphon, and it hangs.

It hangs in the throat of the world, a sprawling vertical labyrinth bolted and bracketed into a colossal rift that splits the high plateau from edge to edge. The rift itself is young—geologically young, almost newborn in the timescale of stone. The edges are sharp, unweathered, as if a blade of impossible scale scored the continent and withdrew only moments ago. There are still facets on the rock that catch the light like fractured obsidian, not yet dulled by wind or ice or the patient rasp of water. The walls plunge nearly three vertical miles, and in their raw, unpolished faces you can read the strata like pages in a freshly opened book: dark basalt giving way to bands of schist, then a pale granite heart exposed to air for the first time since it cooled from magma.

The scale is planetary. The rift runs for over a thousand miles, a suture in the crust deep enough to generate its own weather. When mist rises from the wet floor far below, it does not behave like valley fog. It becomes a weather system. Clouds form halfway down the chasm and rain sideways onto the city's tiers. Lightning arcs between the walls. The air at different elevations moves at different speeds, different temperatures, different densities, and the city's engineers have learned to read these rivers of atmosphere the way sailors read currents. They have to. The city is built into the wind.

And yet for all this scale, the city feels almost empty. The density parameter translates here into a strange, cathedral hollowness. The structures are vast—immense factories, turbine halls, habitation blocks with windows by the thousand—but they are separated by gulfs of open air. Walkways span chasms hundreds of feet across, mere threads of metal and cable. One residential block might house ten thousand souls, but the next habitable structure is a quarter mile away across a void filled with moving cloud. The citizens travel by cable car and suspended tram, by iron bridges that hum with the vibration of the deep machinery, by narrow staircases carved into the living cliff. The spaces between are not wasted; they are the lungs of the city, the channels through which the perpetual wind moves. Sound carries strangely in this architecture of gaps. A voice from a distant platform arrives moments late and stripped of its lower registers, rendered thin and metallic by the journey.

The sound. The sound is constant and immense.

From the industrial heart of Siphon comes a deep, rhythmic thrumming: the great pumps. They are the reason the city exists. The rift floor, three miles down, is a place of perpetual wet darkness, a realm where the planet's groundwater seeps from the fractured stone in weeping sheets and mineral-thick drips. It collects in a black lake that never sees the sky. The pumps—piston-driven colossi, each the size of a small stadium—draw this water upward through a web of pipes that scale the cliff faces like iron ivy. The water is cold, just above freezing, heavy with dissolved metals and strange chemistries leached from the deep rock. It emerges at the city's various levels, where it is heated, filtered, and put to work: driving turbines, cooling forges, feeding the hydroponic stacks, and finally, after its long climb, venting as steam from a thousand exhaust ports that wreathe the upper reaches of the city in perpetual, drifting plumes.

The sound of the pumps is a heartbeat, felt in the bones more than heard—a subsonic pulse that propagates through the rock itself. Layered over it is the higher-frequency chorus of industry: the whine of turbines, the clang of forging hammers, the hiss of steam releases, the grinding of cable cars on their endless loops, the electric hum of transformers. And beneath and through it all, the wind. The wind in the rift never stops. It is a feature of the geography, locked into a stable equilibrium by the temperature differential between the cold depths and the slightly warmer plateau above. It moans through the cable stays, it whistles in the joints of the bridges, it sets whole sections of metal grating to singing in dissonant chords. The citizens of Siphon have long since stopped noticing it consciously. You can tell a newcomer because they shout to be heard. A local knows to speak close and clear, letting the wind carry the words away rather than fighting it.

The vegetation is lush, but in a way peculiar to this cold, high, sun-starved environment. No trees grow here—there is not enough soil, not enough light in the depths. Instead, the city is wrapped in a pelt of mosses, lichens, and moisture-loving ferns that cling to every surface where the pumped water leaks or condenses. The hydroponic stacks—tiered towers of steel trays lit by the waste glow of arc lamps—produce the city's food: dense, nutrient-rich kelps and fungi, cold-tolerant cultivars of leaf vegetables that grow in pale, rapid profusion. The air in the grow-halls is thick with humidity and the smell of wet earth, a startling contrast to the mineral cold of the exterior walkways. There are hanging gardens on the residential blocks, cascades of hardy vines that have been bred over generations to thrive in the constant drip and the filtered gray light. The green is everywhere, but it is a wet green, a green of condensation and seep, a green that slicks the metal rather than softens it.

There are no animals. None. No birds ride the thermals of the rift. No insects hum in the grow-halls. No fish swim in the black lake at the bottom. The water that seeps from the stone is sterile in its deep origin, too cold, too mineral-saturated, too alien in its chemistry to support anything but the simplest extremophile bacteria—and even those are scarce. The city's biomass is entirely vegetal, fungal, and human. This absence is so total and so ancient that it has shaped the culture in ways the citizens no longer register. There are no words for the sounds animals make, because no one has ever heard them. Children's stories have no beasts, no birds, no fish. The monsters in their tales are the wind, the dark, the sound of metal fatigue, the hypothetical silence if the pumps ever stopped. The human mind, denied animal life, has populated the world with animate forces instead—the wind is a presence, the pumps are living things with moods, the pipes sing, the city itself breathes.

The light is normal light, insofar as daylight filtered through three miles of mist and steam can be considered normal. At the top of the rift, where the administrative spires and the landing platforms break out onto the plateau, there is genuine sky, pale and cold and often gray with cloud. The sun, when it appears, is a distant coin of weak silver. As you descend into the city, the light dims through gradations: the upper tiers are daylit, the middle tiers exist in a perpetual twilight like a heavy overcast, and the lower tiers—where the pump stations and the deep maintenance crews work—know only the orange glare of arc lamps and the blue flicker of electrical discharge. The citizens calibrate their circadian rhythms to the shift schedules, not the sun. Time is measured in pump cycles.

And nothing changes. This is the most unsettling aspect of Siphon, the one that visitors from the scattered outposts on the plateau find hardest to bear. The city is in a frozen equilibrium, a perfect balance of forces that admits no variation. The pumps run at a constant rhythm. The wind blows at a constant speed. The temperature in any given tier varies by less than a degree over the course of a year. The mist rises to exactly the same level each day, condenses at the same hour, and falls as drizzle at the same hour. There are no seasons. There is no weather other than the eternal, unchanging weather of the rift. The engineers who built this place—whoever they were, in whatever deep past—achieved such a perfect integration of machine and geography that the two have become a single system without gradients, without fluctuation, a thermodynamic plateau that extends indefinitely in all directions. Even the human society has equilibrated. The same families have worked the same jobs for generations. The same shift patterns have governed waking and sleeping for centuries. Birth and death rates are perfectly matched, held in balance by customs so old they are no longer recognized as choices. The city does not grow. It does not shrink. It is.

Strange, yes. Deeply strange. The weirdness of Siphon is not a surface strangeness, not a grotesquerie or a violation of physics. It is the strangeness of a system that should not be stable but is. It is the strangeness of a place so perfectly adapted to its inhuman parameters that humanity itself has become one of the parameters. The citizens move through the perpetual mist on their eternal errands, crossing bridges that sway with the eternal wind, their voices absorbed by the eternal hum of the pumps. They are not unhappy. They are not happy. They are simply Siphon, as much a part of the city as the pipes and the moss and the weeping stone.

Below them all, the black lake gathers the drip of eons, and the pumps never stop.
