There is no sky. There is no horizon, no sun, no weather. The world is a crevice — a single, contained chamber of heat and noise, embedded deep within something larger, something unseen and perhaps unseeable. You cannot stand. You cannot stretch out your arms. The space is just tall enough to crouch in, a low-ceilinged hollow pressed between two converging planes of black, glassy rock. The walls are not walls in any architectural sense; they are the raw, uneroded faces of a fracture, a wound that opened in the planet’s crust and never had time to smooth or settle. The rock is obsidian-dark, but not uniform — it glints with metallic veins of something that might be iron, or something that might not, and its surface is studded with sharp, fresh facets that have never felt a drop of water or the scouring touch of wind. This is a place that has only just begun to exist.

The heat is the first thing you notice after the confinement. It is a pressing, saturated heat — not the dry bake of a desert, nor the humid smother of a jungle, but an industrial heat, the kind of heat that comes from a process. It radiates upward from the floor, which is not a floor but a continuous, slowly-undulating surface of cooling slag. The ground is black glass, too, but here it is rippled and folded like frozen molasses, still holding the memory of fluid motion. In the deepest creases, a faint orange glow still seeps through the thin crust, and the air above them shimmers with heat distortion. The temperature is constant, unrelenting, somewhere well above the tolerance of unprotected flesh, and it carries with it a heavy, metallic scent — hot iron, hot stone, a tang like the air after a lightning strike but sustained, endless.

And it is loud. The sound is not a single source but a saturation, a full-spectrum drone that fills the crevice so completely it becomes a physical presence, a pressure against the skin. It is the sound of a flywheel the size of a mountain spinning somewhere far below, transmitted up through the rock and into the bones of anyone who enters. A deep, grinding hum — a hundred cycles a second, a thousand — layered with higher frequencies: the hiss of escaping gas, the crackle of contracting stone, a rhythmic, percussive thumping like the beat of a colossal heart. The sounds do not echo; the space is too small, too packed with surfaces. They simply fill it, from wall to wall, floor to ceiling, leaving no silence anywhere.

There is no water here. Not a molecule. The air is bone-dry, a thin mixture of gases that have never known moisture — perhaps nitrogen, perhaps carbon dioxide, perhaps something exhaled by the machinery below. There are no plants, no trace of vegetation, not even the faintest dusting of organic matter. This world has never known life, not in any form recognizable as such. And yet, the crevice is not empty.

The fauna is abundant, though you might not recognize it at first. They are drawn to the heat, or perhaps they are part of the process — living components of the vast industrial metabolism that churns beneath the rock. They are small things, most of them, forced into miniature scale by the cramped dimensions of the crevice. Beetle-forms with carapaces of black chitin that gleam like polished obsidian, their legs tipped with fine hairs that grip the glassy walls. They move in slow, deliberate processions, antennae waving in the superheated air, following chemical gradients too faint to name. There are pale, blind worms, no thicker than a thread, that emerge from hairline cracks in the cooling slag and writhe in slow knots before vanishing again. There are things that might be larvae, fat and segmented, glowing faintly with a sickly orange bioluminescence — or perhaps it is not bioluminescence at all, but actual thermal glow, their bodies so hot they emit visible light. They are slow. Everything here is slow. The beetles take minutes to cross a single handspan. The worms unknot themselves over hours. The pulsations of the larva-things match the deep, distant thumping from below, a circadian rhythm measured in shifts, not days.

This is an embryonic landscape, a place still under construction. The erosion score is nearly zero — not because something prevents erosion, but because there has been no time for it. The crevice was torn open recently, geologically speaking. Perhaps it was yesterday. Perhaps it is still happening, widening increment by slow increment as the machinery below shifts and settles. The raw mineral faces are jagged, unweathered, devoid of dust or sand. Nothing has been worn down, smoothed, or softened. Every surface is fresh, sharp, hot from the mold.

And there is something else. Something about the walls. If you look closely — and you have nothing but time here, no room to move, nothing to do but observe — you can see that the mineral veins are not random. They are organized. Parallel lines of conductive metal run in regular bundles along the fracture faces, like the traces on a circuit board, scaled up to planetary dimensions. Or scaled down, depending on your perspective. This pocket is a single feature, a micro-environment, and it is embedded within something larger — an engine, perhaps, or a factory, or a computational substrate of unimaginable complexity. The crevice is a cooling channel, or a waste vent, or a deliberate interface port, and the creatures that inhabit it are not wild fauna in any traditional sense. They are maintenance organisms, scavengers of waste heat, living components of a machine ecology that builds and repairs itself across geological time.

There is no tech visible in the sense of made artifacts — no pipes, no grates, no stamped metal or plastic housings. But the tech is everywhere. It is the heat. It is the sound. It is the conductive veins in the obsidian walls and the slow, patient fauna that crawl along them, occasionally extruding droplets of molten mineral to patch microfractures or reinforce degrading traces. The technology is not built on top of the landscape; it *is* the landscape. The crevice itself is a component, a part of the engine, and its formation was not an accident of geology but a designed event, a deliberate fracture to facilitate cooling, or access, or waste transport, or something else entirely.

The light, when it exists, comes from below. Faint orange glows through the thinnest parts of the slag floor, the soft bioluminescence-thermal emission of the larva-things, the occasional spark when a worm-thread touches a live trace and completes a circuit for a brief, bright moment. But mostly it is dark. Deep dark. The dark of a sealed room with no windows, buried under a mile of rock, lit only by embers. The dark is warm and loud and very, very close.

There is a slowness to everything, a patience that borders on the eternal. The temperature does not fluctuate; it is a constant parameter, a maintained state. The sound never stops, never changes pitch or volume. The creatures move at their gradual pace, following their gradual cycles. This is a place of minimal flux, of weak gradients that take centuries to shift, of processes that were set in motion eons ago and will continue for eons more. It is a held breath. A steady state. A machine running at idle, waiting for a command that might never come, or that might come tomorrow, or that might arrive in a thousand thousand years.

And yet, for all its strangeness — the glowing larvae, the living circuitry, the industrial heartbeat — the place is, by the standards of whatever world this is, normal. It is not a marvel or an anomaly. It is a functioning component, one of thousands, millions, doing exactly what it was designed to do. The weirdness is background radiation, unremarkable, not worth noticing. It is just a hot, dark, loud, slow, crowded, dry, high, packed, industrial, embryonic, hidden crevice. It is just a part of the machine. It is just a place.
