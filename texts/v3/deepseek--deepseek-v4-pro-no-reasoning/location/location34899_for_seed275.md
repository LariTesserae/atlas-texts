You descend, and the world turns inward.

At first you think it’s a root cellar, or perhaps a mine—some human-shaped hollow clawed from the earth. But the stairs keep going, and after a while the walls stop being walls and start being *everything*. You’re inside the weave of it now: not a cave, not a tunnel, but a continuous fibrous matrix, pale and dry as old paper, spongy underfoot, giving back no echo when you step. The silence is strange—not dead, but listening. The air is cool and still, about the temperature of a shaded morning, and it smells faintly of bruised fennel and something starchier, like raw potato.

You’re in a city of roots.

Not roots as you know them—not the reaching fingers of trees, not tangled underground messes. These are architectural. Vaulted. Intentional. The space opens around you in a great spreading concourse, fifty feet high in places, ribbed with pale buttresses that curve and flow like frozen cream. Every surface is made of the same creamy-beige tissue—fine, hairless, faintly translucent when you press your hand against it, glowing a little from some internal scattering of light. There are no lamps. The walls themselves carry a quiet bioluminescence, suffused and even, as if the whole place had swallowed daylight and was letting it seep back out over centuries.

The city—and it is a city, unmistakably, with arches and galleries and winding ways that branch off into dim blue distances—is alive. Not metaphorically. It is one organism. Every wall, floor, ceiling, stair, and balustrade is a continuous mycelial-plant matrix, a single vast individual that has grown itself into the shape of a settlement. There are no bricks, no mortar, no seams. The buildings flow into the streets which flow into the towers which flow back into the buildings. Walk long enough and you’ll pass the same pale cathedral three times, each time from a different angle, and understand that the organism repeats itself in fractal spirals, a Fibonacci city dreaming its own plan over and over.

It’s cold, but not barren. This is a place of vegetation at its most total: 2.9 out of 3, the scale says, and you believe it. There is nothing here that is not plant, or plant-derived, or plant-in-waiting. Even the thin dust that sifts down when you brush a wall is spores, dormant, patient. There is no soil, no exposed mineral. The material *is* the plant. The plant *is* the world. The idea of “ground” feels quaint—here, ground is just another surface the organism has decided to make horizontal for a while.

And yet it’s dry. Profoundly dry. The water rating is near zero, and you feel it in your sinuses, in the faint crackle of your skin. This isn’t a rotting place. There’s no damp, no mold beyond the healthy pale skin of the structure itself. The chemistry here doesn’t run on water the way you understand it. Some other solvent, maybe, or a metabolism so slow that water is irrelevant—silicate exchange, or direct electron transfer along the miles of silken fiber. The air has a mineral flatness, like the inside of a geode. When you exhale, your breath doesn’t fog. The place takes your moisture and tucks it away somewhere, politely, without comment.

You’re not alone.

The fauna rating is middling but active, and as your eyes adjust you begin to see them: small, pale things moving in the cathedral spaces. Some are clearly animals—six-legged lizard shapes with skin like pressed petals, clinging motionless to pillars for long minutes before skittering upward in a rustle of dry leaves. Others blur the line. There’s a creature that looks like a dangling loop of intestine, pulsing slowly, and when it detects you it withdraws into a pore in the wall without a sound. The pores close behind it with the faintest kiss of tissue reuniting. Predator-prey dynamics play out in miniature dramas across the vaults: a spined ball of what looks like burdock gallops silently along a balcony and is ambushed by flat white discs that peel off the ceiling and smother it. The struggle is brief, soundless, and when it’s over the discs fuse and flow away, leaving nothing. The city cleans itself.

This is an industrial place, somehow. The tech rating insists on it, and after a while you find the evidence. In the deeper galleries, the plant walls have grown sockets—regular, machined-looking apertures where things plug in. You see banks of them, thousands, each the size of a fist, and trailing from some of them are pale cables that merge back into the wall. There’s a low hum you can’t hear so much as feel in your teeth. Once, you come across a hall where the floor is transparent and beneath it, suspended in a yellow gel, rows of repeating organic forms sit motionless—something between a battery and a fetus, each one connected to its neighbors by a fine lace of vessels. A factory. A power plant. Grown, not built, but unmistakably engineered. You realize the city isn’t just a plant. It’s a machine made of plant, manufacturing something—chemicals, energy, information, maybe copies of itself. The scale is regional, this place goes on for miles, and you’re standing in one small lobe of an enormous distributed organism that extends through the crust in layers and loops and great slow pumping chambers.

The strangeness accumulates quietly. There’s a sound—mostly quiet, the rating said, and that’s true, but silence here is a relative thing. The more you listen, the more you hear: a fine, sandy rustle, a billion microscopic movements, the sound of a place *growing* in real time. You pass a wall where a new buttress is being laid down, pale fingers of tissue knitting themselves out of the air at a pace just barely perceptible. It’s embryonal, this place. The erosion rating is practically zero—not because nothing wears, but because everything is being constantly renewed from within. The city is not old. It’s coming-into-being, right now, perpetually. You’re walking through a construction site that has been under construction for ten thousand years and will be under construction for ten thousand more. The raw edges are everywhere if you know how to look: places where the pattern is still feeling its way forward, where a hallway duplicates itself three times in slightly different widths before settling on a final form.

Light is your constant companion. The normal rating feels like a joke until you realize: the whole structure glows, evenly, without source, a steady overcast daylight translated underground. It doesn’t flicker. It doesn’t change. It makes the place feel, against all reason, like a public library at two in the afternoon. You can see perfectly. You wish, sometimes, that you couldn’t.

There’s a plaza of sorts, circular, ringed with pillars that twist gently like barley sugar. In the center, a fountain—but instead of water, a fine mist of spores rises, catches the light, drifts outward. A few of the petal-skinned lizards are gathered there, catching the spores on outstretched tongues. One of them turns to look at you, and its eyes are the same pale beige as the walls, with no pupil you can see. It watches you for a count of ten, then flows up a pillar and is gone. The quiet returns.

You sit on a bench that the wall has thoughtfully extruded for you—or maybe it was always there, who can tell—and you try to hold the whole thing in your mind. The coolth. The dryness. The endless slow deliberate growth. The scattered animal life, finding its niches in the body of something vastly larger. The hum of industry, the sense of production and purpose woven into every strand. The scale that opens downward and outward, balcony after balcony descending into a blue haze, stairways that spiral away to nothing. The weirdness, not aggressive but pervasive, the slow dawning sense that you are inside a mind, or a stomach, or a poem written in cellulose and silence.

After a long time, you notice that the bench has subtly reshaped itself to fit you better. You stand up quickly. The bench doesn’t react. It just waits, pale and patient, for someone else to sit down.

Ahead, a stairway leads deeper, curving left into the dimness. The light follows you. It will follow you anywhere here. The city wants you to see.
