It is called the Chorus Plateau, though no one remembers who named it, or when. The place gives the impression of having always existed just as it is now—though paradoxically it looks freshly made, its edges sharp, its contours unsoftened by time.

You arrive on foot, following a trail of crushed white stone that winds upward through thinning air. The elevation makes itself known first in your lungs, then in the quality of light: thin and clean, casting shadows so crisp they seem etched into the ground. You are high—not mountain-peak high, not gasping-at-the-sky high, but high enough that the horizon has retreated to an almost theoretical distance, a blue-gray suggestion beyond layers of descending terrain. The plateau itself is broad. Open. It extends far enough that you cannot take it all in at once, but it is not infinite; you can sense boundaries, distant ridges, the way the land eventually falls away into lower country.

What strikes you first is the city. You did not expect a city up here. But there it is—a settlement spread across the plateau with a logic you almost grasp, then lose. The buildings are low, rarely more than a single story, constructed of the same pale stone that forms the plateau itself. They are built directly on the rock, no foundations dug, no mortar visible. The walls are dry-stacked but fitted with an impossible precision; each block shares a face so perfectly flush with its neighbors that the seams are barely visible. The construction feels early—primitive tools, patient hands—but the age is unreadable. The edges of the stone blocks are still sharp. Unground by wind, unmossed by moisture. As if carved yesterday. As if carved forever ago and somehow preserved.

The city is sparse. Buildings are scattered across the plateau with wide gaps between them, broad avenues of bare stone, open plazas that hold nothing but the same pale rock floor and here and there a low ring of stones—fire pits, maybe, or gathering places. The arrangement is not random; you sense a pattern, a distribution too even to be accidental, but the purpose eludes you. There is space here. The empty places feel deliberate, held in reserve.

No one moves in the streets. But the city is not empty. You see them now: figures seated or reclining near the buildings, in doorways, on the flat rooftops. They are still. Completely still. As you draw closer, you realize they are not statues—their skin has the slight yielding quality of flesh, their hair lifts in the occasional breeze, their eyes are open. But they do not blink. They do not turn their heads. They are caught mid-gesture: one reaches toward a low table where a clay cup rests, another leans forward as if speaking, a third lies curled on a stone bench with a hand tucked under a cheek. You do not touch them. Something tells you not to touch them.

The air is cool. Not cold—a steady coolness that sits just below the threshold of discomfort, as if the temperature has settled into a single value and held it for a very long time. The light is ordinary daylight, a midmorning brightness that does not change, and after a while you realize with a strange vertigo that it truly does not change. The sun—where is the sun? The sky is a luminous gray-white, no clouds, no visible disc of light, just a pervasive radiance that casts no shifting shadows because it never moves.

Water is present but not abundant. The plateau holds pockets of moisture in unexpected places. Along one broad avenue, a channel has been carved into the stone—shallow, no wider than two hands—and a thin sheet of water moves through it with a silence that is almost perfect. The water is clear and cold, flowing with a steady, unhurried purpose. It emerges from a low stone structure at one end of the channel—not a spring exactly, but a collection point, a condensation surface where moisture beads on sloped stone fins and drips into a basin. The air here is noticeably damper; you can feel it on your face, a cool kiss of humidity. In other parts of the city, water gathers in shallow basins, in carved depressions, in narrow rills that trace geometric paths between buildings. The system is simple. Handmade. But it works without apparent maintenance, and the water runs clear and pure.

Wherever there is water, there is green.

The vegetation is lush—far lusher than the sparse city and cool air would suggest. Around each water feature, plants grow in profusion: broad-leaved ground covers, mosses in deep greens and near-blacks, ferns with uncurling fronds, vines that weave through the cracks between stones. They are soft and vivid and almost aggressive in their thriving, yet they are also orderly. Nothing overgrows the channels. Nothing blocks the pathways. The plants seem to know where they are permitted. In the open spaces, away from the water, the stone is bare. The contrast is sharp: gray-white stone, then a sudden explosion of green wherever moisture pools. The air near these green pockets carries a faint, clean vegetal scent—wet leaves, living soil, something faintly sweet.

Animals are everywhere, and this is the part that unsettles you most, because they are like the people. Wild goats with curved horns stand motionless on rooftops. Birds perch on windowsills—small gray birds, dozens of them—all facing the same direction, all unmoving. Near one of the water channels, a fox is caught in the act of lapping water, its pink tongue extended, ripples frozen around its muzzle. Insects rest on leaves without quivering. A snake lies looped on a warm stone, its body arranged in perfect coils, no tongue flickering. The abundance of fauna is staggering—life crowds the city in numbers that seem impossible for the sparse buildings—but nothing moves. Nothing makes a sound.

The quiet is near-total. Your footsteps are the only noise, and even they are muted; the stone does not echo, does not crunch, seems almost to absorb sound. When you hold your breath, you hear nothing—no wind in the grass (there is no grass, except in the green pockets, and it does not rustle), no birdsong, no distant rumble of weather. The breeze that moves through the open spaces is silent; you feel it on your skin, see the hair of the motionless people stir, but it makes no sound against the buildings. It is not a dead quiet. It is a held quiet, as if the whole plateau is listening, or waiting, or preserving something.

The strangeness accumulates. A primitive city, but built with skill that feels beyond primitive tools. A lush ecology fed by sparse, deliberate water. Motionless inhabitants who are not dead, but not alive in any way you understand. The sharp edges everywhere—the uneroded stone, the unworn paths, the fresh-carved look of the buildings—suggest a place that has just begun. But the stillness, the completeness of the design, the way everything holds its position, suggest a place that has always been this way and will never change.

You find the edge of the plateau eventually. A sheer drop into mist. The water channels, you notice, do not flow off the edge—they circulate, loop back, feed into the condensation structures. The system is closed. Nothing enters, nothing leaves, except perhaps the mist below, which rises now and then to brush the rock faces and retreat.

The people never wake. The cups are never drunk from. The conversations never finish. The fox never drinks. And you, walking among them, feel like a match struck in a dark room—a brief, bright, moving heat in a place that has achieved perfect equilibrium and held it, sharp-edged and green and wet and silent, for as long as the light has been fixed in its unchanging sky.

Somewhere in the city, you heard it said—or read it carved into a stone, or simply felt it in the cool damp air—that the world here ended as soon as it began, that the builders achieved exactly what they intended and then stopped, leaving a snapshot of a perfect moment that erosion will never touch, that time has forgotten to erode.
