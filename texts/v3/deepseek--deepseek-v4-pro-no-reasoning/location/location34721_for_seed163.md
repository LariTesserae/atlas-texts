The first thing that hits you is the noise.

It is everywhere and nowhere, a sound without a source that you could easily walk toward or away from. It is not the noise of engines, exactly—not the chug of pistons or the whine of turbines—but something closer, more ambient, a deep pervasive thrum that seems to hum up through the soles of your feet and down from the bright, featureless white of the sky. A condenser hum. The sound of a machine shedding heat, or gathering water, or simply existing on a scale that makes your skull feel like a tuning fork.

You are high up. That much is clear from the way the air feels in your lungs, thin and cool and with a mineral cleanness that tastes of raw stone. Temperature a steady coolth, maybe fifty degrees, enough that your breath doesn’t steam but your skin stays tight and alert. But “high up” doesn’t mean mountain peaks and distant valleys. The terrain here is something else entirely. It is still being decided.

The ground underfoot is not soil. It is a pale, porous stone, the color of unglazed porcelain, and it rises in raw unfinished hummocks and shelves, as if a gigantic hand had been shaping a landscape from wet clay and then simply stopped, leaving the forms half-emerged. There are no sharp edges. Everything has the soft, blurred look of something still in the process of becoming: swells that might decide to be hills, shallow basins that could become lakes if enough water gathers, low ridges that wander a short distance and then taper into nothing, as if they lost the conviction to continue. The rock itself is strange. Up close you can see it is riddled with tiny pores, a lithic sponge, and from many of these pores water is seeping. Not gushing, not dripping—just a steady slow exudation, a weeping that keeps the entire surface of the world permanently wet, slick with a thin film of cold water that catches the bright diffuse light from every direction.

And that water is the reason for everything else.

Because growing from the rock, upon the rock, through the rock, is the vegetation. The word “vegetation” feels inadequate. This is plant life imagined as total environment, a botanical saturation that leaves almost no surface unclaimed. But it is not a jungle, not a thicket. It is sparse—that is the strange genius of it. The plants are enormous but widely spaced, and each one is a world.

They rise from the wet porous stone as single vertical trunks, some as wide as houses, their surfaces deeply corrugated, wrapped in layers of something that is neither bark nor moss but a composite tissue, dark green verging on black, slick and cool to the touch. They ascend thirty, forty, fifty feet before branching, and when they branch they do so in perfect radial symmetry, sending out arms in all directions like the ribs of an umbrella. From these arms hang curtains of a finer plant material, pale green and translucent, too delicate to be leaves, too structured to be moss. They hang straight down in sheets, overlapping one another, and as you watch you realize they are moving. Not in a breeze—there is no breeze—but with a patient slow contraction, a peristaltic ripple that passes from top to bottom in waves, taking almost a minute to complete a single cycle. Each curtain, you realize, is condensing water from the air. Droplets bead on the fine filaments, gather, run down to the lowest edge, and fall. The ground beneath each of these arborescent structures is draped with a skirt of smaller plants, ground-hugging things like flattened ferns, all of them glistening wetly, all of them angled to catch the falling drips.

Between the great plants, the rock plain extends in its half-finished topography. Pools have begun to form in some of the shallow basins, and each pool is a startling mirror of the bright sky, perfectly still, bordered by more of the low flattened ferns. Nothing moves in the pools. No insects skate on the surface, no larvae wriggle in the depths. The water is clear as glass and just as lifeless, so cold it seems to ache when you touch it.

You walk for what might be half a mile across this terrain. The thrumming sound never fades; if anything it seems to modulate, to have a slow pulse of its own, a deepening and shallowing of tone that happens too gradually to track in real time, like the movement of the sun across the sky. You only notice the change when you stop and realize the note is slightly different than it was before. The whole world is a slow machine. The plants are pulling water from air and rock, cycling it through their translucent tissues, releasing it in a calibrated rhythm of droplets. The rock is still forming, mineral by mineral, some process of deposition that happens on a geological timescale but is, you can sense, still active, still emergent. This landscape is under construction.

Scattered across it, so sparse you could easily miss them, are signs that someone has been here before. Or something.

The first you find is a length of metal, rusted to a vivid burnt orange, half-sunk into the porous rock. It is tubular, about the width of your arm, and where the rock has seeped up around its base, the corrosion has bloomed outward in crystalline formations, the rust and the mineral interacting in some slow chemistry. Farther on, there is a concrete footing, perfectly square, with the nubs of sheared bolts still embedded in its corners. Whatever it once supported is long gone. And at one point you come to a place where the rock has been cut in a straight line, a narrow channel maybe three feet deep and two across, its walls unnaturally smooth. Water runs along its bottom, clear and shallow, moving with a purpose toward one of the distant basins.

These remnants don’t feel like ruins. Ruins imply a past, a fall. These feel more like scraps, test fittings for a structure that was never built, or that is still being built, very slowly, by a contractor who only visits once every thousand years. They are the faint marks of an industrial process that has not yet ramped up to full production. The machinery of this place is not the machinery of pistons and boilers. It is the machinery of a slow planet being terraformed, engineered, coaxed into a new configuration by intelligences that operate on a timescale indifferent to human brevity.

After a while you become aware of fauna. Rare, the parameters said, and rare is the word. But in a place this wet and this thickly planted, there should be more. There should be clouds of gnats, the croak of frogs, the skitter of lizards. Instead there is only the patient dripping and the deep mechanical hum. But then you see it, or you don’t see it so much as sense a change. On the surface of one of the great plant trunks, a patch of the dark green tissue is moving. Detaching. It slides downward, a shapeless mass about the size of your two hands together, gliding on a film of the omnipresent moisture. It reaches the base of the trunk and flows out onto the wet rock, and then it shapes itself, for just a moment, into something with a definite orientation—a wider end, a narrower end, a rippling fringe—before subsiding back into formlessness and disappearing into a crevice. A slime mold, maybe, or something taxonomically adjacent. An animal composed of a single enormous cell, or a colony acting as one, grazing on the biofilm that glazes every surface. It is the only moving creature you see.

The light never changes. That bright blank sky, so evenly luminous you can’t tell where the sun is or whether there is a sun at all, seems to press down with its brightness, a weight of photons. The temperature never changes. The sound never changes, except to make its slow slow circles through the frequencies. This is a place of minimal flux, a world whose dynamic is the dynamic of a clock hand, not a heart. The water seeps, the plants condense, the rock grows, the channel carries its trickle down to the pool, and the pool reflects the featureless sky, and nothing hurries and nothing rests and nothing dies in any way you would recognize as death.

And yet the place is not peaceful. It is too intentional for peace. Every part of it feels like a component in a system whose purpose is not malice but is also not comfort. You are standing inside an engine. A vast, slow, water-driven, plant-staffed engine that is building a world from the raw porcelain stone upward, that is taking the raw materials of geology and climate and biology and shaping them into something with a function. The scattered industrial traces—the pipe, the footing, the cut channel—are the exposed logic of that engine, the rare visible joints in a machine that is otherwise made of flesh and rock and light and sound.

The deafening hum is the engine's idle.

You have the distinct feeling, standing there with the cold wet rock under your boots and the great slow plants condensing water around you and the formless single-celled thing grazing the biofilm at your feet, that you are seeing a world at the very beginning of its operational life. Not a young world—a new world. Commissioned but still in its shakedown cruise. The bugs are still being worked out. The slime molds are probably one of the bugs.

You stay until the light begins, for the first time, infinitesimally, to dim. The sky’s brightness lessens by a fraction of a percent, so slowly you doubt your own perception, and the great plants respond with another fraction-of-a-percent change in their own slow pulse, and the hum’s note shifts one more step along its endless patient scale. The cycle is turning. Night, or something like it, is hours and hours away, but it is coming, in the same unhurried way that everything in this place comes.

You turn and walk back the way you came, following your own faint footprints in the film of water on the stone, and by the time you reach the edge of the surveyed area, your prints have already begun to fade, the porous rock slowly weeping new moisture up from its depths, erasing the evidence that anything warm and quick and temporary ever passed this way at all.
