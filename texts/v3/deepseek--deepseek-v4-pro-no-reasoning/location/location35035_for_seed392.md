The air is the first thing. It presses in, dense and lucid, carrying so much water that breathing feels almost like drinking. The brightness is immense and directionless—not from any single sun but as though the sky itself has been whitewashed with light. No shadows pool anywhere. No shadows at all.

This is a place built by hands, but not hands like ours now. The entire world here has been made, stitched together from pieces of itself, and every surface bears the texture of palm and fingertip and the scrape of a simple tool. There are no stones that haven't been turned, no earth that hasn't been lifted and reset. What first appears as a natural valley resolves, on longer looking, into a vast shallow basin whose rim is perfectly even, glimpsed through heat-haze maybe a mile distant.

The basin floor is a checkerwork of shallow plots, each a handspan lower than its neighbors, separated by raised seams of packed and burnished mud mixed with pale broken shell. Water stands in most of them—still, clear, warm as blood. In some plots the water is darker, carpeted with a wet fur of emerald algae that moves faintly in a current too slow to see. In others, broad floating leaves lie flat as platters, their edges curling upward just slightly, and from their centers rise clusters of tiny trumpet-shaped flowers the color of unglazed clay. The leaves are not anchored. There are no roots here.

The vegetation is abundant but it doesn't compete. Each thing has its place, and the places are widely spaced, deliberate. Between the water plots run paths of the same mud-and-shell compound, hardened and swept clean, wide enough for perhaps two people to walk abreast. It is all curves, these paths. Nothing meets at a right angle. The whole network flows in slow spirals around central features: a cluster of large fired-clay cisterns, their bellies sweating condensation, their tops covered with coarse-woven mats; a low platform carrying dozens of clay bowls arranged in concentric rings, each bowl holding something pale that glistens and does not move; a standing frame of lashed poles from which hang hundreds of thin, translucent sheets, stirring faintly in a breeze too gentle to feel.

And there is sound, but barely. A liquid, intermittent tick—like a single drop falling into a pool, repeating without pattern, now close, now farther away. The wet exhalation of the cisterns. A papery rustle when one of the hanging sheets shifts against its neighbor. But mostly the sound is the sound of warmth itself, a kind of deep quiet hum that might be inside the ear, might be the blood moving.

Creatures are present in numbers that only slowly register. In every pool there is at least one, often several. They are not fish, not lizards, though they borrow from both. Each is about the length of a human forearm, with smooth skin the color of wet terracotta and a broad, flat body that grades seamlessly into a paddle tail. Their eyes are large and round and set high on the head, perpetually alert, gold-rimmed, lidless. They float with only eyes and the ridge of their spine breaching the surface, perfectly motionless, perfectly spaced. No pool holds more than five. When one does move—and movement is rare—it is a slow, undulating glide that parts the water without making a sound, and the others in the pool adjust their positions incrementally to maintain the distance between them. When they reach the edge of their plot, they turn back. They never cross the raised seams into a neighbor's water. There is no visible barrier, only the seam of shell-studded mud, but they do not cross it.

In other zones, scattered across paths and on the sun-warmed platforms, are creatures of a different sort. These are built closer to the ground, many-legged, segmented like millipedes but as long as a full stride, each segment armored with a single curved plate of some dark, polished material. They move so slowly that the eye can't catch their motion—you look away, look back, and the creature has advanced by half its length. They carry on their backs miniature landscapes: tiny gardens of grey-green lichen, clusters of minute orange fruiting bodies on thread-stalks, even, on the largest, a single shallow depression holding a few drops of collected water in which microscopic things dart and spin. They are living terraces. They are tended. On some, the lichen has been recently scraped in certain places, leaving clean scored lines in the dark carapace beneath.

The heat never breaks. It is a constant, a medium, like the light. The air doesn't cool where it moves over the water; the water is the same temperature as the air, sweet and faintly bitter on the tongue, smelling of clean mud and green things growing in warmth. Nothing here seems stressed by the heat. There are no wilted leaves, no panting creatures. It is the temperature the world was made to hold, and it holds it.

The weirdness lies in the edges of seeing. At the boundary of the valley, where the rim meets the white sky, the geometry fails to resolve. Stare too long and the rim appears to be both a solid line and also the inner curve of a tremendous bowl hanging above, as though the valley were inverted somewhere out of sight, continuing overhead. Sometimes a creature in a distant pool appears, for a heartbeat, to be floating not on water but against a vertical wall. Sometimes the interlocking spiral of paths, if traced with the eye, does not end at the rim but seems to continue into the whiteness in a direction the mind cannot name, a fold in the plane. It is not a shift or a shimmer; it is a flaw in the premise of distance. The world is fully built, but its architecture has forgotten some foundation, some guarantee of up and down. The place is not hallucinating. It was made this way.

And nothing changes. The water doesn't evaporate, though the heat would demand it. The creatures do not seem to eat or sleep or reproduce. The plots are always at the same level. The hanging sheets never wear thin. The cisterns sweat and sweat and never empty. Time is absent, or frozen so completely that presence replaces sequence. This is a constructed world that reached a state and stopped, a steady hum of living terms arranged in a circuit with no voltage drop. The paths spiral inward but they also spiral outward, and the difference is merely a matter of where you decide to begin looking. The drop of water falls again, somewhere to the left. It has been falling for as long as the valley has existed, which is neither a long time nor a short time. Temporal words fail here. The place simply *is*, and then continues to be.
