It is not a place you walk into so much as a place you are suddenly inside, though the transition is difficult to pinpoint. There is no gate, no boundary marked on any map. You are climbing, always climbing, through cool air that smells of wet stone and something sharper underneath—not quite mineral, not quite living. The path underfoot is more suggestion than construction: flat stones laid without mortar, some still settling into the earth as if they were placed only yesterday, or are being placed even now, by hands you never see.

The space opens around you not into a valley but into a kind of vertical room. Imagine a cathedral that was never roofed, whose walls are the mountains themselves, but mountains that have not finished becoming mountains. The rock faces are dark with moisture, glistening in the diffuse grey light that falls from no particular direction. Water moves everywhere, not in streams but as a continuous weeping from the stone, a slow exudation that beads on every surface and drips with the regularity of a pulse. The ground underfoot is neither soil nor solid rock but something intermediary—a spongy mat of moss so thick and so ancient that walking on it feels like walking on the lungs of some enormous, sleeping animal. Each step releases a small sigh of water.

And yet this place is inhabited, has been built upon, though "built" might be the wrong word for what has happened here. Structures rise from the wet stone, but they seem less constructed than assembled, or perhaps accumulated—as if the mountain itself were learning to make shelters. Walls of stacked slate, fitted without chisel marks, lean against the living rock. Roofs of oiled hide and woven reed sag between them, dripping steadily. There are dozens of these dwellings, perhaps hundreds, climbing the terraced slopes in a loose spiral that follows some logic of drainage or devotion. They are empty now, or seem empty. The doorways are dark and low, and from within comes only the sound of water dripping into vessels placed to catch it—clay pots, hollowed logs, once even what looks like the bell of a great bronze horn, now green with verdigris and singing a low note with every falling drop. The whole settlement hums with this water-music, a chorale of plosive notes at different pitches, never quite synchronizing, never quite random.

Between the dwellings, the vegetation is extravagant. Not the chaotic tangle of a lowland jungle but something stranger—a lushness that belongs to the cool and the high. Mosses in fifty shades of green carpet every vertical surface, hanging in great drapes and curtains that stir almost imperceptibly, though there is no wind. Ferns unroll from crevices, their fronds beaded with condensation. There are trees, but they are not tall—twisted, patient things that grow sideways from the rock, their roots gripping the wet stone like fingers. Their leaves are small and dark and waxy, and they bear a fruit you do not recognize: pale, translucent spheres the size of a thumb-knuckle, glowing faintly from within. They are the only source of light beyond the grey sky, and at dusk, you imagine, this place must become a constellation of small cold stars.

It is loud here, but the loudness is strange. There is no wind, no birdsong, no animal cry. The sound is all water and stone and the deep, almost sub-audible resonance of the place itself. The dripping is constant, but it is not gentle—each drop falls from a great height, striking stone or hide or bronze with a report that echoes off the wet walls. The hollow spaces beneath the dwellings amplify these sounds, so that the whole settlement rings like a massive, slowly played lithophone. Beneath this, there is another sound: a deep, grinding creak that comes at long intervals, like the sound of a glacier settling, or a mountain range taking a breath. The ground trembles faintly with it. This is the sound of a landscape still in the act of becoming. The rock faces are raw, unweathered, showing the sharp planes of recent fracture. Scree slopes at the base of the cliffs look fresh, the stones still sharp-edged, lacking the rounding of time and water. This place is emerging, pushing upward even as you stand in it, the geology young and unfinished.

And it feels empty, despite the signs of habitation. The dwellings are maintained—the hides are oiled, the pots are not overflowing—but whoever built and tends them is absent, or invisible, or exists at a frequency you cannot perceive. You walk through the spiraling settlement and your footsteps are the only ones you hear. You call out and your voice is swallowed by the dripping, the creaking, the low hum. The air is cool enough to see your breath, but not cold enough to bite. It is the temperature of a root cellar, of a springhouse, of deep shade at high altitude. It does not change as the hours pass. There is no sun to mark time, only the slow, patient cycles of water condensing, dripping, pooling, evaporating, condensing again. The place breathes moisture like a second atmosphere.

And then you see the animal. Only once, and briefly. A small, dark shape moving along a ledge far above, silhouetted against the grey. It moves on four legs, but with a deliberateness that seems almost human—stopping, turning its head as if listening, then continuing out of sight. It makes no sound. You wait, but it does not reappear. The ledge where it walked shows no sign of a path, no worn stone, no scat. It might have been a goat, or something like a goat, but you cannot shake the impression that it was checking something, tending to something, that it had a purpose in its rounds.

This is the strangeness of the place: not that it is strange, but that it is ordinary in a way that does not quite fit. The proportions of the doorways are slightly off for human stature. The placement of the dwellings seems to follow a pattern you almost recognize—a constellation, a nervous system, a mnemonic diagram—but cannot quite name. The fruit glows with a light that casts no shadow. And even the water tastes strange when you finally cup your hands and drink: cold and clean, but with a faint electric tang, as if it has picked up something from the raw stone, some mineral still in solution that has not yet precipitated into the world's geology.

You understand, eventually, that you cannot stay. The place is not hostile, but it is not finished, and you are not meant to witness its becoming. You turn and begin the long descent, and the sound of the dripping follows you for a long way down, diminishing note by note until it is only memory, and the air grows warmer and drier, and the path underfoot becomes path again, and you are back in the world that has already been made.
