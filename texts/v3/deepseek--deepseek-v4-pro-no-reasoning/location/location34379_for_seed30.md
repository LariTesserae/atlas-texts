The air itself is half-liquid, a warm exhalation of stone and sky pressing close in the small bowl of this place. It is a pocket, perhaps a hundred meters across, cupped high on some broad-shouldered ridge, where elevation meets a strange, weeping geology. The ground underfoot is not truly ground—not yet. It is raw stone, dark and glassy in places, but here it buckles and blisters, still forming. The rock is *emerging*, extruding itself from some deeper unseen process, sheets of it lifting in millimeter-thick curls like cooling slag, still warm to the touch, still carrying the deep heat of its creation. In other places the stone looks almost fetal, smooth and unbroken, veined with something that might be metal or might be crystal but catches the dim light in threads too fine for the eye to resolve, a circuitry of mineral being laid down by forces not yet catalogued.

Water is everywhere, but not as streams or pools. It condenses on every surface, a constant weeping from the warm stone as the high-altitude air cools against it. Droplets bead and gather and run in thin films down the freshly-formed rock faces, carrying dissolved minerals into hairline cracks. The sound is a chorus of drips, but multiplied into a hissing static, a wet breath—*loud* in this enclosed space, a constant shushing that fills the ears and makes silence feel like pressure. The air smells of ozone and wet basalt, with an undertone of something organic beginning to rot, faintly sweet.

The stone forms a rough amphitheater, a natural bowl tilted slightly, its high rim catching the clouds. Those clouds drift through constantly, condensing, touching everything with mist that never quite clears. Light filters in from above, diffused and gray, a permanent gloaming. Within this dimness, things move.

The fauna are abundant but wrong. Many of them are tiny, glinting specks that swarm the wet rock faces—arthropod-like things with too many legs, their bodies faceted like the raw crystal veins they seem to feed upon. They rasp at the stone with mouthparts that emit brief, pinpoint flashes of light, bioluminescent or perhaps something else, a cold plasma discharge. Larger forms move through the mist: things that are not quite birds, with wings of stretched membrane that make a dry, rattling sound as they loop through the bowl, snatching at the swarming specks. Their cries are high, piercing, metallic, cutting through the constant wet hiss. There are other things too, hard to see clearly. A long, low shape slides over a blistering rock sheet, its underside leaving a trail that steams. Its skin seems to shift and pixelate, blending not with the background but with some other dimension, showing glimpses of starlight or substrate code when it moves quickly.

Scattered across this embryonic landscape are structures, or the remnants of them. In the center of the bowl, a single slim obelisk rises about four meters from the stone. It is perfectly black, non-reflective, seemingly machined from a material that the eye refuses to focus on. It feels cold, colder than the warm rock around it, and from its apex, a single, continuous bead of water rolls down its face every few seconds. The rhythm of it is metronomic. Near the bowl's rim, half-consumed by the slowly extruding rock, are what look like the foundation rings of other structures—circles of a brassy alloy that has tarnished to a deep purple patina. One ring still holds a faint, flickering glow within its circumference, a ghost of a field that once was. And scattered everywhere, easy to miss among the wet rock and crawling things, are tiny spheres, each the size of a fingertip, made of something that seems to be both glass and smoke. They lie inert, thousands of them, some half-embedded in the new stone, as if they rained down long ago and the world is simply growing up around them.

This place is under construction. The rock is building itself, the water is sculpting it, and the life—if it is life—is in a frenzied, early-stage dance of consumption and flight. It is an ecosystem in fast-forward, predator-prey relationships playing out in hours instead of millennia. A crawling speck is caught by a membrane wing; a condensing droplet alters the path of a crawling trail; the obelisk drips its steady beat, and the rock continues its slow, warm rise, swallowing the past, making new surfaces for the condensation and the swarm. The space feels simultaneously like a cathedral, a laboratory, and a nursery—a small, sealed crucible at the top of the world, where the rules are still being written.
