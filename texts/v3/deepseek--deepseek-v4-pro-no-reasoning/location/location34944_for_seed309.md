The city is woven, not built, and it breathes.

You descend into it down a throat of knotted roots—stairs, if you’re generous, though each step gives slightly underfoot, fibrous and warm as living tendon. The light, what little there is, comes in sickly frequencies: bioluminescent nodules spaced far apart along the root-walls, pulsing a slow amber-green like guttering lamps with a vegetal heartbeat. The air is wet silk on your skin. Not mist, exactly—more like the air itself has learned to hold moisture as a permanent condition, a humidity so intimate it feels like being breathed on by something vast and slow.

This is a place below, but not buried. The ceiling is a tangled mat of root architecture thirty meters overhead, a false sky of interwoven boughs that have grown into arches, buttresses, cantilevers. Everything structural was once alive and, in a sense, still is. The buildings—if you can call them that—are hollowed-out gall-forms, great woody bladders the size of houses that the trees were coaxed into growing generations ago. They cluster along the main thoroughfares in lumpy, organic rows, their walls studded with breathing pores that open and close with the circadian rhythms of whatever immense organism constitutes the substrate of this place. Some still leak sap, slow and golden, and the inhabitants collect it in copper gutters that have been hammered and re-hammered over centuries to fit the ever-shifting contours.

You are standing in what was once a town square, now a cathedral of industry. The space is only “sparse” in the sense that there is room to move between the presences, and the presences are not solid walls but things in their own right. Three enormous distillation engines dominate the central grotto, iron-black and greasy, all pistons and copper coils and glass retorts big as wine barrels. They are processing something—pulped fiber, bright green slurry, the body-fat of the megaflora—and turning it into fuel, into light, into the thin bitter oil that lubricates the city’s other machines. Steam coughs from their exhaust vents in arrhythmic bursts, condensing immediately on the root-ceiling and raining back down in a constant tepid drizzle. *That’s* the intermittent water. It rains every seventeen minutes, give or take, a cycle driven by the cooling needs of the Number Two engine, and the gutters gurgle, and the porous floor drinks, and everything settles back into its baseline wetness.

The sound is a physical pressure. Not one noise but a chord: the basso profundo thrum of the engines, a subsonic vibration that lives in your sternum; the high-register chittering, screeching, warbling of the fauna; the wet percussive slap of something moving through the runoff channels. The fauna is overwhelming because it is *the* fauna. There is no underbrush, no hidden life—the animals are the furniture, the traffic, the walls. Beetles the size of dogs trundle along worn paths in the root-floor, their carapaces stenciled with ownership marks in white paint, some hauling carts behind them on chitinous traces. Slender, wet-skinned creatures—something between a salamander and a spider monkey—swing from the hanging fibers, their four-fingered hands barely touching the strands, moving with the thoughtless precision of something native to three dimensions. They are loud. They call to each other in perfect, echoing four-note phrases that sound like someone tuning a glass instrument, and their calls bounce off the warm living walls until the air is a soup of overlapping signals.

And the moths. The moths are the reason the light is dim and strange. They swarm the bioluminescent nodules in clouds of pale, powdery wing, feeding on the light itself. Each one is the size of your spread hand, and their wings are the color of old parchment, and as they feed they excrete a fine phosphorescent dust that sifts down in lazy spirals, coating every surface in a faint, sourceless glow. The dust tastes metallic on the tongue. The inhabitants sweep it from their doorsteps, collect it in jars, burn it in lamps that cast a steadier, cooler light. You can see the lamplighters moving through the lower levels even now, small figures in waxed-canvas coats, carrying long poles tipped with sucking funnels, harvesting the wet particulate air.

The architecture is layered not in the sense of stories but of palimpsest. The settlement was here long before the engines, and the engines were here before the current configuration of streets, and the streets are not planned but negotiated—widened by the passage of countless beetle-carts, narrowed by the slow inward lean of gall-buildings that never stop growing. Everything is established, weathered, in a state of dynamic equilibrium between the forward creep of vegetation and the relentless maintenance of industry. You can see where old pipes have been encased in new wood, where a valve has been swallowed halfway by a swelling root, where someone has carved a window into a wall that used to be solid and now needs a structural prop because the breathing pores migrated. The weirdness is in the familiarity of it—these are people making do, making homes, making a city in impossible circumstances, and they have been doing it long enough that the impossible has become mundane.

The dynamic processes are most visible at the edges. A predation event unfolds in the corner of the square as you watch: one of the glass-voiced climbers has caught a giant moth, pinned it to a root-strut with three of its hands, and is delicately peeling the wings away from the body while the moth’s glow fades from parchment to gray. A beetle pauses to watch. The climber chitters at it, the beetle moves on, the rain begins its seventeenth-minute fall. The gutters run. The Number Two engine exhales a great white billow of steam that rolls along the ceiling like a low cloud and is absorbed, slowly, by the breathing pores. The city incorporates it. The city incorporates everything, given time.

There are no straight lines here except the ones people have imposed—copper pipes, steel ladders, the rigid geometry of the engine frames against the soft curve of the gall-walls. The tension between them is the whole story of the place. It is a room, a grove, a single coherent hollow in the greater body of the underground forest, and it feels bounded not by walls but by the limits of the lamplight, the reach of the noise, the edge of habitation where the tame beetles stop and the wild ones begin. Somewhere out there, beyond the glimmer of the phosphor-dust, the roots go on forever. In here, there is work to do. The engines need stoking. The climbers need shooing from the valve intakes. The rain is falling, falling, falling.
