Let me begin where the sound begins — though “begin” is the wrong word here, because this sound has no beginning. It has always been. It will always be. That is the first rule of this place.

---

The city has no name, or if it does, no one speaks it. You don’t speak much here. You couldn’t, even if you wanted to. The noise forbids it.

Imagine a basin, broad and shallow, cupped between low hills that are themselves too new to have settled into their final shapes. The land is raw geology, still arriving. In places the ground is smooth and dark as just-cooled basalt, though it’s warm underfoot — not painfully so, but alive with a deep, steady heat that rises through your soles and keeps the air thick. In other places the rock hasn’t finished deciding what it will be: ridges half-pushed from the earth, blisters of stone that look like bubbles frozen mid-pop, seams of something softer wearing away almost as you watch, revealing harder veins beneath. The whole basin is emerging, embryonic, a landscape in the first stages of formation, and yet — paradoxically — it never changes. Whatever process births this place has been arrested at the threshold of becoming. The half-formed ridges never rise further. The soft seams never fully erode. The ground stays exactly as warm as it was when you arrived, and will be exactly as warm when you leave, if leaving is possible.

Water is everywhere, but it’s not the water you know. It doesn’t pool in lakes or run in streams. It seeps. The walls of the basin weep continuously, every rock face glistening with a perpetual condensation that never drips quite fast enough to count as rain but never dries either. The air itself is saturated; you breathe it as much as inhale it. The ground underfoot is never truly dry — a film of moisture coats everything, slick on the stone, beading on every surface. Walk here and your footprints fill immediately, not with water rushing in, but with water simply *being* there, as if the world rejects the idea of absence. It’s a marsh without plants, a post-rain landscape where the rain never fell and never stops falling and yet you never see a drop descend.

There are no plants. Not a blade of grass, not a lichen, not a single green thing. The mineral world reigns absolute. And yet the city is not empty.

The inhabitants are numerous beyond counting. They press close, layer upon layer, in densities that would feel overcrowded anywhere else. They are everywhere — on every ledge, in every shallow cave, clustered along the natural terraces that ring the basin walls, crowded into the open floor of the valley. They are small, these people, or perhaps the scale is simply hard to judge. They build their dwellings from the only material available: stone, and the warm mud that wells up from the ground in certain places. Their architecture is primitive — hand-shaped bricks dried not by sun (there is no direct sun here) but by the ambient heat of the earth, stacked into low domes and clustered cells, mortared with the ever-present moisture thickened with mineral dust. The buildings overlap, one structure growing from the wall of another, passages burrowing through shared walls, rooftops serving as floors for the next tier. There are no straight lines. Everything curves in obedience to the contours of the emerging rock.

And they work. Constantly, ceaselessly, they work. This is the source of the sound.

Every inhabitant is engaged in the same task: they shape the stone. With simple tools — hand-hammers of harder rock, chisels of what might be obsidian but isn’t quite, grinding stones, polishing cloths woven from something that isn’t plant fiber — they work the surfaces of the city. They chip. They grind. They smooth. They carve shallow channels in the rock, most of which go nowhere, geometric patterns that spiral and intersect, grooves that catch the perpetual condensation and hold it, briefly, before the water overflows and continues its slow seep downslope. They don’t speak. They don’t look up. Each one tends a patch of wall or floor or ceiling, and the sound of their labor is a roar.

It’s not a roar of chaos. It’s rhythmic, synchronized, though you can’t identify the pulse they’re all following. Perhaps they can’t either. It’s simply that their movements have found an equilibrium with each other over whatever span of time this place has existed. The chipping of a thousand hammers forms a high, continuous crackle. The grinding of a thousand stones against stone is a bass note, a deep rumble that you feel in your chest. The splash and slosh of water being redirected through carved channels is a liquid percussion. Together, it’s deafening — not painfully loud in the way of a sudden explosion, but loud in the way of a waterfall or a storm sea, a sound so complete that silence becomes unimaginable. You stop hearing it as individual noises. You hear it as one thing: the breathing of the city.

The light, what there is of it, doesn’t come from any sky. The sky here is a low ceiling of vapor, an inversion layer that traps the warmth and the moisture and the sound. The light is ambient, sourceless, a dim amber-gray that filters through the mist from all directions equally. It casts no shadows. It doesn’t change with any cycle of day or night. It is always exactly this bright, which is to say not very. You can see twenty feet clearly, fifty feet tolerably, and beyond that the city dissolves into a haze of suggestion — more domes, more bodies, more hammering, more wet stone, all fading into a golden-gray murk.

The fauna — and there is fauna, abundant — is not what you’d expect in a place without plants. They are small, pale, numerous. Scuttling things that live in the gaps between the bricks, on the ceilings of the domes, in the hair of the workers. They have too many legs and they move in starts and stops, and they seem to feed on the condensation itself, or perhaps on the mineral trace it carries, or perhaps on nothing at all. They are silent, or as good as silent, their tiny sounds swallowed completely by the hammering. They don’t interact with the inhabitants, and the inhabitants don’t acknowledge them. They are simply part of the density.

The city extends. How far, you can’t say. The basin is large enough to be a region, town-or-valley-size, multi-featured, and every part of it that you can see is inhabited, built, layered, crowded. The open parameter isn’t about emptiness — it’s about extent. This isn’t a single cavern or a single street. It’s a landscape of neighborhoods connected by winding passages, of lower districts where the water pools deeper and the workers wade ankle-deep as they carve, of higher slopes where the condensation runs in rivulets down the rock faces and the domes are stacked six or seven high. Scope is visible. Distance is implied. The city fades into the mist not because it ends, but because the mist takes it.

And yet, for all its density, its urban complexity, its thousands of inhabitants working in unison — the technology is nothing. Hand tools. Bare hands, often. The simplest possible interventions: hitting rock with rock, shaping with abrasion, lifting water with cupped palms. The city is vast and intricate and has been built entirely by this, by the accumulation of small actions over time. Perhaps that explains the never-changing quality. The rate of work is exactly matched to the rate of the landscape’s own emergence. They are not transforming a finished world; they are keeping pace with one that is trying to be born.

The rules:

1. Nothing grows, but nothing decays.
2. The water never rises and never falls.
3. The warmth never fluctuates.
4. The sound never stops.
5. No one ages. No one is born. No one dies. The population is constant, fixed, always exactly enough to tend every surface.
6. The work is never complete, but it’s never meaningless either. It maintains something. It stabilizes the emergence. Without the chiseling, the grinding, the channeling, the basin would — what? Finish becoming? Collapse into chaos? No one remembers. No one asks.

7. There is no speech because there is nothing to say. The work is the only communication needed. The sound of your hammer is your voice. The rhythm of your grinding is your name. When you die — though you don’t — someone else would take up your tools and continue your exact pattern, and the sound would never register the loss.

The city is a choir made of stone and water and labor.

---

Stand here long enough and you’ll notice something else. The sound isn’t quite uniform. There are subtle variations, localized refrains. In the lower districts, where the water is deeper, the workers have developed a technique of percussive stone-striking that transmits through the flooded floor — you can feel it through your feet before you hear it, a double tap, a pause, a triple tap. Call and response, district to district, passed along the water. In the upper terraces, the carvers work more slowly, more precisely, and their sound is a continuous hiss of abrasion, polishing surfaces to a mirror sheen that catches the dim light and throws it back in fractured glints. And in the deepest part of the basin, where a natural amphitheater of rock cradles the densest cluster of dwellings, there’s a constant counterpoint rhythm — something that might once have been a song, before words were abandoned, now reduced to percussion and the splash of redirected water falling in controlled cascades from one carved basin to another.

---

This is not a place you visit. This is a place you find yourself in, and you realize, with a slow-dawning clarity, that the hammer in your hand was always there, and the patch of wall before you has been waiting for your attention, and the rhythm you’re meant to keep has been beating in your blood since before you had blood to beat in. The city doesn’t ask. It simply includes you.

The stone under your palm is warm and wet. It’s slightly softer than you expect. It wants to be shaped. You lift the hammer. You begin.

The sound never changes.
