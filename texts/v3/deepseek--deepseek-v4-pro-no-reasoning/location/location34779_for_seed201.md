The ceiling is stone, and it drips. That is the first thing the body knows: a cold, irregular percussion, each drop a tiny shock of wetness against skin or lichen or the glass-smooth rock underfoot. The air is a held breath at the edge of freezing, just warm enough that the water does not turn to ice, just cold enough that every inhalation tastes thin and sharp, like a blade laid against the tongue.

This is a crevasse-garden, a rent in the high backbone of a young mountain range. The peaks outside are still growing, shouldering upward in tectonic fits that crack the crust like a loaf of bread too hastily baked. Here, at extreme elevation, the sky is a searing blue-white dome, thin as paper and twice as bright, but you would not know it from this hollow. The crevasse opens downward, a narrow throat in the world’s roof, and only a sliver of that fierce light falls in, reflected and refracted down the wet walls until it becomes a diffuse, silvery glow that picks out every edge. The shadows are sharp, black as ink, because there is nothing soft here to blunt them. The features are young, ungullied by time, the fracture-faces of the stone still bearing the conchoidal marks of their violent separation.

It is small, this place. A single chamber really, no wider than a village chapel, its walls rising thirty feet to the surface crack that is its only skylight. The boundaries are absolute: wet stone on three sides, a tumble of broken rock on the fourth where the crevice pinches shut. There is no horizon, only the vertical, the sense of being held in a stone palm.

And yet, it is lush. The moisture seeping from the walls—condensation gathered from the snowmelt that works its way down through the mountain’s aching joints—feeds a pelt of vegetation so dense it hides the rock. Mosses, mostly, in a colour palette that seems improvised for another planet. There are deep, oxblood reds, sulphur yellows, and a particular variety that is an almost fluorescent emerald, each tiny leaflet a perfect, crystalline rosette. They grow in thick, spongy pads, cushioning the sharp edges, and from their mats sprout taller things: strange, fleshy stalks with no leaves, only a single translucent, berry-like globe at the tip, glowing faintly with the light they gather. There are ferns too, uncurling their fiddleheads with the slow, visible energy of a place where the growing season is a feverish sprint, their fronds lacquered with a glycerine dampness.

And the garden is alive with motion. This is the paradox: an empty density and an abundant fauna. The space itself is a near-void, a hollow of air and light and dripping water, with vast, cathedral emptinesses between the living things. But what lives here lives in numbers. The moss-pads are highways for beetles the size of apple seeds, their carapaces an iridescent oil-sheen black. They move in silent, purposeful lines, tending to herds of aphid-like creatures that tap the fleshy stalks for their clear, cold sap. There is predation: a centipede, bone-white and many-jointed, that flows like liquid muscle through the mossy understory, a living rope of patient hunger. A tiny, wren-like bird, its feathers the grey of the wet stone, flits from a nest of frozen spider-silk wedged into a high crack, its chirp the only sound that punctuates the steady drip, a sound so sharp and brief it feels like a small, bright needle of noise in the great, absorbent quiet.

The quiet itself is a substance. The sound is almost nothing, a 0.4 that feels weighted, pressing on the eardrums. The drip of water is not a rhythm but an ongoing event, a continuous, almost sub-audible *isness*. The beetle-legs on the moss make no sound a human could hear. The centipede is a ghost. Only the bird, occasionally, and even its call seems to be swallowed immediately by the dense, cold air, leaving no echo. It is the quiet of deep snow, of altitude, of a place so small and bounded that sound waves die before they can travel.

Then you see the marks on the wall. Scattered, as if left by someone in a hurry or with only a partial idea. Near the tumbled rock, on a patch of relatively dry stone where the moss has been scraped away, a handprint. Not painted, but rendered in negative: the rock is dark, and around a splayed, five-fingered shape, the surface has been dusted with a pale, chalky powder that clings to the micro-textures, leaving the hand-shape in silhouette. Next to it, a spiral has been pecked into the stone with a pointed tool, a primitive but precise Fibonacci coil that seems to pulse in the half-light. A scatter of bone fragments—ribs, a section of vertebra too large for the birds—lies arranged in a line that points directly at the deepest patch of glowing, emerald moss. These are not ruins; they are signs of a visit, or a visitation. The tech is of the hand and the eye, the manual gesture, the simple observation of pattern. Whoever left these traces understood the converging lines of the place: the light, the water, the concentrated life in the hollow.

The dynamic heart of the crevasse-garden is the water cycle, a miniature weather system. The cold stone exhales moisture. The bright, high-altitude sun, even in its brief daily arc across the crack overhead, warms the moss just enough for transpiration. The water rises as an invisible mist, collects on the undersides of rock flakes, and falls again as cold, heavy drops. This constant, gentle churning is what drives the abundance, an ecosystem in a state of kinetic balance, a predator-prey dance of beetle and centipede, of wren and aphid, all paced by the silent, silver pulse of the water. The place is a living distillation, a pocket of wet, vivid, ferocious life carved into the empty, frozen body of a mountain that is still, even now, restless and growing toward a bright and airless sky.
