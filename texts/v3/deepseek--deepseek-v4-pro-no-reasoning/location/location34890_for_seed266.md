The city does not sit on the ground so much as it has become the ground, and the ground is salt. Not the white crystalline salt of an evaporated sea, but something older and more mineral—a fused, glassy substrate the colour of bone and lead, shot through with veins of darker impurity that spiral down into the plateau's depths. This is the Salt-Mass, a formation so vast it generates its own slow weather: a thermal breathing that rises and falls across days rather than hours, the entire landscape inhaling warmth and exhaling it again through a billion capillary fissures.

You are standing at the edge of the High District, where the elevation becomes a concept as much as a measurement. The plateau falls away in terraces—not natural ones, but the accumulated edges of older construction, layer upon layer of industrial stone and sintered mineral, each era's building becoming the next era's foundation. Looking down is like looking into a sedimentary cross-section of intent. The drop is not sheer but stepped, and so vast that the lower districts blur into a haze of their own making, a shimmer of heat and fine particulate that never quite settles. The city extends to the horizon in every direction, but "horizon" is the wrong word, because the Salt-Mass curves with the body of a continent, and what you see at the limits is not an edge but a fading—a desaturation into the warm, dry sky.

There is no water here. Not hidden, not remembered. The chemistry of this place has no room for it. What flows instead is a kind of slow mineral exudate, a viscous amber that weeps from certain seams in the Salt-Mass when the temperature gradient is just right. The locals call it *flux-gum*, and it is collected, refined, and burned in the engines that power the city's deeper machinery. You can smell it now, faintly, like hot stone and something almost organic—an aldehydic sharpness that catches at the back of the throat. The engines are mostly silent from this height. What you hear instead is the city's breathing: a low, infrasonic pulse that travels through your bones more than your ears, the accumulated vibration of pumps and pistons and the slow contraction of cooling salt-masonry. Above that, a fine, constant sift of wind across the plateau rim, carrying a faint granular hiss—particles of salt-glass abrading against themselves in the air.

The vegetation is not vegetation. There are no plants. In the spaces between structures, in the wide plazas and the abandoned terrace-levels, something else has taken that ecological niche. They are called *cyst-columns*: upright, hollow chimneys of precipitated mineral, ranging from the height of a hand to the height of a building, their surfaces covered in a fine dendritic furring that is not lichen but a crystalline growth pattern—silicate lattices that capture trace gases from the flux-engines' exhaust and convert them, through a slow non-biological metabolism, into more of themselves. They grow in gradients, thicker near the vents, sparse toward the open salt-fields. From a distance they read as a kind of forest, but a forest stripped of softness, a forest that is all bone and no leaf. The largest ones hum faintly when the wind passes across their open tops, a chorus of frequencies so low and so slow that you might mistake it for the sound of your own blood.

And yet the city is alive. The fauna parameter was not wrong.

Between the cyst-columns, in the layered canyons of the middle terraces, life moves. It is strange life—arthropod-bauplans mostly, but scaled to sizes that don't fit Earth logics. *Salt-skimmers* coast on thermals between the district levels, their bodies flat and wide as kites, wing-membranes of translucent chitin catching the warm upwelling air. They drift for hours without flapping, and their shadows cross the salt-glass like the shadows of clouds that don't exist. Lower down, in the perpetual twilight of the deep terrace streets, *gland-mules* haul loads of flux-gum in rough trains of four or five, their bodies segmented and armoured, their heads blunt and eyeless and perpetually working at the traces of mineral they find in the substrate. They have no mouths—they absorb through ventral grooves that rasp against the salt—and so they make no vocal sounds, only the click and skitter of their leg-rows on the glassy pavement. They are abundant enough that you can see their movement from up here if you watch: slow streams of grey and ochre following the ramps and switchbacks of the city's lower levels, patient as geology.

There are birds, or things that occupy the bird-shape. They are called *needle-flights*, and they are built wrong. Too many wings—four, sometimes six, arranged in staggered pairs along a tubular body that tapers at both ends. They roost on the high ledges of the industrial cathedral-buildings, the great factories and exchange-halls that rise from the plateau like cooled extrusions of the Salt-Mass itself: windowless, sheer-walled, ornamented with the abstract geometries of exhaust ports and heat-exchanger fins. The needle-flights gather there in the hundreds, silent, shifting their multiple wings in slow synchrony as they thermoregulate. When they take flight, which is rare, they do not flap but ripple, the wings moving in sequential waves from front to back, and their passage makes a sound like paper rubbed against paper, dry and frictioned and strangely intimate.

The city's architecture is industrial, but it is an industrialism that has had time to become something else—not decayed, but *settled*. It has been here long enough that function and form have diverged and re-converged. The factories are cathedrals because the people who built them centuries ago began to treat them as sacred, and the people who maintain them now were born into that understanding. You can see it in the detailing: the way the great gear-housings are carved with spiral motifs that serve no mechanical purpose, the way the exhaust stacks taper into forms that echo the cyst-columns in the plazas below. The tech has not regressed; if anything, it has deepened, become more itself. The engines that drive the flux-pumps are beautiful machines, their casings of polished salt-glass inlaid with temperature-reactive alloys that shift colour as the day's heat moves through them. Boilers the size of houses stand in open squares, their surfaces tracing slow chromatic patterns—blue-black in the cooler dawn, coppery now in the midday warmth, and by evening they will glow a deep, bruised purple that the locals call *engine-dusk*.

The streets between these structures are mostly empty. The density reading was low for a reason: the city was built for more people than it now holds. There was a contraction, sometime in the deep past—not a catastrophe, but a slow ebbing, a demographic tide going out and not coming back in. Whole terrace-levels stand vacant, their cyst-columns grown tall and uninterrupted, their pavements cracking in geometric patterns that the gland-mule caravans navigate around with ancient instinct. The people who remain cluster in the mid-levels and the industrial cores, and their presence is felt more than seen from up here: lights in windows, distant calls, the occasional clang of a shift-change bell echoing up through the thermal layers. They are sparse, but not lonely. The city does not feel abandoned. It feels *accommodated*—adjusted to its own reduced presence, like a garment worn soft by long use.

The sky is vast and empty of cloud. The light is normal, unremarkable, a steady amber-white that falls without drama across the salt-plateau. But there is a quality to it—a thickness, a particulate haze that scatters the distant districts into pale silhouettes. The sun, or whatever this world's primary is, hangs high and small and very white, and its heat is a constant pressure on the skin, not uncomfortable but insistent, the way a held breath is insistent. There is no shade except what the buildings cast, and those shadows are sharp-edged and cool and smell faintly of flux-gum and old mineral.

Out beyond the city's furthest terraces, where construction gives way to raw Salt-Mass, the land continues in a kind of formal emptiness. The glassy surface is wind-sculpted into dune-forms, stationary waves of salt that catch the light in long, low-angled gleams. Nothing grows there. Nothing moves, or if it does, it moves so slowly that motion and stillness become the same category. The Salt-Mass extends to the geological horizon, and beyond it, and beyond that, and somewhere out there other cities exist—or existed—but from here, at the edge of the High District, the world is one city and one plateau and one warm, dry wind that has been blowing, more or less unchanged, for longer than the city has had a name.

The sound is mostly quiet. The wind. The subsonic engine-pulse. The faint, high-frequency ticking of cooling surfaces. And under it all, so deep it might be imagination, the slow crystalline growth of the cyst-columns, a sound indistinguishable from silence.
