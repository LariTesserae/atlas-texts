The first thing you notice is the quiet.

It isn't the absence of sound—not exactly. It's that the sounds which exist have been worn down, smoothed by time into something you feel in your sternum more than you hear. A hum so deep and constant that after a while, your own heartbeat starts to feel like an intrusion, a skittish little rhythm laid over a drone that hasn't changed in ten thousand years.

You are standing on a high plateau, though "plateau" implies a flatness that isn't quite true. The ground rises and falls in long, exhausted swells, like the back of a sleeping creature too vast to name. The rock underfoot is dark, nearly black, and polished to a dull gleam—not by water, not recently, but by something else. Wind, maybe, though there's no wind now. Just that hum.

The scale of the place unmakes your sense of distance. There are no trees, no structures, nothing your eye can use as a yardstick. Only the land itself, stretching away in every direction until it blurs into a horizon that seems slightly too far, slightly too curved, as if you're standing on something larger than a planet should be. The air is thin and cold—not brutally so, but persistently, a cold that doesn't bite so much as seep. It finds the gaps in your clothing with patience. It has been cold here for a very long time, and it is in no hurry.

Above you, the sky is a deep charcoal, dim but not dark, like the light just before a storm that never breaks. The cloud cover is permanent and featureless, a lid of vapour so high and uniform it might as well be another stratum of atmosphere. It diffuses what little light there is into a directionless grey that casts no shadows. You can't tell where the sun is. You're not sure there is one.

And everywhere—*everywhere*—the things are moving.

At first you mistake them for boulders. They're the right colour, the same dark iron-grey as the plateau, and they're scattered across the landscape with the casual irregularity of glacial erratics. But they're moving. Slowly. So slowly that you have to hold still for a long moment to be sure, picking one and watching until your eyes ache. Yes. It's turning. Rotating on some hidden axis at a speed just barely distinguishable from stillness. Another, further off, is rising—no, *inflating*, its surface expanding minutely as you watch, like a lung taking a breath that lasts an hour.

They are everywhere. Not clustered, not in herds, but spaced out with a kind of rigorous indifference, each occupying its own wide territory. Some are the size of houses. Others, further away and harder to resolve, might be the size of mountains. The nearest one is perhaps fifty metres from you, and now that you're paying attention, you can see the surface of it clearly: not rock at all, but something organic. A ridged, leathery hide, plated and seamed, glossed with a faint iridescence that shifts in the low light. Condensation beads along its highest ridges, droplets of water that tremble and grow fat and eventually slide down its flanks, disappearing into deep folds. That intermittent moisture is the only water you can see in this place. The creatures—if that's what they are—sweat it out slowly, or perhaps they harvest it from the air itself, drawing vapour through their skins in some patient, chemical process you can't begin to guess at.

You take a step, and the sound of your foot on the rock is obscenely loud—a sharp little clack that seems to travel outward in concentric rings of wrongness. The nearest creature stops turning. It doesn't flinch or startle; it simply *ceases* its motion, with the absoluteness of a machine being powered down. You feel yourself being assessed, though there are no eyes that you can see, no sensory organs at all on that plated surface. Just a quality of attention, heavy and slow and utterly indifferent.

It waits.

You wait too, because there's nothing else to do. After a long time—five minutes, ten, you lose track—the hum shifts slightly in pitch, a change so subtle you're not sure it happened. The creature resumes its rotation, turning its attention away from you with the glacial finality of a continent deciding you're not worth the trouble.

And that's when you understand the erosion score, the 2.5, the *established* nature of this place. This landscape hasn't been shaped by water or wind or ice. It's been shaped by *them*. Over millennia, over geological time, these slow-moving leviathans have worn the rock down with their bodies, their immense patient weight grinding the high places into swales and basins. The polished stone under your feet isn't polished by weather. It's polished by the passage of hides across it, century after century, a sanding so gradual that no single pass removes more than a molecule or two. The hum you've been hearing is the sound of that friction, scaled up to the size of a world.

There is vegetation here, but you have to look for it. In the cracks between the polished swells of rock, in the places the creatures don't traverse, something like lichen grows. It's pale grey, almost translucent, and it forms thin, lacy crusts that crumble to dust if you touch them. It's not photosynthetic in any way you understand—there's not enough light for that, not nearly. It must feed on something else. Minerals, maybe, or the faint chemical exhalations of the creatures themselves. It grows slowly, dies slowly, and its remains form a fine powder that the next rain—whenever that comes—washes into the low places, where it accumulates in shallow, alkaline pools. You can see one of those pools now, a few hundred metres away, its surface perfectly still and reflecting the grey sky with the mercury precision of a mirror. Nothing drinks from it. Nothing lives in it. It's just there, part of a cycle too slow to watch.

The cold intensifies as you stand still. It's the kind of cold that makes you aware of your own heat as a temporary, borrowed thing. You stamp your feet, and the sound is swallowed immediately, absorbed by the vastness and the hum and the patient, waiting presence of the creatures.

In the distance—a distance you can't measure—something lifts. One of the largest forms, a mountain-shape on the horizon that until now you'd assumed was topography, is rising. It rises so slowly that you're not sure it's happening, but the gap beneath it is growing, a sliver of darker dark between its belly and the ground. As it lifts, the hum deepens, takes on a grinding edge, a subsonic rumble you feel in your teeth. The air pressure changes, a subtle drop that makes your ears pop. The creature—and it must be a creature, it must be, because what else could it be—is venting something from its underside. Heat, maybe, or gas. A shimmer rises around it, distorting the air, and for the first time you smell something: a faint, acrid tang, metallic, like ozone and old iron.

The mountain-creature hangs there, suspended a few metres above the ground, and you realize it's not just one creature. There are others around it, smaller ones, that have been hidden in its shadow, and they're stirring now, responding to whatever signal the lifting represents. Their movements are faster—by the standards of this place, which means you can actually see them moving if you watch closely, a slow shifting of weight, a realignment of bodies. They're orienting toward the lifted one. Toward something happening there.

You could walk toward it. It would take you hours, maybe days—distance is strange here, and the creatures are larger than they seem—but you could. The thought crosses your mind and then passes. Something about the deliberation of their movements, the sense of processes unfolding on timescales that make human urgency look like a fever dream, makes the idea feel not dangerous exactly, but absurd. Like a mayfly trying to join a conversation between mountains.

A drop of water lands on your cheek. Cold. You look up, and the featureless grey lid of the sky has developed texture—streaks and whorls of darker grey, and as you watch, more drops begin to fall. Not rain, exactly. A heavy mist condensing out of the clouds, falling slowly, almost reluctantly, as if the sky itself is unsure whether to commit to precipitation. It beads on the polished rock, collects in the shallow depressions worn by millennia of slow passage. It darkens the hide of the nearest creature to a deep, wet black, and the iridescence on its flanks intensifies, shimmering with colours that don't quite exist in the spectrum you know.

The hum is changing again. The rain—the mist—is interacting with something on the creatures' surfaces, some chemical or process that alters the conductivity of the air. You can feel it as a tingle on your skin, a faint vibration in the fine hairs of your arms. The sound is becoming more complex, layering overtones onto that deep basal drone, and for a moment you can almost hear a pattern in it. Almost. It slips away before you can grasp it, dissolving back into noise.

You stand in the rain-mist, on the polished stone, surrounded by the slow, breathing shapes of things that have been here since before this world cooled, and you listen. The quiet is absolute. The quiet is the loudest thing you've ever heard.

The mountain on the horizon settles back down with a vibration that travels through the rock and up through the soles of your feet, a seismic sigh. The smaller forms around it gradually still. The rain continues, patient as everything else here, and the cold seeps deeper, and the hum rolls on, and on, and on, a sound that was old when the first amino acids formed on some younger, warmer world and will be old still when the last star gutters out.

You are very small. The landscape knows it. The creatures, if they notice you at all, have already forgotten you. You are a flicker of heat and noise in a place where time is measured in the wearing-down of mountains, and you will be gone long before any of these patient bodies complete their next rotation.

The rain beads on your skin. The hum continues. Somewhere, impossibly far away, another mountain is beginning to lift.
