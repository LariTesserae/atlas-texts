The tracks run on a causeway of packed black cinders, barely a foot above the water. On either side, the marsh stretches away into a dim, warm haze, its surface broken by scattered clumps of vegetation and the low shapes of industrial ruins. It’s not a flat marsh—the ground here has subsided unevenly, old foundations and forgotten street grids creating a complex microtopography of submerged rectangles, linear channels, circular pits. The water is everywhere, but it’s shallow: ankle-deep on the paths, knee-deep in the pools, deeper only in the straight-cut drainage canals that slice through the whole district.

Step onto one of the raised paths and the cinders crunch underfoot, wet-black and glittering faintly. The material is slag, mostly, crushed to gravel-size and laid down by the rail company decades ago. In places, the slag has fused into a glassy crust where old heat or chemical reactions ran through it. Your boot soles find uncertain purchase on the slick, bubbled surface.

The heat comes from below. There are vents everywhere—cracks in the cinder paths, the mouths of broken pipes protruding from the water, the gaps in a collapsed brick culvert that runs along the old main street. From these openings, steam rises continuously, some of it a clean white mist, some of it tinged faintly yellow or green where it carries a whiff of sulphur, of chlorine, of something metallic and sharp. The steam never stops. It is the engine of this place’s weather, condensing on every surface, dripping from every overhang, keeping the moss lush and the air thick enough to feel on your skin.

Vegetation has taken the heat and the water and run with it. The dominant plant is a pale, fleshy reed that grows in dense but scattered clumps, each cluster perhaps three or four feet across, separated by open water or stretches of bare cinders. The reeds are almost white at the base, shading to a sickly yellow-green near the tips. They whisper against each other when the steam shifts, a sound you first mistake for water running somewhere, then for distant voices, then settle on as just the reeds. In the pools where the water is deeper and the heat less intense, there are other plants: broad, dark-leaved things that float on the surface, their roots dangling down into the murk, and a kind of wiry, reddish vine that crawls across the slag paths and sends runners out into the water, bridging gaps and binding loose stones.

Buildings remain, but scattered, sparse despite the area’s history as a dense industrial ward. The steam vents and subsidence have made most of the original structures uninhabitable, and many have collapsed into their own basements, leaving only a line of bricks like a high-water mark around a rectangular pool. What still stands is mostly low, one- or two-storey, built of the same dark brick that all the factories here used. One is a pump house, identifiable by the massive, rust-sheathed pipe that emerges from one wall, angles down into a sump pit, and from there into the earth. The pump has not run in living memory, but the sump pit still bubbles, feeding a constant trickle of warm, mineral-scented water into the nearest drainage channel.

Next to the pump house is a row of workers’ cottages, three of them, their roofs gone, their windows empty. Inside, the floor joists have rotted and fallen in, but across the exposed interior walls, a thick, woolly moss grows in dense mats, glowing faintly in the dim light. Not bioluminescent exactly—it’s more that the moss has taken up something from the brick, some phosphorescent salts, and now it holds a low, grey-green illumination like the last light before full dark. You could read large print by it, if you held the page close.

Beyond the cottages, the old main street runs east-west, an avenue of cracked asphalt and uneven cobbles exposed where the asphalt has worn away. Along the street, steel lampposts still stand at irregular intervals, their globes dark and often shattered. A few of the posts have been colonized by the pale reeds, their stalks growing up through the fluted iron bases and erupting from the top where the lamp housing used to be, giving the impression of a strange, feathery torch. On the ground around each lamp, a circle of the reddish vine has established itself, spiraling outward across the wet stones.

The atmosphere is saturated, a warm, heavy presence that clings to your clothes and skin. It’s not a clean humidity; you taste metal in it, minerals, and something organic, a sweetness like overripe fruit, though there is no fruit here. The steam carries a noticeable pressure when it vents, a rhythmic pulsing from the larger cracks that suggests machinery still operating somewhere far below, old geothermal exchange systems or flooded boiler rooms that still cycle somehow, driven by residual heat and the slow chemistry of buried waste.

It’s the sound that dominates, more than the heat or the wetness. The steam vents do not hiss gently; they roar, some of them, a constant low-frequency rush like a distant waterfall, punctuated by sharper blasts from the smaller, more constricted openings. The larger vents produce a deep, thrumming quality that you feel in your chest as much as hear, a sub-bass drone that seems to come from all around, from below. Over this, the reeds provide a midrange rustle, that whisper that never quite resolves into words. And there is, every few minutes, a sound like a great metal door slamming somewhere in the earth, a hollow boom that ripples out through the water, setting the pools quivering. A pressure release, maybe, or the collapse of another chamber in the buried labyrinth.

There is no birdsong. No insects hum. The water, for all its warmth and mineral richness, supports no fish you can see, no frogs, no skaters on the surface tension. The mosses and the reeds and the floating plants are the only life. It makes the loudness of the place stranger—all that sound, steam and venting and the deep rhythmic booms, with no living voice at all.

This is a small place. You can walk its length in ten minutes, its width in five. The boundaries are clear: to the north, the canal, its water hotter than the marsh, steaming visibly, too deep and too hot to cross. To the south, a wall of corrugated iron sheeting, rusted to a lacework of holes, erected by the rail company to keep people from wandering into the more dangerous subsidence zones. The eastern and western edges are marked by the raised embankments of other, abandoned rail spurs, now just berms of slag and clay overgrown with the pale reeds. Within these borders, you have the pump house, the cottages, the main street with its lampposts, the scattered clumps of vegetation, and the network of paths and pools. The cathedral is the sky, a uniform pale grey that sheds a diffuse, shadowless light.

The strangeness does not announce itself. It accumulates. The moss glowing with its stolen phosphorescence, the reeds that whisper almost-words, the rhythmic slamming in the deep, the vents that exhale in pulses as though the earth itself is breathing—none of it is overtly impossible. But the longer you stay, the more you feel that this place is not simply a ruined industrial district. It’s obeying a different set of rhythms, a schedule not human. The steam pulses might be geological. They might be metabolic. The reeds, for all their apparent randomness, all lean slightly toward the same point, a particular vent near the pump house, as if attending to it. The floating plants in the pools have arranged themselves, in every pool, in the same pattern: a ring near the edge, a gap of open water, and a single plant at the center, its leaves spread flat like a hand on a table.

The activity is all process. Steam rises, condenses, drips, pools, drains. Moss grows, inch by inch, eating the bricks. The pump house sump never stops bubbling. The deep booms come and go, a pulse you could almost set a clock by if the clock were not quite earthly. It feels like a system, a machine of geological and biological and industrial waste heat running itself through cycles that no one monitors and no one maintains, but which have not yet run down. The city buried it, forgot it, and it has not forgotten itself.
