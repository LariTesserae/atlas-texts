The air is thick with sound before anything else—a continuous, percussive roar that makes the metal grating beneath your feet vibrate at something close to the frequency of bone. You are in a small chamber, no larger than a modest chapel, suspended somewhere in the guts of a machine that has been running for longer than any civilization on the surface has existed.

The walls are weeping.

Water sheets down every vertical surface, not as rain but as a permanent, clinging film that peels away from the riveted steel plates and falls in curtains toward a floor you cannot see. The chamber is a junction, a knuckle in some vast circulatory system. Pipes as thick as your torso enter through the ceiling and exit through the walls, their flanges crusted with mineral deposits the color of old teeth. Every joint leaks. Every seal has surrendered to time. The water is cool against your skin, somewhere around fifty degrees, and it carries the faint metallic taste of dissolved iron and something sharper—copper, perhaps, or the ghost of lubricants that ceased being manufactured epochs ago.

Above you, a single bulb hums in a wire cage, throwing a jaundiced light that barely reaches the corners. It flickers on a slow, irregular rhythm, not the stuttering of a failing connection but something more deliberate, as though the machine itself breathes in surges of current. In the dimness, you can make out the other presences in this space.

They cling to the wet walls and the dripping pipes, small dark shapes the size of outstretched hands. Mussels. Thousands of them. Their shells are a deep blue-black, almost purple where the failing light catches them, and they cluster so thickly that the original surface of the metal has been entirely replaced by the ridged topography of bivalve colonies. They are the sparse vegetation of this world—the only living things that anchor themselves and grow in place, filtering the constant flow for whatever particulate matter still circulates in the flood. Their shells gape slightly in the dimness, revealing the pale, muscular fringe of their mantles, tasting the water.

Between the mussels, movement. The fauna here is abundant, and most of it is translucent. Pale shrimp, eyeless and slender, pick their way across the shell-beds with the delicate, hesitant movements of the blind. They are prey. Hunting them are flatworms that ripple across the wet steel like sentient shadows, their bodies no thicker than a fingernail. And hunting the flatworms are small fish, bone-white and almost featureless except for the downturned mouths that vacuum the surfaces clean. Everything moves in the slow, patient choreography of an ecosystem that has found its final stable form. Predator and prey, grazing and pursuit, all of it cycling in the space between the rivets.

The chamber is fully made. There is no substrate of earth or stone here, no original geology. The walls are boiler plate, lap-welded and riveted, designed for pressures you can only guess at. The floor—when you find it beneath the ankle-deep water that pools and spills over the lip of a corroded hatchway—is expanded steel grating, and through its gaps you can see the drop below: more pipes, more water, more shells, descending into darkness. Long ago, someone built this place with blueprints and intentions. Now the intentions have been overwritten by the slow, patient work of water and life and time.

The sound never stops. It is the sound of the machine itself, a deep mechanical thrum that underpins the higher frequencies of falling water and the wet, clicking susurrus of mussels adjusting their grip. Beneath that, if you stand very still and hold your breath, you can hear something else: the distant, rhythmic chuff of a pump that must be the size of a building, pressing life through arteries on a scale you cannot perceive from this small, saturated node.

The air smells of rust and cold water and the faint, sweet, organic funk of a thriving filter-feeder colony. It is cool against your skin, not cold enough to shiver, but cool enough that you are aware of your own body heat as something foreign and temporary. You think of cathedrals—not because the space is grand, but because it is small and purposeful and completely inhabited by something that has gone on believing in its own function for centuries after the original parishioners vanished.

The bulb flickers again. In the brief darkness, the shrimp glow faintly, a bioluminescence you hadn't noticed in the light. The water continues its slow, endless work of erasing the seams between the made and the grown.
