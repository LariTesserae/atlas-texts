The Scree-Cut rises out of the long dark like a memory of industry worn to its final, irreducible form.

No water has touched this world in an epoch that defies counting. What remains of a sea — if it ever was one — is a salt-plain so ancient it has annealed into a single crystal sheet stretching southward, faintly phosphorescent under a starless sky. The salt glows a deep, bruised violet, photoluminescing from accumulated radiation damage across gigayears, releasing stored energy too slowly for the eye to track, so that it seems merely a property of the mineral itself. The plain is perfectly flat, unmarred, a plane of dead light. Nothing grows there. Nothing ever could.

The city occupies the high ground along an escarpment that was once a continental rift margin, now worn to a gentle rise two hundred feet above the plain. But "city" is a generous word. The scavenger-scholars who first mapped this place — two generations of them, now dust in their own right — argued in their crumbling archives whether it was ever inhabited by anything we would recognize as alive. No bodies were found. No furniture, no art, nothing decorative or wasteful. Only the architecture of pure function, repeated and repeated.

The buildings are cubes and rectangular prisms of a brownish-metallic substance that is neither rusted nor pristine, only *old* in a way that bypasses chemistry and enters mathematics — a material that has forgotten how to react. Their surfaces are textured with what might be circuitry or might be the fossilized mycelial networks of a silicon-based decomposer that consumed the original inhabitants' biosphere before consuming itself. The effect is of deliberate engraving that has softened, blurred, become illegible, like a printing plate used too many times. The structures huddle and separate in odd rhythms along the ridgeline, connected by bridges that are simply beams, no railings, no shelter, as if the beings who built them did not fall, or did not care.

The streets — if streets they are — are wide, twenty meters or more, paved in the same brown-metal alloy, and utterly empty. Not litter-empty, not cleaned-empty, but *never-used* empty, as though the city was a proof-of-concept for a civilization that died in committee before moving day. That emptiness is the first thing that strikes a visitor's ear. The quiet here is not the soft silence of a forest at night, but a *hard* quiet, a pressure on the eardrums. Sound does not carry; it drops dead at the source. When your boot scuffs a surface, the noise is brief and swallowed instantly, eaten by the dense, dry, utterly motionless air.

The air is the key. The atmosphere of this world has fractionated over deep time. Lighter gases long ago bled into space; what remains is a heavy, sluggish mixture of xenon, krypton, and complex organic vapors that leaked from the interior during the planet's slow cooling. It is breathable, barely, if you breathe slowly and do not exert yourself, but it deadens sound and scatters light strangely. The distant salt-plain's glow reaches this high ground as a suffused, directionless radiance that casts no shadows. Everything is visible in the same flat, equalized level of illumination, a permanent twilight of murky amber, as if the entire landscape is submerged in weak tea.

At intervals along the ridge, something still runs. The scavenger-scholars called them the Pumps, but that was a guess born of analogy. These machines — hexagonally clustered cylinders of a darker alloy, each the size of a small house — were clearly the last things built here, added to the city long after the cubes and bridges, connected to the oldest structures by thick braided cables that hum at a frequency just below human hearing, felt in the teeth and sternum. The Pumps do not draw water from a world that has none. Instead they move the heavy air itself, circulating it through the dead city in a closed loop, maintaining a pressure differential between the ridge and the plain that stabilizes the escarpment against some erosion process so slow it would take a billion years of neglect to manifest a single stress fracture. This is the city's only sound: a subsonic throb that never varies in pitch or rhythm, has not varied since the last starship visited, has not varied since before the ancestors of humans climbed down from trees.

Something shares the ridge, but rarely. The fauna — if the term applies — are visible only in peripheral vision, and then only as faint geometric shifts in the air, a subtle warping like heat shimmer over a hot road, though the temperature is uniformly, tepidly warm, perhaps thirty-five degrees Celsius, stale and unchanging. They do not move in three dimensions so much as recompile their geometry, and they seem to be re-reading the circuitry-grooves in the building surfaces, tracing them with their attention, if attention is what they possess. The scavenger-scholars recorded forty-seven encounters across two hundred years. One of the scholars, the youngest, maintained that the beings were the last nested recursion of the city's original architectural software, still running diagnostics. Others thought them ghosts. The distinction, here, is meaningless.

To walk the city is to undergo a peculiar flattening of one's own subjectivity. The scale is human-enough — doorways exist, or rather rectangular apertures that a human could step through, suggesting the builders were of similar stature — but the relentless, invariant purposefulness of the place erodes a visitor's sense of narrative. There are no corners to hide in, no nooks to rest in, no distinction between inside and outside that carries psychological weight. The interior of a cube is identical to the street, differentiated only by the presence of apertures in the walls. You enter a structure and feel you are still outside. You stand on a bridge and feel you are indoors. The categories break down. The city does not care about your categories.

The erosion here is not the erosion of water and wind. It is *time-erosion*, the slow unspooling of atomic bonds under sheer duration. The edges of every cube are rounded to a radius of exactly three centimeters, not by abrasion but by molecular creep. The surface patterns that might have been circuitry have merged into their substrate, becoming merely texture. The ridgeline itself has slumped almost imperceptibly, reducing the relief between city and plain by perhaps a meter per hundred million years. The Pumps compensate, or perhaps they don't — perhaps the city is already in its final, stable equilibrium with gravity and time, having settled into a shape that will persist until the planet's crust finally cools to an isothermal deadness and even the xenon snows out of the sky. That will be a long while yet. The world is still warm, geothermally; the heat that powers the Pumps comes from deep boreholes that tap residual core warmth. The heat will outlast any definition of life.

The view from the highest point — a bridge that spans two of the tallest cubes at the escarpment's edge — takes in the full sweep of the plain. It is an *open* view, vast and unobstructed. It is also a view of nothing: the violet-glowing crystal emptiness extending to a horizon so geometrically pure that the planet's curvature is faintly visible, a slight bending of the line, the only sign that one stands on a sphere and not an infinite plane. Above, the sky is an unrelieved brown-black, starless, cloudless, moonless. The atmosphere does not form clouds; it is too homogeneous, too mixed, too settled. The temperature does not vary between day and night, because day and night are barely distinguishable; the sun is a smudge of slightly lighter darkness near the horizon, the parent star having dimmed to a brown dwarf so ancient that its light is mostly infrared. The plain captures some of that, channels it into its slow phosphorescent sigh.

If one listens — truly listens, pressing through the hard quiet and the subsonic throb — there is a sound beneath the sound. It is the sound of the salt-plain cooling infinitesimally as the planet's core heat decay ticks down another microkelvin. It is a sound like a very distant bell, rung once, forever, too low to hear. The scavenger-scholars never recorded it. They had no instruments precise enough. Their bones probably registered it, though. Their bones are still there, interred in one of the cubes by the last scholar to die, arranged neatly in a grid. Bones last a long time in an atmosphere this inert. They will outlast the Pumps, probably. They will join the city in its final equilibrium, another mineral among minerals.

This place has no seasons, no weather, no migration of animals, no blooming of plants, no cycle of growth and decay. It is a snapshot of a world that finished changing before change had a name. The machines run. The shapes in the air read their dead circuits. The salt glows its exhausted violet. The air hums its unchanged frequency. A visitor from a living world, standing on the bridge, looking out at the curved horizon of lifeless light, might feel they have intruded upon something private — not a ruin, but a *completion*, a place that has already done everything it was meant to do and now simply persists, without need, without regret, without any concept of ending, because ending would be a change, and no change remains possible.
