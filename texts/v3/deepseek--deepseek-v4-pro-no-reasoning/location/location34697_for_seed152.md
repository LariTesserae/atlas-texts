The air is the first thing—it doesn’t just sit, it moves in slow, warm currents that you can feel sliding over your bare arms like the exhalation of some vast, sleeping creature. It smells green and wet and faintly sweet, a sweetness that comes from live wood breathing moisture into the dark. You can’t see far, not because of fog but because the space itself doesn’t allow it; this is a place that has decided to be small, a pocket cupped inside something larger, and the boundaries are close and soft.

The walls are tree trunks. Not individual trees standing in a forest—no, these have grown together so densely and for so long that they’ve fused into a continuous, living cylinder of wood, maybe thirty feet across, towering up into the dimness overhead. The bark is dark and slick with condensation, riddled with deep vertical fissures where smaller things have taken root. Ferns uncurl from the cracks, their fronds heavy with droplets that catch what little light exists and hold it like cold glass. Moss upholsters every surface that isn’t actively running with water, a plush emerald carpet several inches thick, and when you brush against it your hand comes away dripping. Everything drips. The sound is so constant it becomes a kind of silence—individual drops plinking onto broad leaves, a thin musical trickle somewhere nearby, the softer sound of water being absorbed into saturated ground.

The ground itself is not ground, not really. It’s a woven mat of roots, some as thick as your thigh, others hairlike and pale, all of them slick and warm to the touch. Between the roots, pools of water have collected, black and still, no more than a hand’s breadth deep. They reflect the canopy above in fragments: a tangle of branches interlaced so completely that the sky—if there is a sky—doesn’t penetrate. What light reaches this place is green, filtered through layer upon layer of leaf, and it pulses slightly as breezes you cannot feel stir the upper reaches of the forest. The quality of light is like being underwater, a slow liquid dimness that makes the air feel thick in your lungs. Nothing casts a sharp shadow. Edges blur.

There are other things living here, and they are not shy. This is not a wilderness that has learned to fear. Small, pale amphibians with translucent skin cluster along the edges of the root-pools, their bodies so still they seem part of the wood until one blinks its golden eye. Thin, segmented creatures—neither insect nor worm but something between—crawl over the moss in deliberate lines, leaving faint trails of phosphorescence that linger for a few seconds before fading. Higher up, in the crooks where branches diverge, birds with soft gray plumage sleep in tight clusters, their breathing a barely audible rustle, a susurrus that blends with the water-drip into the background texture of the place. You can smell them, a faint animal musk, clean and not unpleasant. Occasionally one shifts and a single feather drifts down, falling so slowly through the still air that you can watch its whole descent, a small gray boat on an invisible current.

There are structures here, but they are not made by hands that use tools. They have grown. Along one curve of the wooden cylinder, a series of shelf-like protrusions extends from the trunk-wall, spiraling upward in a loose helix. Each shelf is broad enough to lie on, shaped from the same living wood as the walls, edged with a fringe of delicate rootlets that hang down like a curtain of fine hair. The shelves are cupped, holding shallow pools of water that have collected from the constant drip, and in these pools small plants grow—not the lush ferns of the walls but something more deliberate, plants with small white flowers that glow faintly in the green dimness, their petals waxy and thick. Water trickles from one shelf to the next, a quiet stair of miniature waterfalls, and the sound it makes is different at each level, a sequence of notes that never quite repeats. This is not a ruin. It is not abandoned. It is inhabited and shaped and tended by the life that lives here, a settlement in the biological sense, an urbanism of symbiosis.

The whole space breathes. You notice it after a while: the walls of the cylinder expand and contract with a rhythm so slow it takes minutes to perceive, a deep visceral pulse that moves the air in and out of the hollow. When the walls contract, water squeezes from the bark in a sudden sheen, and the pools ripple; when they expand, the fissures open slightly and a warmer draft rises from somewhere below, carrying the smell of deeper earth, mineral and old. The temperature shifts with the breath, a degree warmer on the exhale, a degree cooler on the inhale, and the small creatures respond to it—the amphibians stir, the phosphorescent trails brighten, the sleeping birds murmur in their clustered dreams. This place is not finished. It is still making itself, still growing into whatever it will become. The root-floor thickens by imperceptible increments. The shelf-helix extends another fraction of an inch. The walls rise toward a light that has not yet broken through. It is embryonic and ancient at once, raw geology happening in real time, stone and water being made from living things. And it waits, patient, dripping, breathing, in the warm dark.
