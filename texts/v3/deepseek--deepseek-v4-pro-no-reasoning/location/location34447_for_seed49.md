The air doesn’t so much hold moisture as *be* moisture, thick enough that breathing feels like drinking. Temperature hangs steady at body-heat, creating a perpetual confusion about where your skin ends and the world begins. The light—normal, plain, almost aggressively ordinary light—comes from everywhere and nowhere, a sourceless gray-white that eliminates shadow and depth perception together, leaving you in a flat plane of visibility that extends in all directions without horizon.

You are standing on a city that has forgotten it was built.

Beneath your feet, under a handspan of warm, ankle-deep water that never drains, the ground is seamless. Not paved—the idea of paving implies separate stones or segments—but continuous, a single surface that flows over contours you can't see but can feel when you walk: gentle swells, sudden drops of a few centimeters, long shallow bowls that collect the warm water a little deeper. The material is ambiguous. It reads as mineral but gives slightly under weight, not quite elastic, more like walking on whale skin stretched over stone. In some places it's smooth as glazed ceramic, in others it's porous and faintly rough, as though it grew rather than was laid. The color is a warm gray that shifts toward amber where the water pools deepest.

But you cannot look at the ground for long, because the ground is the least of it.

Rising from this unbroken floor is architecture that has abandoned geometry as a constraint. Structures—you call them structures because they occupy space with intention, but the word is insufficient—unfold in every direction. Not built upward so much as *unfurled*, as if the city is a single continuous extrusion from some deeper dimension, blooming into three-dimensional space and then stopping, politely, before it becomes incomprehensible. Towers twist like viscous liquid caught mid-pour and frozen, their surfaces rippled with what might be windows or might be gills. Platforms cantilever from nowhere, supporting nothing, their undersides dripping slow condensation in strings of warm water that never quite detach. Bridges arc between masses, but they don't connect anything—a bridge from one empty air to another—and you realize after watching them that they're not bridges at all but loops, the same structure curving back to its own origin, a city of closed circuits.

There are no right angles. There are no repeating patterns. But there is not chaos, either. There is *order*, only it's an order your spatial reasoning wasn't built to parse, like trying to read a language where every sentence is simultaneously noun and verb and preposition, all words doing all things at once.

And all of it, every surface, every curve, every impossible canted plane, is wet. The warm humidity condenses on the cooler interior of the material—whatever it is—and runs in sheets and rivulets and fat trembling droplets. The water is never still. It drips from overhangs in ceaseless arrhythmic percussion. It slides down vertical (or near-vertical, or questionably-vertical) faces in glistening curtains. It pools in depressions and overflows, sending warm cascades down to the next level, the next basin, the next unintelligible architectural gesture. The sound of it is immense. Not rain, not a waterfall, but something between: a million million individual drops, each landing with its own tiny report, layered into a roar that you feel in your chest before you hear it with your ears. The water sound is constant, but within it are variations—sometimes a sudden surging rush as some larger reservoir somewhere tips past its threshold, sometimes a deep gurgling resonance from cavities below, water moving through unseen throats.

And there is life here. Abundant life. But not a leaf, not a stem, not a root.

The life here has nothing to do with vegetation, with that 0.4 score that means the green world never arrived or departed long ago. Instead, the life is animal. Or something adjacent to animal. The warm water, the endless wet surfaces, the hidden cavities and exposed expanses—they teem. Things move in the corner of your eye, quick and slick, disappearing into pores in the material you hadn't noticed until they vanished into them. The pores are everywhere, you realize. The city is not solid; it's riddled with openings, entrances, exits, passages, all sized for creatures that range from finger-length to something much larger.

You catch a clear glimpse of one: a flat, segmented body perhaps a meter long, gliding on a film of water across a sloping surface, hundreds of tiny legs rippling in sequence, its dorsal side the exact color and slight pearlescence of the architecture itself. It pauses, and you see it's not merely matching the surface—it *is* the surface in some way, its body interfacing with the material, drawing something from it or depositing something into it. Then it's gone, into a slit that seals behind it without a seam.

The air is thick with small flying things—not insects exactly, though they fill that niche. Their wings are clear as water, beating so fast they're visible only as a faint shimmer, a heat-haze around a body the size of a rice grain. They swarm in the billions, feeding on... what? The condensation? Each other? The ambient warmth? They don't bite, don't land on you, but their collective hum adds another layer to the water-sound, a high-frequency drone that sits just at the edge of hearing, more felt in the teeth than heard.

Larger presences announce themselves by sound. A deep, rhythmic thrumming that cuts through the white noise of water—something massive moving through a vertical shaft, its body against the walls producing a vibration that travels through the entire interconnected structure, arriving at your feet as a subtle buzz. You never see it, but you track its progress by the way the smaller creatures react, a wave of sudden stillness that propagates outward from the sound's source and then dissipates as the threat passes.

There are calls. Shrieks, whistles, a low booming that might be communication or might be the city itself settling. The soundscape is so dense, so continuous, that silence would be more alarming than any noise. The city is deafening not with a single roar but with saturation, a thousand species of sound occupying every frequency band, leaving no quiet for thought. You adapt, or you don't. Your ears start to parse it, finding signal in the noise—the particular dripping rhythm that means a basin nearby is full, the change in the swarm-hum that indicates a predator moving through, the deep structural groans that might presage some larger shift.

Shift. Yes. The city is not static.

The dynamism, the turbulence rated at 2.9, isn't weather in any conventional sense. There is no wind, no storm—the atmosphere is too saturated, too warm, too still. The turbulence is in the structures themselves. As you watch, a span that had been solid begins to *flow*. Not collapse, not melt, but reconfigure. The process is slow enough to be almost imperceptible, fast enough that if you look away and look back, the shape has changed. A wall develops an opening that wasn't there. A platform thickens at one edge and thins at another, tilting almost imperceptibly. The city is alive, or the city is a process, or the city is a computation playing out in material form, and the creatures—the abundant, swarming, crawling, flying fauna—are part of the computation. Agents. Maintenance. Or perhaps they are the users, and the city is the operating system, and you are a ghost in the machine, unregistered, unnoticed, walking through code that executes around you.

The density is absolute. Every cubic meter of space is occupied by something—structure, water, or life. There is no empty air, only air so full of droplets and bodies and sound that it has almost material presence. When you move, you push through it like wading. The city offers no plazas, no open gathering spaces, no boulevards. It is packed, saturated, a solid maze in three dimensions with paths that weren't designed for human stride length, human height, human anything. You duck under a low arch that is already beginning to close, step over a channel of fast-flowing warm water where something pale and eyeless flicks away from your shadow, press sideways through a gap where two walls are slowly, slowly moving toward each other, a geological handclap that will take hours to complete.

And through it all, the impossible technology hums. Beyond modern—yes. This is not a machine built by any civilization that thought in terms of circuits and steel. The city is its own physics, materials that phase between states without heat, energy transfers that happen without visible mechanism, information flowing through the water itself, perhaps, the droplets carrying data in their surface tension, in their mineral load, in the precise frequency of their fall. When you cup your hands and catch the warm, endlessly falling water, you wonder what you're holding. A medium. A message. A waste product. The blood of the city.

The scale is planetary. You know this not because you can see boundaries—there are none—but because the structure implies it. The arches and spans and gravity-defying sweeps are engineered for forces that operate at continental magnitude. A single one of those slowly flowing wall-segments, if you could trace it far enough, might curve through kilometers before it met itself. The city isn't built on the world; the city has replaced the world. There is no outside. No horizon, no sky, no edge where architecture stops and nature begins. The sourceless light doesn't come from a sun—there is no sun here, no day, no night—but from the material itself, a uniform glow that suggests the whole structure is faintly luminous, a city that illuminates itself in perpetuity.

The warm condensate drips on your shoulders. A creature like a living staircase unfolds from a wall and ascends toward a higher level, leaving wet footprints that close behind it. The roar of water and life and slowly grinding material fills your skull. The city breathes around you, vast and dense and alive and utterly indifferent, a place that was never meant for you, but that allows you to walk through it anyway, a temporary anomaly in its endless, turbulent equilibrium.
