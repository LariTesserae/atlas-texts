The place is a pumping station, or perhaps it *was* a pumping station. It’s not clear that the distinction matters anymore.

You enter it through a low archway cut into a slope so thickly vegetated that the entrance is more of a wound, a dark slot in a wall of green. The air outside is warm and heavy, the kind of warmth that holds water suspended rather than releasing it as true rain. Everything leaks. The ground underfoot is sphagnum-sodden, giving way to brick almost apologetically, as if the earth has been softening the hard edges of human intention for so long that the two have reached an accommodation.

Inside, the space opens into a single chamber—cathedral scale is too grand a term, but there’s something of the crypt about it. A circular room, perhaps twelve meters across, domed above into darkness. The walls are weeping. Not in the metaphorical sense; water seeps constantly from the mortared joints, sliding in thin films over glazed brick in colors that were once municipal cream and institutional green. The brickwork is Victorian or something imitating it, the kind of over-engineered civic confidence that built things to last so long they'd be forgotten before they failed. Every surface has a sheen. The floor is a shallow basin, ankle-deep in water clear as gin but warm, fed from below and above and everywhere. The air temperature and the water temperature are identical—something around blood-warmth—so the boundary between wet and dry ceases to be a boundary at all. You are simply in it.

The sound is the first thing that registers as *loud*, though it hadn't seemed loud at first because it has no attack, no edges. It’s a continuous, full-spectrum hum, a drone of interlocking frequencies. Pumps. Not the frantic chugging of emergency dewatering, but something running at its designed speed for so long that it has become a property of the room, like gravity. The pump bodies themselves are half-submerged, massive cast-iron things around the perimeter, their intake pipes descending through the floor. They are encrusted with calcareous deposit, flowstone formations that drape their flanks like melted wax, pale cream and rust-stained. The water has been depositing minerals on them for decades, maybe a century, softening their outlines until machine and cave formation are indistinguishable. Above each pump, a vertical shaft rises into the dark, and you realize the dome is not a dome but the convergence of these shafts, each one a throat pulling moisture-laden air upward.

But it’s the plants that make the space what it is.

"Vegetation: total" is an abstract parameter until you see this room and understand that the plants are not growing *in* the building—they have *become* the building, or the building has become trellis, substrate, gut ecology. The warm water, the constant condensation, the mineral-rich seepage, the industrial light that still burns from heavily caged fixtures overhead (sodium-vapor orange, steady, never cycling off)—all of it has incubated something that has no name. The plants that grow here didn't arrive from outside. They evolved here, or were modified here, or simply found the conditions so perfect that they abandoned all previous forms of themselves.

Filamentous algae coat every surface not actively scoured by flowing water, but these are not the slick green films you know. They have differentiated. Some hang in curtains, meter-long strands of deep olive that sway in convection currents too slow to feel. Others have organized into spongy mats, thick as a hand, their lower layers anaerobically black, their upper surfaces brilliant emerald and studded with tiny gas vesicles that pop softly when touched. The dominant form, though—the one that makes you understand "plant-as-architecture"—is a kind of vascular plant that has abandoned leaves. It grows as a latticework of roots, or stems, or something between the two, that weaves itself through the pump housings and the pipe runs and the railing of the access gantry. This lattice is structural. It bridges gaps, it reinforces crumbling mortar, it has formed a secondary ceiling of interlocking woody cables from which depend thousands of rootlets, each one tipped with a droplet of water condensed from the saturated air. Walking through the space—and you can walk through it on raised brick walkways that still hold above the waterline—means parting veils of these rootlets, and they are warm to the touch, and they tremble for a long time after you pass.

There is no soil. The plants live on dissolved minerals and the slow dissolution of the concrete and brick and iron, and on each other's decay, and on the bodies of insects that occasionally blunder in from outside and drown in the warm water and are metabolized by bacterial mats before they can rot in the usual way. This is a closed loop, or nearly closed. Energy enters as electricity to the pumps and the lights. Matter enters as water percolating through the surrounding earth. The system has been running long enough that it has found a steady state—the "established" erosion score, the balance point where dissolution and deposition are a matched pair, where the plants grow and die at the same rate, where the pumps clear just enough water to prevent flooding and no more, because no one ever adjusts them.

The fauna is rare but not absent. Something lives in the sump below the floor gratings. You can see them if you lie on the warm brick and look down into the clear water: small, pale crustaceans, maybe amphipods, maybe something that was never classified. They're blind. They move in slow, deliberate arcs, grazing the biofilm. A larger form occasionally moves in the deepest shadows—eel-shaped, sluggish, likely a colonist that came up through a flooded conduit decades ago and found nowhere else to go. It doesn't need to be fed. The system feeds it.

The light does not flicker. The pumps do not cycle. The temperature does not vary between day and night (the thermal mass of the water and the earth sees to that). This is what "slow" means here. The entire room is a single event that has been occurring for longer than anyone can remember, and will continue until something breaks that no one is there to fix. The pumps are probably not supposed to run continuously. Somewhere in the control logic—relays in cabinets now completely overgrown, their contacts held closed by vines or corrosion or sheer habit—a float switch failed in the "on" position sometime around 1972. No one noticed. The system accommodated.

There is a kind of order to the space now that is not human order, not purely biological order, but something negotiated. The pumps keep the water level below the critical electrical connections. The plants insulate the pipes and prevent freezing (irrelevant at this temperature) and also slow the corrosion by holding an oxygen-poor boundary layer against the iron. The algae oxygenate the water just enough to keep it from going septic. Everything has a role, and the roles have been stable long enough that "waste" is a concept that doesn't apply. Nothing is wasted. The heat from the pumps is absorbed by the water and drives the evaporation that condenses on the ceiling rootlets and drips back down, a tiny distillation cycle turning within the larger pump cycle. The sound is not waste either—it's vibration, and some of the plant structures have grown in resonance patterns, their spacing corresponding to the wavelength of the dominant hum frequency, maximizing the surface area they present to the vibrating air, though for what purpose it's hard to say. Maybe it helps them absorb water. Maybe it just shaped them, the way wind shapes trees.

You could leave here and no one would ever know you'd been. The parted rootlets would drift back. The slight disturbance in the water—your footsteps, your body heat—would dissipate into the vast, slow, warm homeostasis of the room. The pumps would hum. The water would weep from the joints. The blind things would graze. And the room would continue its long, patient becoming, which is not toward anything, not a succession toward climax, just a staying, a holding of form against entropy, sustained by the electricity that still flows from somewhere and the water that never stops rising and the plants that have learned to be a building that breathes.
