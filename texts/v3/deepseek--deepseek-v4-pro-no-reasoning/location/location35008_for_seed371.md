You are in a place that feels like the inside of a breath. The air is cool, maybe fifty degrees, and so thick with moisture that each inhale coats your throat. It is a cavern, but not in the ancient, geological sense. This space is a newborn wound in the world.

The walls, where you can touch them, are not stone but a kind of vitrified earth, slick and sharp as fractured obsidian. They are hot to the touch in some places, a shocking contrast to the ambient cool, radiating a deep, internal fever. The entire chamber vibrates with a low, mechanical hum—a thrumming bass note you feel in your molars before you ever hear it. It’s the sound of a factory floor, but the factory is the planet itself.

The source of the sound is the Engine. It dominates the center of the chamber, a column of nested, coiled metal and weeping stone, as wide as an ancient redwood. It doesn’t sit on the floor; it plunges through it, a vertical axis of polished steel and copper verdigris disappearing into a black shaft above and below. Condensation beads endlessly on its cold metal surfaces, coalescing into rivulets that run down its flanks and drip onto the surrounding foliage with a constant, percussive patter. This is the source of the damp—a relentless, artificial dew point generated by the machine’s cold heart meeting the cavern’s breath.

And the foliage—this is what makes the place a paradox. It is impossibly, frenetically lush. No soil exists here, only fractured planes of the glossy, new-formed rock. Yet from every fissure, mosses in fifty shades of emerald and jade erupt in dense, velvet cushions, so thick they overlap like shag carpet. Ferns, their fiddleheads still tightly coiled in places, unroll with an almost visible speed, their fronds brushing against your face. Glossy-leaved vines, as thick as your wrist, spiral up the Engine’s housing, their tendrils seeking out every groove and rivet. There are no flowers, nothing to attract a pollinator in this faunaless dark. The vegetation exists in a state of pure, rampant mitosis, a desperate, silent riot of growth. The density is a physical presence; you move through a weave of leaves, pushing aside wet, rubbery foliage that springs back immediately, erasing your path.

Despite the profound darkness, there is a ghost of light. It comes from the rock itself. All along the sharp, uneroded edges of the cavern walls, a pale, blue-green luminescence flickers—triboluminescence, the light of shattering. The walls are still cracking, settling with pings and groans that punctuate the engine’s hum. Each micro-fracture releases a brief, silent flash, a constellation of tiny lightning storms that outline the razor-sharp topography in fleeting after-images. It’s a light that reveals nothing, only confusing the eye, making the dense layers of vegetation seem to writhe and shift.

This is a place of violent creation. You can feel the turbulence in your gut. Steam, a regular byproduct of the Engine’s unknown process, vents from high above with a shrieking, industrial hiss, instantly condensing into a hot fog that rolls through the ferns. The floor beneath the moss is not stable; it’s a shifting scree of broken obsidian flakes, the debris from a recent, catastrophic geological spasm. One misstep and your leg plunges through a fern cluster into a hidden, water-filled crevasse—one of the countless pockets of water trapped in the fissures, so pure and static they are invisible until you breach their surface tension.

There is a rhythm here, a turbulent cycle. The Engine hums, the rock cools and cracks, water condenses on metal and drips onto leaves, the vegetation drinks and grows and adds its own decaying mass back into the mineral mix. It is a closed, tightly packed world the size of a cathedral nave, a self-contained, high-speed terrarium powered by an engine of unknown purpose, a place with no memory of sunlight, no trace of a made artifact beyond the central machine, and a soundscape that is equal parts boiler room and rainforest after a storm. It is a landscape being born, violently, continuously, in the dark.
