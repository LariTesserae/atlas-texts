The first thing that arrives is the sound: a sustained, breathy roar, like a great exhalation held too long, pressed through infinite small apertures. It has no rhythm, no pause, no single source you can turn toward. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, filling the cool, dim air until the air seems to *be* the sound.

You are standing on a plain of dark stone, and the stone is wet. Not with standing water—there are no pools, no streams you can see—but with a slickness that lives on the surface, a perpetual condensation that beads and runs in slow, minute rivulets. The ground underfoot is not flat; it is a landscape of low, rounded hummocks and shallow, winding channels, all of it shaped from a single material: a dense, black, faintly iridescent rock that seems to swallow the dim light. It feels like basalt but is strangely warm to the touch, as if it has only recently cooled from some unimaginable heat, though the air is cool, carrying a mineral sharpness that tastes of iron and cold stone on the tongue.

This is a young place. The edges are sharp where they haven't been worn smooth by the omnipresent moisture. There are cracks, fresh and precise, that forking through the hummocks like lightning frozen in stone. You can feel the recentness of it all—not recent in hours or days, but recent in geological time. This world is still being born, and you are walking on its fresh skin.

Extending across this open, undulating plain are the structures. At first, you mistake them for more rock formations: low, irregular pillars and squat towers, rarely taller than your waist, built from the same black stone. They are scattered without discernible pattern, some solitary, others clustered in groups of three or four, as if a small, forgotten village had been turned to obsidian. But as your eyes adjust to the dimness, you see they are not natural. The surfaces are too smooth, the angles too deliberate, though softened by the constant film of water. Some are simple plinths. Others are ringed with shallow, worn steps. A few have hollows carved into their tops, perfect bowls now brimming with water that trembles with the ever-present sound.

And from every surface, the source of the sound rises: steam.

It is not boiling steam, but a thick, cold vapor that seeps from the entire landscape. It pours from the fresh cracks in the stone and rises from the wet surfaces of the hummocks. It issues most densely from the carved structures, curling from their hollows and clinging to their edges in slow, heavy coils. The vapor does not dissipate quickly; it hangs in the cool air, dense and near-touching, creating a layered atmosphere of shifting grey and black curtains. Visibility falls to mere arm's-lengths in the thicker veils, then opens again into small, dim clearings where the ground slopes just slightly. This is the density of the place—not of solid objects, but of presences. Each coil of vapor feels like a distinct, slow-moving entity, a breath from the earth itself, filling the volume of the air until it is saturated with movement on the edge of perception.

The light is a soft, directionless glow that seems to emanate from the vapor itself, a faint phosphorescence that gives the fog a silvery-grey luminescence. It is a dim, crepuscular world, without sun or shadow, where depth is measured in the varying opacity of the mist. The only true darkness comes from the stone itself, which remains a perfect, light-drinking black beneath its watery sheen.

In all of this, you see no single living thing. There is no green shoot, no crust of lichen on the weeping rocks. The cool, wet, mineral world is a blank slate. The idea of "vegetation" belongs to another universe. Yet, it is not entirely dead. The rare signs of something else come as you walk. A sudden, quick vibration through the stone underfoot, a single, deep *thump* felt rather than heard above the roar, as if a great bell had been struck miles below. Once, the vapor ahead of you billows outward, pushed by a slow, silent displacement, and you catch a glimpse of a form—a low, many-angled crest of stone that was not there before—moving with the patience of a glacier before sinking back into the grey. A scent, fleeting and sharp, like ozone and heated metal, passes through the perpetual wet-iron smell. The fauna here is the land itself, or something within it.

The technology is none. The scattered structures, upon closer inspection, bear no marks of tools. They are not built of separate blocks but are extrusions, grown from the bedrock as if the earth here once bubbled up in orderly forms before freezing solid. They are like the first sketches of architecture, dreamed by a planet, now abandoned to the slow, loud exhalations of its cooling body. They are not ruins; they are prototypes that have not yet found their purpose.

The cycles here are long. You can sense the slowness. The sound never changes pitch, but you feel it is the note of a shrinking, a settling, a planet's deep breath that will take a million years to exhale. The water does not rain; it beads. The rock does not erode; it slowly, patiently, grows a new skin of condensation, molecule by molecule. The movement in the fog is a single event that might take a day to complete. The whole landscape is a process unfolding on a timescale that makes your own presence a flicker too brief to register. The loudness is the sound of deep time made audible, compressed into an eternal now. And the strange, quiet heart of it all is that you are standing inside this process, in its very throat, on the raw, wet, singing surface of a world that has not yet learned the meaning of silence or the colour green.
