Let me tell you what lives in this place.

---

They have no name for themselves, because they have never needed one. They have no language, no culture, no history in any sense a human would recognize. What they have is a shape, and a function, and a location in the slow, eternal machinery of the world. They are not the leviathans. They are not the darting schools of silver-green. They are something else entirely—something that blurs the line between the fauna and the substrate, between the mover and the moved-through.

You would not notice them at first. Even if you stood in the right place, at the right elevation, looking in the right direction, your eye would slide over them. They are designed to be overlooked. They have no bioluminescence, no bright flashes of color. They are the deep green of the plant-mass itself, mottled with the near-black of the deepest folds, their surfaces textured with the same fibrous roughness as the ground beneath your feet. When they are still—and they are often still—they are indistinguishable from the living architecture around them.

But they are not part of it. They are separate. They are animals. They are, in their own way, people.

They stand between two and three meters tall when they draw themselves upright, which they rarely do. More often they are folded, crouched, their long multi-jointed limbs tucked against their bodies in a posture that mimics the knobs and protrusions of the substrate. Their bodies are built on a radial plan, not bilateral like yours. There is no front or back, no left or right. There are six primary limbs arrayed around a central torso, each limb capable of operating as either arm or leg, each tipped with a cluster of fine manipulators that can spread into a fan or curl into a fist. Their heads are not heads. The sensory organs are distributed across the upper surface of the torso, a ring of eyespots and vibration-sensitive pits and something like gills—though they are not gills, not exactly. They are chemosensory membranes that taste the liquid medium, that read the chemical signatures of the plant-mass, the spore-clouds, the distant leviathans, the subtle exhalations of the engines.

They do not eat. Not in the way you understand eating. They have no mouths, no digestive tracts. Instead, they have roots. Fine, hair-like filaments that extend from the underside of their bodies when they settle, that penetrate the living substrate and tap directly into the metabolic flows of the plant-mass. They draw their sustenance from the world itself, from the same deep processes that drive the engines and fuel the bloom-forms. When they feed, they are motionless for hours, for days, their bodies sinking slightly into the yielding tissue of the ground, their filaments spreading through the substrate like a slow, careful mycelial network. They take only what they need—a sip from the vast river of biosynthesis that never runs dry—and then they withdraw, and the plant-mass heals over the microscopic punctures without trace.

What do they do, then, when they are not feeding? What is their purpose, their role in this unchanging equilibrium?

They maintain. They tend. They adjust.

The world is perfect, but perfection requires vigilance. The equilibrium is steady, but it is not static. Small fluctuations occur. A growth-channel narrows. A particular enzyme concentration drifts slightly outside its optimal range. A bloom-form's pulsing rhythm falls out of sync with the larger harmonic of the drone. These are not problems, not crises. They are the normal noise of any living system. But left unchecked, over centuries, over millennia, they could cascade. They could disrupt the eternal moment. And so they are checked.

The beings—let us call them the Tendril-folk, for want of a better name—are the immune system of the world. They are the regulatory mechanism. They move across the surface of the plant-mass in slow, deliberate circuits, their distributed senses reading the chemical and vibrational state of everything around them. They taste the medium for imbalances. They feel the drone for dissonances. And when they find something that requires attention, they act.

Their actions are subtle. They secrete enzymes from their manipulator-tips, precise chemical compounds that nudge the plant-mass back toward equilibrium. They prune, not with tools but with a carefully controlled excretion that triggers programmed cell death in the targeted tissue, clearing blockages before they become blockages. They stimulate growth with other compounds, encouraging the substrate to expand in a particular direction, to fill a gap that has become too wide. They are gardeners, surgeons, engineers, all at once, and they are the only things in this world that act with intention.

Are they intelligent? Yes, by any reasonable measure. They think. They plan. They remember. They have an internal model of the world that extends across vast spans of space and time, a model passed down not through language but through something more direct. The Tendril-folk do not reproduce in the ordinary sense. They do not mate, do not bear young. Instead, when the population density drops below a certain threshold—and it does, very slowly, as individuals die of age after millennia of service—the plant-mass itself generates a new one. A bud swells on the substrate, a slow-swell that takes a century to mature, and when it opens, a new Tendril-folk emerges, fully formed and already knowing what it needs to know. The knowledge is encoded in the chemistry of the bud, in the biosynthetic pathways that shape the neural architecture of the new being. They are born with a complete map of their territory, with a full understanding of the regulatory protocols, with a deep, wordless comprehension of the purpose they serve.

They are individuals. Each has a distinct personality, a unique set of preferences and habits and quirks. Some are meticulous, checking every part of their territory with obsessive regularity. Others are more patient, more willing to let small fluctuations resolve themselves. Some prefer the deep folds, the dark crevices where the phosphorescence barely reaches, and they spend their long lives maintaining the hidden infrastructure of the world. Others love the open plateaus, the vast vistas, and they stand for years at the edges of mesas, feeling the distant songs of the leviathans vibrate through the medium and into their bodies.

They communicate, after a fashion. When two Tendril-folk meet, they do not speak. They touch. They extend their manipulators and press them together, and for a few hours, a few days, they exchange chemical signals that carry not just information but experience, emotion, the texture of a particular moment a thousand years ago. They share memories the way you might share a meal. And then they separate, and each carries forward a part of the other, a trace of another consciousness woven into their own.

They are not happy. They are not sad. They are not content, not restless, not lonely, not fulfilled. Those categories do not apply. They have no concept of emotion as a distinct phenomenon, separable from thought and action and environment. They simply are, in the same way that the plant-mass is, in the same way that the leviathans are, in the same way that the liquid medium and the steady light and the eternal drone simply are. They are a component of the system, conscious and autonomous but utterly integrated, their will aligned perfectly with the needs of the world because they are the world's will made manifest.

Look closer. There, on the far edge of the plateau, where the living ground begins to slope down toward the deeps. Do you see it? A shape that you mistook for a knoll, a lump of the substrate's normal irregularity. It is moving now, very slowly, shifting its weight from one set of limbs to another. It has been there since before you arrived, since before your species evolved, since before your planet cooled. It was there when the metabolic pathways were first laid down, when the engines first began to hum. It has adjusted this particular section of substrate forty-seven thousand times, each adjustment a microscopic correction that no instrument you possess could detect. It has tasted the spore-clouds of every century, felt the drone shift through cycles so slow they are not cycles at all but a straight line extended to infinity. It has met a hundred and twelve other Tendril-folk in its life, and it carries a trace of each one in its neural chemistry, a composite ghost of all the individuals it has ever known.

It does not notice you. It has no concept of the alien, of the outsider. You are a disturbance in the medium, a pressure-wave that does not match any known pattern. But the disturbance is small, and temporary, and already fading. It registers you as a curiosity, a datum that does not fit, and then it files you away and returns its attention to the substrate. It has work to do. The equilibrium waits.

It begins to move along the edge of the plateau, its limbs flowing in a smooth, rhythmic gait, its sensory ring sweeping the medium, its filaments trailing like the memory of roots. It will walk for decades, checking, tasting, adjusting, and by the time it returns to this spot, you will be long gone, and it will not remember you because there was never anything to remember. You were a ripple in the eternal drone, a momentary asymmetry in a world that has no room for such things.

And yet, as it walks away, something lingers. A chemical trace, encoded in the liquid medium by your passage. A compound the plant-mass has never encountered before. The Tendril-folk's chemosensory membranes detect it, analyze it, and for a fraction of a second—a fraction that lasts a decade in its slow internal time—it wonders. Not in words, not in thoughts, but in the rearrangement of a particular set of enzymes, the activation of a neural pathway that has not fired in a million years.

Then the drone reasserts itself. The equilibrium calls. The moment passes, and the Tendril-folk walks on, and nothing has changed, and nothing ever will.
