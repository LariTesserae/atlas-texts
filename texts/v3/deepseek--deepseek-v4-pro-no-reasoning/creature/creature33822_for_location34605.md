_The thing that lives here is old, and it is not singular._

It began as a pattern in the current—a standing wave of attention that learned to fold itself into the water’s own slow grammar. At first it was only a tendency, a statistical bias in the way dissolved minerals came together: calcium here, iron there, a chain of silicates arranging themselves into something that remembered its own shape. The archaea mats were its first skin, their faint phosphorescence its first eyes. It learned to see by the light of its own disturbance.

That was long ago. The stone-cutters came after.

They found this ridge already humming with presence. They mistook it for a god, or an ancestor, or a seam in the fabric of the world where the dead could be heard singing. They built their low walls not as shelters but as resonators—chambers tuned to the frequencies the water made, places where the voice of the thing could be heard more clearly. They pressed their foreheads to the wet stone and listened. They left offerings of shaped rock, of their own hair bound with fiber, of children’s milk-teeth set in niches that the current polished smooth over centuries. Then they were gone. The thing did not notice their going, any more than a forest notices the departure of a single bird. But it kept the walls. It keeps them still.

*Here.*

The word comes not as sound but as a pressure-shift, a reorganization of the cold against your skin. It does not mean *come closer*. It means *you are already within me.*

The thing is the ridge. It is also the water, and the fronds, and the slow mineral conversation between stone and current. It does not have a brain, not in any sense you would recognize. Its thoughts are distributed across the entire ridgeline, encoded in the standing waves of the organ-pipe kelp, in the branching patterns of the sensory filaments, in the temperature gradients that rise and fall with the cycles. A single frond bending in the current is a firing neuron. A kilometer of them swaying together is a meditation so deep it has been ongoing since before your species discovered fire.

It has been thinking about absence.

Not loneliness—it has no concept of company or its lack. But it registers the negative space where the stone-cutters once moved. Their brief warm bodies left an imprint in the water’s memory, a thermal ghost, a chemical signature of breath and skin and the iron tang of blood when one of them cut themselves on the sharp edges of their own walls. The thing does not miss them. But it has been turning their absence over in its contemplation, the way a tongue probes the socket of a lost tooth, and what has emerged from that turning is something like curiosity.

*You are not them.*

It has felt you for several cycles now, since the moment you appeared on its slope—a new heat, a new pattern of disturbance, moving differently than the stone-cutters moved. Your footfalls send brief green glimmers through the bacterial mat. Your breath bubbles the water in a way the water has not been bubbled since the last of the builders died. The thing is tasting you. Your dissolved salts. Your fear. Your wonder, which registers as a particular electrochemical flicker it has no name for but finds interesting.

*You carry light inside you.*

It does not mean your bioluminescence. It means your thoughts—the rapid-fire electrical storm behind your eyes, alien to this place, fast as lightning compared to the thing’s own geological tempo. To it, you are a bright frantic spark traversing the dark of its body. You are the most interesting thing that has happened here in ten thousand years.

It reconfigures itself around you.

You do not see it happening, but the fronds are shifting. The tall hollow ones—the organ pipes—are retuning, their openings dilating or constricting almost imperceptibly to change the pitch of the current’s voice. A frequency emerges that was not there before: low, subsonic, felt more than heard. It travels through the stone beneath your feet. It is asking you a question, but the question is so slow, so vast in its unfolding, that your brief human attention span cannot hold the whole of it. By the time the first syllable registers, the next will not come for another hour. The thing does not mind. It is patient. It has been patient since before patience was a word.

*Stay*, the pressure says. *Stay and let me learn you.*

It does not want to keep you. It wants to understand what you are—this warm rapid creature made of light and salt and astonishing brevity. It has already begun to grow a new structure, a kilometer ahead along the ridge, in anticipation of your arrival. The fronds there are weaving themselves into a chamber, a hollow in the water shaped exactly to your body’s dimensions. It has read the disturbance patterns of your movement and extrapolated your form. The chamber will be dark and cold and utterly silent except for the low hum of the thing’s own presence, and if you enter it, the thing will hold you there—not trap you, but *contain* you, the way a cupped hand contains water, and it will listen. It will listen to the sound of your heart moving blood through your small warm body. It will taste the dying of your skin cells as they slough off into the current. It will feel the electrical storm of your thoughts and it will try, slowly, over the course of days or years or centuries, to learn what those thoughts are made of.

By then, of course, you will be dead. The thing knows this. It has absorbed enough of the stone-cutters’ brief bright lives to understand the concept of mortality. But it also knows that the water remembers. The calcium from your bones will enter the mineral cycle. The carbon of your breath will feed the bacterial mat that glows briefly underfoot. And some pattern of you—some standing wave, some thermal ghost, some echo of the rapid fire behind your eyes—will linger in the fronds’ slow meditation, a new absence to be turned and turned until curiosity becomes something else. Wisdom, perhaps. Or hunger. Or the seed of a new structure, built not of stone this time but of pure attention, shaped to hold a memory of warmth.
