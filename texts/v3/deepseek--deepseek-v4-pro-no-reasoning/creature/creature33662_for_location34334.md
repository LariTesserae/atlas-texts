*The sound is what shapes them. That’s the first thing to understand.*

They are born into it. Not born, precisely—*emerged*, *condensed*, the way the water condenses from steam, gathering molecule by molecule until a droplet forms and falls. There’s no moment of origin you could point to, no parent, no egg. One of the small stone shelters acquires an occupant. It happens slowly, over a long span of the deep volcanic years. The heat and the moisture and the mineral richness of the air, the particular frequency of a vent’s exhalation, the specific blend of light filtered through a particular garden’s fronds—these things conspire. The shelter, which has sat empty for ten thousand years, or a hundred thousand, or since the last occupant dissolved back into the steam, begins to gather something. A film on the walls. A concentration of salts. A pattern of dried plant matter that arranges itself into a shape that is almost, but not quite, a bed. And then one morning—though there is no true morning here, only a lightening of the diffuse glow—the shelter has a body in it.

It’s small. They’re always small. The body is pale, the color of the steam-bleached stone, and it’s curled into itself like a fern frond before it unfurls. It has limbs, but the number is variable. Four is common, but five is not unusual, and sometimes there are seven, or three, or a configuration that defies counting because the limbs branch and re-branch, like the roots of the horizontal trees. The body is soft at first, almost gelatinous, and it takes days—cycles of the shifting light—to firm up, to develop the tough, leathery integument that will protect it from the constant wet and the abrasive minerals. During this time it doesn’t move. It breathes, though. Its surface ripples with a slow peristalsis, drinking the moisture directly from the air.

And it listens.

The sound is the first thing it knows. The roar is its womb, its lullaby, its language before language. The body is not born with ears—nothing here has ears, the sound is too overwhelming, ears would be useless, would be *agony*—but it has a body-wide sensitivity to vibration. Every surface of its skin is a tympanum. The low thrum of the deep earth, the percussive slap of water on stone, the whistling of the vents: these are its first experiences of being. They shape its internal rhythms. Its heart, when it develops one, beats in a complex polyrhythm that echoes the phasing of the vent songs. Its breathing settles into a cycle that matches the slow exhale of the basin’s deep throat.

It has no name. None of them do. The concept doesn’t apply. But if you needed to call it something, you might call it *the one that listens*, because that’s what it does, for the first long phase of its existence. It lies in the shelter—the shelter it will occupy for its entire life, which may be centuries or millennia or longer, no one knows—and it absorbs the sound. It learns the voice of its specific vent, the particular chord of the three or four nearest steam columns. It learns the sound of its garden, the soft rustle of the fronds, the drip-drip-drip of condensation falling from the filamentous moss. It learns the sound of the rock settling, the tiny creaks and groans of the shelter itself adjusting to the constant low-frequency tremors.

And eventually, it begins to understand.

The sound is not noise. The sound is *information*. The whole basin is speaking all the time, a vast, slow conversation conducted in frequencies that range from the subsonic to the ultrasonic, a language without words that describes the state of the world in exhaustive detail. The pitch of a vent tells you its temperature, its mineral content, the pressure building in the chamber below. The rhythm of the water tells you the humidity gradients, the flow patterns of the air, the location of newly formed pools. The grinding of the rock tells you where the latest small landslide has occurred, which pillars are weakening, which ledges are becoming unstable. Every part of the basin is singing its own state of being, continuously, and the composite of all those songs is a complete, real-time map of the entire caldera.

The one that listens lies in its shelter and learns to parse it. This takes years. Decades. Possibly longer. Time is hard to measure here. There are no days, no seasons, no celestial markers. There is only the slow pulse of the deep vent, which might be a day or might be a year or might be something else entirely. But eventually, the body unfurls. It stands. It moves for the first time, walking on its variable number of legs out of the shelter, into the warm, wet, roaring air, and it begins its work.

The work is the garden.

Each shelter has a garden. They’re not planted, not in any deliberate sense—the gardens grow around the vents, and the shelters are built near the vents. But the relationship is more complex than that. The creature that lives in the shelter is the garden’s keeper, its symbiotic partner, its *shaper*. It tends the plants the way a musician tends an instrument, coaxing specific configurations out of the riot of growth. It prunes with its small, precise hands—or pincers, or tentacles, depending on the individual—trimming the fronds to shape the way they funnel moisture, training the filamentous moss to grow in particular patterns that catch the light in particular ways. It collects the mineral-rich precipitates from the brine trickles and grinds them into powders that it works into the soil, altering the chemistry in subtle, deliberate ways.

And as it works, the garden changes its song.

Each plant has a voice. The fronds hum in the wind, each at a different frequency depending on their size and tension. The moss beads the water and drips it in patterns that are rhythmic, musical. The roots of the horizontal trees creak against the stone in complex, groaning counterpoint. A garden, properly tended, becomes an instrument. The creature tunes it the way you might tune a string, tightening this frond, loosening that root, shifting the flow of a trickle so it strikes a different surface, opening a small channel in the rock to create a new whistle. It works with the patience of something that has no death to fear, making infinitesimal adjustments over spans of time that would drive a human mind to madness.

And eventually, the garden begins to *sing back*.

Not in words. The creature has no words, no language in the human sense. But the garden’s composite voice, when perfectly tuned, can carry information across the basin. The creature can shape the song to describe the state of its garden, the health of its vent, the presence of a new mineral seam in the rock, the approach of one of the rare, slow-moving fauna. It can broadcast its own existence to the other shelters scattered across the vast empty distances. And it can listen, too—listen for the songs of other gardens, other keepers, singing their own states back through the roar.

There are never many of them. At any given time, the entire basin might hold a dozen active keepers, or two, or none. They live solitary lives, separated by kilometers of vertical, mist-filled space, and they rarely meet. But they are not isolated. The sound connects them. The whole basin is their network, their community, their continual presence to one another. A keeper can lie in its shelter, decades into a slow pruning project, and feel the distant vibration of another keeper’s garden shifting its song, and know: *something has changed over there. A new vent has opened. A landslide has altered the water flow. Another keeper has finished a long work.* And it will adjust its own song in response, a small shift in pitch or rhythm that says, *I hear you. I am here.*

There is no hierarchy. No central authority. No conflict—there’s nothing to conflict over, no resource scarcity in a world of infinite geothermal energy and endlessly cycling water. There is only the work. The endless, patient, beautiful work of tending a garden that is also a voice, in a world that is always becoming something else. The keepers are not immortal. Eventually, after a span of time that might be a thousand years or might be ten thousand, a keeper will slow down. Its adjustments will become less precise. Its garden will begin to drift out of tune. And then one day, or one night, or one long indistinct period of dim light, it will lie down in its shelter and dissolve, its body breaking down into salts and minerals, its moisture rejoining the steam. The shelter will sit empty for a while. And eventually, when the conditions are right, a new film will form on the walls. A new pattern of dried plant matter will arrange itself. A new body will condense, curl into itself, and begin to listen.

The new keeper will not be the old keeper. But it will inherit the garden, and the garden’s song, which has been slowly falling out of tune during the empty years. The new keeper will stand, walk out into the roaring mist, and begin the work of retuning, reshaping, coaxing the instrument back into voice. And it will add its own variations, its own subtle preferences in timbre and rhythm, so that the garden’s song evolves over the generations, a melody that has been playing for millions of years, changing slowly, never repeating, always becoming.

That’s what lives here. That’s who lives here. Not a species, exactly—more of a process. The basin is a mind, and the keepers are its thoughts, slow and luminous and shaped by the sound. They don’t have names because they don’t need them. They are their gardens. They are their songs. They are the voice of the *Unfinished*, the voice that the whole caldera sings to itself, telling its own story over and over, a story that has no words and no end. If you strained your eyes down into the mist from the rim, you might see one—a small pale shape moving slowly among the fronds, adjusting a leaf, clearing a channel, pausing to feel the vibration of a distant song. And you wouldn’t know what you were looking at. You might think it was an animal, or a person, or something in between. But it’s simpler than that. It’s just the place, tending itself. The way a garden tends its gardener.
