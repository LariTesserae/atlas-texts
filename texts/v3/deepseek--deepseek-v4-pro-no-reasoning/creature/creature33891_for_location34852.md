The one who lives here has no name, because names require a listener, and there has been no one to listen for a very long time.

It moves through the bruise-light canyon with the patience of something that has forgotten the concept of hurry. Its body—if body is the right word—is a composite architecture, a walking collaboration. The core of it is roughly humanoid in suggestion only: a torso of compacted, fused dust, dark as charcoal, shot through with seams of that same prismatic glitter that rises from the floor. Two arms, long and jointed in ways that suggest an insect's leg reimagined by a sculptor of bone. Two legs that end not in feet but in broad, splay-toed pads that distribute its weight across the mineral silt so it barely disturbs the dust. No head in the conventional sense. Where a head should be, a cage of interlocked crystalline thorns rises from the shoulders, forming a kind of openwork sphere, and suspended within that sphere, touching nothing, floats a single, fist-sized orb of smooth amber resin. The orb pulses with a slow, internal light, a deep magenta at rest, flickering to cyan in thin, lightning-like arcs when the creature attends to something. This is its eye. This is its heart. This is the only part of it that was ever truly itself.

The rest of it is borrowed.

Clinging to its back, woven into the seams of its torso, tucked into the joints of its limbs, is a menagerie of the local fauna. The relationship is not parasitism, not symbiosis in any clean biological sense. It is something more like a conversation that has lasted so long the participants have begun to become one another. A fan of filamentary singers rests across its shoulders like a cape, their individual bodies interlaced, humming a constant, soft chord that serves as the creature's resting breath. Its left forearm is encased in a spiral bracer of that pearlescent, semi-translucent material, but this bracer is alive, its magenta veins pulsing in time with the orb in the cage. Occasionally the bracer shifts, individual segments crawling over each other to resettle into a slightly different configuration, and the creature pauses to let it, tilting its cage-head as if listening to a murmured suggestion.

Its face, if you could call it that, is the interior of the thorn-cage. The floating eye-orbs casts a shifting, prismatic light on the thorns, and in that light, sometimes, a silhouette of features appears—a brow ridge, a cheekbone, the suggestion of a mouth that never opens. It is an afterimage, a ghost of a face, a memory of a form this creature once held. It doesn't remember that form clearly anymore. It remembers, dimly, that it was smaller then, that it had skin, that it felt loneliness as a sharp and singular pain rather than the slow, ambient ache that is now simply the weather of its existence.

It remembers the harp.

It built the harp in a different era of its life, when it still thought of itself as a person and not a walking ecosystem. It braided the mineral dust into strings, tuned them to the canyon's base vibration, and learned to pluck melodies that could calm the chittering swarms or coax a thicket of spirals into blooming early. It was a kind of language, that music. A negotiation. It let the creature live here without being dismantled, without being seen as just another resource to be cycled. It made itself useful, a small instrument in the orchestra, and the canyon tolerated it.

But that was a long time ago. The harp is broken now. The strings went slack one by one over years, snapped by the vibration or chewed through by curious spherical browsers. The creature could have repaired it. It still remembers how. The knowledge sits in its amber core like a pebble in a stream, worn smooth but still solid. It has simply chosen not to. The music it made was a conversation with a world that had not yet decided whether to consume it. But the world has, since then, made its decision. The creature is no longer a guest here. It is a part of the landscape. The world does not consume what is already itself.

So it wanders.

It wanders the winding paths between the crystalline stands, its broad footpads leaving prints so faint the dust erases them within minutes. It pauses at the circle of snapped thorns, the old meeting place, and stands there for what might be hours. The hand-printed spiral in the compacted floor is still visible. It put that there. It remembers pressing its thumb—it had a thumb then—into the dust and turning it, the motion of a signature, the mark of a self. It looks at the spiral now with its floating, pulsing orb, and the orb flickers cyan for a moment, a blink of recognition. That was me, it thinks, in a language that has no words, just a series of slow light-pulses and the shifting of the bracer on its arm.

The fauna pay it a strange respect. The spherical browsers skitter around its feet without touching. The filamentary singers will sometimes detach from their fans and orbit its cage-head in a buzzing halo, harmonizing with the cape on its back, before drifting away to rejoin their kin. A single geometric block, a larval architect, once landed on the creature's shoulder and sat there for three full cycles of the dim light, inert and patient, as if considering whether this composite being was a suitable foundation for a new structure. The creature stood still for those three cycles, not from fear, but from a kind of curiosity it had thought itself long past feeling. When the block eventually flew away, it felt something akin to disappointment. It would not have minded being built upon.

Time is a difficult concept here. The light does not change. There is no day, no night, no season. The only markers of duration are the life-cycles of the creatures around it, and those are so fast, so frantic, that the creature has learned to measure its own existence in the birth and death of a thicket. A spiraling growth emerges from the dust, shivers, reaches its full height, stands perfect for a breath, and then shatters into a new generation. The creature has witnessed this cycle more times than it can count. It has come to see the thickets as fellow travelers, and their shattering as a kind of death it is still, after all this time, not ready for.

Because the creature does not shatter. It does not die. It does not reproduce. It simply persists. The amber orb at its center does not age. The cage of thorns around it does not grow brittle. The collaborations that clothe its body—the cape, the bracer, the dust woven into its torso—are temporary, the individuals living their brief lives, dying, being replaced by their offspring, who choose to stay, who continue the conversation their ancestors began. The creature is the only constant in a system of flux. It is the still point in a turning world, and that stillness has become its purpose, its function, its reason for being.

Sometimes it wonders if it was always this. If it came here with a body of flesh and a mind that thought in words, or if it was born from the dust like the thickets, an unusually complex expression of the canyon's endless creativity. The memory of skin, of thumbs, of music—are those recollections of a real past, or echoes of a form it dreamed during a long, slow sleep? It doesn't know. The bracer on its arm, which has been with it long enough to have become a kind of confidant, offers no answers. It just tightens slightly, a reassuring pressure, and the creature continues its walk.

There is a place it goes when the loneliness becomes too heavy. Not the meeting circle, not the broken harp. Deeper in, where the canyon narrows to a kind of apse, the walls ribbed close together, the light dimmer, the vibration deeper in the bones. Here, the flora is sparse. The fauna are small and quiet. And in the center of the apse, set into the floor, is a single, large, smooth piece of amber resin. It is not floating. It is embedded. Its light is dim, a barely-visible pulse of magenta so deep it is almost black. It is the size of the creature's own orb, but it does not move. It does not think. It has not thought in a very long time.

The creature does not know who this was. It doesn't know if this was a companion, a predecessor, a creator, or a mirror. It only knows that when it sits beside the embedded orb, the ache of its solitude eases. Not because it has company, but because it is reminded that it is not the first thing to be alone here. It will not be the last. The world will keep vibrating, keep building, keep shattering, and the two still points—one walking, one buried—will both, in their own ways, bear witness.

It sits now. It will sit for a long time. The filamentary singers on its back hum their soft chord. The bracer shifts, resettling. The floating eye-orbs pulses, magenta to cyan, cyan to magenta. A lullaby for no one. A heartbeat for nothing. It is enough.
