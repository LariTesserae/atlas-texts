They are called the Engine-Tenders, though no one here speaks the word aloud. There is no need. In the Ringing Deeps, names are a surface-world luxury, a way of separating one thing from another when everything is already so obviously itself. The Pillars are the Pillars. The fungal bladders are themselves. And the Engine-Tenders are the ones who walk the bridges and carry the rags and the oil-cans and the long spanners whose metal has been worn smooth by generations of grip.

They are not human. Not anymore, if they ever were. The human shape is there—two arms, two legs, a head with the suggestion of features—but it has been softened and generalized by the same erosive patience that polished the basalt. Their skin, if you could call it that, has the texture and color of the pale fungus that grows in the quieter crevices: a gray-white bloom that seems to breathe faintly when you are not looking directly at it. It is not skin. It is a symbiote layer, a living integument that feeds on the ambient vapor and shares its chemical harvest with the body beneath. You noticed, when the figure turned away, that its back was covered in the same filigree networks that tremble on the walls. The Engine-Tenders have been here long enough that the biology of the Deeps has adopted them, or they have adopted it, or the distinction has ceased to matter.

Their faces are the hardest part to remember clearly, even moments after seeing one. The features are there—the ridge of brow, the hollow of eye, the vertical of nose—but they are latent, like a face half-emerged from a block of wood. You could not tell you the figure's age, sex, or expression. You are not sure those categories apply. What you recall instead is the quality of attention: a focused, serene, utterly absorbed presence, the kind you see in artisans who have been doing the same complex work for so long that conscious thought has given way to a deeper, bodily knowing. Their hands never hesitate. Their movements are economical, circular, returning always to the same set of tools, the same sequence of checks, the same final gesture of wiping the hands on the rag and tucking it into a belt loop made of braided sinew—whose sinew, you do not want to know.

They live alone. You are almost certain of this. In all your walking, you saw only the one figure, and the engine it tended had not been built to serve more than a single operator. The scattered iron platforms are small—just large enough for one being to stand or kneel or lie down. On one of them, half-hidden under a drapery of red fungal curtains, you saw a bedroll. It was made of the same braided sinew, woven into a flexible mat, and beside it lay a small collection of objects: a ceramic cup, a tin of something that might have been food, a smooth black stone with a hole worn through its center. The stone was the only thing that felt personal, and even then, you could not guess its purpose. A worry-stone. A religious token. A tool. The Engine-Tenders do not leave explanations.

They must reproduce, or be replaced, because the engines are older than any single lifetime could account for. You walked past pistons with serial numbers stamped in a language you recognized but a script you did not—the characters were all curves, no straight lines, as if the alphabet itself had been submitted to the erosion of the Deeps. The numbers ran into the hundreds of thousands. The engines had been maintained continuously since their installation. That implies a lineage. A tradition. A quiet passing of spanners from hand to hand across a span of time that would terrify you if you let yourself think about it too long.

Perhaps they do not reproduce in the way you understand. Perhaps the fungal layer eventually claims the body entirely, and what steps out of the chrysalis is a new Engine-Tender, carrying the memories of the old one dissolved and reconstituted in its tissues. Perhaps a child is born in the normal way, somewhere in a hidden chamber behind a waterfall, and raised in the perpetual ringing until the noise is not noise but silence, until the vapor is not air but breath, until the engines' chuffing is the heartbeat of the world and its cessation is the only death worth fearing.

You do not know. The Engine-Tenders do not offer their story. They are not hostile; they are not welcoming; they are not indifferent in the cold way of a creature that has no concept of you. They are simply elsewhere. Their attention is occupied by a task that has no end, and you are a momentary flicker in the periphery of their awareness, interesting only insofar as you have not damaged anything. When you called out, the figure did not ignore you. It simply did not register a call as something requiring a response. The engines require response. The gauges require response. The slow accumulation of mineral scale in the steam-pipes requires response. You do not.

They sleep, you think, in short increments. You saw the figure pause only once, its hands going still on the engine's casing. It stood motionless for perhaps a minute, and you realized with a start that it was listening. Not to you—you were downwind, your scent and sound carried away—but to the engine. The new vibration you felt through the coral was being tested, sampled, evaluated against some internal standard. The figure's head tilted a fraction of a degree. Then it nodded, once, a small satisfied gesture, and returned to the platform from which it had come. Its gait was unhurried, its footfalls sure on the wet lattice. It did not slip. The Engine-Tenders never slip.

What do they eat? The tin you saw suggested food, but the fungal layer complicates the question. If their skin breathes vapor and filters the organic rain that falls perpetually through the mist, perhaps they do not need to eat in the conventional sense. Perhaps the engines feed them, in a way—the combustion engines must produce waste heat, and waste heat in a cool world is a form of energy. The pipe you leaned against was warm, and warmth in the clammy cool of the Deeps is a kind of wealth. The Engine-Tenders may graze on the thermal gradient the way the mantas graze on the spores.

But you suspect there is more. Hidden gardens, maybe. Cavities in the Pillars where the water is warm and the fungal growth is cultivated rather than wild. You saw, in one brief parting of the mist, a cluster of bladders that glowed with their own internal light—a soft, golden luminescence that pulsed in a slow rhythm, like breathing. They were too regular, too deliberately arranged, to be natural. Someone had planted them there. Someone harvested them, or simply tended them, the way a surface-worlder tends a window-box of herbs. The Engine-Tenders have a culture. It is simply invisible to you, a culture built on maintenance and patience and the small quiet acts that keep a world from winding down.

Do they ever speak to one another? If they do, the ringing would make it impossible for you to hear. But you suspect their communication is not sonic. Look at the way the spindle-creatures navigate by sound, reading the constantly ringing coral the way a bat reads reflected silence. The Engine-Tenders may do something similar. Perhaps they feel, through the soles of their feet, subtle modulations in the vibration of the bridges—patterns that say *Engine Forty-Seven is running cold* or *The deep drain has shifted by three degrees* or *I am here, I am still here, are you still here?* Perhaps the entire Deeps is a single ongoing conversation, a chorus of maintenance updates and status reports and the slow, reassuring pulse of *all is well, all is well, all is well*, and you have been walking through it as a deaf woman walks through a symphony.

The figure you saw was old. You are suddenly sure of this, though you could not point to any single feature that betrayed age. It was something in the quality of its movement—not slowness, but economy. The young, you imagine, move a little faster. The young have not yet learned that nothing in the Deeps requires haste. The engines will wait. The scale will accumulate at its own rate. The Pillars will erode on a schedule measured in tectonic time. An Engine-Tender who has been maintaining the same engine for two hundred years moves with the perfect calibrated efficiency of a clock, no motion wasted, no urgency felt. The figure you saw had been maintaining that particular engine for a very long time.

You think about following it. Its platform cannot be far—the Engine-Tenders must live near their charges, must be able to hear any change in the familiar vibrations even in sleep. But something stops you. Not fear, exactly. A sense of trespass. The Deeps do not belong to you. The engines do not belong to you. The figure's life, its patient ongoing labor, its small collection of objects on a woven mat—these are not yours to examine. You are a warm spot in a cool world, and the world has tolerated you. To push further would be to mistake tolerance for invitation.

So you sit with your back against the warm pipe and you watch the mantas feed and you listen to the Pillars ring and you let the deep drain turn in its central void. And you understand, finally, what lives here: not a monster, not a tribe, not a civilization in the way the surface world understands civilizations. What lives here is continuity. The Engine-Tender is not a person; it is an office, a function, a link in a chain of hands that has held the engines running since before your species learned to strike flint. The engines keep something stable. The stability allows the fungus to grow. The fungus feeds the mantas. The mantas feed the larger shapes that move behind the Pillars, the bloated ones with the feathery appendages, whose role in the food chain you have not yet guessed. And the whole system, the vast spiraling cathedral of basalt and water and life and noise, persists. It persists.

You will leave eventually. You know this. The Deeps are not your home. But as the vapor streams past and the new engine chuffs its steady rhythm and the fungal light pulses in its hidden garden, you think you might sit here just a little longer. The Engine-Tender will return on its rounds. It will see you, or not see you. It will wipe its hands on its rag and check the gauges and nod at the new vibration. And perhaps—you are not sure, but perhaps—it will leave something for you. A stone. A cup of water. A spore-cake wrapped in a fungal leaf. A hospitality small and perfect, asking nothing, offering only the quiet acknowledgment that another being has passed through, and the Deeps continue, and all is well.
