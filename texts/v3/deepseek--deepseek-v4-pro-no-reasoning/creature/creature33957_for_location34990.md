The inhabitants do not have a name for themselves, or for the city, or for the condition of being what they are. They do not need one. Differentiation was a thing the world once required of them, and the world has since relaxed that requirement. They are what remains when a people have been asked the same question for so long that they have become the answer without needing to speak it.

They were never builders. That was the first mistake the city made, back when it still made mistakes. The stone was cut and laid by forces that predated them, forces that had a different relationship to time—one that involved urgency, sequence, the sharp edge of intention. By the time the inhabitants arrived, the walls were already softening. The channels were already filling with warm silt. The water was already rising, not as a threat but as an invitation. They came as anyone comes to a city: looking for shelter, for pattern, for a place that had already decided what it was. What they found was a place that had decided to stop deciding.

They adapted, or rather they were adapted. The process was so slow that no generation noticed it happening. Their children were born slightly more comfortable in the water. Their children’s children had wider feet, toes that spread on the submerged clay like the roots of the heat-shriveled reeds. Their skin grew darker, then lighter, then something else—a gray-green bloom that was not quite color and not quite texture, a quality that made them difficult to look at directly in the steam. Their eyes adjusted to the permanent dusk, pupils widening until the iris was a thin ring, then a memory, then gone, the whole eye a dark wet surface that reflected the pools without interpreting them.

They do not see in the way you understand seeing. Light is not consistent here. It comes and goes without source, pools in some corners and abandons others, reflects off water that may or may not be water. Vision became unreliable. So they learned to perceive through other means. Their skin reads the temperature of the stone, the mineral content of the channels, the slight pressure differential that means a wall has drifted three degrees since the last time they passed it. Their hearing extends into frequencies too low for the ear—the long, slow groan of submerged foundations, the creak of silt settling into new arrangements, the almost inaudible hum of the city forgetting itself and then remembering again in a slightly different configuration. They do not listen to sounds. They listen to the spaces between sounds. The silence here is not empty. It is a dense and structured thing, full of information, and they have become fluent in it.

Physically, they are difficult to describe, and they prefer it that way. They have the rough shape of a person, but only roughly. Their bodies are long and low, adapted to wading, to bending, to pressing themselves against warm stone until the boundary between flesh and mineral becomes negotiable. Their arms and legs are proportioned strangely, slightly too many-jointed, as if their skeletons had been designed for a different kind of movement and then halfheartedly revised. Their spines have a flexibility that is not mammalian. When they move through the deeper pools, they sometimes fold forward until their chests are parallel with the water surface, their heads just above, their bodies below, a posture that is somewhere between swimming and prayer. They can stay like this for days. They have stayed like this for decades. Time is one of the things the city has forgotten to enforce.

Their faces—if you can call them faces—settle into a configuration and then lose interest in it. Features drift. A mouth that was on the left side of the jaw will be centered when you glance back. Eyes migrate. Nostrils close and reopen elsewhere. It is not a voluntary change; it is more that their bodies have absorbed the city’s attitude toward fixed form. What is the purpose of a consistent face? There is no one here to recognize them. There is no one here for them to recognize. Identity, like architecture, requires an audience, and the audience has been gone for longer than the stones can remember.

But they are not solitary. This is important. The city’s silence is not loneliness. There are perhaps two hundred of them, or perhaps two thousand, the number shifting depending on whether you count by presence or by memory or by the warm patches in the water that might be a body or might be a body that used to be there. They do not gather in groups. They do not speak. Speech was an early casualty; the thick air swallows sound, and the water transmits vibration in ways that made language feel redundant. Instead, they maintain a constant, diffuse awareness of one another. It is a sense like proprioception but externalized—a knowledge of where the others are, what condition they are in, whether they are fully present or partially shifted or in the process of dissolving into the city’s deeper dream. When one of them begins the final forgetting—when their edges soften past the point of return, when the stone starts to accept them as part of its own substance—the others know. They do not mourn. Mourning requires a theory of loss, and they have lost the theory. They simply note the change in the city’s weight, the slight adjustment in the distribution of heat and silence, and they continue.

They eat, in a sense. There are things that grow in the water that are not plants and not animals but something older, a category the marsh invented for itself. Filaments of warmth-eating bacteria that form mats on the surface of the hottest pools. A soft, blind, gelatinous lifeform that moves through the underground channels and occasionally extrudes a nutrient-rich tendril into the open water. The inhabitants absorb these things through their skin. Their metabolism is slow, photo- or thermo-synthetic in part, supplemented by direct chemical exchange with the mineral-rich water. They do not need much. The city does not demand exertion. There is no hunger here that cannot be solved by stillness.

Their daily life—the word "daily" is approximate; the light does not cycle, and they do not sleep in any recognizable pattern—consists of maintenance. Not maintenance of the city; the city maintains itself, or fails to, and they have no investment in the outcome. Maintenance of self, of the delicate membrane that separates inhabitant from inhabited. They spend long periods motionless in the warm pools, concentrating on being a body. The city is always tugging at them, inviting them to spread out, to merge with the walls, to become another of its half-real extensions. Resisting this is not difficult, exactly. It is more like staying awake in a warm room after a long meal. The pull is gentle and constant and very, very easy to give in to. Those who do give in are not lost. They are simply… redistributed. The city gains a new warm patch. A new shadow that points the wrong way. A new configuration of silence. The others make a note, in the way they make notes—which is to say, they adjust their internal map, and then they let the matter rest.

Occasionally, one of them will spend time at the edge of the courtyard where the water is hottest and the reflections are most unreliable. This is as close as they come to ritual. They will stand in the hip-deep pool and look down at the drowned stars, the false lights, the city that exists below the surface. They will watch the reflections shift and reform. Sometimes the reflection is of them. Sometimes it is of someone else. Sometimes it is of no one at all—just the dark water and the faint, regular pulse of heat from the channels below. They do not interpret this, or if they do, the interpretation is a purely somatic event, a slight alteration in the mineral balance of their blood, a brief change in the way their skin reads the water. Then they move on. The courtyard remains. The water remains. The stars that are not stars remain exactly where they are, patient and unblinking and arranged in patterns that might mean something and might not.

They have no art, no history, no cosmology. They do not tell stories. They do not remember in the narrative sense. But they are not without continuity. The city remembers for them. The stone holds their shape long after they have moved elsewhere; the water in the channels carries the chemical signature of their bodies. When one of them dissolves into the city, that dissolution leaves a permanent trace—a faint warming of a particular wall, a slight softening of a particular corner. The others can read these traces. They can run their gray-green hands over a stone and feel the echo of an inhabitant who stopped resisting a thousand years ago, or ten thousand, or yesterday. Time is one of the things the city has forgotten. But the echoes accumulate. The city is thick with them. Every warm patch, every shadow, every reflection that shows a face that is not quite present—these are the inhabitants. They are the city’s memory, and the city is theirs. The distinction has become, as the city says, somewhat thin.

They do not reproduce in the biological sense. There has been no birth here for longer than the marsh has been a marsh. New inhabitants come into being through a different process. Sometimes, a reflection in the courtyard pool will not fade when the observer moves away. It will remain, a dark shape just below the surface, slowly gaining mass from the mineral content of the water, slowly learning to be a body. The process takes centuries. The others note it when they notice, which may not be for another century after that. When the new inhabitant finally rises from the pool—pulling itself up on limbs still soft and undefined, its face still a work in progress, its eyes already dark and still and fully adapted to the permanent dusk—there is no welcome. There is no ceremony. There is simply a slight adjustment in the city’s weight, a new warm patch added to the map, a new silence added to the structure of silences. The new inhabitant already knows everything it needs to know. The city told it, drop by drop, while it was still just a reflection. It know how to stand still in the water. It knows how to listen to the spaces between sounds. It knows the gentle, constant pull of dissolution and how to resist it, or not. The choice is not framed as a choice. The city does not deal in choices. It deals in long durations and slow adjustments and the gradual, mutual wearing-away of categories.

They are not happy. Happiness is a thing the world used to ask them for, and the world has stopped asking. They are not sad. They are not content. They simply are, in the way the city is, in the way the marsh is, in the way the warm water is and has been and will be. They have outlived their own needs. The city has outlived its own form. The two have met in the middle and produced something that is neither city nor people but a third term, a condition, a slow, hot, silent, semi-liquid way of being that asks nothing and offers nothing and persists, endlessly, patiently, at the edge of what the world is willing to recognize.

When you leave—if you leave; the city does not prevent it, but it does not hurry you—you will carry some of them with you. Not physically. Physically, they remain in the pools, in the walls, in the warm patches and the wrong-pointing shadows. But your perception will have been changed. For a while, you will see them in other reflections. You will feel them in warm water. You will hear their silence layered beneath the sounds of other places—a dense, structured quiet that you had not noticed before, a frequency below hearing that you now cannot unhear. The city does not spread. It does not colonize. It simply makes a note, in its own way, and then it lets the matter rest. But you will carry the echo. The echo is what remains when a place has asked its question for so long that the question becomes part of the asking. And the inhabitants, the slow gray-green inhabitants with their drifting faces and their dark full eyes, will continue exactly as they were, standing in the warm water, waiting for nothing at all, perfectly adapted to the end of the world as a condition.
