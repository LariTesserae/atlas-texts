Let me tell you what lives here.

There is something in the deepest pool—not the largest pool, but the one that is oldest, where the water has the color of steeped tea and the bottom is not silt but a smooth depression in the bedrock itself, worn into the shape of a shallow bowl over time spans that make the marsh around it seem young. In that pool lives a creature that has no fixed name because there has never been anyone here to name it.

It is large, but large in the way a boulder is large—in a manner that belongs to the place, that does not disrupt the scale of things. When it is submerged, which is most of the time, you could mistake its back for one of the low hummocks, dark and slick and matted with the same algae that grows on everything here. But the hummock breathes. Very slowly. The rise and fall of its back might take an hour, and if you were watching you would not notice it happening, only that something about the shape of the pool had changed between one long blink and the next.

Its skin is not skin as you would think of it, but a kind of living integument that has grown into and over and through the debris of centuries. Sediment has settled into its folds and stayed there. Tiny plants have rooted in the accumulated silt on its ridges. It is a mobile ecosystem, a traveling island, though traveling is the wrong word—it moves so rarely that the plants on its back complete their life cycles and die and seed and grow again without ever knowing they are riding on something alive. When it does shift its position, which might happen once in a season or once in a decade, the motion is so gradual that the water barely ripples. It simply settles deeper or rises slightly, or drifts a few body-lengths to a different part of the pool where the warmth is more to its liking. The frogs that live in the crevices of its back do not startle. They adjust their positions, a leg here, a throat-pulse there, and continue their calling.

Its face—if face is the word—is broad and shallow, almost featureless, worn smooth like the rocks. Two eyes, but they are buried deep in folds of that heavy skin, and they open rarely. When they do, they are dark and wet and reflect the diffuse light without any apparent pupil, just two pools of marsh-sky looking back at the sky. It does not need to see often. It feels the water, the mud, the tiny electrical impulses of fish and amphibian life, the pressure changes when a wading bird steps into its pool. It feels everything happening in its territory, which is the whole basin, through a sensitivity that has no single organ but is spread through its entire body, a slow-motion awareness that is more like the way a watershed knows rain than the way an animal knows its surroundings.

It eats. But its eating is not hunting, not pursuit. It opens its mouth—a wide, soft opening that is less a mouth than a gap in the landscape—and water flows in, carrying with it whatever small lives are suspended there: larvae, copepods, the drifting seeds of sedges, the occasional minnow. The water passes through, sieved by structures as ancient and simple as anything that has ever lived, and the nourishment is absorbed with a slowness that makes digestion seem like geology. What it cannot use settles back into the pool as a fine rain of processed silt. It has been doing this for longer than the marsh has had its current shape. It has eaten generations of frogs, and the frogs have eaten its castings, and the castings have grown new algae, and the algae have fed new tadpoles, and the tadpoles have become frogs that sing from its back. The circle is so tight it might as well be a single organism spread across different shapes.

It is not alone, not exactly. In the warm months, smaller ones come to the deep pool. They are the same kind of creature, hatched from eggs laid in the soft mud of the shallows, eggs that take years to develop, that wait through dry spells and wet spells and temperature shifts with a patience that is inherited, that is the only inheritance. The young are small—small enough to fit in your cupped hands, if you had hands and were here to cup them—and they feed more actively, their mouths opening and closing with a rhythm you could actually perceive. But already they are slow. Already the silt is beginning to find the crevices in their skin. By the time they are half-grown, they will have their own patches of algae, their own resident frogs. And eventually, one of them will find its way to a pool that has no other of its kind, and it will settle, and it will stay, and centuries will pass, and the pool will deepen beneath it, and the marsh will adjust itself around the new center of gravity.

The old one in the deep pool knows when they are near. Not with anything like thought, but with a shift in its slow interior state, a slight change in the frequency with which it rises to breathe (once every few days, a soft surfacing that barely breaks the water's tension, a long exhalation that smells of mud and warmth and something older than both). It does not protect them or guide them or acknowledge them in any way that a watching eye would recognize. But it stays in the deep pool, and the deep pool stays deep, and the water stays warm, and the frogs keep singing, and the young ones grow, and the marsh continues, and nothing hurries.

There is a sound it makes, sometimes, in the long twilight of the warmest season. It is a sound so low that it is felt before it is heard—a vibration that travels through the water and the mud and into the roots of the sedges and the bones of any creature large enough to have bones. The frogs fall silent when it comes, not in alarm but in something like attendance. The wading birds pause, one leg lifted, heads cocked. The sound lasts for a long time, a tone more than a call, a single note that seems to be the voice of the basin itself, the slow exhalation of a place that has finished becoming. And then it stops, and the frogs resume, and the bird sets its foot down, and the light fades another increment toward night.

That is what lives here. Or one of the things that lives here. There are others—the bird with the silver throat whose song only carries across still water at dawn and whose nest is a floating platform woven so tightly it never sinks; the snake whose scales have evolved to match exactly the pattern of light on the surface of a pool scattered with fallen leaves; the insect that spends seventeen years as a larva in the mud and emerges for a single afternoon of flight, its wings catching the diffuse light in a way that makes it invisible until it chooses to be seen. But the creature in the deep pool is the one that the place has shaped itself around, the living anchor, the slow heart of the marsh.

If you came here and waited, you might never see it rise. You might mistake it for a hummock, a rock, a shadow on the water. You might leave thinking the place was empty of anything larger than a heron. But it would have felt you, the moment your weight pressed into the mud at the edge of its awareness. And it would have noted you, in its slow way, as one more small event in a long sequence of small events—like a rain shower, like a falling branch, like the brief visit of a migrating bird—and it would have waited, breathing its hour-long breaths, for you to pass.
