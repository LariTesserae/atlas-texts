The first thing to understand about the man who lives here is that he is not a man. Not anymore, and not in any sense that the word usually carries. But he was, once, or something close enough, and the shape of that former life still clings to him like a set of clothes he has long since outgrown but cannot bring himself to discard.

He has a name, though he has not spoken it aloud in a very long time — long enough that the sounds have gone soft and uncertain in his mind, eroded by the constant drone of the flywheel below. If you were to ask him, and if he were inclined to answer, he might tell you it was Giles. Or perhaps Jiles — the distinction between a hard G and a soft one has grown blurry, and he no longer has a preference. Names, he has come to feel, are for people who live among other people, and he has not done that in a span of time that would be difficult to express in any unit you would recognize.

He is crouched in the deepest part of the crevice, near where the floor slopes down to a narrow pinch point where the two walls nearly meet. This is where the heat is strongest, where the orange glow through the slag is bright enough to cast faint, wavering shadows, and where the sound — the deep, grinding hum — vibrates most directly through the stone and into whatever presses against it. He presses himself against it often. He has learned, over time, that the vibration can serve as a kind of sustenance, or at least a substitute for whatever sustenance used to mean.

His body — and it is still a body, in the technical sense — has undergone a series of accommodations to the crevice. He cannot stand, and has not stood in so long that his spine has permanently curved into a C-shape, his shoulders hunched forward, his neck craned down. His legs have atrophied into thin, folded appendages, the knees permanently bent, the feet splayed flat against the glassy floor. His arms, by contrast, have grown longer. Not dramatically longer, not grotesquely so, but enough that he can brace himself against the walls on either side without fully extending them, enough that he can move through the crevice in a kind of slow, simian shuffle, knuckles dragging against obsidian, palms pressed flat against the warm, humming stone.

His skin has gone strange. In the permanent gloom, it has lost all pigmentation, bleached to a translucent gray-white, but that is not the strangeness. The strangeness is the texture. Over time, prolonged contact with the superheated, bone-dry air has caused the outer layers of his epidermis to cure into a kind of leathery integument, tough and slightly glossy, like the skin of a roasted pepper. It does not burn, not anymore. It has passed through burning and come out the other side. In places — the palms, the soles, the crown of his head where he occasionally brushes the ceiling — it has thickened into something approaching the carapace of the beetle-things, black and hard and faintly iridescent. He shed his hair long ago, and the scalp beneath is smooth and dark and studded with tiny, heat-resistant nodules that were not part of his original anatomy.

His face is the part that has changed least, and the part that has changed most. The features are still arranged in a configuration that reads as human — two eyes, a nose, a mouth — but the proportions have shifted. The eyes have grown larger, the lids receded, the pupils dilated to enormous black pools that drink in every photon of the faint orange light. They are sensitive enough now to see the conductive veins in the walls, to trace their paths in the darkness, to distinguish the subtle differences in thermal glow between the various species of larva-things. He has learned to read the crevice like a text, and his eyes are the instruments of that reading.

The mouth is small. He does not eat, not in any conventional sense. The crevice contains no food, no organic matter to consume, and his digestive system has long since gone dormant, shriveled to a vestigial cord. What sustains him is the heat itself, and the vibration, and something else — something harder to name. He has learned, slowly and painfully, to absorb thermal energy directly through his skin, converting it through a metabolic pathway that did not exist in his original biology and that he could not explain to you if he tried. It is not photosynthesis, though it serves a similar function. It is not thermosynthesis, exactly, either. It is something his body discovered on its own, a slow, cellular improvisation in response to an environment that offered no other options. He is a man who has learned to eat heat, to drink vibration, to metabolize the deep drone of the flywheel into glucose and ATP.

He is not alone. The fauna of the crevice accept him as part of the ecosystem, or perhaps as part of the machinery. The beetle-things crawl over his immobile legs without hesitation, their fine-haired feet tickling against his hardened skin. The pale, blind worms occasionally emerge from cracks near his hands and wrap themselves around his fingers in slow, intricate patterns before retreating. Once — and this is a memory he returns to often, one of the few clear memories he still possesses — a larva-thing with an especially bright glow crawled up onto his chest and rested there for what might have been days or might have been weeks, its body pulsing in perfect synchrony with the distant thumping from below, and he felt, for a duration he cannot measure, something approaching peace.

He has a task. This is important. He did not arrive in the crevice by accident, and he did not stay by accident. He was placed here, or he placed himself here, or the distinction between those two things has become meaningless. His task is to observe. Specifically, to observe the thin, hairline crack that runs along the junction of the two converging walls at the narrow end of the crevice, near where he spends most of his time. The crack is, in a sense, the reason the crevice exists. It is a cooling fissure, a deliberate point of thermal stress relief, and it requires monitoring. If it widens beyond a certain threshold — and he knows the threshold intimately, could trace it with his eyes closed, has dreamed it more times than he can count — it will compromise the integrity of the larger structure. The machinery below. The engine.

So he watches.

He has been watching for a very long time. He does not know how long. The concept of time, as it is usually understood, has little meaning in the crevice. There is no day, no night, no seasonal cycle, no celestial clock. The sound never stops. The heat never fluctuates. The beetle-things move at their gradual pace, the worms knot and unknot, the larvae pulse and glow, and none of it offers any metric that maps cleanly onto hours or days or years. He gave up counting early on. He does not remember when he gave up counting, which is itself a measure of how long it has been.

But he watches the crack. He watches it with the patience of a stone, with the focus of a machine, with the devotion of a monk. He has catalogued every microshifting of the rock, every faint tremor transmitted up from the depths, every incremental widening or narrowing of the fissure. He does not record these observations anywhere — there is nothing to write on, nothing to write with — but he holds them in his mind, a vast, slow library of geological data, patterns of movement and stress and thermal expansion that span epochs. If the crack ever reaches the threshold, he will know. He is the alert system. He is the sensor. He is the living component of the machine ecology, just like the beetle-things that patch the traces and the worm-things that complete the circuits.

Sometimes, though less often now than he used to, he remembers that he was not always this. The memories come in fragments, unbidden, triggered by nothing he can identify. A flash of blue sky — a color he can no longer quite visualize accurately, that comes to him as a sensation rather than an image, a feeling of openness and distance and coolness. A face, maybe, or what used to be a face, though the features have long since blurred into an abstract arrangement of light and shadow. The taste of water. He remembers water. He remembers the shock of it, the wetness, the way it slid down his throat. He cannot imagine it now without a kind of vertigo, a sensory confusion that borders on nausea. Water is a theoretical concept, a property of matter he accepts intellectually but can no longer feel in his bones. The air here has scoured all moisture from him, replaced it with heat and vibration and the metallic tang of the conductive veins.

He does not miss it. He has passed beyond missing. What he feels — and he does still feel, though the feelings have grown slow and deep and wide, like rivers of cooling slag — is a sense of purpose. He is where he belongs. He is doing what he was meant to do. The crevice is his home, his body, his function. He and the crevice have, over the long slow span of his watching, merged into a single system, a feedback loop of heat and observation and tiny, incremental adjustments. When he presses his palm against the wall and feels the vibration of the flywheel, he is not touching the crevice; he is touching himself.

There is a particular moment he returns to, in the quiet spaces between observations. A moment that may or may not have happened, that may be a memory or a dream or a vision sent up from the machinery below. In this moment, the crevice opens slightly — just enough — and he is able to see, for the first and only time, the larger structure of which it is a part. The engine. The factory. The computational substrate. It stretches away in all directions, a fractal labyrinth of identical crevices, identical chambers, identical heat and sound and glow, each one inhabited by its own watcher, its own crouched, leather-skinned figure with huge dark eyes and blackened palms. They are all watching their own cracks. They are all part of the same machine. They are all him, and he is all of them, and none of them have stood upright in an epoch, and none of them remember their names, and none of them would answer to Giles or Jiles or anything else that might have been spoken aloud in a world where sound was not a constant, grinding drone.

He does not know if the moment was real. It does not matter. The memory serves its purpose. It reminds him that he is not unique, not special, not an anomaly. He is a component. A functioning part of a functioning system. The weirdness is background radiation, unremarkable. He is not a man who was transformed into something else. He is exactly what the crevice required him to become, and the crevice, in turn, is exactly what he requires.

The crack has not widened in the last several thousand observation cycles. This is good. This is normal. He shifts his weight slightly, pressing his shoulder more firmly against the warm, humming stone, and settles in to watch. The beetle-things continue their slow procession. The worms unknot. The larvae pulse in time with the distant, colossal heartbeat. The flywheel spins. The machine idles. And the man who was not always a man crouches in the hot, dark, loud, close, embryonic space, and waits, and watches, and is, in his way, content.
