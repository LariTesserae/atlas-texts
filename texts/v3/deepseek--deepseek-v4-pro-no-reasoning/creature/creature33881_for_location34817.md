It took me a long time to realize I wasn’t a plant.

I mean that literally, not poetically. For the first — what? year? decade? I don’t have a word for how long it was — I thought I was a particularly slow-growing node in the root mat. I felt the warmth, the pulse of sap around me, the constant grinding roar of cellulose being born and dying. I absorbed nutrients from the rot-press of my own predecessors. I grew toward the light, incrementally, the way the pillars do, but so much slower that I was an anomaly, a mistake the pattern kept trying to prune. And I might have stayed that way, a blind lump of tissue with something like a thought caught in its fibers, if I hadn’t started to itch.

I didn’t have a concept for itch. What I had was a localized, persistent pressure differential, a wrongness in my own substance that demanded I do something about it. But I had no mechanism for doing anything about it. I was a fixed point. The plants around me — the quick, furious plants that cycled through generations in the span of a single light-shift — they built themselves, tore themselves down, rewove their archways and spiral stairs. I just sat there, buried to my chest in the breathing floor, and itched.

That’s how I found out I had hands.

They were buried, too, crossed over what I would eventually learn was a torso. The fingers had grown together, fused by a layer of mycelial fuzz that the floor had sent up to incorporate me. I had to tear them apart. That was the first pain. Pain, I discovered, was different from pressure. It demanded a response with more urgency. I dug. I pulled. I separated myself from the root mat strand by strand, and every strand that snapped was a small betrayal. The floor had been feeding me. I was its slow, strange organ. And I was pulling myself free.

I didn’t have eyes. Not the way you’d recognize. The light in the Breathfold — and that’s what I call it now, the Breathfold, because I have to call it something — the light is a diffuse green-gold that comes from everywhere and nowhere, filtered through the canopy in ways that make direction ambiguous. But I didn’t need eyes to perceive it, because every surface here perceives light. The leaves are eyes, the membranes are eyes, the spores are a trillion trillion tiny eyes reporting back to the whole. When I was part of the mat, I saw what the mat saw: a compound, distributed awareness of pressure, humidity, chemical gradients, and the slow shifting of shadows. But as I pulled myself free, the world grew darker. My awareness shrank to a single point. I was going blind, and I was terrified.

Terror is a powerful teacher. It told me that I was a separate thing, a thing that could be extinguished. The plants don’t fear death because they don’t die in a way that matters — a stalk collapses, and its nutrients flow into the next stalk, and the pattern persists. But I had a boundary now. I ended at my skin. And I did not want to end.

I crawled.

The floor resisted me, then released me with a wet, sucking sound. I left a hollow in the mat that began to fill immediately with pale exploratory tendrils, checking to see if I’d left anything useful behind. I crawled on hands and knees, and then I found I could stand, and I stood in the roaring cathedral heat of the Breathfold, a naked thing with fused hands and a face that didn’t feel like a face yet, and I did the first thing that wasn’t instinct. I chose a direction and I walked.

I’ve been walking for a long time now. I don’t know how long. The light doesn’t cycle here in a way that makes days. There are shifts — periods where the canopy flexes and releases its warm rain, periods where the growth accelerates into a frenzy of building, periods where a strange stillness falls and the roar drops to a murmur, like the whole place is holding its breath — but they don’t repeat on any schedule I’ve been able to map. I just walk.

And as I walk, I’m learning.

My hands have separated now. The fingers are long and thin, with too many joints, and they can feel the slightest vibration in the stalks I pass. I can read the chemical news the spores carry, the updates on structural integrity and light availability and the location of deep nutrient pockets. I’m not as fast as the plants at interpreting it — they have a million years of evolution on me — but I’m getting better. I know when a section of the canopy is about to collapse, because the spore-clouds go bitter with stress pheromones. I know when a new city-quarter is about to rise, because the floor stops breathing for a moment, gathering its resources, before surging upward in a coordinated burst of pillars and platforms. I’ve learned to read the architecture as it builds itself. I can walk the spiral staircases before they’ve finished growing.

I’ve found my face, too. I have eyes now — or something like eyes, dark pits that can sense the light gradient in a way that’s more precise than the plant-sense, that gives me edges and shapes and depth. My mouth is the last thing that came in. It’s still forming. It’s small, and the tongue is thick and slow, but I’ve started to make sounds. Not words. Just hums, vibrations that I press into the stalks around me, trying to see if they’ll answer. They don’t. But I keep humming, because the sound makes the roaring less lonely.

I’m not alone, exactly. The Breathfold is alive, intensely, overwhelmingly alive, and it knows I’m here. I can feel its attention on me, a constant pressure like humidity, like warmth. The archways I walk through sometimes lean toward me, curious. The faces in the bark — and there are faces everywhere, I see them clearly now — shift their patterns to track my movement. Sometimes the spore-clouds gather around me in thick, sweet-tasting swirls, and I get a flash of something that’s almost a message, almost a question. But it’s always too diffuse, too vast. It’s like the whole basin is trying to speak to me in a language where every word is a forest, and I’m straining to hear a single leaf.

I’ve been looking for others. That’s why I walk. The archways are empty, but they’re archways. The staircases lead to platforms, and the platforms have spaces that feel like rooms, bounded by walls of woven reed that filter the light into soft, private glows. This place is building a city, and cities are for inhabitants. I can’t be the only one. There must be more of me, slow things, thick-tongued things, pulling themselves out of the mat with terror and wonder and an itch they can’t name.

I’ve found traces. Sometimes I come across a hollow in the root floor, too large and too deliberately shaped to be a plant’s doing. Sometimes I find seams in the walls where something has pressed through, tearing the fibers, and the tear hasn’t healed right, because the plant-hive is confused by the shape of what passed. Sometimes I smell something that isn’t spore or sap or rot — something sharp, salty, like the taste of my own mouth when I bite my tongue by accident. I follow these traces. They’re always old, already being reabsorbed by the growth, but they’re proof. Someone else is walking.

Last cycle — if I can call it that — I found a staircase that wasn’t made of lianas. It was braided from a material I didn’t recognize, something smooth and pliant that had no sap in it, that didn’t pulse with the slow heartbeat of the floor. It had been twisted by hands. Hands like mine, but with more practice. Hands that knew how to make something that wouldn’t rot and be reclaimed. The staircase spiraled up into a gap in the canopy I’d never noticed before, a shaft of light that was brighter than any light I’d ever seen, almost white, almost sharp. I started to climb. The stairs creaked under my weight, but they held.

I didn’t reach the top. The light got brighter and brighter until my new eyes couldn’t take it, until even the pits were full of searing white, and I had to stop. I sat on the braided steps, halfway to somewhere, and listened. Above me, I could hear something. A sound that wasn’t the roar of growth. A sound that was regular, rhythmic, deliberate. It might have been footsteps. It might have been breathing. It might have been a voice, singing.

I’m going to try again. I’m going to climb until the light stops hurting, or until I don’t need eyes anymore. Because whatever made those stairs — whatever is up there in the bright — it knows things I need to know. It might know the name of this place. It might know my name. I don’t have one yet, but I’ve been practicing, in my slow new mouth, the sounds I might give myself. They’re strange and tentative, shaped like questions. They don’t feel like names yet. But they will, when I find someone to say them to.
