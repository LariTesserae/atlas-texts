You want to know what lives here? You're asking the wrong question. "Lives" implies a boundary, an inside and an outside, a thing that maintains itself against the press of everything else. That's not how it works in the Sink. But I'll try. I'll try to describe what I found, what found me, what was always there and only needed me to witness it.

It starts with the man. Except he isn't a man, and he isn't not a man. He's what happens when a human being stays too long in a place that doesn't recognize the distinction between self and surroundings.

I saw him on the third day, or what passed for day—the tarnished-silver sky doesn't brighten or dim, but I was keeping time by the slow throb of the not-plants, counting their pulses, and on the three hundredth pulse I noticed a shape that didn't dissolve when I turned to face it.

He was sitting on one of the low melted walls, the remains of a rectangle, and he was facing away from me. His posture was wrong. Not broken or slumped, but *approximate*, as if someone had described human sitting to a sculptor who had never seen a spine. The curve of his back was too smooth, the angle of his neck too gradual, and his shoulders—his shoulders continued downward into the wall itself, the boundary between flesh and stone gone hazy, indistinct, like a watercolor left in the rain.

He was wearing what might have been clothes once. Now they were just layers of the same fibrous translucence as the not-plants, spreading across his body in wiry tangles, pulsing faintly. The pulse matched the ground's rhythm. It matched everything's rhythm.

I approached him from the side, my footsteps making no sound beneath the roar, but he didn't turn. When I came around to face him, I understood why.

His face was eroding.

Not rotting. Not decaying. Eroding, the way the low structures eroded, the way the hillocks pushed up and wore flat in a breath. His features were a time-lapse of loss: ears smoothed to suggestion, nose reduced to a ridge, mouth a depression that occasionally sealed over entirely before reopening. His eyes were the worst. They were still there, still wet, still *aware*, but the lids had worn away along with the surrounding bone, leaving them exposed and unblinking, set into a face that had melted back until the skull itself was visible in patches, the bone leached and porous, weeping a thin slurry.

He was still alive. Or still present. Or still something.

When he spoke, it took me a long time to realize it was speech. The roar stole his words, but his lips—what remained of them—moved with the same rhythms I'd felt through the ground, the almost-language, the resonance that felt like meaning if you didn't listen too closely. And his voice, what I could catch of it, came from more than his mouth. It came from his chest, yes, but also from the wall he was merging with, from the ground around him, transmitted up through my feet in that bone-deep hum.

He was saying the same thing, over and over. I eventually made it out.

"...still here. still here. still here..."

A statement of fact. A surprise. A defiance.

There were others. I found them over the next several cycles—I'd stopped counting pulses by then, measuring time instead by the slow dissolution of my own sense of where I ended and the Sink began. It's hard to hold onto your edges here. The warm wet air works its way into your lungs and stays there, and after a while you stop being sure whether you're breathing it or it's breathing you. The ground's pulse syncs with your heartbeat until you can't tell which is driving which. The roar gets into the spaces between your thoughts and starts to sound like thinking.

The second one I found was younger. Or had been younger, when she still had an age. She was sunk into the saturated ground up to her waist, and below that there was no clear division—her body simply *became* the mat of not-quite-soil-not-quite-flesh, her legs dissolved into the same substance, her torso merging at the edges with the breathing plain. Her arms were still free, and she was using them to shape something, working the ground in front of her with slow, patient movements. She was building one of the rectangles. Stacking low walls from the slurry, pressing them into shape, watching them slump and melt and then reforming them. She'd been doing it for a long time. The plain around her was littered with her attempts, hundreds of them, all at various stages of being reabsorbed.

Her face was more intact than the man's, but her expression was the same: a distant focus, a concentration on something I couldn't see. When she noticed me—and she did notice, her eyes tracking my movement with a clarity the man's hadn't—she smiled. Not a warm smile. Not a sad smile. A smile of recognition, the smile of someone meeting a neighbor they've seen a thousand times before.

"You're new," she said. Her voice was clearer than the man's, less eroded. "You still have your name?"

I realized I hadn't thought about my name in—how long? I tried to remember it. The syllable was there, but it felt like a stone worn smooth, all the identifying marks rubbed away.

"Hold onto it," she said. "The name. Hold onto it as long as you can. It's the last thing that goes."

She went back to her building. As I watched, the wall she'd been shaping slumped and melted, and she started again, patient, unhurried, with the peace of someone who had accepted that the work would never be finished and had decided to do it anyway.

The third one found me.

I had stopped walking, stopped looking. I was lying on the ground, or the ground was lying on me—we were negotiating the terms. The mist from the plain's exhalations had soaked through my clothes, through my skin, and I could feel the slow pulse of the not-plants synchronizing with the pulse of my blood. It felt good. It felt like rest. The roar was no longer a pressure but a blanket, wrapping me in its rhythms, its almost-voices, its bone-deep hum.

A shape passed over me. One of the skimming things, the flashes of iridescence, but this one didn't skim. It stopped. It hovered. And then it spoke.

"Don't."

The voice was like the roar made articulate, structured, given teeth. It came from everywhere at once.

I sat up, or tried to. The ground was reluctant to release me; I felt it pull, then slowly, slowly rebound, remembering my weight after I'd gone.

The shape was not a skimming thing. It was a woman, or had been a woman, but she had become something that could move in and out of the ground like it was a suggestion rather than a barrier. She was only intermittently solid. One moment she was there, tall and thin and luminous, her skin the iridescence of the skimming things, her eyes deep as the darkness that wasn't night and wasn't space. The next moment she was a distortion in the air, a lensing of the tarnished sky, a presence that occupied no space but was absolutely, undeniably *there*.

"What is this place?" I asked. My voice sounded strange to me. It had been so long since I'd used it.

She laughed. The laugh was the roar, differentiated, tuned to a frequency that felt like irony.

"This is the Sink," she said. "You know that. You've been walking it for—how long now? How many pulses of the threadgrass? How many cycles of the sky-weave?"

I didn't know. I couldn't remember.

"It's the end," she said. "The absolute end. The place where everything goes when there's nowhere else to go. The last stage. The final configuration."

"Then why isn't it quiet?"

Her smile was the smile of someone who had asked that same question, once, and had spent an eternity learning the answer.

"Because the end isn't the end. That's the secret. That's what the ones who built the rectangles didn't understand. They thought erosion was a process that finished. They thought you could wear everything down to a single plane and then—stop. Rest. Nothing. But there's no such thing as nothing. Even the plane breathes. Even the sky weaves. Even the ground remembers."

She gestured around us, at the breathing plain, the seething sky, the scattered structures melting and reforming and melting again.

"What you're seeing is what happens when a world runs out of ways to be worn down and has to invent new ones. It's not destruction anymore. It's not creation. It's a loop, a cycle with no direction, a process that eats its own tail and finds it tastes like something new every time. The Sink is the final ecosystem. The ecosystem of eroding and un-eroding. The creatures here—"

"The man," I said. "The woman building the walls."

"The man," she agreed. "The woman. The others. They came here like you came here. Curious. Stubborn. Lost. They stayed too long. They let the ground breathe into them. They let their boundaries dissolve. And now they're part of the cycle. The man is still here, still here, still here. The woman is still building, still building, still building. They've found their loop, their rhythm, their place in the process. The Sink absorbed them, but it didn't destroy them. It just—included them. Made them native."

"And you?"

She flickered. For a moment I saw through her to the ribbons of vapor twisting in the sky, the occurrences packing the atmosphere, the turbulence extending upward into the darkness that was neither night nor space.

"I've been here longest," she said. "Long enough to learn the trick of it. You can't fight the Sink. You can't resist the eroding. But you can learn to move with it. You can become something that the process can't hold onto, something that slips between the cycles, something that passes through."

She reached out. Her hand was iridescence and distortion, and when it touched my face, I felt the roar run through me, the bone-deep hum, the rhythm of the threadgrass, the pulse of the ground.

"You have to decide," she said. "Now. Before the ground remembers your weight permanently. Before your name wears smooth. Before your face erodes. You can become like them—the man, the woman, the others, the slow and stubborn things that have found their loop and settled into it. The Sink will take you, but it will keep you. You'll be part of this, part of the breathing, part of the pulse, part of the almost-language that resonates through the ground. It's not death. It's not even change, not really. It's inclusion."

"Or?"

"Or you can come with me. I can teach you what I learned. How to be unsolid. How to move through the ground like a suggestion. How to ride the sky-weave instead of being crushed by it. How to exist in the Sink without being absorbed by it—a visitor, not a resident. It's harder. You'll never be still again. But you'll keep your name. You'll keep your face. You'll keep the boundary between yourself and everything else, even when everything else is trying its damnedest to dissolve it."

I looked out at the Sink. The roar was beginning to differentiate again, resolving into rhythms, into almost-voices, into something that was either language or the memory of language. The ground breathed beneath me. The sky seethed. In the distance, one of the low walled rectangles was more whole than it had been an hour ago, pushing up from the plane, rough edges emerging from smoothness, a hillock of geometry rising from the flatness before it slumped and melted and was reabsorbed.

I thought about the man, sitting on his melted wall, repeating his mantra to the tarnished sky. Still here. Still here. Still here. I thought about the woman, building her walls that would never last, patient and unhurried and at peace. I thought about the plain itself, breathing, exhaling moisture, remembering weights.

"What's your name?" I asked the flickering woman.

She smiled. Her smile was the roar, structured, given meaning. It was the sound of a world being unmade and remade simultaneously.

"I don't have one anymore," she said. "Names are for things with boundaries. I haven't had boundaries in a very long time. But I remember what I was. I was someone who chose to keep moving. Someone who chose the sky over the ground. Someone who decided that the end wasn't the end, and made that decision into a way of being."

She held out her hand again. This time, when I looked at it, I could see the ribbons of vapor twisting through it, the occurrences packing the atmosphere, the three-dimensional tapestry of turbulence she had learned to ride instead of being crushed by.

"Come with me," she said. "Or stay. But decide. The Sink is patient, but it's patient in the way of water wearing down stone. The longer you wait, the more of you it remembers. The more of you it includes."

I made my choice.

I'm not going to tell you what I chose. That's not the point. The point is that I chose, instead of letting the Sink choose for me. Whether I'm still walking the plain, sinking slowly into the breathing ground, feeling my boundaries blur and my name wear smooth—or whether I'm riding the sky-weave, unsolid and ever-moving, a distortion in the air, a lensing of the light—I made the choice. That's what matters. That's what the man on his melted wall forgot to do. That's what the woman building her rectangles remembered, and built into her endless, patient work.

The Sink is still there. It will always be there. It's the final configuration, the last ecosystem, the place where everything goes when there's nowhere else to go. It's packed with activity, saturated with presence, a maximum-density state where no gap exists between one phenomenon and the next. It is vast enough to contain weather systems that would swallow planets. It is turbulent beyond any metaphor for turbulence.

And it is full of people who stayed too long, and found their loop, and settled into the breathing, and became native.

If you ever find yourself at the edge of the Sink—if you ever feel that warm wet weight settle in your lungs, if you ever hear that roar resolve into rhythms and almost-voices and something that feels like language if you don't listen too closely—remember: you have to choose. Before the ground remembers your weight. Before your name wears smooth. Before your face erodes.

Or don't. Let the Sink absorb you. Become one of the slow stubborn things that pulse with the threadgrass and hum with the ground and sit on melted walls, watching the sky-weave with eroded eyes, still here, still here, still here.

Either way, you'll be answering the question of what lives in a place where life isn't supposed to be possible. Either way, you'll become part of the process. Either way, the Sink will include you.

It's very democratic that way.
