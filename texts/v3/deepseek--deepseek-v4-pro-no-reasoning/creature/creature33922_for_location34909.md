The people here don’t have a name for themselves that translates cleanly. The nearest gloss might be *the adjusted*, or perhaps *those who stay*. They are not a species, not exactly, though enough generations have passed in the fissure that the boundaries are blurring. Most began as baseline human stock—miners, geothermal engineers, desperate colonists from a failed terraforming effort two systems over—but no one arrives here without modifying themselves, and no one is born here without inheriting those modifications. The city itself demands it. The air is a slow poison to unaltered lungs, the chill a constant drain on unmodified metabolisms, and the soundscape—that endless, susurrant scratching of a billion clicker legs—would drive an unprepared nervous system into psychosis within weeks.

So they changed.

Walk the narrow, resin-lacquered gantries of the mid-city and you will see them. They are tall and thin, most of them, with a deliberate, almost prehensile economy of motion. Their bodies have been lengthened and de-bulked over successive gene-edits and surgical retrofits to better navigate the cramped verticality of their home. Long fingers, often with an extra joint or a subtle webbing of tactile cilia, hook casually into the knapped obsidian walls when they pause. Their feet are narrower than a baseline human’s, the toes more articulated, the soles padded with a tough, calloused tissue that grips the resin-coated mesh of the walkways without sound. They move like spiders, all joint-awareness and slow, continuous placement. Quick, jerky motions are a liability here, where a single misstep could mean a three-hundred-meter plummet or, worse, a stumble into a dense clicker colony, which would not attack—the clickers are not aggressive—but the sensation of a million tiny, dry bodies swarming up one’s leg is a trauma the community culture treats with something close to religious horror.

Their skin is pale. Not the pinkish-pale of sun-deprived humans, but a grayish, almost opalescent white, like the underbelly of a deep-sea fish. It’s a by-product of the melanin-suppression edits that allow them to synthesize vitamin D from the narrow spectrum of light emitted by the city’s bioluminescent and geothermal glow-grids. In the dim, bruised-purple light of the fissure, their skin picks up the iridescence of the obsidian, so that a person standing still against a wall can seem, for a moment, like a natural extrusion of the rock. It’s a camouflage that serves them well, though it was never intentionally designed. The first settlers simply found that those with paler skin could see better in the low light—less pigment scattering in the retina—and so the trait was encouraged, generation by generation.

Their faces are still recognizably human, but the eyes are not. The adjusted have large, dark-adapted eyes with a tapetum lucidum—a reflective layer behind the retina, like a cat’s. It gives their gaze a faint, unsettling glint in the dark, a quick flash of green or silver when they turn their heads. The irises are nearly black, opening to pupils that can dilate to almost the full width of the visible eye. In the brighter patches of the city, near the geothermal vents or the heat-lamp nurseries, they wear goggles of smoked resin. To be seen without one’s goggles in a bright zone is a sign of vulnerability, of intimacy, of being unguarded. Friends will sometimes remove their goggles in each other’s presence as a gesture of trust. Lovers do it as a matter of course. Enemies, never.

Their clothing is practical, close-fitting, and almost entirely silent. Multiple layers of soft, woven fabrics, often made from the spun and treated silk of a clicker-adjacent symbiote—a pale, wormlike creature that lives in the colony middens and produces a fiber stronger than spider-silk. Over this, they wear harnesses. Everyone in the city wears a harness, from the youngest child to the eldest bio-tinker. The harnesses are made of acid-etched metal and clicker-chitin plates, and they serve as both safety gear and toolbelt. Carabiners and quick-release tethers hang at every hip. A fall in the fissure is rarely fatal if you’re clipped in, which is why the adjusted have a cultural obsession with knots, hitches, and the quiet, constant clipping and unclipping of lines as they move. The sound of a carabiner snapping shut is, to them, a comforting, domestic noise—the click of safety, of home.

Their voices are soft. They speak in a register that sits just below the dominant frequency of the clicker colonies, a low, murmuring hum that doesn’t carry far. Their language is heavily inflected with gesture and touch; a conversation between two adjusted standing on a gantry will involve fingers tapping on wristbones, brief touches to the shoulder, a whole sub-vocal choreography of tactile emphasis. They have little use for shouting. The city is too dense, and sound travels strangely in the vertical fissure, echoing in unpredictable ways. A loud noise can trigger a clicker bloom-response, a sudden wave of defensive bioluminescence, which is harmless but disruptive, and the adjusted are, above all, a people who value non-disruption. Their society floats on a sea of delicate, interlocking rhythms—farming cycles, heat-engine maintenance, gas-scrubber rotations—and to shout is to risk throwing a stone into the intricate machinery of communal survival.

They are not a dour people, though. The stereotype of the grim, silent cave-dweller is a misreading. The adjusted have a deep and abiding love of pattern, of intricate detail, of small, beautiful things that reward close attention. Their art is all miniature: carvings on clicker-chitin pendants, intricate knot-work that records genealogies and personal histories in the twists of a single silk cord, musical instruments that play in the ultrasonic range, audible only to the adjusted ear and to the clickers themselves, who sometimes respond with synchronized light-shows. A popular form of entertainment is to sit on a viewing platform—a rare, open space cantilevered out over the abyss—and watch the clicker blooms. The farmers will seed a distant wall with a particular mineral salt, and the colonies will respond in cascading waves of cold-blue light, patterns that shift and pulse and die. The adjusted watch these displays in quiet, rapt attention, their reflective eyes glinting in the dark.

Their social structure is fluid, organized around function rather than family in the traditional sense. The smallest unit is the *shift-crew*, a group of six to twelve individuals who share a work cycle and a living space. Shift-crews are not necessarily related by blood, though they often become as close as siblings or spouses. A crew will typically specialize in one aspect of the city’s endless maintenance and harvest: some manage the clicker colonies, some the heat-engines, some the gas-scrubbers, some the volatile, ever-shifting architecture. Children are raised collectively, passed from crew to crew as they age, learning the full breadth of the city’s necessary skills. The concept of a nuclear family—two parents and their biological offspring living in a sealed unit—is considered vaguely archaic, even slightly sinister. To isolate a child with only two adults is, in the adjusted worldview, to deprive them of the full weave of communal knowledge. “It takes a fissure to raise a child” is a common saying.

They are, by necessity, excellent bio-tinkers and geologists. The clickers are their lifeblood, the source of food, chitin, silk, and a dozen other secondary products, but the clickers are also unpredictable, prone to sudden population crashes and equally sudden blooms that can overload a district’s infrastructure. Understanding the clickers means understanding the deep geology of the fissure, the shifting heat-plumes, the micro-seismic tremors, the chemical signatures in the CO2 seeps. Every adjusted grows up with an intuitive grasp of mineralogy, of the brittle, glassy properties of young obsidian, of the way a dyke intrusion can be predicted by a subtle change in the ambient vibration. They read the rock like sailors read the weather.

Their relationship with the clickers themselves is complex, almost reverential, but not sentimental. The clickers are not pets. They are not even quite livestock in the traditional sense. They are, in the adjusted cosmology, something closer to a *process*—a living, mineral-eating force that flows through the fissure, and from which the adjusted extract a careful, measured tithe. A clicker farmer does not so much “own” a colony as “tend” a stretch of wall, coaxing the creatures into productive patterns, monitoring their health, harvesting the excess population when it peaks. The farmers speak of the clickers in a soft, almost affectionate register, but there is no illusion of reciprocity. The clickers are not intelligent. They are simply vast, and ancient, and essential. Every adjusted knows that if the clickers were to vanish, the city would die within a month.

Death is common here, but it is rarely sudden. The most frequent killers are respiratory acidosis from a CO2 seep that overwhelms a scrubber, or slow malnutrition from a clicker colony that collapses faster than the food reserves can compensate, or a fall—not the plummet itself, but the slow, stranded death of someone dangling from a tether in a remote shaft, too injured to climb, too quiet to shout. The adjusted do not fear death, but they fear dying badly, dying alone in the dark, listening to the clickers scratch as they wait for a rescue that doesn’t come. Their funeral rites are simple: the body is given to a special, bio-engineered clicker strain that strips it to clean bone in less than an hour, and the bones are then carved into tools or jewelry or architectural inlays. To be built into the city itself is the highest honor. The elders—those few who survive into their sixties or seventies—sometimes choose a “living integration,” having their bones etched and set into a public wall while they are still alive, so that they can watch the city grow around them for their remaining years.

The children are quiet, pale, long-fingered, with eyes that already glint in the dark. They learn to tie their first knot before they learn to speak in full sentences. They are given tiny, blunt-carabiner toys to play with, and their first independent journey—a rite of passage around the age of eight—is to climb a twenty-meter vertical shaft, alone, clipped in, with no adult assistance. They return from this ordeal changed, or at least the culture says they do. They return with a new stillness in their eyes.

And all of this—the quiet voices, the clicking carabiners, the pale skin, the dark-adapted eyes, the endless, gentle scratch of the clickers—stitches together into a single, cohesive way of being. The adjusted are not unhappy. They are not stoic, not resigned, not grimly enduring a harsh existence. They are, in their own strange way, content. They live in a world that makes sense to them, where every sound has meaning, where every shift in the rock is a piece of a story they know how to read, where the constant, seething life of the clickers is a comfort rather than a threat. The fissure is a wound in the planet, yes, but it is also a cradle. And the adjusted, suspended in their vertical city, wrapped in their harnesses and their quiet, feel, more often than not, held.
