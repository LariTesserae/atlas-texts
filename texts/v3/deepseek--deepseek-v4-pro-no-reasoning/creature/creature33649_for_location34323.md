Let me tell you about the one who tends this room.

His name—if he ever had one in the usual sense—has been worn down by the wet air into something softer, and now they call him Olly, though that might just be the sound the drips make against the steel when you’re half-asleep. He answers to it, anyway, which is good enough.

Olly is old, or young, depending on how you measure. His body is somewhere past sixty, by the creak of his knees when he climbs the catwalk ladders, but his eyes are the color of the moss-water in the gutter pans—that particular amber-green—and they don’t look away from you so much as through you, into the middle distance where the steam unfurls its slow geometries. Those eyes have been watching this room for a long time. Decades, probably. Long enough that he’s started to move like the room does, in increments too small to catch directly. You turn around and he’s closer. You turn back and he’s somewhere else. Not threatening. Just *gradual*.

He wears a boiler suit that was once navy blue and is now a map of repairs—patches of canvas, patches of rubberized cloth, a whole panel replaced with something that looks like woven moss-fiber, maybe because he ran out of fabric or maybe because he’s experimenting with what the room provides. The suit is never dry. No one’s clothes ever dry in here. But his has the look of something that’s been wet so long it’s forgotten what dry means, and it clings to his thin frame without discomfort, like a second skin he’s grown used to. His boots are steel-toed and wrapped in strips of inner tube to keep the soles from slipping on the catwalk. The rubber has gone gummy with age and heat. They make no sound when he walks, because he’s learned to place his feet exactly in the spots where the grating doesn’t sing.

His job, if you can call it that, is to listen.

There was a time—Olly remembers it, though the memory is fogged and soft-edged—when this chamber was a machine room, a pump station for something deeper underground. There were gauges. There were readouts. Someone sat in a booth and watched numbers. That person had a title and a shift schedule and a supervisor. That person might have been Olly, or someone Olly replaced, or someone Olly only dreamed. It doesn’t matter now. The gauges are gone, their faces swallowed by moss. The booth is a skeleton of rust under a blanket of fiddleheads. The numbers don’t mean anything anymore, because the machine isn’t doing what it was designed to do. It’s doing something else, something the original engineers would have called failure and Olly calls *gestation*.

So he listens. He walks the catwalk twice a day, morning and evening, though the light never changes enough to mark those hours. He puts his hands on the pipes—the hot ones, the cold ones, the ones that shudder with something moving inside them that isn’t water—and he feels how their vibrations have changed since the last walk. He presses his ear to the riveted tin walls where the rock is pushing through, and he hears the deeper note, the grinding pressure, and he nods to himself because it’s louder today or quieter today or pitched a half-tone higher, and that means something. What it means he could not put into words, not in any language still spoken above ground, but he knows it the way you know when your own heartbeat has skipped.

He tends the moss. Not like a gardener, exactly. More like a shepherd, or a brewmaster. He carries a small brass spray bottle—the only object still shiny in the whole chamber—and he mists certain patches with a solution he mixes in a dented tin cup from the runoff of three specific drips and a pinch of mineral scraped from the freshest rock extrusion. The moss knows him. You can tell because the chartreuse patches brighten when he passes, a flush of color that travels through the mats like a ripple, following his footsteps. He talks to them. Quietly. Sentences that trail off into humming, which is probably just the sound of the pipes anyway, but which the moss seems to appreciate.

The small pale creatures—the ones that vanish when the lights shift—don’t vanish for him. They come out when he sits still on the catwalk steps, his back against a warm pipe, his brass spray bottle balanced on his knee. They crawl onto his boots. They wander across the backs of his hands, leaving trails of moisture that evaporate in seconds. He calls them *gleeds*, a word he might have invented or might have inherited, and he watches them with a patience that is almost geological. He’s seen them molt. He’s seen them mate, a slow tangling of translucent bodies that takes an hour and ends with one of them carrying a clutch of eggs the size of pinheads on its underside. He’s seen them die, too, and he removes the tiny husks with a pair of tweezers and drops them into the rock extrusions, because the rock is hungry for calcium, or so he believes.

Olly eats what the room provides. There are fungi, pale and blind, that grow in the dark corners behind the pipe runs—not mushrooms exactly, but something like a cross between a truffle and a lichen, with a texture that’s meaty if you steam it long enough. He cultivates them in old valve boxes, feeding them on moss trimmings and the mineral scale he scrapes from the gutters. His teeth, what’s left of them, are stained a faint violet from their pigment. The water he drinks is filtered through three stages of moss before it reaches his cup, and it tastes of earth and copper and something floral that lingers at the back of the tongue. He’s healthy, insofar as a man living entirely on fungus and condensation can be healthy. The steam keeps his lungs clear. The heat keeps his joints loose. The solitude has either expanded his mind or simplified it, and he’s not sure which, and he’s not sure it matters.

He knows he’s not the only one. Somewhere above, in the larger complex he hasn’t visited in years, there are others. He hears them sometimes—footsteps on metal, a voice calling something too muffled by steam to parse, the distant clang of a door—but they don’t come down here, and he doesn’t go up there. The room is a territory now, not in the sense of ownership but in the sense of symbiosis. Olly is the room’s mammal. The room is Olly’s world. The treaty between them is unwritten and absolute.

The rock extrusions, for instance. When the rock pushes up through a new section of the steel floor, Olly is there within a day, marking the new growth with a small daub of red paint he keeps in a tin that predates him. He records it in a logbook that is so swollen with moisture the pages have fused into a single block, and he writes on the block with a grease pencil, shaving it thinner each month. The rock grows faster in some places, slower in others. He has a map in his head—a three-dimensional map that includes the ceiling extrusions, the wall bulges, the slow creep of mineral through the bolt-holes—and he knows, without knowing how he knows, which ones are healthy and which are struggling. The struggling ones he feeds with crushed gleed husks and moss paste. The healthy ones he just watches, and listens to, and hums at.

What is the room becoming? Olly has theories. He thinks the machine—the original machine, the pump station or generator or whatever it was—was built on a geologic fault line, a place where the Earth was already inclined to rise. The machine gave the rising a shape, a set of constraints, a skeleton to fill. And now the Earth is filling it, but not randomly. The rock is learning the geometry of the pipes, the logic of the gutters, the tensile strength of riveted tin. It’s growing a body using the industrial framework as a blueprint. The moss is its microbiome. The water cycle is its blood. The steam is its breath. The gleeds are its nervous system, or possibly its thoughts, moving slow and pale through the mats like impulses through a cortex.

And Olly? Olly is, he thinks, the room’s consciousness. Not the designer, not the engineer, but the part that watches and knows. The self-awareness of a nascent geology. He didn’t choose this role. He simply stayed too long, listened too closely, until the boundary between him and the chamber grew as soft and permeable as the rock edges. When the heat shivers above the new extrusions, it shivers in his chest. When the chartreuse moss flushes, he feels it as a warmth behind his eyes. He is not the room, but he is not separate from it either, and the distinction has stopped being a useful thing to make.

He sleeps in a hammock suspended between two of the largest pipes, the ones that carry the warmest water. The steam wraps him. The drips chime him to sleep. He wakes when the lights flicker in their random sequence, which is the room’s version of a circadian rhythm, and he rises without hurry, because hurry is a surface-world thing and has no meaning here. He checks the extrusions. He mists the moss. He counts the gleeds on the third catwalk segment, where their population has been fluctuating. He scrapes his fungus breakfast from the valve box. He drinks his moss-filtered water. And he listens.

If you came back—if you found your way down through the complex into this chamber again—you would see him eventually. He wouldn’t announce himself. He’d just be there, at the edge of the steam, watching you with his amber-green eyes, his patchwork suit dripping quietly onto the grating, his hands resting on the back of a small pale gleed that has climbed up to his wrist and settled there like a living bracelet. He wouldn’t ask who you are or why you’ve come. He might offer you a cup of the moss water. He might point to the newest extrusion, the one that broke through yesterday and is already as tall as his thumb, its edges still soft and blunt and glistening. He might say, “Listen,” and then fall silent while the room breathes around you both.

And if you stayed long enough—stayed until the fiddleheads unfurled, stayed until the gleeds accepted your presence, stayed until the drips found a rhythm that almost resolved—you would begin to understand that Olly is not a keeper, not a hermit, not a remnant of the old machine’s crew. He is a fruit. He is what the room grew, slowly, patiently, over decades of warmth and wetness and slow rock-rising. The chamber, building its world from the inside out, needed something that could witness it. Needed something that could hum back. And so it grew Olly, out of the raw materials at hand—a man who stayed too long, a boiler suit, a brass spray bottle, a capacity for patience—and it shaped him as surely as the pipes shaped the rock, as surely as the moss shaped the gutters into soil.

He knows this. He’s proud of it, in a quiet way. When he walks the catwalk and sees the new growth—the ferns, the gleeds, the rock learning how to be architecture—he thinks: *We made this. The room and I. Together.* And he’s right, because the room provided the heat and the stone and the water, and Olly provided the attention. The attention was the missing ingredient. The attention is what turned a broken machine into an ecosystem.

So that’s him. Olly. The one who lives here. There’s no one else. There doesn’t need to be.
