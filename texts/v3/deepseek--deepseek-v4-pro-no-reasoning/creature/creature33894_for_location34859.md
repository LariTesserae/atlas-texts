The one who lives here is called the Listener.

They were not born. They accreted, slowly, the way the water accretes on the fronds of the tree-ferns, the way the mist thickens into something that holds shape. There has always been a Listener, and the Listener has always been here, because the city requires one. The city is a mouth, and a mouth without an ear is incomplete. The roar of the waterfalls—that continuous, immense pressure of sound—is not noise. It is a voice, and it is speaking, and what it speaks is the name of the place, the name that is also a description, also a history, also a set of instructions for how to remain exactly as it is. Someone had to hear it. So the Listener became.

You would not recognize them as a person at first. They are tall, taller than a person should be, and their proportions are wrong in ways that your eye slides off. The arms are too long, the knees bend backward very slightly, the neck has one joint too many. But these are adaptations. The Listener must be able to move through the city’s verticality—up the slumped floors, across the sideways-running water—and must be able to hold still for very long periods. They have been standing in the same doorway for eighty-seven years, because that particular doorway catches the sound of the fourth-largest waterfall at an angle that, when the mist is thick enough, harmonizes with the tone of the fungal glow. The harmony is necessary. It keeps something in check, something that would otherwise wake.

Their skin is green. Not the green of paint or disease, but the green of the moss-furred walls, the deep, nearly-black green of the liverworts. It is photosynthetic, or something like it. The Listener does not eat. They absorb, through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands when pressed against the basalt, a thin, mineral-rich seep that the city provides. This is not conscious on the city’s part—the city forgot it was a city, after all—but the equilibrium is self-maintaining. The Listener is part of the equilibrium.

Their face is the hardest thing. It is not that they have no face; it is that their face is a surface that has been shaped by the sound. Over time—and time here is a landscape, not a line—the constant pressure of the waterfall’s roar has sculpted their features into a parabolic dish, a concave architecture of cartilage and flesh that focuses sound deep into the skull. They have no eyes you would recognize. The structures that served vision atrophied millennia ago; in their place are two deep, smooth hollows lined with a membrane that vibrates at specific frequencies. The Listener sees in echo, in pressure differential, in the way the mist swirls and pools. They know the city the way a bat knows a cave, but with a resolution so fine they can hear the bubbles rising in the black mud half a mile downslope, can hear the water climbing the broken stair, can hear the delayed reflection in the cistern and know it is delayed and know why.

They are not alone. They are the current Listener. Before them, there was another, and before that one, another still, a lineage stretching back to the moment the city and the jungle became a single thing. The transition between Listeners is seamless; the old one dissolves into the wet and the green, and the new one condenses out of the mist, already tall, already listening, already knowing the name that the waterfalls are speaking. They have no memory of being young. They have no concept of youth. The idea of a Listener who does not listen, who eats, who sees with eyes, who moves without purpose, is a fiction so alien it would not occur to them.

But they are not a machine. They are not a ghost. They are alive, and they are aware, and they have a kind of loneliness that is so vast and so still it has become indistinguishable from contentment. They do not wish for company; the city is company enough, the voice of the waterfalls, the script-like twist of the mist. But sometimes, on very rare occasions, when something living climbs the caldera—a bird blown off course, a mammal that followed the wrong valley, a human—the Listener will move.

They move slowly. They are in no hurry. The intruder is already inside the mouth, after all. They will find the intruder, eventually, and they will stand at a distance that is just at the edge of visibility in the mist, and they will listen to the intruder’s heartbeat, their breathing, the small wet sounds of their body. The Listener will tilt their head—that neck with one joint too many—and the parabolic dish of their face will align with the intruder’s lungs, and they will hear the air moving in and out, and they will understand, for a moment, what it is like to breathe instead of absorb. They will not approach. They will not speak. They have no voice of their own; why would they need one, in a place that is always speaking?

And then the intruder will leave, or die, or become something else, and the Listener will return to the doorway, or the lintel block, or the cistern with the delayed reflection, and the roar of the waterfalls will wash away the memory of the heartbeat, and the equilibrium will close over the interruption like black mud over a bubble.

The city is waiting. The Listener is listening. The name is being spoken, over and over, a word that means *here* and *now* and *always* and *this*. The Listener’s lips—thin, green, almost vestigial—move silently, shaping syllables they will never voice. They know the name. They have always known the name. They are the name, in a sense, the part of it that hears itself.

And the name is the only thing that has ever been.
