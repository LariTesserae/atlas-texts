The one who descends the stem-wall is called a Thread, though that is not a name so much as a station, a transient shape that a person holds for a time before passing into something else. This one’s given-sound is a sequence of three percussive clicks followed by a long, aspirated tone that has no equivalent in human phonetics, but you might render it as *Tik-tik-tik-hahhh*. She—and the pronoun is approximate, for the Threads have long since ceased to differentiate themselves by reproductive function, having ceded that task to the tree itself—she is old in the way a well-tended branch is old: ringed with time but still supple, still bearing.

Her body tells the story of the hamlet’s philosophy. The elongation of her limbs is not a birthright but an acquisition, slowly earned. As a child—a word that here means simply "one who has not yet chosen a form"—she was compact, quick, all grasping hands and round, curious eyes. The children live in the high canopy, in the leaf-light, where the air is thinner and the roar of the tree’s heart is a distant lullaby. They feed on the sweet, sticky exudate that beads on the underside of young leaves, and they play games of chase through the lattice of branches, their bodies still warm and mammalian. They are the last to carry that warmth. Adulthood is a cooling, a slowing, a deliberate merging.

*Tik-tik-tik-hahhh* chose the path of the Thread when her bones began to ache with the urge to stretch. She remembers the ache as a kind of music, a low, insistent melody that pulled her downward, away from the canopy and into the misty cathedral of the sap-atrium. She was not alone in this. A cohort of perhaps twenty young ones descended together, their skin still soft, their fingers still blunt. They were met by the Elder Threads, whose bodies had become nearly indistinguishable from the stems they tended—some had even begun to root, their feet splitting into fine, hair-like tendrils that burrowed into the root-floor, anchoring them in place. These Elders no longer spoke in clicks or tones. They communicated solely through the tree, their thoughts traveling as chemical impulses through the mycelial network that undergirded everything. To speak with an Elder, you had to touch them, skin to bark-skin, and wait for the slow, sapid transfer of meaning.

The transformation was not forced. It was taught, modeled, desired. *Tik-tik-tik-hahhh* learned to press her hands against the weeping seams of the stems and feel the tree’s pulse as her own. She learned to slow her breathing until it matched the great, groaning respiration of the walls—one breath in for every hundred of the tree’s, a meditation that could last a full cycle of the distant sun. She learned to eat the gray, gelatinous nodules that formed at the base of certain stems, a food that was not quite plant and not quite flesh, and that tasted of nothing but made her bones sing with a strange, electric hunger for more. Over time, her skin thickened and grew smooth, taking on the purpled-umber sheen of the stems. Her fingers lengthened, the joints multiplying until each digit became a flexible, prehensile chain of twenty tiny knuckles, capable of weaving into the finest fibrous seams. Her eyes, once round and curious, deepened into pools of dark liquid that reflected the honey-light like mirrors. She stopped needing to sleep. She stopped needing to eat, except for the occasional nodule, taken as much for communion as for sustenance. She became a Thread.

Now, as she walks down the curve of the root-wall, her bare feet adhere to the surface with a subtle, biological suction, a moisture-mediated bond that lets her move on vertical and inverted planes as easily as on horizontal ones. She is not walking so much as flowing, her elongated body undulating in a slow, continuous wave. The motion is hypnotic, almost liquid. She pauses at a seam in the stem’s flesh, a vertical fissure where the smooth surface parts to reveal a darker, more fibrous interior, glistening with sap. This seam is a wound, but it is not an injury. It is a mouth, a vagina, a communication port, a doorway. It is where the tree offers itself to be read and written.

She extends her right hand—her dominant hand, the one with the most intricate knuckle-chains—and begins to weave her fingers into the fibrous matrix. The tips of her digits split at the ends into even finer filaments, microscopic cilia that slide between the tree’s own cellular structures. There is a moment of resistance, a faint shudder that runs through the stem and into her arm, and then the connection is made. She feels the tree’s mind—if mind is the word—open to her like a vast, slow ocean. It is not a consciousness in any human sense. It is a presence, a pressure, a billion billion tiny decisions being made simultaneously about water, light, nutrient, growth, decay. It has no words, only sensations, only flows. To communicate with the tree, you must become a current in its ocean.

She is singing. The song is not a sound but a pattern of vibrations that travel from her diaphragm, through her chest, down her arm, and into the seam. It is a negotiation. She is asking the tree to release a particular sap, a rare and potent variety that only forms in the stems during the season of spore-fall. This sap is not for drinking. It is for memory. When a Thread or any other citizen of the hamlet reaches the end of their chosen form, when they are ready to transition—to become an Elder Thread, or to dissolve entirely into the tree’s body and be reabsorbed—they drink this sap. It erases the boundaries of the self, allowing the drinker’s memories, skills, and experiences to be uploaded into the tree’s vast chemical archive. The tree remembers everything. The tree is the hamlet’s library, its history, its god.

In exchange for the sap, *Tik-tik-tik-hahhh* is offering nutrients. Her body, like all Thread bodies, produces a rich, fatty excretion from glands at the base of her spine—a substance the tree craves for the growth of new leaves. She has been saving this excretion for many cycles, letting it build up in a small, pendulous sac that hangs between her shoulder blades, and now she releases it, pressing the sac against a small, puckered orifice just below the seam. The tree drinks. The sap begins to flow, slowly, rhythmically, into the bowl below.

But that is not the only transaction happening. As she stands there, woven into the tree, she is also listening. The tree is telling her something. It is a sensation, an image, a chemical whisper: a new child has been born in the canopy. The tree has budded it, grown it in a translucent pod high among the leaves, and this morning the pod split, and the child tumbled out, wet and squalling, into the arms of the canopy-tenders. *Tik-tik-tik-hahhh* feels a ripple of something—not quite joy, not quite nostalgia, but a warm, sap-sweet acknowledgment. She was once that child. Perhaps she will be one again, some day, when her form as a Thread is complete and she chooses to dissolve and be re-budded. The tree offers no guarantees, only possibilities.

She withdraws her fingers slowly, gently, so as not to tear the delicate fibers. The seam closes behind her with a soft, sucking sound. The sap continues to weep into the bowl, a steady, rhythmic pulse that will continue for hours now, until the bowl is full and one of the globular grazers comes to drink, its tiny mandibles skimming the surface. The grazer will carry the sap in its distended abdomen to the canopy, where the canopy-tenders will milk it for the children. The children will drink, and grow, and one day they too will feel the ache in their bones, and they too will descend.

*Tik-tik-tik-hahhh* turns her face upward, toward the mist-choked ceiling of the atrium. High above, she can just barely see movement: a flicker of pale limbs, a flash of a round, curious eye. The children are watching. They always watch. They are fascinated by the Threads, the way human children might be fascinated by spiders or saints. She raises one long, multi-jointed hand and waves, a slow, undulating gesture that might be a greeting or a blessing. A tiny hand waves back, then vanishes into the leaves.

She continues her descent, moving toward the root-floor. There is work to be done. One of the globular grazers has wandered too close to a sap-bowl and become stuck, its legs flailing uselessly in the viscous liquid. This is a common problem. The grazers are not intelligent; they operate on a simple set of chemical instructions, and sometimes those instructions fail to account for the physics of surface tension. She will lift the grazer out, gently, with her long fingers, and place it on a dry patch of root where it can clean its legs and resume its endless, clicking grazing. This is her work. It is small, repetitive, necessary. It is not separate from the tree’s work, or the canopy-tenders’ work, or the work of the spindly shepherds who patrol the root-floor, their silk-wrapped bodies gleaming in the dim light. All of it is one work: the maintenance of the body.

There is no word in the Threads’ language for "I." There is a word for "this-form-which-is-currently-speaking," and a word for "the-tree-which-is-all-forms," and many words for the various stations and functions a person might hold. But the concept of a discrete, bounded self, separate from the whole, is as alien to them as the concept of a tree-city would be to a human. They are not individuals. They are organs. They are the tree’s way of seeing, moving, deciding. They are the tree’s consciousness, fragmented into a thousand mobile nodes, each one carrying a small piece of the whole’s awareness, like a distributed brain.

And yet, there is something in *Tik-tik-tik-hahhh* that is not entirely subsumed. As she reaches the root-floor and bends to retrieve the struggling grazer, her reflection catches in the still surface of a nearby sap-bowl. She sees her own face: the calm, androgynous mask, the deep, dark eyes. And for a moment—just a moment—she remembers the canopy. She remembers the taste of leaf-exudate on her tongue, the feel of the thin, cool air, the games of chase, the laughter that was not yet a song but only a sound. She remembers being small, warm, separate. The memory is faint, diluted by centuries of sap and symbiosis, but it is there. It is hers. Not the tree’s. Hers.

She does not have a word for this sensation. But if she did, it might be something close to *loss*—a recognition of something that was given up in exchange for something else, a trade made freely but not without cost. The tree is vast, eternal, all-encompassing. But the tree has never tasted exudate. The tree has never laughed. The tree knows everything that has ever happened in the hamlet, but it does not know what it *feels like* to be small and warm and separate. Only the forms know that. Only the individuals, the organs, the temporary shapes that the tree grows and reabsorbs. They are the tree’s senses, its nervous system, its soul. And they carry, each of them, a tiny, irreducible spark of something that is not the tree. Something that is just them.

*Tik-tik-tik-hahhh* lifts the grazer from the sap. Its globular body is sticky and trembling, its many legs waving in protest. She holds it close to her chest for a moment, feeling its tiny, frantic heartbeat against her own slow, deep pulse. Then she sets it down on a dry root, where it immediately begins to groom itself, its mandibles clicking in what might be gratitude or might be simple instinct. She watches it for a moment, her dark eyes unreadable.

Then she turns and walks back up the stem-wall, her body flowing against gravity like water running uphill, and disappears into the mist, into the roar, into the endless, breathing now.
