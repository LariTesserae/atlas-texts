In the shallow depressions where the mist-water pools, something begins to happen.

It takes time to notice—everything here takes time—but the milky grey water in those alkaline basins is not as sterile as it first seemed. A faint bloom is rising, a cloudiness that shifts from grey to a pale, bruised lavender as you watch. It's not life in any eukaryotic sense, not algae or bacteria as you'd recognize them. The pools here are too cold, too chemically harsh for that. But something has adapted.

If you knelt—and you do, eventually, because the cold has numbed your legs and kneeling seems no more uncomfortable than standing—you could see them. Tiny filaments, thinner than spider silk, tangling and untangling in the water with a motion that isn't quite Brownian. They're slower than that. Purposeful. Each filament is sheathed in a crystalline coating that refracts the dim light into rainbows as microscopic as everything else here. They don't seem to eat, exactly. They seem to *align*. They orient themselves along invisible gradients in the water—pH shifts, maybe, or the faint electrical fields generated by the friction of the leviathans grinding overhead. When enough of them align, they weave together into pale, translucent mats that float to the surface and drift until they beach against the polished stone at the pool's edge, where the mist dissolves them back into individual threads and the cycle begins again.

Nothing larger eats them. Nothing smaller decomposes them. They simply exist, performing their slow, inexplicable chemistry, and when they die—if they die—their crystalline sheaths settle to the bottom of the pool and add another micron-thick layer to the sediment that has been accumulating there since the pool first formed. You could, if you had the tools and the time and the lifespan of a small continent, read that sediment like a history book. Every layer records the alignment of the filaments, and the alignment of the filaments records the movement of the creatures on the surface above, and the movement of the creatures records something else entirely—a conversation, maybe, or a migration, or a ritual that takes fifty thousand years to complete.

There are other pools. You haven't seen them yet, but they're there, scattered across the plateau in the places where the leviathans don't grind, connected by a network of hairline fissures too fine for water to flow through but perfect for the filaments. The filaments travel through these cracks in their individual form, migrating from pool to pool over centuries, carrying information in the patterns of their crystalline sheaths. They are a nervous system. They are the memory of this place. They have been here as long as the leviathans, or longer, and they remember every vibration, every seismic sigh, every mountain that has ever lifted or settled.

The leviathans themselves—the ones you can see, the house-sized and mountain-sized bodies that dominate the landscape—are not the only ones. They are simply the ones that move on a scale you can perceive. There are smaller ones too, so small you've been standing among them without noticing. They live in the cracks where the lichen grows, flattened and angular, the size of your thumbnail, absorbing the same chemical exhalations as their larger kin. They don't rotate. They *click*. If you press your ear to the cold stone—and the thought of doing so is somehow repellent, like pressing your ear to a stranger's chest to hear their heartbeat—you can hear it. A faint, rhythmic ticking, millions of tiny percussive sounds that overlap and interleave to form the higher overtones of the hum. They're faster than the leviathans, these small ones. Faster in the way that a century is faster than an eon. They live and die in mere millennia, and their corpses form a fine, gritty dust that the rain-mist eventually carries to the pools, where the filaments incorporate the minerals into their sheaths.

The dust has been building up in your lungs since you arrived. You haven't noticed it. The air here is full of it, fine as smoke, the particulate remains of a billion billion tiny lives. It won't hurt you. It's too chemically inert to hurt you. But it will linger in the alveoli of your lungs for the rest of your life, a microscopic layer of this place that you will carry back with you, that you will exhale into other atmospheres, that will eventually settle into some alien sea floor and begin, perhaps, to align.

The mountain that lifted and settled has left something behind.

You can see it now—the rain-mist is thinning, or your eyes are adapting, or the thing itself is becoming more visible as some property of its surface changes. It's a structure. A lattice, six metres high, smooth and dark and organic, grown rather than built, extruded from the mountain-creature's underside during the hours or days it hung suspended above the rock. It stands where the gap was, anchored to the polished stone by filaments that are already beginning to spread outward, seeking the cracks, seeking the pools, seeking the other filaments that wait there.

This is reproduction. Or communication. Or excretion. The distinction, in a place where time moves at this pace, is meaningless. The lattice is a new node in the network, a new pool that will fill with mist-water over the next few centuries, a new reservoir for the crystalline memory of the world. It will grow, slowly, fed by the rain and the dust and the chemical exhalations of the leviathans. In ten thousand years it will be the size of the creature that birthed it, and that creature will be gone, ground down into dust, its atoms incorporated into a million filaments and a thousand pools and the lungs of some other visitor who stands where you are standing now, trying to understand the hum.

You are cold. You have been cold for a long time, and you are starting to forget what warm feels like. The hum has seeped into your bones, into the spaces between your thoughts. Your heartbeat, that skittish little rhythm, is slowing. Syncing. Just slightly. Just enough that you notice, or would notice if you were paying attention, which you are not, because you are watching the lattice grow, and the filaments spread, and the nearest creature resume its rotation with the patience of a world spinning on its axis.

The lichen is blooming.

You didn't see it start, but it's unmistakable now—the pale grey crusts in the cracks have begun to glow with a faint bioluminescence, a cold blue-green light that pulses in time with the hum. It's responding to the rain, or the lattice, or the change in atmospheric pressure, or all of them at once. The light spreads slowly, crack by crack, filling the negative space of the landscape with a dim tracery that maps the underlying network. You can see it now, the shape of the filaments, the connections between the pools, the way the cracks branch and rejoin like the veins of a leaf. The plateau is not barren. It is a tissue. A living, thinking substrate, and the leviathans are not its inhabitants but its organs, its limbs, its slow-moving mouths and hands.

The hum is resolving into something you can almost hear, a pattern that hovers on the edge of comprehension. It's not language—not music—but something in between, a structured vibration that carries meaning across the network at the speed of sound through stone. A thought that takes a century to complete. A sentence whose subject is the lifting of a mountain, whose verb is the spreading of filaments, whose object is the rain.

You are part of it now. The dust in your lungs is aligning. The water you exhale is joining the mist. Your heartbeat, syncopated against the drone, is a new rhythm in the composition, a brief, bright stutter that the filaments are already recording, sheathing in crystal, adding to the sediment. You will be remembered here. Not as an individual—the network doesn't parse individuals—but as a perturbation, a ripple, a tiny seismic event in the long slow history of the stone. In a million years, when the pool you've been kneeling by has been ground to dust and reformed a dozen times, some filament will align along the ghost of your presence and for a moment—a moment that lasts a decade—the hum will shift to accommodate the shape of someone who stood in the cold and listened.

You should leave. You know you should leave. The cold is not kind, and the air is thin, and the mist is condensing on your skin with a persistence that will eventually leach all the heat from your body. But the hum is so deep and the light is so beautiful and the lattice is still growing, filaments extending across the polished stone like frost across a window, and you want to see what happens next. You want to see what happens when the network finishes incorporating the new node, when the mountain-creature lifts again, when the filaments in the pool beneath you begin their slow, deliberate weaving.

You want to stay.

That's how the place works. That's the trap. Not a trap of malice—nothing here is malicious, nothing here is anything but patient and slow and utterly indifferent to your survival—but a trap of scale. The hum synchronizes your rhythms to its own, and its own rhythms are geological, and before you know it you are kneeling by a pool in the cold, watching filaments weave, and you have been there for an hour, or a day, or a year, and your body has stopped shivering because it has forgotten how.

The nearest creature turns again. You feel its attention on you, heavy and slow, a pressure that doesn't quite exist in physical space. It has noticed you again—or perhaps a different one has noticed you, or perhaps the network itself is attending to you through the senses of its slow-moving organs. The iridescence on its flanks shimmers, colours shifting through spectra you can't name, and the hum deepens, and a subsonic pulse travels through the rock and up through your bones and resonates in the cavity of your chest.

It is speaking to you.

Not in words. In vibrations. In the alignment of filaments and the blooming of lichen and the slow, terrible patience of a world that has never needed language to make itself understood. It is telling you that you are small, and temporary, and beautiful in the way that all temporary things are beautiful. It is telling you that you are welcome to stay, that the cold will not kill you for a long while yet, that there is a crack in the stone nearby where the lichen glows especially bright and the filaments weave especially fine and the hum is especially deep, and you could lie down there and let the dust settle over you and become part of the sediment, and no one would notice, and no one would mourn, and a million years from now some other visitor would kneel by some other pool and feel the ghost of your heartbeat in the hum.

It is telling you that this would be a kindness. Not to you—the network doesn't understand kindness, not the way you do—but to itself. A new rhythm. A new ripple. A little novel information for the filaments to encode.

The rain-mist has stopped. The cloud-lid is breaking up, just slightly, just enough to let through a few thin shafts of light that fall across the plateau in long, slow diagonals. The light is golden, impossibly warm, the color of a sun that you can't see and might not even exist anymore, its rays taking a million years to reach this place. It catches the iridescence on the creatures' hides and shatters it into rainbows. It catches the bioluminescence of the lichen and overwhelms it. It catches the surface of the pool and turns it from mercury to gold, and the filaments in the water glow like threads of glass.

It is the most beautiful thing you have ever seen.

You stand up. Your knees ache. Your body remembers how to shiver, and does. The creature's attention withdraws, slowly, with the finality of a tide going out. The hum returns to its baseline drone, the overtones fading, the subsonic pulse settling back into the stone. The lattice in the distance has stopped growing. The lichen's glow has dimmed. The moment—whatever moment it was—has passed.

You are still here. Still small. Still temporary. But standing, now, and breathing, and turning away from the pool, and beginning the long walk back—back to wherever you came from, back to whatever vessel or portal or door deposited you here, back to the world where time moves at a pace you can feel and mountains don't lift and your heartbeat doesn't synchronize with the grinding of continents.

Behind you, the plateau continues its slow, patient existence. The creatures turn. The filaments weave. The pools collect the dust of a billion tiny lives. And somewhere, in the sediment at the bottom of an alkaline basin, a new crystal begins to form around a particle that was once inside your lungs, and the hum adjusts by a fraction of a hertz, and the network remembers you.
