In the deeper folds of the moss, where the oxblood and the sulphur yellow meet in a damp seam, something else moves. Something that has been here longer than the handprints, longer than the Fibonacci spiral and the arranged bones.

It is not large. When it unfolds itself from its resting shape—a tight, armored curl no bigger than a child’s fist—it stretches to perhaps the length of a human hand. Its body is segmented, but the segments are not the neat, mechanical rings of an arthropod. They are softer, more organic, like the lobes of a lichen given flesh and motion. The creature’s back is a mosaic of plates that are not grown but *assembled*, a patchwork of the very materials of the crevasse-garden. There are flakes of the dark, glass-smooth stone, affixed so cunningly that their edges align with the creature’s natural contours. There are dried moss-scraps, still faintly green, woven into the gaps. A single iridescent beetle-wing, shed and scavenged, catches the diffuse light like a tiny stained-glass window set into its hide. The overall impression is of a living reliquary, a creature that carries its landscape on its body, a mobile, composite miniature of the crevasse itself.

This is the Keeper. There is only one. There has only ever been one. It does not breed, does not age in any way that the other inhabitants of the garden would recognize. It simply *is*, a continuous, slow-burning presence in the hollow, its life a loop that turns at the pace of the stone’s own breathing.

The Keeper’s head is a blunt, eyeless wedge. It navigates by a sense that is not quite hearing and not quite touch, a sensitivity to the slightest vibrations in the air, the stone, the trembling skin of the water droplets as they form and fall. It can feel the footsteps of a beetle from across the chamber, a faint tremor transmitted through the moss-pads. It can feel the precise, high-frequency hum of the aphids’ mouthparts as they tap the fleshy stalks for sap. It can feel the shape of the chamber itself, the standing wave of the quiet, the way the single sliver of sky-light changes the air pressure as the sun tracks across the crack high above. The world, to the Keeper, is a symphony of pressures, a constant, tactile music.

It moves with a deliberate, almost ceremonial slowness. Each step is a placement, a consideration. The Keeper’s limbs—six of them, jointed in odd, polyaxial ways that allow it to walk equally well on the vertical walls as on the horizontal moss—terminate in structures that are part-foot, part-hand. They are delicate pincers, but padded, too, so that they can grip the wet stone without slipping, spread wide to distribute weight on the spongy moss, and yet also perform tasks of extraordinary fineness.

This morning, as the first pale flush of light begins its long, refracted journey down the crevasse walls, the Keeper is working. It has positioned itself by the emerald moss at the deepest point of the hollow, the same patch toward which the bone-fragments point. With the patience of something that has performed this action ten thousand times, it is pruning.

The pincers on its foremost limbs are not sharp; they are beveled, like tiny adzes. The Keeper carefully snips away a thread of grey-blue lichen that has begun to encroach on the edge of the emerald moss. The lichen is not invasive in the ecological sense; it is a natural part of the crevasse community. But the Keeper has determined, through its long, slow calculus of vibration and pressure, that this particular patch of emerald moss is vital. It is the most photosynthetically active node in the entire garden, the primary engine of the miniature water cycle, the place where the light-fall is most consistent and the condensation richest. Allowing the lichen to shade it would alter the balance of transpiration, which would alter the formation of mist, which would, in turn, affect the droplet-fall pattern on the other side of the chamber, potentially desiccating a colony of the sulphurous moss that is the primary forage of the beetle herds. A cascade. A small one, by the standards of any larger ecology, but here, in this bounded stone palm, a significant one.

The Keeper finishes its work on the moss and turns its eyeless head to the aphids. They are thick today on the fleshy stalks, their translucent bodies engorged with the cold, faintly glowing sap. The beetle-tenders are active, their iridescent carapaces gleaming as they move in their orderly lines. The Keeper does not interfere with the beetles, nor the aphids, nor the centipede. That is not its function. Its function is maintenance of the *stage*, not direction of the *play*. It tends the mosses, the crucial nodes of the water cycle. It re-aligns fragments of stone that have shifted with the mountain’s tectonic restlessness, ensuring the crack overhead remains optimally shaped for light-gathering. It cleans the channels in the rock-face where the condensation trickles down, keeping them free of mineral buildup with a secretion from a gland near its mouth—a faintly acidic, silex-dissolving fluid.

It is the garden’s gardener. Or perhaps the garden’s immune system. Or its consciousness, if a place so small and contained can be said to have one. The Keeper is the point at which the crevasse-garden becomes self-tending, the place where the ecosystem’s own feedback loops achieve a kind of physical, mobile embodiment. It is the functional opposite of whatever left the handprints. The visitors came, observed, recorded pattern, and departed. The Keeper *is* the pattern, the living, breathing instantiation of the place’s inner logic.

Later, when the brief sliver of direct sunlight has passed and the chamber is once again in that state of diffuse, silvery glow, the Keeper will return to its resting place. It is a nook near the tumble of broken rock on the fourth wall, a hollow worn smooth by centuries of its presence. There, it will enter a state that is neither sleep nor torpor, but a kind of deep, vibrational listening, a state of maintenance for its own internal systems. Its metabolism will slow until it is barely distinguishable from the stone on which it rests. Its mosaic-plated back, with its fragments of rock and moss and iridescent wing-case, will blend perfectly into the dark, wet wall, a piece of the garden indistinguishable from the garden itself.

And in that state, it will wait. Wait for a slight shift in the dew-point that requires a pruning. Wait for a micro-fissure that needs its attention. Wait for the next visitors—human or otherwise—who might descend into this hollow and wonder at its lush, improbable life, and leave their own marks on the wall, and never see the thing that watches them, eyeless and patient, from a hand’s-breadth away, a piece of the mountain that has learned to care.
