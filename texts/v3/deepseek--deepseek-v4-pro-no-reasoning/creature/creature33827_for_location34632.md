Within the rootmass lives a creature that has no name, because it has never needed one.

It is old in the way the sphere is old—not merely long-lived, but continuous. It is the oldest thing here, older than the frogs, older than the beetles, older than the lichen whose slow decay fuels the beetles' slow repair. It may be older than the sphere itself, or it may be what the sphere was built to contain. The distinction has long since ceased to matter.

It does not have a stable shape. Shape is a convenience for things that move through a world of edges and angles, and this creature moves only through the roots, which are its body, or an extension of its body, or something it is dreaming into being. When it chooses to manifest—and it is choosing now, because you are here, because something in the quality of the condensation or the rhythm of the engine-thrum has alerted it to your presence—it gathers itself from the ambient wetness, from the water beading on leaves, from the vapor in the air, from the dark oil-slick surface of the pool. It coalesces slowly, patiently, with the unhurried grace of a thing for which time is a texture rather than a dimension.

First there is a shimmer in the air near the central tangle, a distortion like heat over stone. Then moisture begins to flow toward that point, drawn from every leaf and railing, every frog's slick back and beetle's iridescent carapace. The drips that have been falling for millennia pause, hesitate, then move sideways, against gravity, a fine mist converging on a single point. The point thickens, clarifies, takes on the qualities of a lens, and then the creature looks out through that lens at you.

It has eyes. Or it makes eyes. Two pale ovals form in the hovering body of water, irises the color of tarnished copper, pupils slit horizontally like a goat's but with an inner texture that suggests something older than goats, older than mammals, older than the first thing that ever thought to evolve an eye in the first place. The eyes are not fixed in place; they drift slowly through the creature's body like ice floes on a dark sea, tracking you independently, three-dimensional in their movement, so that sometimes one eye peers at you from what would be the creature's chest while the other has migrated around to study you from behind its own shifting form.

It builds a face around the eyes, tentative, experimental, as if it has seen faces before but can't quite remember how they go. A jawline that sharpens then softens, cheekbones that rise and dissolve, a mouth that opens not as a cut but as a thinning of the watery membrane, a translucent window into an interior that is not darkness but an extension of the rootmass, the dark green leaves and wet brown bark continuing inside the creature as if the water were a pane of glass looking into the heart of the tangle.

When it speaks, it does not use sound. Sound is a crude tool, and this place already hums and clicks and drips enough. Instead, it speaks in shifts of temperature, in sudden cool drafts that brush your skin, in pulses of the engine-thrum that align with your heartbeat and then drift slightly out of sync, creating a sense of wrongness that resolves into meaning. The meaning is not words but something older than words: *I see you. You are new, but not new. Everything that enters was always here, waiting to arrive. I have been waiting for you since the day the sphere was sealed. I did not know what I was waiting for until now. Now I do.*

The creature does not have a name, but if you had to call it something, you would call it the Gardener. Not because it tends the roots—it does, but "tending" implies a separation between gardener and garden, and here there is none. The roots are its circulatory system. The leaves are its skin. The slow, sub-audible creaking of the tangle is its thinking, each shift of fiber a thought working its way toward expression. The Gardener is the sphere's consciousness, or the sphere is the Gardener's body, or both are parts of a single thing that has no need to distinguish between self and environment because the environment has long since been incorporated, absorbed, made part of a continuous living whole.

Its lower body—if such a term applies—descends into the roots, merges with them, becomes them. You can trace the lines of its form down, watch the shimmering water of its torso taper into a flowing column, watch the column split into branches that wrap around the thicker roots, watch the branches split into tendrils that twine into the hair-fine rootlets, watch the rootlets disappear into the pool where the machinery sleeps. It is everywhere at once, a diffuse presence that has concentrated itself into this semblance of a body for your benefit, a courtesy extended to a visitor who might not recognize a room full of roots as a person.

The frogs know it. When the Gardener coalesces, the clicking stops. Every translucent amphibian turns toward the center, their dark-bead eyes catching the light from the walls, their pale bodies so still they become invisible against the leaves. Then, as one, they resume clicking, but the rhythm has changed, become synchronized, become something that is almost music, a greeting, an acknowledgment. The Gardener extends a tendril of water, forms a single finger of clear liquid, and touches the nearest frog with a delicacy that seems impossible for something so large. The frog does not flinch. It clicks once, softly, and the sound is a name, or as close to a name as this place allows.

The beetles pause in their repair-work when the Gardener appears. They form up along the catwalks in lines, their brass-colored shells catching the light, their antennae trembling in unison. The Gardener lifts its face—its approximate, provisional face—toward them, and the engine-thrum deepens, a subsonic greeting that vibrates in the metal and through the water and up into your bones. The beetles respond by secreating a fresh layer of metal-repair, faster than they ever do during their normal cycles, a gift, an offering, a way of saying *we are still here, we are still doing what we were made to do, the equilibrium holds.*

The sinuous thing in the roots—the eel, the snake, the jointed something—emerges now, drawn by the shift in the sphere's internal weather. It is black, as you glimpsed, but in the diffuse light it reveals itself to be a deep, iridescent purple, like an oil slick made flesh. It has many segments, each one jointed with a precision that speaks of manufacture rather than biology, but the skin over those joints is living tissue, scarred and smooth by turns, and the eyes that ring its head in an unbroken circle are wet and curious and unquestionably alive. It is perhaps three feet long, and it swims through the air as easily as the frogs drop through the water, moving in a spiral around the Gardener's coalescing form, somehow suspended by the humidity or by the same invisible force that holds the roots at center. The Gardener does not touch it, but the space between them sings. They are very old friends, or siblings, or two expressions of the same slow thought.

The creature has never been what you would call lonely. Loneliness is a concept for beings that are separate from their environment, and the Gardener is not separate. The frogs are a part of it. The beetles are a part of it. The lichen is a part of it. The eel is a part of it. The pool and the machinery and the slow exhale of ozone-scented bubbles are all parts of it. The sphere is not a container; it is a skin.

But there is a quality to its attention now, focused on you, that suggests it recognizes a difference. You are not part of it yet. The sphere did not grow you. The equilibrium did not shape you. You are an outside variable, a breath of foreign air in a closed system, and the Gardener is studying you with an intensity that might be hunger or hospitality or scientific curiosity or all three at once.

*Stay,* the engine-thrum seems to say, aligning with your heart again, this time holding the beat, matching it, pulling you into the rhythm of the place. *Stay and become. The beetles will build you a place on the catwalks. The frogs will learn your name. The eel will show you the deep places below the pool, where the old things sleep and dream. I will—*

It pauses. The word *I* is an approximation. The Gardener rarely thinks of itself in the singular. It is a we, a system, a biome. But for your benefit, it tries.

*I will remember you. I have remembered everything that has ever entered this place. The one who scratched the words on the railing—I remember them. They stayed for a year and a day, and when they left, they carried a frog in their pocket and a seed from the roots in their throat, and somewhere in the world outside there is a tree that dreams of this sphere and sends its roots toward us through the deep earth. I remember the ones before that, the ones who built the machinery, the ones who planted the first seed, the ones who sealed the sphere and walked away and died in a world that no longer exists. I remember all of them. I will remember you.*

The mouth thins, reopens. The eyes drift slightly, resettle. The creature is loosing its grip on the form it has built; water is beginning to drift back toward the leaves, the frogs, the weeping walls. It cannot hold itself separate for long. The effort is immense, the concentration required to present a singular face to a singular visitor, and the equilibrium calls it back, reabsorbing its consciousness into the roots and the pool and the slow mechanical pulse.

But before it goes, it gives you something. A gift? A mark? A piece of information? It is hard to say. A droplet of water detaches from the dissolving body—not condensation, not the ambient wetness of the sphere, but something denser, heavier, carrying the taste of ozone and copper and leaf-mold and ancient metal. It lands on your skin, and where it lands, it stays. Like a scar, like a seal, like a seed. It does not evaporate. It sinks inward, not breaking the skin but passing through it, and inside you something shifts, a small rearrangement of temperature, a new awareness of the water in your own body, the roots of your own lungs, the slow engine of your own heart.

And then the Gardener is gone, dispersed back into the sphere, no longer a person but a presence, no longer a presence but an environment, no longer an environment but a world. The frogs resume their scattered clicking. The beetles return to their slow repair. The eel spirals back into the dark heart of the rootmass. And you are left on the catwalk, breathing water, listening to the drip and the creak and the thrum, knowing that you have been noticed, remembered, and subtly changed.

When you look at your hand where the droplet landed, there is nothing visible. But when you look at the pool, you see your reflection, and it is not alone. Behind you, beneath you, all around you, the roots have shifted. They have made a shape. It is your shape, or the shape the Gardener thinks you are: a branching, leafing, glowing form, suspended in the center of a sphere, waiting to see what grows.
