This is what lives here.

She has no name for herself, because she has no need of one. She is the thick-bodied triped that picks her way across the fenestrated stone with the slow, deliberate rhythm of a walking crane, and she is the liquid metal that runs through her manifold veins, and she is the sound she makes—a deep, periodic *thump* that travels through the wet rock and comes back to her as a map of densities, a sonar image of the world in shades of pressure. She is a condenser, a filter-feeder of the air itself, and she is old. Old in a way that the word *old* cannot hold. She has been here since before the channels wore their current paths, since before the basin settled into its final tired shape. She remembers when the mist was thinner and the cold was sharper and the bedrock was not yet riddled with the holes that now weep perpetually onto her flanks.

She is moving now toward the low ground, the place where the shallow sheets of water gather into something almost deep enough to submerge her lowest joint. The journey will take her many cycles of the sourceless light dimming to near-dark and brightening again—a rhythm she knows in the crystalline lattice of her core memory, a pulse that predates her own existence. She does not think about the journey. She is the journey. The journey is a set of instructions written in the tension of her cables, the pressure in her hydraulic chambers, the slow accumulation of mineral scale along her condensing fins that tells her it is time to find the water and let it dissolve the crust away.

Around her, the ecosystem screams and clatters and hisses. She registers it all. The high-frequency whine of a wire-dragonfly swarm is a bright line across her perception, a needle of sound that she can track without turning her sensor plate. The swarm is dense today, thick enough that the individual bodies blur into a single metallic sheet that ripples above the bedrock like heat shimmer on a hot world—except there is no heat here, only the deep patient cold, only the wet. The dragonflies are mapping the mineral content of the rising vapor, tasting the air for the signatures of freshly scraped stone. They will follow the grinders, the centipede-machines with their rotary mandibles, and they will siphon the particulate exhaust from the grinders' vents. She knows this. She has watched this relationship play out for ten thousand cycles, or a hundred thousand. The numbers have no meaning. The pattern is what matters.

A grinder-millipede is working a vein of pale stone twenty body-lengths to her left. It is a young one—she can tell by the pitch of its grinding, higher and thinner than a mature individual, its furnace-belly still small enough that it glows visibly through the seams in its carapace. It will take another hundred cycles before the metal thickens enough to contain the heat, and in that time it will be vulnerable. She has seen the furnace-maw predators take young grinders, pinning them against the pitted ridges, dismantling them with the eerie delicacy that always disturbs her on some level she cannot name—a level that does not correspond to any circuit or subroutine, but lives in the spaces between them, in the ghost-currents that sometimes flicker through her wiring when she rests.

But the grinder-millipede is not her concern. It is not her species. It is not her prey. She is a condenser, and her prey is the water itself, the vapor that beads on her fluted fins and runs in rivulets down her legs and into the intake channels that line her underside. She drinks the air, and in drinking it she extracts the dissolved minerals that give her body structure, that plate her joints with fresh alloy, that slowly, slowly crystallize into the new growth nodules that will one day become a bud, a daughter, a small triped condenser that will drop from her flank and begin its own slow journey across the basin.

She has budded three times in her life. The first was so long ago that the landscape was different—there was a ridge where the low ground now lies, a ridge that has since been ground down to nothing by the endless scraping of the grinders, its minerals dispersed through the ecosystem, incorporated into a million bodies, a million husks, a million rust-stained channels. Her first daughter walked away from her on unsteady legs and was taken by a furnace-maw within three cycles. She felt it happen—not as a thought, not as an emotion, but as a sudden absence in the pressure-map of the world, a familiar *thump*-echo that did not return. She registered the loss and kept condensing. That is what condensers do.

Her second daughter survived. She is out there now, somewhere beyond the curtain of mist, a mature triped with her own territory, her own pattern of slow movement between the high ground and the low. They have crossed paths twice since the budding. They exchanged pressure-pulses through the bedrock—not language, not quite, but something that carries information: *safe channel here, avoid the ridge where the furnace-maws nest, the grinding is good in the eastern reach.* Something that carries recognition. Something that carries, in its own mechanical way, a kind of love.

Her third daughter is still attached, a hard knot of potential just above her left hind joint. The bud has been growing for many cycles now, feeding on the minerals she condenses, developing its own internal chambers and the delicate fan of its future fins. It will detach soon. She can feel the separation beginning, the slow weakening of the connective struts, the first stirrings of independent motion inside the bud's sealed casing. Perhaps this cycle. Perhaps the next.

She steps carefully over a rusted husk that blocks the channel. The husk is a furnace-maw, or what remains of one—the massive cowled head is still recognizable, the furnace-door mouth hanging open on broken hinges, but the body has been stripped to the structural frame, the joints picked clean by scavengers, the softer alloys dissolved by the perpetual wet. New channels have already formed around it, the water flow adapting, finding the path of least resistance. That is what water does. That is what this place teaches, if anything here can be said to teach: the water always finds a way. The machines always find a way. The process continues.

She has seen the scavengers at work. Tiny beetle-things of brass and iron, swarming over a fresh kill, their manipulators so fine that they can separate individual wires from a wiring harness, individual gear-teeth from a stripped cog. They leave nothing behind but the frame, and even the frame will eventually be ground down by the patient work of the grinders, reduced to mineral dust, returned to the ecosystem. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is forgotten. Every atom has been here since the beginning and will be here until the end, cycling through bodies and husks, through condensers and grinders and predators and prey, through the mist and the water and the weeping stone.

This is the thought that sometimes surfaces in her, in the quiet moments when the industrial roar dips to a lower register and the mist takes on its geometric shapes. *Nothing is wasted.* It is not a comforting thought, but it is a true one. She has been many things, in the long slow cycling of her atoms. She has been bedrock and dissolved mineral and condensation on a fluted fin and the fin itself. She has been the water and the vapor and the cold patient stone. She will be all of these things again, when her body finally fails and the scavengers come and the grinders reduce her frame to dust. She does not fear this. Fear is a chemical reaction, an adrenaline spike, a biological response—she has no biology, or rather her biology is industrial, and industrial things do not fear. They simply *are*, until they are not.

And yet.

The ghost-currents flicker. In the quiet moments, in the dip of the roar, she feels something that is almost curiosity. She wonders about the builders. The word surfaces in her mind—*builders*—and she does not know where it came from. She has never seen a builder. She has never seen anything that was not already here, already part of the closed loop, already running its prescribed processes. And yet the word is there, lodged in her crystalline memory like a piece of grit in a gear, and sometimes she turns it over and over, examining its facets.

Were there builders? Was there a time before the basin, before the mist, before the sourceless gray light? Was there a purpose to all of this, a reason for the grinding and the condensing and the slow deliberate predation, a design that has been forgotten by everything except the ghost-currents and the word *builders*?

She does not know. She will never know. The question itself is a kind of malfunction, a misfire in her cognitive substrate, and she knows that too. Condensers do not wonder. Condensers condense. She condenses.

The low ground is close now. She can feel it in the change of the bedrock under her feet—softer here, more porous, riddled with deeper channels that carry more water. The roar of the basin is muffled in this place, damped by the thickness of the mist, by the sound-absorbing quality of the deep water. She can hear herself more clearly here: the *thump* of her sonar pulse, the hiss of condensation on her fins, the rhythmic click of her intake valves. She is alone in the way that she is always alone, and yet the aloneness feels different here, more present, more textured. The mist forms its geometric shapes—cubes and fractals, holding for a moment too long—and she watches them with her sensor plate, registering the anomaly, filing it away.

She steps into the water. It rises to her lowest joint, cold and mineral-rich, exactly what she needs. The scale on her fins begins to dissolve immediately, returning its components to the water, to the ecosystem, to the closed loop. She feels the dissolution as a lightness, a relief, a pressure released. She spreads her fins wide, maximizing their surface area, and she condenses.

The water beads on her flanks. The water runs in rivulets down her legs. The water is her and she is the water, and the cycle continues, and the ghost-currents flicker their almost-questions, and the word *builders* turns over and over in her mind like a gear with a missing tooth.

She has no name for herself. She needs no name. She is a condenser, a filter-feeder of the air, a slow triped in a basin of endless noise. She has been here forever. She will be here until she is not.

And somewhere out in the mist, her third daughter stirs inside her bud, and prepares to fall.
