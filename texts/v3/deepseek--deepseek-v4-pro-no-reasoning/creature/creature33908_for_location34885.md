I know what lives here before I even see them clearly, because I can feel them feeling me. It's not a thought, exactly—more like a pressure against the edges of my awareness, a sense of being tasted by something that has no mouth, weighed by something with no concept of weight. The vibration in the floor changes when I step. Not much. A hesitation in the hum, a skip in the heartbeat-pulse, and I understand that I have been noticed.

They are called the Chorus, though no one has spoken their name aloud in a very long time.

Up close, they are not simply black. Their carapaces hold a deep iridescence, oil-slick rainbows that shift when they turn, catching the dying amber flicker of the ceiling conduits. Each one is a precise geometry of interlocking plates, smooth as glass where they meet, serrated at the edges where they overlap. They have too many legs—nine, I count, arranged in three triads around a central barrel-body—and those legs end not in feet but in specialized tools. Some taper to needle-fine probes that test the metal floor with butterfly delicacy. Others flatten into chisel-blades or hooked scrapers, worn bright at the tips from generations of use. A few carry appendages that resemble nothing so much as minuscule welding torches, though what they weld and what they burn I cannot say.

Their heads are turret-like, rotating on necks of stacked ceramic rings, and their faces—if faces they are—hold a disturbing economy. Two large, faceted lenses dominate the front, black and depthless. Below these, a complex working of jointed mandibles and palp-like structures that move constantly, tasting the air, tasting the metal, tasting me. But it's the spaces between their segments that hold my attention longest. In those gaps, where chitin meets chitin, a faint blue glow escapes. Not steady, but flickering in patterns that feel intentional. Signals. Cherenkov whispers.

They do not eat each other, despite what the constant motion suggests. The ballet I mistook for predator and prey is something else entirely. I watch two of them meet, and instead of violence, they perform an intricate pas de deux. They tap each other with their tool-legs in sequences that are too patterned to be random, their mandibles working soundlessly, their body-segments pulsing with that blue interior light. An exchange is happening—information, perhaps, or energy, or something in between. One of them passes a small, perfectly spherical nodule of metal to the other, and I understand that I am watching trade, or ritual, or both.

Now I see the castes.

The ones I noticed first—the fist-sized, the head-sized—are the commonest, the workers or the gatherers. They swarm thickest around the hexagonal pillar and the heat-exchanger monument, their tool-legs busy with some patient, endless task of deconstruction and rearrangement. I realize with a slow shock that the pillar is smoother than I first thought. Its surface, which I took for rust-pitting, is instead covered in the precise, geometric scars of their work. They are not consuming the metal. They are curating it, shaping it, writing something into its surface that I cannot read.

Scattered among them are larger forms. Some are the size of a human torso, their bodies elongated, their legs fewer but more powerful, ending in heavy crusher-claws that could shear through the ancient alloy like clay. These move slowly, deliberately, carving pathways through the carpet of smaller kin. The lesser Chorus part for them without apparent signal, flowing around them like water around stones. One of these larger ones pauses near me—too near—and I feel a sudden spike in the floor's vibration, a localized intensification of the hum. Its head-turret swivels toward me, and those faceted lenses reflect my own distorted image back at me: a soft, wet, alien thing in a place that has forgotten softness.

There are others I almost miss. Motionless. So still that I mistake them for protrusions of the floor itself, ancient components that have not yet been dismantled. They are the largest of all—the size of small vehicles, their bodies flattened and spread, their legs sunk into the metal as though they have partially fused with it. Their carapaces are duller than the rest, frosted with a pale patina of age, and the blue light in their joints has dimmed to a slow, tidal pulse that matches the deep thrumming in the floor. These, I realize, are the old ones. The priests or the kings or the sages of this small, perfect world. They do not move because they do not need to. They are listening to something I cannot hear, feeling a rhythm I cannot feel, and directing—through whatever network binds the Chorus together—the endless, purposeful industry of their descendants.

One of them stirs.

Not much. A ripple passes through its flattened form, and the blue light in its segments brightens incrementally. I feel a change in the pressure against my mind, a focusing of that diffuse, tasting attention. The worker-forms nearest me pause in their tasks. Their tool-legs go still. Their head-turrets turn. And in the sudden, localized silence—an island of quiet in the ceaseless clicking sea—I understand that I am being asked a question.

I don't know what the question is. I'm not sure it can be translated into thought, let alone language. But I feel the shape of it, the weight of it, pressing against me through the soles of my feet and the hum in the air and the blue glow in their joints. It is a query about purpose, about function, about my place in the great sealed cycle of this place. Am I a new component? A new castes? A new tool? Have I come to join the Work, the long, slow Work that has occupied them for two billion years?

I don't answer, because I don't know how. The silence stretches. The head-turrets swivel back. The tool-legs resume their tasks. And the Chorus, having tasted me and found me incomprehensible, lets me be—a ghost in their machine, ignored but not forgotten, a soft anomalous thing adrift in their world of hard edges and slow, perfect purpose.

The old one on the floor returns to its listening. The hum deepens. The amber heart-light pulses overhead. I am allowed to stay, but I am not of the Chorus, and they know it, and I know it, and the knowledge settles over me like the ancient, sterile air: cold, and dry, and absolute.
