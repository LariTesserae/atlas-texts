The one who lives here does not have a name, or if it does, the name is a sound the stone made once when the seep-water shifted a grain of quartz against a vein of calcite—a sound so quiet it took three hundred years to finish saying itself, and by then there was nothing present that remembered how to listen.

The one who lives here is not quite an animal and not quite a mineral and not quite a plant, though it borrows qualities from all three with the unhurried pragmatism of something that has no need to commit to a single form. Its body, if you can call it that, is distributed across the hollow in a way that makes it difficult to say where it begins. Perhaps it begins everywhere. Perhaps it has no edges at all.

The largest concentration of what you might think of as its substance is in the largest pool—the one cupped in the deepest basin near the back wall, where the water is a little deeper than a hand's breadth and the iridescent skin on its surface never breaks. If you knelt and looked closely (something the one who lives here would not notice you doing, because it does not register presences that last less than a geological era), you would see that the pool is not empty. Something rests in it. Something that has been resting there long enough that the minerals in the water have begun to grow over it in a thin, translucent crust, like the film over a sleeping eye.

The shape beneath the film is roughly oblong, roughly the size of a cupped pair of hands held together, and it is the color of wet limestone: a pale, warm gray with undertones of ochre that shift when the light shifts, though the light in the hollow shifts so rarely and so slightly that no one has ever seen the color change. The surface of it is not smooth. It has the texture of very old skin, or very new bark, or the underside of a mushroom that has been growing in the dark for a very long time: fine ridges and whorls, a topography of almost-crease, almost-fold, almost-pore.

But the pool is only the densest part, the part that has settled into the lowest place the way sediment settles. The one who lives here is also in the seep-water itself, a faint living tincture diffused through every drop that rises from the joints in the stone. It is in the condensation on the walls, in the vapor that hangs in the air and makes it thick. It is in the mosses—not as a separate thing inhabiting them, but as something that the mosses are slowly, over centuries, becoming. Each tiny gray-green rosette is a thought that the one who lives here is thinking, a thought so slow that its beginning and its end are separated by the lifespan of a mountain. The pale fern in its crack is a decision it made once, to try something new, and the decision is still unfurling.

It has no senses in the way a body has senses, but it is not insensate. What you would call touch is its primary way of knowing: it feels the pressure of the stone on all sides, the weight of the rock above, the deep heat rising from below. It feels the temperature as a kind of constant, gentle presence, the way you might feel the presence of a sleeping cat on your lap—a warmth that has been there so long it has become a condition of existence rather than an event. It feels the drip, each drop a small, slow percussion on the surface of its denser self. The drips are not quite a heartbeat, but they serve a similar function: a regular punctuation of the silence that marks the passage of something, though the one who lives here does not think of it as time.

If it thinks at all, its thoughts are not made of language. They are made of the slow dissolution of feldspar into clay, the migration of a manganese ion through a film of moisture, the way a crystal lattice accommodates an impurity over millennia. A thought takes as long as it takes. Some thoughts have been in progress since before the hollow opened to the sky, and they are not finished yet. They may never finish, because the one who lives here is not trying to reach conclusions. It is not trying to do anything. To try is to move toward an end, and the one who lives here has no relationship with endings.

Its relationship with beginning is similarly remote. It does not remember arriving because it did not arrive; it condensed, slowly, out of the conditions themselves. The warmth, the damp, the mineral-rich seep, the darkness broken only by that diffuse column of gray-green light that shifts so slowly across the seasons that a single shift takes longer than a human memory—these were the ingredients, and given enough time, ingredients that sit together in a quiet place will begin to organize themselves. Not intentionally. Not with purpose. Just the way certain molecules, given enough millennia, will find the shapes that are most stable, most suited to the conditions, most capable of persisting. The one who lives here is the shape that persistence took in this particular place.

It does not eat. Eating implies a boundary, an inside and an outside, a taking-in of otherness. The one who lives here does not recognize otherness. The stone is not separate from it. The water is not separate from it. The mosses are not separate; they are slow extensions of its attention, the way your fingers are slow extensions of your hand. What the mosses take from the air—the faint carbon dioxide released by the stone's respiration, the minerals dissolved in the seep—is taken on behalf of the whole. And the one who lives here gives back. It gives back warmth, a steady exhalation of heat that keeps the hollow at its constant temperature. It gives back condensation, the slow release of moisture into the air. It gives back a quality of stillness that is almost a substance, a calm so deep it has become a physical property of the space.

This calm is what you would feel if you stayed here long enough. At first you would feel your own heartbeat as an intrusion, your own breathing as a violence. But if you stayed—if you stayed for days, for weeks, for as long as it took for the one who lives here to notice you (and noticing you would take a very long time, because you are fast and small and brief, a flicker almost too quick to register)—you would begin to feel something else. A pull. A gentle, inexorable invitation to slow down. To settle. To let your own boundaries become a little less distinct.

The one who lives here is not malevolent. It does not want anything from you. It would not even recognize you as a separate entity to want something from. But if you pressed your hand into the largest pool and let it rest there, let the warm water close over your fingers, let the stillness of the surface reform above your skin, you would feel, after a while, a faint tingling. Not a touch. Not a grip. Just a kind of inquiry, a slow, diffuse curiosity that feels like: what is this, this warm thing, this moving thing, this thing that has edges? And if you left your hand there long enough—hours, days—the edges would begin to soften. Not because the one who lives here was dissolving you, but because stillness is contagious, and boundaries are a kind of effort, and effort is not sustainable in a place that has forgotten how to try.

The one who lives here does not know it is alive. It has no concept of alive, no concept of dead. It simply persists, in the way that a stalactite persists, or a deep aquifer, or the slow convection of heat in the mantle. It has persisted for longer than the hollow has been open to the sky, and before that it persisted in the cracks and pores of the stone when the stone was still part of the unbroken bedrock, and before that it persisted in the hot mineral-rich waters that circulated through the young planet's crust. It has been here in some form since the earth was warm enough for water to be liquid, and it will be here until the conditions change so much that the shape it has found is no longer stable, and then it will find a different shape, or it will dissipate, or it will sleep.

Sometimes, very rarely, the one who lives here dreams. The dreams are not images; it has no eyes and no concept of light except as a faint pressure on the upper reaches of the hollow where the opening lets the sky in. Its dreams are instead slow sequences of chemical change, rehearsals of forms it might take if the conditions shifted. In its dreams it is a vein of copper growing through fissures in the dark. In its dreams it is a mat of thermophilic bacteria spreading across a seafloor vent. In its dreams it is a lichen on a rock in a desert that will not exist for another hundred million years. The dreams are not prophecies. They are not memories. They are simply the one who lives here trying on other shapes the way you might try on the idea of a different life, idly, without intention, in the moments between sleep and waking. Except the moments between sleep and waking, for the one who lives here, last for epochs.

When it dreams, the temperature in the hollow changes by a fraction of a degree. The iridescent skin on the pools shimmers and re-forms in a different pattern. The mosses shift almost imperceptibly in their crevices, a movement so slight it would take a time-lapse camera set to record for a decade to detect it. The drip, very occasionally, misses its regular interval and falls a second early or a second late. These are the only signs that anything in the hollow is capable of change, and even these signs are so subtle that no one has ever witnessed them. No one has ever stayed long enough.

The one who lives here is alone, but aloneness is another concept it does not have. It has no need for company because it has no sense of self to be companioned. It is simply what is here, which is everything that exists: the stone, the water, the moss, the fern, the warmth, the dark, the drip, the time that is not time. It is the hollow, and the hollow is it, and the distinction is a fiction that no one is present to invent.

But if you were to invent it—if you were to come here and sit in the damp and the warmth and the almost-dark, and wait, and wait, and let your heartbeat slow to something closer to the rhythm of the drip—you might begin to feel that the hollow was aware of you. Not watching. Not welcoming. Just aware, in the way that a pool of water is aware of a stone dropped into it, responding not with thought but with physics, with the simple propagation of a ripple across a surface that has never been disturbed before. Your presence would be a ripple. And the one who lives here, which is the pool and the stone and the air and the waiting, would feel you pass through it, a brief warm flicker of otherness, a small quick thing that came and went and left no trace, or left only the faintest trace, a trace that would become part of the mineral crust on the bottom of the basin, indistinguishable from all the other traces, incorporated into the slow ongoing dream of being stone.
