The people of the Long Dive call themselves the Between-Folk, or sometimes simply Those Who Stayed, though no one now remembers what they were supposed to leave. They are not tall—the low ceilings have seen to that, generation by generation pressing shoulders down and forward, until the average adult walks with the slight stoop of someone listening for something just below the threshold of hearing. Their skin is pale as mushroom flesh, not from a lack of sun but from the quality of light they receive: the diffuse, filtered glow that makes it down through the crack, the steady amber of the fat-lamps, the faint green phosphorescence of the glowing beetles they call live-coals. Hands and cheekbones are stained faintly gray from the stone dust that hangs perpetually in the air, a fine powder that settles into the whorls of fingerprints and the creases of eyelids until everyone carries a map of their own face in negative.

Their eyes have changed. Not dramatically—they are still recognizably human eyes—but the pupils tend toward a slight dilation even in full lamplight, and the irises have deepened into dark browns and blacks, the better to gather what light there is. They blink less often than surface folk, a habit born of the constant damp that makes dry eyes a distant memory, and they have developed a way of looking that is less a gaze and more a patience: their eyes rest on things without demanding them, waiting for detail to resolve itself out of shadow. Children are born with a faint, watery squint that deepens into something else as they age—not a squint exactly, but a habitual soft-focus, a way of seeing that includes the peripheral darkness as part of the image. They can read the walls by touch the way a surface person might read a face, their fingers finding the micro-ridges of basalt, the cooler seams of pumice, the places where water is about to bead and drop.

They do not have a single language. What they speak is a creole of something older and several things newer, a tongue that has accreted like the stone itself, layer upon layer of borrowed words and re-purposed grammar. It is a soft language, full of unvoiced consonants and long vowels, built for spaces where sound carries and returns. The word for "rain" is the same as the word for "later," because rain on the plateau arrives in the Dive as mist long after it has fallen. The word for "home" is a compound meaning "where-the-water-meets-the-stone," which is how they describe the particular intersection of seep and surface that marks each dwelling. They have seventeen words for different kinds of quiet: the quiet of a sleeping child, the quiet of stone settling, the quiet of someone holding their breath, the quiet of a gap in a conversation where meaning passes without sound. The word for "stranger" is a single syllable that means "loud-walker," and they use it rarely.

Family is not reckoned by blood alone. A household in the Dive is a cluster of people bound by proximity and care, not necessarily by birth. Marriage exists, but it is called "ledge-sharing," and it is a flexible arrangement that can include two people or three or four, and can dissolve as easily as it forms—which is to say, not easily at all, but without the weight of law or judgment. What matters is the care of the young and the old, the tending of the lamps, the maintenance of the ropes and bridges, the slow, communal work of keeping the water from taking back what they have built. Children are raised by the whole ledge, by the stretch of houses that share a roof, and they learn early to recognize a dozen voices as that of a parent. A child might sleep in one room tonight and another tomorrow, following the warmth of the lamps or the presence of a favorite storyteller or simply their own whim. No one counts the days of gestation or marks a calendar of birth; instead, they celebrate the first time a child reaches for the wall and finds a handhold, the first time they walk a bridge without help, the first time they identify the sound of a particular drip and can say its name.

Time moves differently in the Dive. There are no seasons as the surface knows them, only a slow fluctuation in the volume of seep: the wet months when the walls run with a constant, silvery sheen and the floor grows soft and treacherous, and the dry months when the basalt is merely damp to the touch and the mosses retreat into dormancy. The Between-Folk measure time by the slow crawl of lichen across a wall, by the number of times a particular rope bridge needs re-braiding, by the lifespan of a live-coal beetle—about two years, give or take, during which it will glow a little brighter each night until it finally goes dark and is placed in a small stone niche with the others, a constellation of tiny black bodies that the children use to learn counting. A person's age is not given in years but in "do you remembers": do you remember the winter the basalt split and opened the new crack by the lower pools? Do you remember the bridge-builder who fell and was caught by the rope she'd woven? Do you remember before old Marta's lamp went out? Memory is the only calendar, and it is kept communally, passed from voice to voice in the long, dark hours when the lamps are turned low and the whole settlement settles into storytelling.

They do not worship, exactly. But they have a sense of the crack as alive, as a presence that is still in the act of becoming. They leave offerings not to gods but to the stone itself: a bead of water collected from a particularly pure seep, a strand of hair braided with moss, a live-coal beetle released into a new crevice. They speak to the walls in the same low voice they use for each other, and they believe—or act as though they believe, which is the same thing—that the basalt listens. When someone dies, the body is placed in a crevice where two walls have not yet fully separated, and over time the stone closes around it, slow as a fist unclenching in reverse, until the dead are part of the crack itself. No one marks these graves, because the whole Dive is a grave, and a womb, and a home, and no one sees a contradiction in this.

They trade with the surface, though not often. A few times a year, someone climbs the long staircase cut into the basalt—a thousand steps, each one worn smooth and concave by generations of bare feet—and emerges onto the plateau with a pack full of dried moss for medicine, lichen for dye, the small, perfect carvings they make from basalt chips during the long still hours. In return, they take salt and cloth and metal tools, things the Dive cannot produce. The climbers are always volunteers, always people who have begun to feel what the Between-Folk call "the pulling"—a restlessness that comes on in middle age, a sudden desire to see the sky not as a strip but as a whole, to feel wind that actually blows. Some of them come back. Some of them don't. The ones who return are quiet for a long time afterward, and they never speak of what they saw except in fragments, and the children gather around them and listen and try to imagine a place where the ground does not end in walls.
