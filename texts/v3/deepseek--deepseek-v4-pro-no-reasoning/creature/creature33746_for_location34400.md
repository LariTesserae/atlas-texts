What lives here does not simply live. It abides.

The largest and most permanent residents are the **hollow-bellied ones**, the pale-furred animals that den in the tower bases. They have no name in any human tongue because no human has ever needed to name them. They are the size of a small bear but shaped more like an enormous, soft-shouldered marmot, with short limbs and a rolling, deliberate gait. Their fur is a dirty ivory, thick and dense, with an undercoat that traps the warmth of their bodies against the permanent chill. In the dim light, their eyes shine milky blue, the pupils reduced to vertical slits that never fully dilate, even in the darkest corners of the basalt chambers. They do not hibernate. Hibernation is for animals that live in a world with seasons. Here, the only season is condensation.

They are not social in the way of wolves or apes, but they are communal. A clan of forty or fifty hollow-bellied ones will lay claim to a tower cluster, marking the lowest twelve meters of the structure as theirs. They sleep in tangled, humid piles in the condensation gullies, on ledges warmed by the slow decay of whatever radioactive or chemical process still smolders deep below. Their young are born in the dark, wet chambers beneath the floor plates, in nests woven from the gray moss and lined with shed fur. The pups are blind for nearly two months, and during that time their mothers feed them with milk so rich in fat that it congeals in the cold air when it leaks from a sleeping pup's mouth.

The hollow-bellied ones eat the moss. Not by grazing—the moss does not grow thick enough to sustain browsing in the open—but by scraping it from the surfaces where it accumulates. They use their claws, which are long and curved and surprisingly dexterous, to peel the gray felt from warm pipes and under gratings, from the drip-edges of condenser coils and the inside walls of dead valves. They also eat the birds' eggs when they can reach them, and occasionally the birds themselves, swatting them out of the air with a lazy, almost insulting precision when one flies too low. But the moss is their staple. It grows everywhere in the tower clusters, in thick, spongy mats that feel like wet wool and smell like river stones and old bread. It is a partnership of two species—an alga that captures what little light filters through the overcast, and a thermophilic bacterium that breaks down the complex hydrocarbons seeping from the towers' joints and gaskets. Neither could survive alone. Together, they form a living fabric that covers every warm surface, converting chemical seepage and weak light into edible biomass.

What the moss cannot absorb, the hollow-bellied ones drink. They have learned, over however many generations, to read the installation's plumbing. They know which pipes carry steam and which carry coolant. They know which valves hiss and which valves weep. They know that the clear, viscous fluid that drips from the pipe-bridges in long, syrupy strings is not water but something closer to a nutrient slurry, and they guzzle it greedily when it accumulates in the shallow basins of cracked basalt. It is not a perfect diet. Many of the animals have a faint tremble, a neurological tic that makes their heads tilt every few seconds as if responding to a sound or a smell that is not there. Some have sores at the corners of their mouths. But they are fat and they are numerous, and in any ecology, that is a kind of success.

The birds are less patient than the hollow-bellied ones. They are not swifts, though they superficially resemble them. They are longer-bodied, with dark, oily plumage that refracts the dim light into faint, iridescent blues and greens. Their wings are long and scythe-shaped, built for soaring rather than flapping, and they ride the thermals that rise from the towers' warm vents in endless, drifting spirals. They do not sing. Their only vocalization is a high-frequency click, produced not by their throats but by a specialized ridge in their beaks, and it is used for echolocation. The interior of a cooling tower, with its curved, smooth walls and deep shadow, is a labyrinth of echoes, and the birds navigate it perfectly, never once touching the damp ceramic surface. They spend most of their lives in flight. They eat in flight, snapping tiny, floating invertebrates from the steam plumes. They mate in flight. They even sleep in flight, one half of their brains at a time.

But they roost in the high gantries, and that is where their nests are. The nests are not built from twigs or grasses, since there are none. Instead, the birds excrete a fine, sticky paste from their beaks—a mixture of guano and mucus—and mold it into latticework structures that cling to the metal beams like wasps' nests. Each nest is a tiny, inverted amphora with a narrow entrance, defended fiercely from all intruders. The colonies are immense. Hundreds of thousands of birds occupy the space between the towers and the cloud layer, and their numbers wax and wane with the temperature. When the towers thrum louder, when the hidden machinery works harder and the steam rises in denser columns, the insects bloom, and the birds with them. When the machinery quiets, the birds drop by three-quarters. It is as if the colony breathes in sync with the installation, expanding and contracting with its hidden rhythms.

The insects that sustain the birds are small and pale and almost transparent, with wings so fragile they dissolve in the human eye's attempt to focus on them. They drift up from the warm, wet surfaces of the cooling tower's internal walls in slow, vertical rivers, rising on the updrafts. They have no mouths as adults. They are born, they fly, they mate, they die, all within the space of three or four days. Their larvae live in the moss mats, feeding on the microbial bloom that grows at the boundary between the moss and the warm basalt. The birds catch the adults as they rise, bug after bug, an endless harvest of soft, molecular flight. Because the insect populations are tied to the moss, and the moss is tied to the towers' leaks, and the towers are tied to whatever process still runs beneath the valley, the entire aerial ecosystem is a single, four-dimensional web, woven not through space so much as through the trembling machinery of the place.

And then there is the **Widow's Child**.

It is not a species. It may not even be an animal in the ordinary sense. It lives alone, or there are several of them, but they are so solitary, so bound to their own private circuits of the valley, that no observer would ever see two together. It is large—much larger than any known predator of this or any other ecosystem—but its size is hard to estimate because it is never seen fully. It moves in the far reaches, beyond the last clusters of towers, where the mist thickens to a gray, occlusive soup and the basalt floor breaks into vertical fissures. The hollow-bellied ones do not go there. The birds do not fly over it. The mist itself seems reluctant to part for it.

The only direct traces of the Widow's Child are tracks. Deep, splayed footprints in the hard, cracked basalt, each one as long as a human forearm. They are not made by a creature with claws, but by something with broad, soft feet, six-toed, with a split at the heel. The tracks appear suddenly, without an origin point, and disappear just as abruptly, as if the creature steps between the atoms of the air. They are filled with clear water, always, even when no water has dripped from above. The water is cold and slightly radioactive. The hollow-bellied ones will not approach a fresh track for days.

There is another trace, too: a sound. The Widow's Child is silent when it moves—impossibly silent for such a mass—but when it pauses, it makes a deep, rhythmic thrumming, not unlike the lowest register of a cooling pump. It is a sound that never repeats in the same place twice. The birds alter their flight patterns when they hear it. The hidden machinery, for a few seconds, runs in synchrony with it.

No one has seen the Widow's Child fully. But on nights when the lid of cloud grows briefly thinner and the orange glow behind it brightens, a shape can sometimes be glimpsed at the limit of vision: a long, low silhouette, like a bear, like a reptile, like a machine, standing very still between the towers, its head raised to the sky as if listening for something no one else can hear.

And what of the humans?

No. There are no humans. There were never humans. The place does not allow them. The coriolis force of the valley's paradox—new rock, old moss, old machines, new animals—repels the human gaze. Visitors who descend the basalt spine never stay long. Their bodies reject the place inch by inch. Their skin crawls. Their balance fails. Their thoughts repeat, like a skipping record, on a single question: *Why does this exist? Why does this exist?* And because no answer comes, they climb back up the basalt spine, shaken and wet, leaving no sign that they were ever there. Only a few set of bootprints, pressed into the newborn stone for a few brief minutes, then lost to the slow, patient condensation.

In the end, what lives here is not the bear-marmots, not the swifts, not the larvae, not the moss. What lives here is the fact of living. It is a place where the boundary between machine and animal, between artificial and evolved, has blurred into a single, low hum. The towers are not skeletons. They are not ruins. They are more like reefs—geological bodies on which life has accumulated, and around which life has organized its manners and its hungers. The valley is a body, and the life within it, from the spores in the steam to the lonely shape in the far mist, is an organism in the broadest sense: an untranslatable metabolism, warm in some places, cold in others, endlessly cycling water from the air to the gut to the pipe to the air again.

The question is not what lives here.

The question is whether the place itself is alive.

And the answer, if you listen long enough to the thrum beneath the basalt, to the valve hiss and the click of wings and the low, rhythmic sound of a six-toed footfall in the far mist, is that it has never been anything else.
