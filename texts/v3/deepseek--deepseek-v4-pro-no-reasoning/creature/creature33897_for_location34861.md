The one who lives here is not a person, though she was, once. She is what remains when a person has been worn by a place the way water wears stone—not eroded, exactly, but fitted, shaped to the contours of the slope and the slow rhythms of the condensation until the boundary between inhabitant and inhabited becomes uncertain.

She has no name that she remembers. If she ever had one, it has been so long unspoken that it has crumbled like the roof tiles, its edges softened, its meaning settled into the earth. She does not think of herself as *I* so much as *here*—a point of awareness that moves along the contour path, that rests in the darkness behind the ajar door, that sometimes cups its hands in the cistern and drinks and feels the cold settle into a chest that is still, technically, alive.

Her body is old. Not old in the way of the olive trees, whose age is written in gnarled wood and counted in centuries, but old in the way of the path itself—a thing that has been trodden and weathered so long that its original form is more memory than fact. She is small, and the smallness is partly the natural shrinkage of great age and partly a kind of folding-in, a compression that has come from spending decades in doorways and terrace corners, from making herself fit the spaces the settlement offered. Her spine has curved to match the slope of the hill, so that when she stands on the path she leans slightly into the mountain, as though she is always walking uphill even when the ground is level. Her shoulders are narrow, her hips narrow, her whole frame reduced to what is necessary for slow movement and long stillness.

Her skin is the color of the local clay, not because she was born to it but because she has lived in it for so long that it has become her. The dust of the path has settled into the creases of her palms and the folds of her eyelids, a pale silt that never quite washes away because she does not wash, not in any thorough sense. When the condensation is thick on the walls, she will sometimes stand against the stone and let the moisture bead on her face and arms, and then she will wipe the moisture away with a cloth that was once white and is now the gray of unglazed pottery, and the cloth will come away faintly brown, and her skin beneath will be no lighter. The clay is in her. It has worked its way into the outermost layers of her epidermis, so that she is slightly gritty to the touch, like a ceramic piece that has been bisque-fired but never glazed.

Her hair—what remains of it—is white, but it is a white that has yellowed with the same resinous sediment that lines the base of the oil lamp. It grows in thin tufts, like the grasses on the slope, and she does not cut it or arrange it. It simply is, a sparse cloud around a scalp that shows through in patches, the skin there the same clay-pale as the rest of her. Sometimes, when she is sitting still for long hours on the low retaining wall, the small brown pipit will come and investigate her hair, picking at it for insects or nesting material, and she will not move, and the bird will find nothing and fly away, and the stillness will close back over her like water.

Her eyes are the color of the water in the cistern—a dark gray-brown, cool, slightly clouded but still clear enough to see the path and the terrace edges and the raven's shadow when it passes. They are set deep in a face that has collapsed inward around them, the cheeks hollowed, the jaw receded, so that her expression from any distance is one of mild, perpetual surprise. But up close, if anyone were close, they would see that the expression is not surprise at all. It is attention. A steady, unblinking attention that has nothing to do with expectation and everything to do with presence. She looks at the path, the wall, the lichen rings, the thistle down, and she receives them. That is all.

She wears a garment that was once a dress and is now a collection of patches held together by habit. The base fabric is a coarse wool, woven by someone—her? someone else?—on a loom that no longer exists, from the fleece of sheep that no longer graze these terraces. The wool has felted over time, the fibers matted by moisture and friction until the cloth is more like a thin, flexible felt than a woven textile. Over the wool, she has layered other things: a shawl of undyed linen, so worn it is translucent in places, its edges frayed to a soft fringe; a vest of knitted wool, the stitches dropped in several places, leaving small holes through which the linen shows; an apron of leather so old it has stiffened into a shape that holds the curve of her body even when she is not wearing it. The apron has a pocket, and in the pocket are three things: a smooth ceramic shard the size of her thumb, worn to an oval; a sprig of dried thyme that still releases its scent when crushed, though she never crushes it; and a small, flat stone with a natural hole through it, which she found on the path decades ago and has carried ever since for no reason she could articulate.

Her feet are bare. They have been bare for so long that the soles are a thick, cracked layer of callus, as tough as the leather of her apron, and when she walks on the ceramic-shard path the shards do not cut her. They press into the callus and leave small temporary indentations, like the footprints of a bird in drying mud, and then the indentations fade. Her toenails are yellowed and thickened, curling slightly over the ends of her toes, and embedded in them are tiny fragments of path-grit that have accumulated over the years and become part of her keratin. She does not notice them.

She does not notice many things that a person would notice. She does not notice the cold, though the air is cool enough that anyone else would shiver. She does not notice hunger, because she eats so little and so regularly that hunger has ceased to be a signal and become merely a background hum, like the distant water running through its gravel sink. She eats what grows on the terraces: the wild thyme, the young leaves of a dandelion relative that sprouts in the cracks of the retaining wall, the occasional olive from the twisted trees, which she chews slowly, the bitter oil coating her mouth, the pit held under her tongue for an hour before she spits it onto the path where it will perhaps, in some future she will not see, become a tree. She eats insects, sometimes. The beetle laboring across the path, if it is large enough and slow enough, she will pick up and place on her tongue and swallow whole, feeling its legs scrabbling briefly against her soft palate before it goes down. She does not think of this as strange. It is simply what the place provides.

She drinks from the cistern, cupping her hands and lifting the water to her mouth, and the cold stays in her chest, and she is grateful without knowing she is grateful, because gratitude is a human emotion and she is no longer quite human enough to name what she feels.

What she feels, mostly, is the condensation. She feels it coming before it arrives—a shift in the air's weight, a slight cooling on her clay-dusted skin, a quality of silence that deepens just before the moisture begins to bead on the stone. She has lived with this rhythm for so long that her body has synchronized to it, her own internal moisture responding to the mountain's exhalation. When the condensation is thick, her skin grows damp, her hair gathers tiny droplets, and for a brief period she is as wet as the walls, a living thing participating in the mountain's slow breath. When the condensation fades, she dries, her skin tightening slightly, and she feels this too, a faint pulling sensation that is as close to discomfort as she ever gets.

She does not sleep in any pattern a person would recognize. Instead, she drifts into stillness at intervals, settling into the doorway of the dwelling with the ajar door, or onto the low retaining wall, or onto the packed earth of her own terrace—for she has a terrace, the last one before the path fades, the one with the twisted olive tree and the single volunteer thistle. She will sit or lie or simply lean, and her attention will loosen from the particulars of the path and the wall and the lichen, and she will enter a state that is not sleep but is not waking either, a suspension in which the boundaries of her body become porous and the mountain's slow processes move through her unimpeded. In this state, she is aware of the water percolating through the hill below the cistern, the small shift of a tile on a roof three dwellings away, the spider in the doorframe rebuilding its web for the first time in weeks. She does not see these things; she is these things, briefly, and then her attention returns to her body and she is separate again, a point of awareness on the path.

She has no language. She once did—she spoke a dialect of the valley below, a tongue with rolling vowels and a vocabulary for olive cultivation and stonework and the phases of the moon—but she has not spoken to another person in so long that the words have dissolved. They are still there, somewhere, in the deeper layers of her mind, but they are like the buried kilns on the upper terrace—circular depressions that once held fire, now filled with scree and wild thyme. If she tried to speak, she would find only sounds, the kind of sounds a person makes when they have forgotten that sound carries meaning: a low hum, a click of the tongue, a breathy exhalation that is almost a sigh but isn't, because a sigh implies an emotion and this is simply breathing.

She does, however, make one sound regularly. When she drinks from the cistern, when the cold water hits her chest, she makes a small noise in the back of her throat—a soft *ah* that is not quite a word, not quite a gasp, a sound of acknowledgment. She is acknowledging the water. She is thanking it without the concept of thanks. The water receives the sound, and the sound falls into the cistern and is absorbed, and the stillness returns.

She has a task. It is not a task in the sense of a duty or a purpose; it is simply something she does, the way the raven crosses the slope or the pipit works the terrace edges. She tends the cistern opening. The stone-framed aperture, worn smooth on one side by centuries of hands and ropes, sometimes accumulates debris—a fallen olive leaf, a drifting thistle seed, the small gray body of a spider that has died and blown into the opening. She removes these things. She reaches into the opening with her clay-colored hand and she lifts out the leaf or the seed or the spider and she places it on the ground beside the cistern. The leaf dries and crumbles. The seed blows away. The spider becomes part of the path grit. She does this whenever she passes the cistern, which is several times a day, or several times a week, or whenever the condensation cycle has completed its round and she finds herself near the upper edge of the cluster. She has done this for as long as the cistern has been the cistern, or at least for as long as she has been the person she is now, and the act has worn a groove in her the way the mountain's dissolution has worn a groove in the world.

Sometimes, when she reaches into the cistern, her fingers touch the water. The cold climbs up her hand, into her wrist, along the tendons of her forearm, and she pauses, her arm in the opening up to the elbow, and she feels the slow percolation of water through the hillside, the seepage that feeds the cistern and the seepage that drains it, the balance that has held for longer than she can conceive. She feels herself as part of that balance—a creature of the same material as the hill, taking in water and giving it back, condensing and evaporating, a small loop nested inside the larger loop, requiring nothing from outside.

She has no memory of how she came to be here. She has flashes, sometimes, when her attention loosens and she drifts into her not-sleep: an image of a hand that might be hers, younger, pressing a tile into place on a roof; a smell of woodsmoke and baking bread; the sound of a voice calling a name that might have been hers, a name with two syllables and a vowel that rose at the end like a question. But these flashes do not coalesce into a story. They are like the shards in the path—fragments of a whole that no longer exists, worn smooth and repurposed, their original context lost.

She does not wonder about her origins. She does not wonder about anything. Wonder is a human activity, a reaching-out toward what is not known, and she no longer reaches. She receives. The condensation comes. The pipit visits. The raven crosses. The lichen grows. She receives these things with the same steady attention she gives to the cistern opening, and she asks nothing of them, and they ask nothing of her.

There is a particular spot on the low retaining wall where she sits more often than anywhere else. It is a place where the wall makes a slight angle, creating a corner just wide enough for her narrow hips, and the lichen there has been worn away over the years by the pressure of her body, leaving a patch of bare stone the exact shape of her seated form. When she sits there, she fits into the depression as neatly as a tile into a roof, and the wall holds her, and she holds the wall, and the boundary between them becomes thin. She sits and she looks downslope toward the valley, blue with distance and haze, and she does not think about the people down there or the smoke or the noise. She simply looks, and the looking is a kind of participation, a joining of her attention to the valley's existence, and after a time—an hour, a day—she rises and walks the contour path to the cistern and checks the opening and, if necessary, removes a fallen leaf.

This is her life. It is not a life in any sense that the valley people would recognize, but it is a life, nonetheless. It is a life shaped to the mountain's rhythms, a life that has become a property of the place. She is the hamlet's inhabitant, its single living resident, and she is also the hamlet itself in some small, irreducible form—the clay walls reshaped by hands and returned to stillness, the path trodden to a single surface, the water held in balance, the condensation coming and going without falling. She is the record still running, the making finished, the place continuing to be what it has always been.

A drop gathers on the cistern's stone lip. She is there. She watches it hesitate, and she watches it fall, and when it strikes the stone with its small wet sound, her lips part slightly, and she makes the sound she always makes—*ah*—and the silence receives it, and the stillness closes back, and she returns to the wall, to the depression worn in the lichen, to the looking and the being and the slow, suspended time in which nothing changes and everything is the product of change.

The raven crosses. The pipit works the terrace edges. The condensation cycle completes its round. And the one who lives here—the one who is here, who is the here—continues.
