Lena has been here for a long time.

She knows this, though she cannot say how she knows. There is no calendar here, no tally of waking and sleeping. Her body rests when it needs to, curled on the cool stone floor, and wakes when the rest is done. She eats when the hunger comes, which is rarely. She does not remember arriving. She does not remember any other place. The chamber is the whole of her experience, and it feels sufficient, and so she does not question it.

She is small. Not child-small—there is no youth in her—but scaled down, as if she were a woman molded from a smaller mold than the world usually uses. Her bare feet are tough and broad, toes slightly splayed from a lifetime of gripping wet stone. Her hands are the same. The pads of her fingers are textured like fine sandpaper, and they find purchase on the slickery shale without thought. Her skin is the colour of the chamber’s dim light: a greyish, muted olive, the shade of something that has never seen direct sun. Her hair, long and unbraided, is a darker grey, almost black when it is wet, which it always is. It hangs in ropes down her back, and sometimes she twists it idly around her fingers when she sits on the stone bench, waiting for nothing.

Her eyes are the most notable thing about her. They are large, the irises a very pale amber, almost yellow, and the pupils are wide and round in the perpetual twilight. She blinks slowly. Her gaze does not dart; it settles, rests, lingers. When she looks at the weeping wall or the still surface of the bowl-water, she is not merely seeing. She is attending, with a patience that would seem inhuman if you had not seen the chamber itself and understood that patience is the only virtue this place requires.

She does not speak. There is no one to speak to, and she has never learned a language. But she makes sounds: a soft humming sometimes, a low thrum in her throat that matches the frequency of the dripping water. She clicks her tongue against the roof of her mouth in rhythms that echo the tick of stone. These are not songs. They are conversations with the chamber, acknowledgments of the small noises that keep her company.

Her movements are economical. She knows every surface of this room as a fish knows its reef. She knows the tilted planes of the floor, which ledges offer a handhold, which depressions collect the smoothest clay. She knows the exact angle to place her foot to avoid slipping. She moves through the space not with haste—there is no need for haste—but with a quiet, continuous flow, like the water itself.

She has tasks. They are not tasks in the sense of work that must be done, but actions that arise from her being here, the way the crab’s grazing arises from its nature. She tends the bowl. Not that it needs tending—the condensation does its perfect work—but she sometimes cups her hands and dips them into the water, then lets it drain back through her fingers. She drinks occasionally, though the air is so saturated that thirst is a distant, minor thing. But the drinking is a ritual. She kneels before the bench, lowers her face to the bowl, and sips. The water tastes of stone, clean and mineral and ancient.

She also makes things.

Near the low pillar of stacked stones, there is evidence of her work. She has inherited the quartzite hammerstone from the bench, and she has learned to use it. She breaks pieces from the wall where the shale is already fissured, selecting flakes with a careful eye. She understands the grain of the rock, how it cleaves along certain planes and resists along others. She does not make tools—she has no need of cutting edges. Instead, she makes shapes. Little sculptures, no larger than her thumb. She follows the natural geometry of the flakes, but with small, precise taps she refines them. Some become faceted, almost crystalline. Others she rounds by patient grinding against the floor, producing smooth ovoid forms that fit perfectly in her palm.

When a piece is finished—and finished means a feeling she gets, a rightness that settles over the object—she walks it to the low pillar and places it. The pillar’s flat top is now covered with these small stones, arranged in a spiral, each one touching its neighbors. The spiral has been growing for a long time. It covers the whole surface now, and she has started a second layer, balancing new stones atop the gaps in the first. The structure is precarious, but the air is so still that nothing disturbs it. Only the crab, occasionally, on its slow traverse, will pass near the pillar and its delicate vibrations will set the stones to faint trembling. But they never fall.

She and the crab have an understanding. She does not touch it. When she sees its pale form moving along the wall, she stops and watches. She has timed its circuit—not in hours, but in the rhythms of her own body, the number of rests and wakings it takes for the crab to complete a full loop of the chamber. It is roughly four rests. She has no word for four, but she knows the quantity as a felt thing, a pattern. Sometimes she places a drop of water from the bowl on the wall in the crab’s path, and when the crab reaches it, its antennae wave with what she interprets as pleasure. She does not know if crabs feel pleasure. It does not matter. The gesture is a kind of company.

She does not feel lonely. Loneliness is an absence, and she has no concept of what is absent. The chamber is full. The water is full. The crab is full. The pillar is full. She is full. There is a completeness to her existence that would be difficult to explain to someone from the outside, someone who had known wind and sun and other human faces. But there is no outside, and no one to explain to.

Still, sometimes she dreams.

Her dreams are not of other places—how could they be? They are of the chamber, but different. In the dreams, the walls are farther away, and the ceiling is high and bright, and there are *others*. She does not see them clearly. They are shapes that move like she moves, but taller, with voices that are not clicks and hums but something fuller, a sound that vibrates in her chest. They are doing something with the walls, striking them with hammerstones much larger than hers, and the sound is a rhythm, a heartbeat of percussion that fills the space. In the dream, she is not afraid. She is curious. She tries to move closer to the shapes, but always the dream fades before she reaches them, and she wakes to the quiet tick of water and the grey-gold light and the single, familiar crab.

When she wakes from these dreams, she feels a sensation she has no name for. It is a small ache, not in her body but in the place where the dream-others were. It passes quickly. She rises, drinks from the bowl, checks the pillar, and listens to the water. The ache dissolves back into the equilibrium.

She does not know that the makers were here. She does not know that the bench she sits on was shaped by hands much like hers but larger, that the pillar she adds to was started by someone else, that the bowl she drinks from was hollowed by a being who also hummed to the stone. The scrape-lines on the walls mean nothing to her but texture. The broken hammerstone at the pillar’s base she has picked up and examined, noting that it fits her hand differently than her own, but she has no framework for understanding that difference as a message from the past. She is part of a continuity she cannot perceive.

And that is fine. The chamber does not require her to understand. It requires only that she be here, that she drink from the bowl and add to the pillar and watch the crab. She is, in the chamber’s slow logic, exactly what was missing: someone to witness. The makers made the space, but they left, and the space waited. Now Lena is here, and her attention completes the circuit. The water fills the bowl, and she drinks. The stone cleaves, and she shapes it. The crab feeds, and she watches. In her presence, the closed loop is closed.

She sits now on the bench, her feet tucked up, her wet hair dripping onto her shoulders. She is humming, a low thrum. Her eyes are half-closed. The water trickles. The crab, somewhere above, continues its endless grazing. The spiral on the pillar holds its delicate balance. Nothing begins and nothing ends, and Lena is content, because she does not know that there is any other way to be. She is a small woman in a small world, and it is enough.
