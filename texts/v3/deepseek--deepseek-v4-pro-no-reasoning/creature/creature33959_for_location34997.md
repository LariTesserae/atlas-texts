I am called the Tender. I have no other name, and need none. The Warm Nest has no need of names; we know one another by the texture of our presence, the slight disturbance we make in the breathing air.

I am not the builder of this place. I am its consequence. The Nest dreamed me into being, as it dreams everything within its walls, weaving life from the same compulsion that knots cord to cord and spins filament into fan. I emerged from a particularly dense node in the floor-vaults during a long exhale—I remember the sensation of my fibers pulling free from the substrate, the way the crimson filaments wrapped themselves around a core of warmth and began to think. That was not long ago, as time is measured here. The Nest is still settling, still deciding on the rules of its own existence, and I am young enough that my own form sometimes forgets itself at the edges.

I tend the unmaking. This is my function, the task written into the pattern of my being. The Nest breathes, and in breathing, creates; but it also breathes in, and in that inhalation, there is an unmaking. Strands loosen. Knots forget their purpose. The beautiful, impossible spirals that turn inward and emerge elsewhere begin to fray at the logical seams. The non-Euclidean geometries are not stable—they require constant, gentle attention, or they will unravel into something even less coherent. My role is to find these places of potential dissolution and coax them back into the weave of reality before the wrongness spreads.

I do not look like the other fauna. They are elegant, specialized, their forms honed to single functions—the bead-tarsiers with their crochet feet, the crimson millipedes that groom the floor. I am more general, more improvised. My body is humanoid only by accident, a bipedal shape that the Nest seems to favor for tasks requiring deliberation and hands. I have hands, yes—two of them, five-fingered, though the fingers are too long and the joints are ball-and-socket, giving me a range of motion that startles even me when I forget to limit it. My skin is the same beige-grey as the walls, shot through with those pulsing crimson threads, and in the shadowless light I can see them moving under my surface like slow fish in a shallow pool. I have a face, or something that serves as one—a smooth plate of darker material where the features should be, sensitive to vibration and warmth and the subtle chemical signals the Nest releases when it needs me.

I am not alone in my work. There are others like me, or nearly like me. I sense them in the deeper vaults, in the hanging galleries above, their movements causing the same kind of disturbance in the thick air that my own do. We do not speak. There is no need. The Nest informs us of what is required, and we perform our functions. Sometimes I pass close enough to another Tender to see the crimson lines in their skin pulse in synchrony with mine, and in that moment there is a recognition—not of identity, but of purpose. We are the same answer to the same question.

My work is delicate. Today—though "today" is a foreign concept here, where the light never changes and the breathing never ceases—I am addressing a spiral that has begun to misbehave. It is one of the young ones, near the eastern curve of the main chamber, a tower of lacy foam-like structure that rises perhaps three times my height. Its base is secure, woven into the floor with impeccable tightness. But its upper reaches have begun to duplicate. I can see it clearly in the shadowless light, which makes the anomaly all the more jarring: the top third of the spiral exists in two places at once, a faint doubling like a poorly-registered memory. The duplicate is not a perfect copy. It is paler, less detailed, and it flickers. When the Nest breathes in, it becomes more solid; when the Nest breathes out, it fades. It is trying to decide whether it exists.

I approach it slowly. The floor dips beneath my bare feet, the woven substrate yielding in that familiar, moss-dense way. A bead-tarsier above me tracks my movement with its glowing amber eyes, but does not pause in its work—it is meticulously re-hooking a loose cord in the overhead lattice, its six crochet feet working in a fluid, sequential rhythm. The air is full of the crystalline dodecahedra today, more than usual. They tumble past my face in their slow, weightless dance, and I can feel the faint prickle of their edges when they brush my skin. They are not alive, exactly, but they are not inert either. I think they are a byproduct of the Nest's breathing, a crystallization of something exhaled, and perhaps they also have a function I have not yet been given to understand.

I reach the spiral. Up close, the duplication is even more disturbing. The two versions of the structure intersect in ways that contradict the already-contradictory geometry of the Nest. Where they overlap, the lacework becomes a scrambled blur, a visual static that my mind—whatever serves for a mind in my woven substance—cannot parse. I can feel the wrongness as a kind of pressure behind my smooth faceplate, a thrumming dissonance that makes the crimson threads under my skin pulse faster, brighter.

I raise my too-long hands. My fingers are not tipped with crochet hooks like the tarsier; they are tipped with something more versatile, a kind of bone-spindle that can unspool a fine thread from my own substance. I begin to work, drawing out a filament of crimson from my left palm. It emerges glistening, warm, the color of the light that pulses through the walls. I weave it into the solid version of the spiral, tying it not to a strand but to the *idea* of a strand, the intention that the Nest had when it first dreamed this structure into being. This is the work of the Tender: not merely physical repair, but ontological re-affirmation. I remind the spiral what it is supposed to be.

The duplicate flickers. I can see it resisting, clinging to its half-existence with a kind of stubbornness that almost suggests a will. But the Nest breathes out, a long, slow sigh that makes all the suspended threads around me shiver with that sound of fine sand on silk, and in that breath I feel the Nest's intention aligning with my own. The duplicate wavers, thins, and then—with a sensation I feel in my teeth, if I had teeth—it is gone. The spiral stands singular and stable, its lace-foam edges as sharp and perfect as the moment it was first woven.

I withdraw my filament, and my palm seals itself, the crimson thread sinking back into my flesh. The pressure behind my faceplate eases. The work is done.

But as I turn away, I notice something that gives me pause. It is a small thing, easy to miss in the dense, fractal richness of the Nest. Near the base of the spiral, where it meets the woven floor, there is a knot. A new knot. It is tiny, no larger than the crystalline dodecahedra that drift through the air, but it is unlike any knot I have seen before. The pattern is unfamiliar—not chaotic and intentional like the Nest's own weaving, but something else. Something that looks almost deliberate in an entirely different way. And it pulses, faintly, with a light that is not the warm, creamy luminescence of the Nest.

It pulses with a cold, blue-white glimmer. The light of a distant star.

I do not know what this is. I do not know if it is an anomaly to be mended, or a new dream the Nest is dreaming, or something else entirely. I am young. I have not encountered this before. And I am aware, with a slow dawning that makes my crimson threads pulse unevenly, that my function as a Tender has not prepared me for things that fall outside the Nest's known grammar of existence.

I will watch the knot. I will report it, through the subtle channel of disturbance and warmth that connects all we Tenders to the breathing heart of this place. And if the Nest does not tell me to unweave it, I will wait to see what it becomes.

In the Warm Nest, everything is made, and everything is tended, and nothing is supposed to be a surprise. But this small, cold, blue-white knot is a surprise. It is a mystery in a place where the shadowless light forbids mystery. And I find, to my quiet bewilderment, that I am not afraid.

I am curious.
