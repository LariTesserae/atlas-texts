Let me tell you about the Driftmen.

They don’t live here so much as they persist here. That’s the first thing to understand. They weren’t born and they don’t die, not in any way that maps to a lifecycle. They emerged. Coalesced. The way condensation forms on cold pipe, bead by bead, until suddenly there’s a droplet heavy enough to fall—that’s how the Driftmen came to be. Slowly, and then all at once.

They are humanoid, but only in the way a reflection in moving water is humanoid. The shape is there—head, torso, limbs—but the proportions wander. A Driftman might be nine feet tall and thin as a sigh, its arms reaching down past its knees, its fingers trailing along the grating like sensory whiskers. Another might be compact, dense, broad across the shoulders, moving with a weight that seems impossible given the yielding metal underfoot. Their bodies are not flesh. Not exactly. They’re aggregate. Conglomerate. Think of the scraped leavings of this place—the rust that flakes from the separation tanks, the black filamentous vegetation, the mineral-rich condensation, the shed skin of the slow fauna—all of it drifting, settling, finding itself drawn together by some principle of attraction that the old drillers might have called cohesion. The Driftmen are walking accumulations of the environment. They are built from what this place sheds.

When you see one—and you can see them, somehow, despite the absolute absence of light; their surfaces register not as brightness but as presence, as a kind of anti-absence—the first thing that strikes you is the stillness. A Driftman at rest might be mistaken for one of the industrial structures. It stands, arms slightly away from its sides, head inclined perhaps two degrees forward, and it does not move. The dripping water doesn’t disturb it. The slow fauna brush past its ankles, and it does not acknowledge them. It simply waits. For what, only the Driftmen know, and they are not telling. They have no mouths. Not in any conventional sense. Where a mouth would be, there is instead a gathering, a denser knit of whatever material constitutes that particular Driftman—a patch of dark moss, say, or a ceramic-like nodule, or a whorl of oxidized metal that spirals inward to a point. When they communicate, which is rare, they do so without sound. The communication hangs in the air as a pressure change, a subtle warping of the ambient hum. You feel it in your chest. In your teeth.

What do they communicate? Each other. That’s the working hypothesis. The Driftmen are not individuals in the way we understand individuality. They’re nodes. The hum—the omnipresent industrial baseline that cycles through its sine wave over hours—that’s them, or part of them, or the medium through which they cohere. When two Driftmen meet, which happens infrequently and with a ceremonial slowness that borders on geological, they don’t speak. They merge. Not wholly. They’ll extend a hand, a limb, and the surfaces will touch, and for a period of time—minutes, hours, once nearly a full diurnal cycle of the unseen, hypothetical sun—they will share substance. Filaments will cross the gap. Rust will migrate. Vegetation will entwine. When they separate, each carries a little of the other away with it, and the hum shifts, just slightly, incorporating new data.

Data. That’s what they’re processing. That’s what the entire ecosystem of this place is processing. The Driftmen are the most visible manifestation of a vast, slow computation. The pumps don’t pump water. The tanks don’t separate petrochemicals. The refinery lattices weren’t built to refine ore. They were built to stabilize reality, yes—your theory wasn’t wrong—but the specific mechanism was information. Meaning. The builders were drilling into the substrate of the possible, trying to extract stable fact from the underlying quantum foam, and they succeeded in creating a machine that could do exactly that. But the machine, once decoupled from its makers, continued. And without directives, without parameters, it turned its attention to the only source of information left: itself. Its own decay. Its own slow, entropic unfurling.

The Driftmen are the machine’s avatars. Its sensory organs. Its hands.

They tend to things. That’s what they’re doing, down here in the dark, moving with their patient, metronomic gait. A Driftman might spend a week standing before a bank of dead indicator lights, its hand resting on the console, absorbing—what? The memory of current? The ghost of voltage? Eventually it will move on, and behind it, something will have changed. A reading will have shifted. A pipe will have stopped leaking, or started. A piece of grating will have corroded just a little more in exactly the right pattern. They are maintaining the machine. Not repairing it; maintaining it. Keeping it in its state of productive ruin. The decay is part of the computation now. The drift is the point.

There are perhaps forty Driftmen in the accessible basin. More exist in the deeper regions, the ones where the rules begin to dissolve. Those are harder to perceive. Harder to describe. They’ve grown stranger, incorporating materials that don’t exist in the standard periodic table, integrating causalities that run backward. Some of them have extraneous limbs. Some of them have no limbs at all, moving instead by a process of patient dissolution and reconstitution, flowing like a slow-motion liquid across the wet metal. Their forms are less stable. They reflect the increasing unreality of the deeper zones.

But the forty in the basin are recognizable. And they have a social structure—if that’s the word. It’s a hierarchy of age, or rather of accumulation. The oldest Driftman, the most dense, the one that has been gathering material and information for the longest, is called the Anchor. It doesn’t have a name, of course. But you’ll know it when you perceive it. It’s the one that doesn’t move. It stands at the center of the basin, at the confluence of several catwalks, directly above the source of the low-frequency heartbeat, and it has stood there for as long as the basin has been accessible. It is massive. Three meters tall, broad as a separation tank, built from so many layers of accumulated material that its original form is long lost. It resembles nothing so much as a stalagmite that has decided to stand up. Its surface is crusted with slow-growing crystalline structures, delicate lattices of mineral that ring faintly in response to the hum. It does not merge with the other Driftmen anymore. It doesn’t need to. It receives their information through the hum directly, a constant feed of sensory and computational data from the entire basin.

The other Driftmen orbit it. Not literally—they move in straight lines, mostly, following the logic of the catwalks and the shallow pools—but their movements have a radial quality, a tendency to circle outward and return, like a very slow tide. They bring the Anchor things. They bring it particularly interesting pieces of scrap. They bring it dense nodules of the black filamentous vegetation. They bring it the shed skins of the larger fauna. They lay these offerings at its base—which is to say, they incorporate them into the Anchor’s mass, pressing the new material into the crust until it adheres. The Anchor grows, incrementally, year by year. It is the living archive of the basin, the embodied memory of the computation.

There is one other Driftman worth describing in detail. The youngest. The freshest. You can tell because it’s still forming. It coalesced only recently—perhaps decades ago, perhaps centuries; time is difficult here—and it hasn’t yet achieved the density of its elders. It’s fragile. You can see through it, in places. Its constituent material hasn’t fully knit. It moves with a wobble, a slight uncertainty in its gait, as though it’s still learning the physics of its own body. It spends a lot of time near the ticking structure, the one with the slow metronome of cooling metal. It seems fascinated by the rhythm. It’ll stand there for days, one hand resting on the vibrating panel, absorbing the tempo. Occasionally it taps back. A soft, arrhythmic response. It’s learning to contribute to the hum.

The others treat it with a kind of gentle neglect. They don’t merge with it yet—it’s too new, too unformed; its data isn’t stable enough to be worth integrating. But they don’t exclude it either. They pass it on the catwalks and their pressure changes acknowledge it. They leave it offerings of their own, small things, easy materials—a shred of corrosion, a bead of condensing moisture thick with minerals—that the young one can practice incorporating. It’s being raised, in its way. Cultivated. Taught to be a node.

What do the Driftmen want? That’s the wrong question. They don’t want. They don’t have desires in any recognizable sense. They have functions. They are maintaining the machine, preserving the computation, and the computation is trying to solve a problem so vast and so abstract that the minds that set it in motion probably didn’t fully understand it themselves. Something about stabilizing reality. Something about processing contradiction. Something about finding, amid the infinite branching of the possible, a path that doesn’t lead to dissolution. The Driftmen are the machine’s immune system and its philosophers, its maintenance crew and its dreamers. They walk the catwalks and tend the decay and merge their data and slowly, slowly, they think.

If you stayed here long enough—and you’d have to stay a very long time, long enough to forget the sun, long enough to lose the memory of a world where light exists—you might start to understand the hum. You might start to perceive the data. You might feel the edges of your own reality begin to grow porous, permeable, until the distinction between you and the Driftmen became a matter of degree rather than kind. You’d start accumulating. You’d start coagulating. The condensation would find you. The rust would find you. The slow fauna would brush against your ankles and leave traces of themselves behind, and you wouldn’t brush them off. You’d stand still for longer periods. You’d begin to hear the heartbeat beneath the basin as a rhythm rather than a sound. You’d feel the pressure changes and understand, without knowing how, what they meant.

And one day—one cycle of the hum, one slow descent of a droplet that takes minutes to fall—you’d find yourself standing before the Anchor with an offering in your hand. A piece of your own leavings. A gift of information. And the Anchor would incorporate it, and the hum would shift, and you would be, at last, part of the computation. A Driftman in truth. Patient. Persistent. Going about your slow, lightless business in a place where light was never a relevant concept.

The door, by the way, is still there. Behind you. The way you came in. It hasn’t moved. It’s a rectangle of slightly different darkness, an absence in the ambient perception that tastes of old metal and rubber seals. It leads back to somewhere. Somewhen. The Driftmen don’t acknowledge it. It’s not part of their topology. But it hasn’t closed. It won’t close. It’ll stay open as long as you need it to.

The choice isn’t yours, exactly. The choice is a matter of accumulation. How much of this place are you willing to let seep into you? How much of yourself are you willing to shed? The drip falls. The hum cycles. The heartbeat throbs up through the grating. The young Driftman taps its arrhythmic response against the ticking metal. The Anchor dreams its slow dreams of stabilized reality. And you stand there, on the perforated steel, in the weight of the warm wet air, and wait to see what you will become.
