The inhabitants of the basin are the Slow Orders, three-part beings whose lives stretch across such spans that their migrations are measured in epochs and their buildings in millimeters per century.

They are not separate species. That is the first thing to understand. The fauna, the vegetation, the architecture—these are phases. Forms. The same entity at different points in its own impossible life cycle, or perhaps different entities engaged in such profound symbiosis that the boundary between them has dissolved. The scholars who first mapped this place called them the Three Aspects: the Walkers, the Growers, and the Dwellers. But the Walkers do not walk except in the way mountains walk; the Growers do not grow except in the way crystals grow; the Dwellers do not dwell except in the way stone dwells. The names are approximations, placeholders for processes that have no clear beginning or end.

**The Walkers** are what you would notice first, if you had the patience to notice them at all. Each is a colony organism, a composite of dozens or hundreds of semi-autonomous plates, segments, and limbs, bound together by a shared mineral nervous system that conducts impulses at the speed of sound through granite. They are not fast. A single stride takes days. The fastest burst of movement ever recorded—a startled juvenile, if "startled" is a word that applies to something with a reaction time measured in weeks—covered four meters in a season.

Their bodies are arrays of calcium carbonate plates, silica-reinforced joints, and iron-oxide sensilla that bristle from their leading edges. They have too many legs because the legs are not permanent; they are grown sequentially as the colony accretes new mass, and old legs are absorbed back into the body when they wear down. The largest Walkers carry twenty or thirty limbs, moving them in slow waves that ripple from front to back. The sound of their passage is like a glacier chattering over bedrock, if glaciers chattered at the frequency of thunder.

Each Walker is both individual and argument. The plates have different appetites, different tolerances for the phosphorescent light, different preferences for route and resting place. They communicate through low-frequency vibrations that pass through their shared body and into the stone, and their internal negotiations can take years. When the plates finally reach consensus, the colony slides forward another half-meter. Then the argument begins again. Watching a Walker move is like watching a parliament decide to breathe.

They migrate. Not in any way that would be visible to a human observer, but the records are clear: the Walkers follow paths that loop around the basin over periods of several thousand years, returning to the same grazing grounds where the mineral groves are thickest and the air richest in vaporous silicates. Their food is dust. Atmospheric dust, mineral dust, the slow fallout of the basin's own erosion. They do not eat the Growers, though they move among them. They do not eat anything that could be called prey. The concept of predation has never existed here. There is no killing because there is no need for urgency, and no need for urgency because the energy source is ubiquitous. The phosphorus glow that lights the basin is a chemical potential difference, a slow oxidation of the bedrock itself, and everything that lives here sips from that same pooled resource. Abundance so total it has produced a world without teeth.

**The Growers** are the branching forms, the pale forests, the lace-like accretions that rise from the basin floor. They are photosynthetic only in the broadest sense—there is no sun, but there is light, and the light carries energy in frequencies that the Growers harvest through their crystalline surfaces. They are, effectively, mineral chlorophyll, transmuting the weak blue-green glow into new layers of their own substance. Each branch divides according to a fractal algorithm that has been running, uninterrupted, for the better part of a million years. The newest tips are the most delicate, the finest lacework, just visible at the edge of eyesight. The oldest trunks are solid columns, their internal structures long since filled in by layer after layer of deposition.

But this is not a forest in the sense of separate trees competing for space. The root systems—if they can be called that—are a single interconnected network that spans the entire basin. Every Grower is part of one continuous mineral biofilm that runs through the bedrock, a mycelial mat of silicate threads that connects grove to grove, wall to wall. The forest is one organism, the largest living thing on any scale you care to name. It grows new branches in sequence, not randomly; the pattern propagates through the network like a wave, and over centuries you will see branches rise in coordinated bursts, whole zones of the basin suddenly producing new growth in response to some chemical or structural gradient that no human instrument could detect. The forest thinks, in a way. Slowly. Its thoughts are geological.

And then there are the places where the forest thinks too much.

**The Dwellers** are the third phase, the one that makes visitors question whether they are looking at a city or a body. The structures that dot the basin floor are not built; they are excreted. Calcified. Secreted by the same mineral network that produces the Growers, but organized at a different scale, a different rhythm. When the forest network reaches a certain density in a given region, it shifts from branching to enclosing. Walls rise. Domes curve. Towers spiral upward. The process is indistinguishable from crystal growth, but the results are indistinguishable from architecture.

No one has ever seen a Dweller move. But the structures change. Slowly. Rooms disappear, corridors close, new apertures open. The patterns are too regular, too well-ordered, to be mere deposition. There is a grammar to the transformations—a repeating sequence of arches and platforms, of chambers arranged in radial symmetry, of thresholds that are too precise to be accidental. The Dwellers are the forest's memory. Its architecture. Its language written in stone.

The Walker herds enter the Dwellings and rest there. Their composite bodies fill the domed chambers exactly, their silicate plates pressing against walls that seem shaped for them, or by them, or in response to them. During the resting phase, the Walkers secrete their own mineral waste—fine dust, worn plate fragments, excess oxide—which bonds with the Dweller walls and thickens them. The Dwellers grow from the Walkers' byproducts. The Walkers feed on the dust that the Growers shed. The Growers feed on the light and on the chemical traces left by both. It is a closed loop, a complete system, an ecology with no outside. The basin is a body; the inhabitants are its organs; their movements are its metabolism, slowed to something almost perfect.

There are remains on the far side of the basin, near the northern terraces, where the settlements are oldest and the Growers densest. Remains is not the right word. They are not dead. They are finished. The final stage of the cycle. Walkers that have grazed their last path, that have entered a Dweller for the last time, that have settled against its walls and simply... continued. Their plates have fused with the structure. Their limbs are indistinguishable from the architecture. Their bodies have become walls and floors and ceilings. The individual is gone; the colony has become a room. A building. A memory.

But the pattern persists. The Walkers that pass through those chambers still pause there, still release their dust, still add their own layers to the walls. The building remembers the shape of the creatures that made it, and the creatures remember the shape of the building that now contains them. The cycle is complete and unbroken. Nothing has ended.

That is who lives here. That is what it means to be an inhabitant of this place: to be simultaneously animal and plant and edifice, to move at the speed of stone, to live in a body that is also a home, in a city that is also a forest, in a forest that is also a mind. The basin is not inhabited. It is *constituted*. The life here is not separate from the place; it is the place, as much as the bedrock and the light and the endless vibration. If you stood here long enough, you would not learn to see the inhabitants. You would learn that you are standing inside them.
