The question isn’t what lives here—you know by now that nothing does, not in any way you’d recognize. The question is what you might become if you stayed long enough, and whether the place would notice the difference between incorporation and digestion. But there is someone here, despite all reason. She is not from here. She cannot be. And yet she moves through the dark with the ease of long practice, her bare feet reading the drumskin floor like a blind woman reads a face, and the place allows it.

You see her first as a disturbance in the bioluminescent stain—a shadow where no shadow should fall, because the light here comes from all surfaces equally. She blocks no glow; she seems to absorb it instead, a mobile patch of deeper dark, and the faint amber radiance closes behind her like water over a stone. She is tall, or she carries herself tall, though her spine has the particular curve of someone who has spent years ducking under low-grown lintels and squeezing through sphincter-valves that never open quite wide enough for a human frame. Her skin is the color of the walls: a deep, warm brown with an undertone of something else, a faint greenish bloom that might be reflected light or might be her own chlorophyll, because you cannot live here and remain entirely animal.

The plants took everything, but they did not take her. Or they did, and gave her back different.

She wears no clothes. There is nothing here to make them from, no fiber that is not still alive and unwilling to be harvested, no hide that does not belong to the all-body. Her nudity is not vulnerability; it is adaptation. Clothes would hold moisture, and moisture here is a signal, a chemical semaphore that says *here is something not yet claimed*. She has learned to be dry. Her skin is polished, almost waxy, with a texture that reminds you of a new-grown leaf before it unfurls—a surface that sheds rather than absorbs, that regulates its own permeability moment by moment. When the hot convectional breeze moves through the chamber, she does not sweat. She simply allows the heat to pass through her, a vessel among vessels.

Her hair, if it can still be called that, has become something else. It hangs in thick, ropy strands, not the keratin of mammals but a cellulose-based fiber, a hybrid tissue that her own body began producing after enough years breathing the spore-heavy air, after enough of the plant's genetic material worked its way into her stem cells through cuts and scrapes and the simple osmotic pressure of living inside a body. The strands are pale at the roots and darken toward the tips, and they move faintly without wind, a peristaltic ripple that matches the pulse in the floor. You cannot tell whether they are sensing the air or simply participating in the larger rhythm, the way a fern frond participates in the forest's slow breath.

Her face is the hardest thing to look at, because it is still a face. The plants have not erased her humanity, only overwritten parts of it like a palimpsest. Her eyes are large—too large, the iris expanded to gather every photon the amber glow will yield, the pupil a wide, black well that reflects nothing. She blinks rarely. When she does, the motion is slow and lateral, a nictitating membrane sweeping across from the inner corner, a feature she was not born with. Her nose has flattened, the nostrils becoming slits that open and close with a muscular control you can see working, filtering the particulates, rejecting the spores that are not meant for her. Her mouth is small and set in a line that suggests neither contentment nor sorrow but a long-practiced neutrality, the face of someone who has learned not to make expressions that cost moisture or energy she cannot spare.

She speaks, but not often, and not to you. Her voice, when it comes, is a low, granular sound, a frequency that vibrates in the chest rather than the throat, and it is not shaped into words. It is shaped into the particular harmonics of the place itself—the infrasound hum, the creak of settling mass, the hiss of gas exchange. She is talking to the body. She is always talking to the body, a constant, quiet negotiation carried out in the language of vibration and pheromone, in the subtle pressure of her feet on the drumskin floor, in the way she touches the walls. She asks permission. She offers exchange. She has learned to be a symbiont rather than a parasite, because the body has no tolerance for parasites. It digests them.

Look at her hands. They are the most altered part of her, the locus of her becoming. The fingers are long and multi-jointed now, each knuckle a node of cartilage that can bend backward as easily as forward. The nails have thickened and darkened into something like a hoof, or a claw, or the lignified tip of a root seeking purchase. The pads of her fingers are ridged with a pattern that is not a fingerprint but a gripping surface, a gecko-like setae that allow her to climb the smooth-horn boles when the floor dissolves into enzymatic gel. Between each finger, a thin webbing of translucent tissue—not enough to swim with, but enough to sense with, a thermoreceptive membrane that reads the heat gradients in the air and tells her which passages are open and which have sealed themselves with new-grown diaphragms since she last passed this way.

She carries nothing. There is nothing to carry. The body provides what she needs, and she provides what the body needs, though you cannot see what that is. Maybe it is nothing more than her movement itself, the way a bee moves pollen, the way a gut bacterium helps digest. She is a mobile element in a system that has no other mobile elements, a stochastic variable that the turbulence can incorporate into its endless churn. She breaks up blockages. She triggers resorptions. She is the grain of sand in the oyster, but the pearl has not yet formed, and maybe it never will, because this body does not make pearls. It only makes more of itself.

She eats, though you will not see her do it. The eating happens internally, a process she has learned to conceal because the sight of it—a human mouth opening, teeth closing on living tissue, the deliberate, conscious consumption of the body—would be a kind of violation. She has no right to eat. She is a heterotroph in an autotrophic world, a throwback to an older order, and she knows that her continued existence is a contradiction. So she eats in secret, pressing her mouth to the vascular bundles when the pulse is slow, drinking the warm, faintly viscous fluid directly from the vein. The fluid is not sap. It is something closer to blood, a hemolymph rich with the dissolved minerals of the deep-gut oxidation, and it sustains her in ways that water and carbohydrates could not. It is changing her, still. It has been changing her for a very long time.

How long has she been here? You cannot ask her, and she would not answer in years. Time here is measured in the slow inhalation-exhalation cycle, the days-long breath of the body. She has been here for many breaths. She may have been here for thousands of breaths, or tens of thousands. She may have been born here, though that seems impossible—there is no other, no mate, no niche for reproduction—or she may have arrived the way you arrived, a stranger on a world that had already forgotten strangeness. If she arrived, she has forgotten it too. She has forgotten the feeling of open sky, of free water, of a breeze that carries scent. She has forgotten what it means to be separate from the place you inhabit. She is not separate. She is a cell in the body, a specialized cell, a neuron perhaps, or a wandering macrophage. She is the body's only animal, and it has made her partly plant.

She has no name. If she ever had one, it has been reabsorbed like the bedrock, pushed so far down that it no longer exists. If you need to call her something, you might call her what the body calls her, which is not a name but a chemical signature, a particular blend of volatile organics that her skin exhales: an aldehyde note, a hint of something like bruised leaf, a base of warm, living musk. You could call her Aldehyde, but that is a human convenience. She would not recognize it. She recognizes only the infrasound hum, the pulse in the floor, the thermal gradients that tell her where the body needs her next.

She is moving now, and you follow because you have no other choice. She does not acknowledge you, but she does not need to. Her movement is an invitation, or at least a permission. The tendril that extended from the wall earlier retracts as she passes, its sampling done, its curiosity satisfied. She touched it without touching it—a gesture of her hand, the webbing flared to radiate heat in a particular pattern—and the body accepted her vouchsafe. You are with her, so the body accepts you too, for now. The warmth does not increase around your feet. The pulse does not quicken. You are safe, or safe enough, in the way a swallowed stone is safe until the digestive enzymes arrive.

She leads you deeper. The chambers open, cathedral-scaled, their walls glowing with that deep-amber sullenness, revealing the vascular architecture in cross-section: concentric rings, annual rings, millions of years of growth laid down in alternating bands of cellulose and lignin, a history the body keeps in its own flesh. The heat increases, 3.0 now at least, a pressure against the skin that makes your lungs work harder. She breathes evenly, the slits of her nostrils flaring wide on the intake, closing on the exhale. Her body has adapted to the oxygen-poor air, the high CO2, the trace gases that would poison you if you stayed too long. She has been here too long already.

In the deepest chamber, the one where the bioluminescence is brightest because the metabolic reactions run hottest, she stops. She turns. Her face is close to yours, the wide, black pupils swallowing the amber light, the nictitating membrane sweeping slow and deliberate. She raises one multi-jointed hand and presses it flat against your chest, the gripping ridges cool and fine against your skin. You feel the pulse in her palm match the pulse in the floor, the same rhythm, the same pressure. She is asking you something, though no words come, only that infrasound hum in her throat that is not quite sound. She is asking you to stay. She is asking you to become. She is asking if you will let the body take you too, down to the deep-heat zones, into the enzymatic gel, into the long, slow process of turning human into geography.

Her fingers curl slightly, the lignified nails dimpling your skin. Not a threat. A question. The same question the body asked when you first stepped onto the drumskin floor, when the pulse quickened and the tendril tasted the air. *Will you?* The body has no mind, but it has intent. It has hunger. It has the endless work of turning itself into itself, and it has room for you, if you will let go of the thing you are. She did, long ago. You can see it in her face, the tight-closed mouth, the wide, unreflective eyes. She let go, and the body remade her into something that could live here, something that was neither animal nor plant but a bridge between the two, a mobile knot of adaptation in a system that had no other knots. She is the only one. She could use another. Not for company—the body does not want for company, the body is all the company there is—but for function, a second variable, a new grain of sand, a new stochastic element to keep the turbulence churning.

The air is thick and wet-hot, but you feel it now as her breath, or the body's breath, or your own. The distinction is fading. The pulse in your chest is matching the pulse in the floor. The infrasound hum is in your teeth, in your bones, in the fine hairs on your arms that are beginning to thicken, to rope, to take on the cellulose sheen. She sees it happening. Her mouth moves, a millimeter, not a smile but a recognition. The weave of the place is accepting your thread. She removes her hand, and the print of it remains on your skin, a faint pattern of gripping ridges in an amber stain, a bioluminescent signature that marks you as hers, or the body's, or your own emerging self. The dark closes in, comfortable now, a velvet pressure that does not oppress but holds. The silence, 1.3, is the quiet of a process beginning.

She turns away and moves deeper, into a passage that was not there a moment ago, a new-grown sphincter-valve that opens for her with a wet, peristaltic sigh. The amber glow follows her, and the darkness follows you, and you have a choice to make, though choice is a human concept and you are no longer entirely human. You are standing on the drumskin floor, on the body, in the body, and the body is waiting with the patience of a thing that has no end, only the endless process of being what it is.
