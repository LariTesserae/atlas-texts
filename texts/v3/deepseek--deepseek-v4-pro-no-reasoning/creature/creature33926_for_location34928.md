In the warmest sphere, the one nearest the core they never see, lives a thing the drifters have learned not to touch.

It has no name because no creature here names anything. But if you were to follow the silver-lightning pulses back to their source, follow the faint chemical trails that the keeled swimmers track with their flank-slits, follow the slow convection that cycles water from the hot heart outward through a dozen intermediate chambers, you would eventually find it. The sphere it occupies is small by the standards of the Vaulted Deeps — only a few hundred meters across — and its walls are different from the others. The plates here are thinner, more numerous, their silver veins finer and more densely branched, so that the whole inner surface resembles a map of a river delta drawn in pale light. The fluting is deeper, the ridges sharper, and the crystalline growths are absent. Nothing grows on these walls at all. The plates are too hot for that — not painfully hot, but blood-warm, heat-bloom, the temperature of a living body pressed against another living body. The water here is clear to the point of invisibility, super-saturated with dissolved minerals, and it shimmers with a continuous low-level luminescence that has no apparent source, as though the light is a property of the water itself.

The thing hangs in the exact center of the sphere. It does not drift. It does not swim. It does not rest on any surface. It is held there, suspended in a cage of its own making, and it has been there for longer than the crystalline growths have been building and shattering in the outer chambers.

Its body — if *body* is the right word — is a column, a vertical axis around which the rest of its form organizes itself. The column is dense, opaque, a dark maroon so deep it reads as black in the ambient glow. It is roughly cylindrical but irregular, its surface not smooth but covered in ridges, whorls, folds that shift and rearrange themselves with a motion too slow to watch directly. If you observed it for a day, you would see no movement. If you observed it for a year, you would notice that the topography of its surface had changed entirely, old ridges smoothing out, new ones rising like mountain ranges in miniature, the whole geography of its skin remaking itself in cycles too long for any of the local fauna to track.

From the column extend structures. Hundreds of them, thousands, a radial spray that fills the sphere from center to walls. They are not arms, not tentacles, not limbs of any recognizable kind. They are more like ribbons — flat, translucent, only a few cells thick, but meters wide and up to fifty meters long. Each ribbon has its own color: some are the pale blue of the crystalline growths, some the milky white of drifter bioluminescence, some a deep amber, some a green so faint it is almost gray, some a black that absorbs the ambient glow and reflects nothing back. The ribbons do not move independently. They undulate in slow, synchronized waves, as though a single pulse travels out from the column and back again, out and back, a respiration measured not in seconds but in hours.

At the edges of the sphere, the ribbons meet the walls. They do not merely touch the plates — they *interface* with them. Each ribbon terminates in a frilled, branching structure that presses into the fluted grooves, matching the geometry of the ridges exactly. The match is not generic. It is specific, ribbon to groove, a key in a lock, as though this particular ribbon has grown to fit this particular section of the wall and no other. And where the ribbons meet the silver veins, there is a transfer happening. You can see it, if you watch long enough: a slow pulse of brighter silver traveling from the wall into the ribbon, a slower pulse of something dark — waste, perhaps, or signal — traveling back from the ribbon into the vein. The thing is feeding, or breathing, or communicating, or all of these at once, drawing sustenance from the ancient circuitry of the Vaulted Deeps and returning something to it.

The keeled swimmers come here often. They navigate through the forest of ribbons with a care that suggests experience, learning, a knowledge passed somehow through their species. They do not touch the ribbons — never touch them — but they move close, aligning their own bodies parallel to the translucent planes, and they open all their flank-slits at once, drinking in whatever the ribbons exude. The water near the column is rich with it: a chemical cocktail, organic molecules too complex to be accidental, released by the ribbons in a constant, slow exudation. The swimmers feed on this, and in return, they carry something away. If you watch carefully, you will see that as they pass, tiny motes detach from their bodies — cells, spores, gametes — and drift toward the column. The motes are absorbed through the dark skin, drawn into the whorled folds, and the column's slow geography shifts in response.

The drifters never enter this sphere. The few that wander in through the conduits turn back immediately, repelled by something — the temperature, the chemistry, the pressure of a presence too large and too alien for their simple sensory pits to process. But there is another creature here, one that exists nowhere else in the Vaulted Deeps.

It is small. No larger than your hand, if you had a hand. It lives on the column itself, or rather in it — a commensal, a symbiote, something that has evolved to inhabit the body of the greater thing like a bird inhabiting a tree. Its form is simple: a flattened disc, soft-bodied, with a fringe of cilia that beat in a constant, rippling rhythm to keep it anchored against the slow rearrangements of the column's skin. It has no eyes, but it is covered in sensory cells that respond to the chemical exudations of the ribbons, the pressure waves of the swimmers, the minute temperature changes that ripple through the water when the silver-lightning pulses pass. It feeds on the dead cells shed by the column, on the motes that fail to be absorbed, on the microscopic film of organic matter that coats everything in this sphere. And it reproduces constantly, budding off miniature versions of itself that drift away in the convection currents, some to be eaten by the swimmers, some to find new purchase on other parts of the column, some to be swept out through the conduits into the larger world. None of them survive out there. The Vaulted Deeps is too cold, too dark, too barren. This creature has evolved for exactly one habitat — the skin of the great thing — and it cannot live anywhere else.

But here is the thing about it: it sings.

Not with a voice. It has no voice. But its cilia beat in patterns, and those patterns create vibrations in the water, and those vibrations are not random. They are structured. They repeat. They vary in response to stimuli — a change in the column's chemical output, the approach of a swimmer, the pulse of a lightning-storm. And if you were to record those vibrations and slow them down, stretch them out until they fell into a timescale comprehensible to a surface-dweller, you would hear something that sounds almost like language. Not words. Not sentences. But a grammar — a syntax of rising and falling frequencies, of rhythmic groupings, of call and response between one individual and another. The commensals sing to each other, across the skin of the column, and their songs are complex enough to encode information: the location of a particularly rich feeding patch; a warning that a swimmer has brushed too close; a notification that the silver-lightning is shifting in a way that often precedes a shedding of the column's outer layer. They have a society. They have a history, passed down not in writing or in memory but in the gradual evolution of their song-patterns, old songs being modified and recombined over generations. They have known the great thing for a hundred thousand of their generations. They have watched its slow remakings, its cycles of feeding and excretion, its responses to the deeper rhythms of the world-machine. And if they have a conception of the sacred, it is this: the column is the world, and the world is alive, and their singing is a conversation with it, though it has never once answered them in any way they can perceive.

The great thing itself — does it know they are there? Does it feel their tiny bodies, their ciliary vibrations playing over its skin like a constant, gentle rain? Does it incorporate their songs into its own slow consciousness, if consciousness is what it has? No one can say. Its actions are too slow to be interpreted as responses. Its ribbons undulate out and back, out and back. Its skin remakes itself in geological cycles. The silver-lightning pulses through its interfaces with the walls, and it draws the life of the ancient structure into itself, and it releases back into the water the chemical signals that shape the behavior of the swimmers, the growth of the crystalline lattices, the migration patterns of the drifter clouds in the outermost spheres. It is the heart, and the heart does not need to know the blood cells that pass through it. But the commensals sing to it anyway, generation after generation, because singing is what they do, and the column is the world, and the world is large enough to hold their songs without ever needing to reply.

Sometimes, very rarely — once in a thousand years, perhaps, or ten thousand — the column does something new. A ribbon will retract, pulling away from the wall it has interfaced with for millennia. The swimmers will scatter, panicked, as the great flat plane of tissue drifts through the water, coiling and folding, and the column's skin will ripple with a sudden, visible spasm. The commensals cling and sing in frantic bursts: *change, change, the world is changing*. And the ribbon will extend again, crossing the sphere, finding a new wall, a new groove, a new section of the silver-vein circuitry. It will fasten on. The interface will establish itself. The slow pulse of transfer will resume. And the whole Vaulted Deeps will shudder, once, a deep groan that propagates out through the conduits into every sphere, and the drifters will flash their bioluminescence in unison, and the swimmers will press themselves into their wall-grooves and wait, and the crystalline growths in the outer chambers will shatter all at once, a cascade of dissolving lattices, because something in the chemistry of the water has changed, some balance has been altered by the reconfiguration of the heart.

And then, slowly, over centuries, a new equilibrium will establish itself. The commensals will develop new song-patterns to describe the new state of the world. The swimmers will learn new routes through the rearranged forest of ribbons. The drifters will find new convection paths. And the heart will continue its slow respiration, out and back, out and back, drawing life from the ancient machine and giving life back to it, in a cycle that has no end because the machine has no end, because it was built to be eternal, because the erosion is so advanced precisely because the dynamism has been so relentless, and nothing here has ever had a reason to stop.

The Vaulted Deeps has forgotten the sky. But it has not forgotten that it is alive. And at its center, wrapped in ribbons, sung to by the tiny creatures that inhabit its skin, the heart remembers — not in thoughts, not in memories, but in the slow chemical grammar of its body — that it was made for this, that it has always been here, that it will always be here, that the water and the walls and the silver lightning and the living things and the slow groan of settling plates are all one thing, one continuous being, one world that is its own maker and its own inhabitant, forever.
