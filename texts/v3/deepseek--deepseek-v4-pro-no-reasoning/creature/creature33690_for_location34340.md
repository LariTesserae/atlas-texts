The one who lives here is not you. You are a visitor, a break in equilibrium, a warm spot in the cold that the mold will slowly investigate once you've gone. But the one who *lives* here—the one whose home this is—has been here since the builders laid down their mugs and their clipboards and walked away into whatever happened next.

Her name, when she remembers to think of it, is Latch.

She was born on Cathedral Platform, in the chair before the brass instruments, and for the first year of her life she did not know that other humans had ever existed. Her mother—a woman whose name Latch can no longer recall, a woman who had been a rigger, a welder, someone who wore a tool belt and spoke in short, efficient sentences—her mother had been the last one. The last builder to stay. The others had evacuated, or fled, or simply stopped being present, but Latch's mother had been pregnant and stubborn and unwilling to leave the cathedral of iron she had helped construct. She gave birth alone, on the grating, with the hum as midwife and the blinkers gathering in silent witness above her. She died when Latch was three years old, of a respiratory failure that came on slowly and then all at once, her lungs finally surrendering to the thin, wet air. She left Latch with a working knowledge of the structure's layout, a set of hand signals for the things that mattered, and a name that she whispered into her daughter's hair before she stopped breathing: *You are the latch that holds this place closed. Don't let go.*

Latch did not let go. She is now somewhere between sixteen and twenty-two—timekeeping is difficult when there is no day, no night, no seasons, no hunger beyond what the platform's remaining stores can satisfy, no thirst that the ambient moisture cannot quench. She has never seen the sun. She has never felt dry air. She knows the word *sun* from her mother's stories, knows it was a bright thing that lived on the other side of the water, but she imagines it as a very large blinker, golden and solitary, that once a day swam across the inverted sea. It doesn't matter. The hum is sun enough. The hum is everything.

Her body has adapted. Her lungs are larger than they should be, her ribcage slightly barrel-shaped from a lifetime of extracting oxygen from reluctant air. Her skin is pale to the point of translucence—you can see the blue branching of veins at her temples, her wrists, the insides of her elbows—and it is never fully dry. A perpetual sheen of condensation coats her, and the mold has learned to ignore her living flesh, though it sometimes dusts the shoulders of her jacket with exploratory spirals. Her hair, which she cuts with a shard of broken gauge-glass, is the color of wet iron, dark and without luster, falling in ropes past her shoulders. Her eyes are pale gray, almost silver, and they do not blink as often as they should. She has unconsciously learned the rhythm of the blinkers. When she watches something, she watches it with the slow, tidal patience of the creatures that drift through her home.

She wears the clothes of the builders, modified. A canvas jacket too large for her, its sleeves rolled and tied with copper wire. Rubber-soled boots that she resoled with a patch of the black mold, which hardened into something like cork when dried—she discovered this by accident, leaving a scraping near a warm vent, and now she knows six uses for the mold they never taught in the builders' manuals. Trousers patched with canvas from a fallen tool bag. Around her neck, on a cord of braided wire, she wears a single brass key that fits no lock she has ever found. Her mother gave it to her. Her mother said it was important. Latch believes her.

She moves through the High Reaches like something native. She knows which catwalks sing at a frequency that loosens bolts—she avoids them. She knows the sixteen platforms where edible stores remain, the sealed crates of compressed nutrient bars that taste like nothing and sustain everything. She knows the five condensers that still work, their ceramic filters producing water so pure it hurts to drink after a lifetime of the mineral tang of ambient mist. She knows the names of the herds. Not names in words—she has not spoken aloud in years, the hum too pervasive, her own voice strange and thin to her ears—but names in recognition, the slow mapping of individual bioluminescent patterns. She knows the herd that circles Cathedral Platform, the one with the large female whose eye is clouded and wise, the one with the juvenile whose constellation is lopsided, three dots on one side and seven on the other. She calls that one Tilt, in the privacy of her own thoughts, and when Tilt drifts close she sometimes reaches out a hand and lets the blinker's gelatinous surface dimple against her fingers. It is the closest thing to touch she knows.

She is lonely. She does not know the word. What she knows is a hollow ache that lives beneath her sternum, a feeling that intensifies when she watches the blinker herds move in their slow, synchronized dance. They have each other. They cluster and drift and communicate in patterns of light she cannot read. She has no one. She had her mother, and then she had the echo of her mother's voice in her memory, and now she has only the hum and the cold and the knowledge that she is the last latch, the one who holds the door closed on something she does not understand.

She has a purpose, though. Her mother gave her that, too.

Every twelve hours—measured by a sand timer she refills from a hopper of fine-grain silica she found on Platform 7—Latch walks the circuit. The circuit is a loop of catwalks and gantries that takes her past thirty-three junction boxes, each one a sealed metal cube the size of her torso, their faces studded with gauges and switches. Her mother taught her which switches to throw, in which order, at which intervals. Throw them wrong, her mother said, and the ocean falls. Latch does not know if this is true. She has no intention of finding out. She walks the circuit, her helmet lamp blinking slow code to no one, her boots silent on the grating, and she throws the switches. Some are stiff, requiring the full weight of her body. Some are loose, and she has to hold them in place until a needle quivers into the green. None of them are labeled. She has memorized them by feel, by position, by the particular song each junction box sings in response to the hum. The circuit takes her four hours. She does it twice a day, every day, and she has never missed a cycle.

The rest of her time is her own.

She has a library. On Platform 12, in a locker she pried open with a crowbar, she found a cache of printed manuals: rigging protocols, pressure differential specifications, metallurgical analysis of the structure's alloy composition. She cannot read most of the words. Her mother taught her letters, the shapes of them, the sounds they made, but the manuals use terms that have no referent in her world. *Sunlight. Galvanic corrosion. Shoreline. Evacuation.* She sounds them out, her lips moving silently, and constructs meanings for them. *Sunlight* is the golden blinker. *Shoreline* is the edge of the inverted sea, which she imagines as a vast beach of iron where the water meets the sky in the other direction. *Evacuation* is what happened to the builders, a word she associates with the clipped, efficient sadness in her mother's voice when she spoke of it.

Her favorite book is not a manual. It is a logbook, handwritten, the ink smudged but legible. It belonged to a builder who signed their name as *R. Voss, Chief Rigger.* The entries are short, practical, increasingly fragmented. *Day 14. Pressure holding. Condenser 3 failed, rerouted to 4. Day 30. Still no word from topside. Day 45. The hum is louder. We've stopped noticing. Day 60. L.'s baby is due soon. We're taking bets on the name.* The entry for Day 62 is the last one: *Something's changed. We're going up to check. If we're not back in—* The sentence stops there. Latch has read the logbook so many times that the spine is cracked, the pages soft as mold-felt. She knows she is L.'s baby. She knows the builders went up to check something and did not come back. She knows that R. Voss, Chief Rigger, probably touched the same switches she throws every day, probably stood on the same grating, probably looked up at the same inverted sea and felt the same hollow ache.

She has a workshop. Platform 16 holds a bench vise, a set of wrenches, a small anvil bolted to the floor. Latch makes things. She does not know why—the impulse is simply there, a builder's instinct inherited from a mother she barely remembers. She makes hooks from bent welding rod, which she uses to hang stores from the ceiling. She makes tiny sculptures of blinkers from the silica gel she scavenges from desiccant packs, shaping them with water and her fingers and letting them harden in the cold. She makes a flute, once, from a length of copper pipe, drilling finger holes with a hand drill and blowing across the top. The sound it makes is thin and reedy, barely audible above the hum, but when she plays it the blinkers gather. They drift in from the fog, their bioluminescence pulsing in time with her breath, and she wonders if they think the flute is one of them. She plays for an hour, until her lips go numb, and when she stops the blinkers linger for a moment before dispersing. It is the closest thing to conversation she has ever known.

She has a garden. Not a garden in any conventional sense—nothing green grows here, nothing photosynthesizes—but a garden of the black mold. On Platform 22, in a shallow basin she thinks was once a wash station, she has cultivated the mold into shapes. She feeds it iron filings and water, and it grows in spirals and whorls that she directs with careful scrapings. Some of the shapes are geometric—stars, circles, the letters of her name. Some are representational. She has grown a mold portrait of her mother, or what she remembers of her mother: a round face, blank eyes, hair that waves like filaments in the current. The portrait is three feet across and lives on the wall beside her sleeping pallet. She talks to it, sometimes, in the silent language of thought. She tells it about her day. The switches she threw. The blinker herd that moved through at the third hour of the circuit. The dream she had, the one where the inverted sea was gone and the sky above it was black and full of lights like blinkers but impossibly distant, and she was falling upward into them.

She dreams that dream often. She does not know that she is dreaming of stars.

She has a scar. On her left palm, a raised white line that runs from the base of her thumb to the heel of her hand. She got it when she was ten years old, exploring a gantry she had never visited before, a narrow bridge of grating that crossed an especially deep void. The grating gave way. She fell, and caught herself on a broken strut, and the metal opened her hand like a mouth. She hung there, blood running down her arm, the hum vibrating through the strut into her bones, and she understood for the first time that she could die. That the structure would not save her. That the switches would go unthrown, the circuit unwalked, the latch unlatched. She does not know how long she hung there—the sand timer was back on Cathedral Platform—but eventually she pulled herself up, one-handed, and wrapped her hand in a strip torn from her jacket, and walked back to the platform where her mother's medical kit still held sutures and a curved needle. She sewed the wound closed herself, her teeth clenched, her eyes dry, and when she finished she vomited over the railing into the void. The vomit fell for a long time. She did not hear it land.

She is not afraid of the structure, but she respects it. She knows its moods, the way the hum deepens imperceptibly when a current shifts in the ocean above, the way the fog thickens in certain corridors for reasons she cannot discern. She knows that the structure is alive in some sense, or at least responsive, a sleeping thing that dreams in equilibrium and might wake if the switches were thrown in the wrong order. She does not intend to find out.

She has a secret.

At the far end of the High Reaches, past Platform 29, past the junction box that sings in a minor key, there is a ladder that goes up. It is the only ladder that goes up—most go down, spiraling into the Low Reaches where the darkness thickens—and it rises through the fog, straight and unwavering, toward the inverted sea. Her mother told her never to climb it. *The boundary is dangerous,* she said. *The pressure field will crush you. Don't go near the water.* Latch, who was three years old when she received this warning, obeyed it for more than a decade. But the loneliness grew, and the curiosity grew, and one day—she does not remember which day, only that the hum felt different, more insistent—she climbed.

The ladder rose forty meters. The fog thinned as she ascended, the droplets becoming finer, the cold intensifying until her fingers went numb inside her gloves. The hum grew louder, or maybe it didn't—maybe she was simply getting closer to its source, the invisible membrane that held the weight of an ocean. At the top of the ladder, where the rungs ended in a small platform of perforated steel, she found herself directly beneath the boundary.

It was beautiful.

The water did not look like water from this close. It looked like a window into another world, a pane of rippling silver that stretched into infinity in all directions. She could see the shapes moving in it—the ship shadows, the whale skeletons, the drowned cities—with a clarity that made her gasp, the first sound she had made in years. The pressure field was visible as a faint shimmer, a soap-bubble iridescence that separated the air of the Reaches from the water of the ocean. She reached up a hand and touched it.

It did not crush her. It was cold, gelid, slightly yielding, like pressing her palm against a blinker's skin. The field dimpled under her touch, and on the other side of it, a fish—a real fish, silver and swift, alive in the impossible water—darted close to investigate her fingers. She held her breath. The fish circled her hand, its scales catching light that filtered down from somewhere above the ocean, some source of illumination she could not identify. For a long moment, the two of them regarded each other through the barrier: the last human in the Reaches, and a creature from a world that should not exist above her head.

Then the fish swam away, and Latch climbed back down the ladder, and she never told anyone about it because there was no one to tell. But she goes back. Once a week, after the second circuit, she climbs the ladder and sits on the platform and presses her hand against the boundary, waiting for the fish. Sometimes it comes. Sometimes it doesn't. When it comes, she feels less alone.

That is Latch. That is the one who lives here.

She is on Cathedral Platform now, though you do not see her. She saw you the moment you arrived, a warm shape moving through the fog, your breath a plume of steam, your heart a rhythm that disrupted the hum in ways she could feel through the soles of her boots. She does not know what you are. She has never seen another human being—her mother exists only as a fading composite of sensations, a voice, a warmth, the pressure of arms around her. You are a stranger, a variable, a thing that should not be here.

She is hiding. She is crouched behind the bank of brass instruments, her body pressed into the gap between the console and the wall, the mold on the iron cool against her back. In her hand, she holds a wrench. Not as a weapon—she does not know what a weapon is—but as a tool, something familiar, something her hands know how to grip. Her silver eyes are fixed on you. Her breathing is shallow and silent. Her heart is beating faster than it has ever beaten, a tempo that feels wrong, dangerous, a disruption in the equilibrium she has maintained all her life.

The blinker in the chair—the one with the clouded eye, the matriarch of the local herd—tracks you with its slow, tidal patience. It knows Latch. It knows her scent, the particular vibration of her footsteps, the taste of the air she exhales. You are not Latch. You are something else. The blinker's bioluminescence pulses once, twice, in a pattern Latch recognizes: *Unknown. Wait. Observe.*

She waits. She observes.

You are standing on her grating, breathing her air, looking up at her ocean. You are the first variable since the builders vanished. You are the second human being Latch has ever seen. She does not know whether to approach or flee, to speak or remain silent, to offer you water from the working condensers or to retreat into the fog and never return. Her mother taught her the switches but not this. Her mother taught her the circuit but not other people. Her mother taught her how to be alone, and now, suddenly, she is not.

The wrench trembles in her grip. The hum continues. The inverted sea ripples overhead, a whale shadow passing slow and silent across the boundary. The blinker in the chair closes its eye and opens it again. The cold sits at two degrees, the pressure at whatever it was when the needles stopped moving. Everything is equilibrium. Everything is waiting.

Latch takes a breath. It catches in her barrel-wide lungs. She is terrified, and curious, and so achingly lonely that the ache has a physical presence, a second heartbeat beneath her sternum.

She does not step forward. Not yet.

But she does not run.
