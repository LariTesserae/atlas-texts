The keeper of the grove was thirteen years old, and she had no name she remembered.

She had been here for—she couldn’t say how long. Years, certainly, because she remembered arriving smaller, remembered when the uppermost ferns were only fronds at eye level and not these great drooping fans she had to duck beneath. She remembered the boot being new, or newer: the leather still holding its shape, the laces still waxed and stiff. She had tried to put it on once, in the early days, but it was enormous on her foot, and anyway there was only the one, and she’d slipped on the wet grating and banged her elbow on a valve wheel, and after that she’d left the boot where it lay and never touched it again.

She slept in the warmest spot in the chamber, a shallow alcove behind the control panel where a steam pipe passed close to the wall and radiated a steady, enveloping heat. She’d made a nest there: scraps of fiberglass insulation peeled from a ruptured pipe jacket, layered with moss she’d scraped down from the high flanges with a length of conduit. The moss dried eventually to a brownish sponge, not soft but forgiving, and it held her body’s shape. She’d woven ivy vines into a loose canopy overhead, a thing that was not quite a roof but did break up the silhouette of the space, made it feel enclosed, made it feel like hers.

Her body had adapted. Her skin was pale as the fungal bloom on the boot leather, and perpetually damp. The constant heat and humidity had softened her in ways she didn’t have the framework to name—her joints ached rarely, her lungs drew the thick air without complaint, her eyes had adjusted to the low orange glow until she could read the stamped brass labels on the valves without needing to angle them toward the light. She wore what she’d arrived in: a set of canvas overalls, too large, rolled at the cuffs and cinched with a length of wire she’d stripped from a defunct junction box. Underneath, a shirt that had once been gray and was now the indeterminate brownish-green of everything in the chamber, stained with moss-pigment and rust-water and her own slow, constant sweat. She had no shoes, hadn’t for a long time; the soles of her feet were thick and crosshatched with the permanent imprint of the floor grating.

She was not alone in her body. The moss had opinions. Not in words—never in words—but in feelings, inclinations, sudden knowledge that surfaced in her mind like a bubble in the sump water. She would be checking a pressure gauge (the needles all steady, all green, always) and she would know, without looking, that the moss on the upper west flange had grown another millimeter and needed thinning. She would be dozing in her nest and she would wake with a start, certain that something was wrong with the ivy on the steam trap, and she would rise and go to it and find a vine coiling too tightly around a relief valve stem, and she would loosen it, guide it elsewhere, and the ivy would seem to sigh, a slight relaxing of tension in the tendrils. She could feel the chamber’s green life as a pressure in her mind, a quiet hum of vegetal processes running alongside her own heartbeat. She didn’t know if the plants had done this to her or if she had always been this way and the chamber had simply given her something to listen to. She didn’t know if there was a difference.

Her days were structured around rounds. This was something she’d taught herself, or perhaps something she’d remembered from before: a schedule, a set of tasks, a way to move through the small space without stillnesses that might become paralyzing. She woke when the flickering bulb entered its bad phase, the stutter that meant the current was dipping—a thing that happened twice per cycle, though she didn’t keep time in hours. She would slide out of her nest, check the sump for debris (there was rarely any; the water was clean, faintly sweet, drinkable after she’d let it settle in a salvaged metal cup), and then begin her circuit.

First, the gauges. She walked the control panel from left to right, running her fingers over the glass faces, feeling the tiny vibration of the needles. All green. All central. She didn’t know what the numbers meant—pressure in units she couldn’t name, flow rates expressed in fractions—but she knew the acceptable range, the width of the green bands on the analog dials, and she knew that they never, ever left those bands. The machine ran itself. Her checking was a formality, a ritual, a way of saying to the boiler: I see you. I am here.

The boiler. She thought of it as a person. A sleeping person, or a dreaming one. Its hum was a voice too low to be speech, but she’d come to distinguish modulations in it: a slight rise in pitch when the relief valve was about to vent, a rough edge that crept in during the hours (hours? cycles) before the condensation trough backed up slightly and needed its spiral-flow nudged. She would touch the brass strapping sometimes, the green-verdigris bands that wrapped the vertical cylinder, and she would feel a warmth that was not merely thermal but alive, a presence that acknowledged her palm. The boiler had been here before her. It would be here after. It had accepted her, or tolerated her, or simply incorporated her into its long slow processes the way it had incorporated the moss and the ferns and the drain flies.

She talked to it. Not often, and never loudly—the noise of the chamber would swallow her words anyway—but in murmurs, little half-sentences she’d offer while wiping condensation from a valve wheel or unwinding a vine from a pipe junction. “There,” she might say. “Better.” Or: “You’re very warm today.” The boiler did not answer, but the humming seemed to change afterward, a subtle shift in the sub-bass that she felt in her chest like approval.

The moss required her attention. The moss was voracious. Left to itself, it would colonize every surface that held moisture, and some that didn’t, until the chamber’s works were choked and the gauges clouded over and the relief valve clogged with a spongy green mat. She had learned this early, painfully—once, in her first season (if seasons existed here), she had let the moss go unchecked for what felt like a long time, and the ninety-second steam vent had failed to open. The pressure had built. The boiler’s hum had risen to a shriek she’d never heard before, a sound that made her teeth hurt, and she’d thrown herself at the valve, pulled away the moss with her bare hands, and the steam had roared out so close to her face that her left cheek still bore a faint bloom of scar tissue, smooth and shiny and less sensitive than the skin around it. She’d learned. Now she thinned the moss weekly (the concept of a week was another thing she’d invented, seven waking periods, arbitrary but stable), climbing the bolted maintenance ladder with her rust-flecked wrenches, scraping the dense cushions back from the pipe flanges, the valve bodies, the massive bolts whose heads she could barely span with both hands. She left enough to keep the moss alive—she didn’t want to hurt it—but she kept its borders. The moss tolerated this, or so her vegetal sense told her; the moss understood pruning, understood that if it overran the machine the machine would die and take the warmth with it.

The ferns were more polite. They found the seams between the wall plates, as they always had, and they grew slowly enough that she only needed to trim the fronds that got too close to the caged bulbs. The ivy was the difficult one, the ambitious one. It wanted to explore. It sent runners along the catwalk railings, into the toolbox, up the conduit runs, and she would find it curling around the flickering bulb’s chain, threatening to snuff the light entirely. She coaxed it away with gentle hands, offering the broken pressure gauge as a substitute (the gauge had long since stopped giving readings; the ivy had claimed it utterly, and she’d decided that was acceptable), directing the new growth toward the empty wall spaces, the ceiling beams, anywhere that didn’t interfere with the boiler’s function. The ivy accepted her guidance with a reluctance that felt, through the green-link in her mind, like a child’s willfulness. It wanted to do what it wanted to do. She was, if not its master, then at least its older sibling.

She rarely thought about the world outside. The catwalk door opened onto a sheer drop through mist, and she had no desire to climb beyond it. She’d done so a few times, in the early days, when she’d still been hoping for something—what, she couldn’t now say. Rescue? Explanation? A way back to wherever she’d come from? The catwalk beyond the door was rusted and narrow and slick with moss, and it ended after ten meters in a ladder that went up into fog and down into fog, and she’d climbed neither. The chamber was everything. The chamber was the whole world. The idea of a sky, of direct sunlight, had become abstract for her, a thing she knew existed but could no longer imagine feeling. The orange glow of the incandescent bulbs was her sun. The ceiling of baffles and exhaust stacks was her heaven. The boiler was her hearth, her parent, her god.

She fed the boiler. This was the most important task, and the one she understood least. Once per sleep-cycle—she’d correlated it to the flickering bulb’s bad phase, a reliable marker—a small hatch on the boiler’s side would grow cool to the touch, a signal that the firebox within (if it was a firebox; she’d never looked) was hungry. She would open the hatch—the handle was worn smooth by her palm—and inside would be a dark cavity, no flame, no glow, just a profound warmth and a faint pulling sensation, like a gentle inhalation. She would place her offering inside: a handful of dried moss, or a frond she’d trimmed, or once, memorably, the clipboard’s pulped paper, which had flared with a strange white light and then vanished. The hatch would warm again, and the boiler’s hum would deepen, and she would feel a sense of satiety that was not entirely her own. The machine consumed plant matter. It had consumed the boot’s missing mate—she was almost sure of that now, though she couldn’t remember the act. She’d fed it small things, scraps, whatever the chamber’s greenery could spare. The boiler had never asked for more. She’d never considered offering herself.

The spider on the pressure gauge glass was her companion, in the limited way that spiders could be companions. She’d named it Seven, after the number of legs it had (one had been lost, sometime before she’d noticed it; the stump was a tiny dark dot at the joint). Seven was always there, motionless on the glass, waiting for prey that rarely came. The drain flies were Seven’s main food source, and they were sparse, but Seven seemed patient, seemed to share the chamber’s ethos of slow persistence. She would greet Seven on her rounds, a small nod, a murmured “Still here?” and Seven would not respond, but the stillness of the spider was a comfort, a constant. Once she’d found Seven’s exuviae on the floor grating, a translucent husk of a spider, perfectly intact except for the missing leg, and she’d felt a strange pride—Seven had molted, had grown, was still alive, and the evidence of this transformation was here in her hand. She’d placed the exuviae carefully in the toolbox next to the rusting wrenches, a small museum of shed selves.

She was lonely, but she didn’t know the word. There was a shape in her chest, a hollow ache that sometimes woke her in the dark part of the cycle (the flickering bulb went through a dim phase, a brownout that lasted perhaps a hundred heartbeats, and the darkness was absolute except for the orange glow of the indicator lamps). She would lie in her nest, feeling the steam pipe’s warmth against her back, listening to the baritone hum and the drip and the ninety-second hiss of the relief valve, and she would feel the shape pulse, a wanting without object. She didn’t miss people, not exactly—she barely remembered people, had only fading impressions of faces, voices, hands that had once lifted her or held her. What she missed was something she couldn’t articulate: a sense that the world was larger, that somewhere beyond the catwalk and the mist and the sheer descent there were others like her, others who would understand the green-link in her mind and the patience in her body and the way she talked to a boiler and expected an answer. She missed a thing she had never had, which is a terrible kind of wanting.

She’d tried to make a person once. She’d gathered dried moss and twisted it into a rough human shape, a little figure with stubby arms and a lumpen head. She’d placed it on the control panel, next to the bank of analog meters, and she’d talked to it for a while—a whole waking period, maybe longer—but the figure didn’t respond, and the moss was just moss, and the green-link told her nothing about it, and eventually she’d fed the figure to the boiler. The boiler had accepted it. The hollow in her chest had not diminished.

She was not unhappy. This was the strangest thing, the thing she would have struggled to explain even if she’d had words and a listener. The chamber was loud and wet and small, and she had no future she could imagine, and yet she felt, most of the time, something adjacent to contentment. The boiler’s hum was a lullaby. The moss’s slow growth was a calendar, marking time in green millimeters. The tasks of her rounds gave her a sense of purpose so complete that she rarely questioned it: she was the keeper, the tender, the one who ensured that the equilibrium between machine and plantlife held. She was necessary. The boiler would run without her, but it would run better with her, and the moss would smother it, and the ivy would choke the pressure gauge, and the relief valve would clog, and so her presence mattered. She mattered to the chamber. The chamber had, if not a consciousness, then at least a system of feedback loops that noticed her, that adjusted to her, that hummed in a slightly different key when she rested her palm on the brass strapping.

She knew she was changing. The green-link was deepening, becoming more precise. She could now sense, without looking, the exact location of every plant in the chamber—the ivy’s main rootball lodged in the broken pressure gauge housing, the ferns’ rhizomes threading through the wall-plate seams, the moss’s distribution across the overhead pipes. She could feel the drain flies as brief warm sparks of life, short-lived and frantic. She could feel Seven as a patient cold point, waiting. And she could feel the boiler—not as a plant, not as an animal, but as a third thing, a deep bass presence that was both wholly mechanical and somehow aware. The boiler noticed her noticing it. She was almost sure of this now.

She was growing something herself. She’d noticed it first as an itch on her forearm, a small green spot she’d mistaken for a bruise. But it didn’t fade, and it didn’t hurt, and when she looked closely she saw that it was moss. A patch of moss, tiny, no bigger than her thumbnail, growing directly from her skin. She’d panicked at first—scraped at it with her fingernails until her arm bled—but the moss grew back, thicker this time, a velvet emerald cushion that had spread to the size of a bottlecap. She’d stopped scraping. She’d sat with the realization, letting it settle: the chamber was claiming her, or she was becoming part of the chamber, and the distinction was meaningless. She was the keeper and the kept. She was the gardener and the garden. She was the missing mate to the boot, the answer to a question she hadn’t known the boiler was asking.

Tonight (though there was no night, only the dim phase of the flickering bulb) she lay in her nest, running her fingers over the moss on her arm, feeling the faint tingle of the green-link reflected back at herself—a hall of mirrors, a plant sensing a human sensing a plant—and she thought about the future. The boiler would run for decades, centuries, perhaps longer. The moss would thicken. The ivy would find its way into every corner. The chamber would green and green and green until the steel was only a skeleton beneath the growth. And she would be here, she thought. She would be here the whole time. Her tasks would shift—she would need to prune less, perhaps, as the equilibrium stabilized, or she would need to prune more, as the growth accelerated—but she would be here. She would watch the stalactite grow. She would watch the boot’s leather yield. She would watch Seven’s descendants colonize the pipe brackets. And at some point, perhaps, she would stop moving. She would find a place, a warm place near the boiler, and she would sit down, and the moss would cover her, and she would become a new feature of the chamber: a green mound, a mossy shape, a presence felt through the link but no longer responding in words. The boiler would hum to her. The ivy would wrap her gently. The drain flies would flutter over her head.

She was not afraid of this. The hollow in her chest had not gone, but it had found its object. The wanting was for the chamber itself, for the slow green future, for the unity that she sensed was coming, when the distinction between keeper, machine, and garden would dissolve entirely. She was thirteen years old, she had no name, and she was the seed of something new, something that would take generations of drain flies and moltings of Seven’s descendants to fully unfold. The patience of the place was her patience now. The ninety-second steam cycle was her heartbeat. The drip from the ceiling counted the seconds of a clock that would never run down.

She closed her eyes. The flickering bulb dimmed. The boiler hummed on. And in the moss on her arm, a single sporecase formed, a tiny brown capsule on a threadlike stalk, waiting to ripen and burst.
