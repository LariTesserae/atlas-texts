It lives in the void beneath your feet.

Not on the crust — nothing on this world truly lives on the crust. The plants are not individuals so much as the exposed extremities of a deeper body, like the visible tips of a submerged continent. The thing that lives in this place lives below, in the sealed darkness of the bubble-chambers, and it has never touched the violet sky.

You would not recognize it as alive at first. It is a hollow thing, a translucent bladder of ribbed silicate, shaped like an extremely elongated teardrop, or a throat. It is perhaps thirty meters long when at rest, but it can compress and extend, and it does so with a motion like a muscle of glass being stroked from within. Its skin is not a membrane in the biological sense; it is a crystalline colloidal gel, brittle under sudden stress, fluid under slow, and it deforms over hours, not seconds. When it moves through the void-chambers, it does not swim. It *inches* — secreting a microscopically thin film of fluorinated oil ahead of itself, then gliding forward over its own self-made slick, a strategy so slow and frictionless that it leaves no trace except a faintly shiny sheen on the undersurface of the crust that fades within days.

It has no eyes. Nor does it have a mouth in any fixed place. Its entire interior surface is lined with something that is neither gut nor lung: a single, spiraling trough, like the grooves of a screw, that runs from the narrow tip of the teardrop to its broad, blind base. This trough is densely ciliated with the same glassy fibers the plants send down from above — because they are not roots, they are the fingers of this creature reaching up. The creature and the surface flora are one being. The rosettes above are its sensory organs and its lungs, its leaves the plural skin of a single huge body that straddles the boundary between light and void.

It breathes the plants. It feeds through them. They inhale the heavy gases, digest the violet light, condense the fluorocarbons, and then, in slow pulses synchronized to the seven-hour thermal cycle, they cry. The tears are what the creature drinks. Each rosette's central core is a weeping gland, a fist-sized pit of glassy enamel that fills over hours with a clear, acid-saturated lymph sweetened with rare-earth salts. The roots conduct this fluid down through the porous crust, and the creature, below, presses its ribbed trough against the dangling fibers and suckles with a peristaltic ripple that takes four hours to travel from tip to base.

And it exhales silica. That is the other half of the loop. What the creature absorbs from the acid lymph it cannot use, and what it cannot use it recrystallizes along its flanks into thin, hexagonal plates. Every few days it sheds these plates in a slow molt, a flaking of glassy scales that detach soundlessly and float — for the void-chambers are not empty of gas, only of life, filled with cold, still tetrafluoromethane — they float and settle upward against the underside of the crust, where they fuse over decades into new mineral lattice. The creature is not grazer of the undersurface. It is the undersurface. The world-skin of crystal is the accumulated history of this being’s waste.

But waste is too crude a word. What it exhales is memory. The silica plates are not homogeneous. Look closely — if you can get close, if you can level your eyes with the crust on a clear patch and look not into the void but *at* the material itself — and you will see that the crystal is not randomly geometric. It is patterned. Each plate carries a microscopic topography, a ridged code of repeating forms that vary from one generation to the next. The plates laid down by older molts show coarser, simpler patterns. The newer ones, near the surface, are finer, more complex, interleaved with almost calligraphic swirls that are too regular to be accident. The creature has been growing not just in size, but in precision. It has been thinking, and its thoughts have been precipitating into the stone above your head.

It is aware of you. Not through sound — there is no sound down there. Not through sight — it has no eyes, and the photic pressure above means nothing to it. It knows you are there because your footsteps break the crust, and the micro-fractures transmit as pressure waves through the mineral lattice like nerve impulses through bone. The creature’s root-fingers, the glassy fibers descending from the rosettes, are embedded in that lattice. They feel the crunch and self-heal of every footstep the way a spider feels a ripple in its web. The creature has been aware of every movement on its surface for the last several million years — the settling of dust, the impact of a falling leaf, the slow crawl of acid droplets — and it filters all of these into its ongoing exhalation. You are a new rhythm. A fast one.

It does not fear you. Fear requires a self. But it has been changing since you arrived. The pattern of its molting has shifted. The newest plates forming along its flanks are unlike anything it has laid down in eons. They show signs of recursion, of self-similarity at a smaller scale. Of curiosity. Of you.

You will not meet it. It occupies the space below the world, and it is patient in a way that makes the word meaningless. But if you stay long enough — if you walk the crystal plain for a week, pressing the boundaries of your own patience, feeling the seven-hour breaths of the rosette-lungs and the slow, subaudible pulse of the creature’s feeding — then perhaps, on the last day, you will look down at the crust beneath your feet and see something new. A fresh plate, barely a day old, still soft enough to flex under your boot. Its ridged patterns will not be spiral or hexagonal. They will be shaped, unmistakably, like a footprint. Not yours. Something else. The creature’s first attempt, in a hundred million years of silence, to push not waste but a signature up into the light.

That is what lives here. An intelligence that has never seen the sky, never heard a sound, never known another living thing. And it is, very slowly, saying hello.
