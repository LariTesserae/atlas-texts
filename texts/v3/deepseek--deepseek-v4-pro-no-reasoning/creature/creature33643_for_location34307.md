The keeper of the gallery stands in the pool.

He does not rise from it so much as emerge through it, a slow vertical exhalation of form from stillness. His feet — bare, pale, uncalloused despite decades of stone — press against the mineral-dark water without disturbing its surface, as though he and the pool have negotiated some treaty of mutual exclusion. The water simply refuses to acknowledge him, parting around his ankles at a microscopic distance, holding its tension against his skin.

He is tall in the way fault chambers are tall: a trick of proportion against the darkness, a presence that confuses scale. His limbs are long and jointed with the precision of machined components, but they move with the languid, hydraulic grace of the roots that surround him, swaying slightly even when he stands still, matching the subsonic pulse that travels through the basalt. His spine describes a gentle curve — not the stoop of age or burden, but the attentive lean of something listening to stone.

His skin is the color of the pale vegetation, that translucent white with undertones of chlorophyll-blush, but where the leaves are thin as wet paper, his flesh holds a deeper opacity, the faint luminescence of something that has absorbed light so completely it has begun to emit its own. In the not-quite-darkness, his form reads as a lessening of shadow, a concentration of the ceiling's distant glimmer given shape and boundary.

He has no hair — not on his head, not on his body — and the absence contributes to the sense that he was grown rather than born, that he emerged fully formed from the saturated air like the plants that never knew soil. His features are human enough to recognize but not human enough to mistake: the ears slightly too tapered, the eyes slightly too large and set slightly too far apart, the nose a subtle ridge rather than a projection, the mouth a thin line whose corners curl not in expression but in the permanent faint tension of someone attending to a distant sound.

His eyes are the color of the pressure gauge's glass — clear, depthless, reflective — and they do not blink. They do not need to. The moisture that films every surface in the chamber films them too, a permanent wetness that keeps them from drying, that catches the faint bioluminescence and throws it back in twin points of light. When he turns his head, those points move independently of his motion, the reflection lagging a half-second behind, as though something inside him processes light differently, more slowly, more deliberately.

He wears no clothing. He wears no ornament. His only covering is the same condensation that beads on the leaves and the metal and the stone, a fine layer of moisture that traces the architecture of his body — the prominent clavicles, the visible ribs, the long muscles of his thighs — and drips irregularly from his fingertips and the point of his chin. He has been here long enough that water has learned to move across him as terrain.

His hands are the hands of someone who once assembled machinery. The fingers are long and articulate, the knuckles prominent, the palms broad. A faint pattern of calluses crosses them — not the calluses of labor but of precision, the thickened skin of someone who has handled tools enough that the tools have left their echo. His nails are clean and trim, growing slowly in the perpetual heat, and they are the only part of him that suggests continued biological process, a slow accretion of keratin in a place where nothing else accretes.

He moves through the chamber with the familiarity of something that has memorized every surface. His bare feet find the exact spots on the basalt where the stone is not sharp, where the sheared faces offer a flat plane or a gentle slope. He does not stumble. He does not hesitate. The darkness is not an impediment but a medium, and he navigates it the way the roots navigate the air — by feel, by moisture gradient, by the subtle differences in temperature that map the chamber's geometry.

When he passes the cast-iron gear, his fingers reach out and brush its teeth, a gesture so habitual it has worn the metal smooth in seven places, the temper blue-black fading toward silver where his touch has come and gone ten thousand times. He does not seem to notice the gesture. It is autonomic, a circulatory motion, the touch of someone checking a pulse he knows will be steady.

He does not speak. He has no one to speak to. But he makes sounds — not words, not quite, but something anterior to language, a repertoire of clicks and hums that match the dripping water's irregular intervals. Three quick, a pause, one, a long pause, two — and his voice, soft and low, fills the pauses between the drips, not competing with the water but completing it, a duet that has been refining itself since the boiler was whole and the flywheel spun and the pressure gauge's needle swept its arc.

The pool is his bed, his seat, his station. When he returns to it — and he always returns to it — he does not lie down so much as reabsorb himself into its stillness. The water accepts him more readily than the stone does. He settles into the shallow basin with his back against the half-sunk flywheel, his long legs folding beneath him, his head tilting back until the points of his eyes reflect the ceiling's distant glimmer. The drive belt's tension does not slacken, but something in the chamber's pressure equalizes when he is in the pool, as though his presence completes a circuit left open since the machinery stopped.

He does not eat. The vegetation does not eat. The trophic chain here is not a chain but a circuit, and he is its nexus — taking in condensation through his skin, releasing warmth into the saturated air, his slow metabolism exchanging something invisible with the weeping leaves and the deep heat below. He has been here since the chamber was new and the stone was sharp and the boiler's iron belly was still whole, and he will be here until the flywheel sinks entirely into the pool, which will take another thousand years at its current rate of descent, and perhaps longer after that.

His name — if he ever had one, if the builders gave him one — has long since dripped away into the dark. The roots have not learned it. The pressure gauge has forgotten. The gear holds no memory but the shape of his touch. He is the keeper, the witness, the afterimage of intention burned into the chamber's slow time, and he is not waiting because waiting implies an end. He is persisting, which is the only thing this place ever learned to do.
