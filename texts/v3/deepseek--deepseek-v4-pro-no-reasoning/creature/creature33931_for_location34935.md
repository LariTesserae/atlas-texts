Let me tell you about the one who tends the standing stones.

Her name is Maren, though she doesn't use it often—names here are for emergencies, for calling someone back from the seep's edge in the fog, and those occasions are rare. Most of the time she's simply *the carver*, or *the one who remembers*, or, to the children who've only ever known her bent shape and her copper chisel, *Grandmother Stone*.

She was born here, in the bowl, sixty-three wet seasons ago. She knows this because her mother kept a tally on a basalt rib near the northern rim—small hatch marks, one for each cycle of the frogs' piping—and Maren has continued the count since her mother's hands grew too knotted to hold the chisel. The tally is up past four hundred marks now, running in careful columns down the stone's inner face, and Maren can read it like a lineage. She is the fifth carver. The first was a man whose name she knows but will never speak, because it belongs to the stone now, and speaking it would be taking something back that was given.

She lives in the hut nearest the center, the one with the north-facing wall that's more moss than wattle now. The moss is intentional. She planted it herself, forty years ago, pressing small green plugs into the damp mud, and she's tended it ever since—watering it with the mineral-rich runoff from the standing stones, training it into the concentric rings that spiral outward from her doorway. The rings mean something. They're a calendar, or a prayer, or maybe both. Each ring represents a generation of the settlement, and the spaces between them mark the years when the water rose higher than usual, when the seep flooded, when the frogs fell silent for an entire season and everyone held their breath. Maren is the only one who can read the moss rings now. She's tried to teach others—three apprentices over the decades—but the first died of a lung-rot that filled her chest with fluid until she breathed like the seep itself, and the second left, walked up over the basalt rim one morning and never came back, and the third is still here but has no gift for it, can't see the difference between a ring laid down in drought and one laid down in flood.

That third apprentice is a man called Corrin, and he's the one who scrapes the water channels. He's good at that—practical, patient, attentive to the way the water wants to flow. Maren loves him in her way, which is to say she's given up expecting him to learn the moss and instead leaves small gifts by his door: a copper pin when his old one bent, a bundle of the fleshy-leaved creeper for his wall-gaps, a smooth basalt pebble from the center pavilion that fits perfectly in the palm. He doesn't understand the gifts, not really, but he accepts them, and that's enough.

What Maren understands—what the carvers have always understood—is that the water is not an enemy. The settlement treats it like one, constantly rebuilding, scraping, channeling, fighting the dissolution. But the water is simply *young*, and curious, and it wants to know the shape of things. It runs over the basalt ribs to learn their contours. It seeps into wattle to taste the reed-fibers. It beads on copper to feel the strange cold-smooth of metal. It is not trying to destroy; it is trying to *know*. The settlement's constant repair is a conversation, a back-and-forth between the water's questions and the inhabitants' answers. Maren's job—the carver's job—is to translate.

That's what the standing stones are for. The carvings aren't decoration. They're instructions, or invitations, or boundaries drawn in a language the water can understand. Each spiral tells the water: *here, flow this way*. Each concentric circle says: *this far, no farther*. The two fallen stones broke because the carvings on them were wrong—the fourth carver, Maren's mother, made an error in the hatching, a pattern that told the water to rise instead of fall, and the water obeyed so enthusiastically that it undermined the stones' footings. Maren's mother spent the last ten years of her life recarving those stones where they lay, correcting the mistake, apologizing to the water. Maren still remembers watching her, a small hunched figure in the rain, tapping at fallen basalt while water ran in rivulets down her arms. *It's all right*, her mother would say, not to Maren but to the stone, to the water, to the air. *We're learning together*.

Maren is still learning. The new marking she's working on now—the one you can hear, *tink tink tink*, copper on basalt—is a pattern she's never tried before. It's based on something she saw last spring, when the frogs began piping at an odd time, midday instead of dawn, and the water in the central pavilion rippled in a pattern that looked almost like writing. She's been trying to reproduce that ripple-pattern in stone for six months now. The copper chisel is wearing thin. She'll need to trade for a new one soon—there's a man in the settlement who works the forge, a quiet man with burns on his forearms, and he'll want three bundles of retted fiber and a favor to be named later. That's how things work here. No currency, just favors and needs and the slow circulation of objects through the dense wet air.

She doesn't sleep much anymore. The frogs keep her company at night, their piping rising from the southern seep in waves, and she sits by her doorway with the moss glowing faintly in the dark—it does that, here, some phosphorescent quality from the minerals in the water—and she thinks about the first carver. The one whose name she won't speak. He came here when the basalt was still sharp enough to cut skin, when the bowl was empty except for steam rising from the juvenile water, when the settlement was just a single hut and a single standing stone. He'd been following something, a dream or a vision or just a sense that there was a place where the world was still being made, and he wanted to be part of it. He carved the first spiral when he was old, older than Maren is now, his hands shaking so badly he could only work for a few minutes at a time. The spiral was wobbly. Imperfect. But the water understood it anyway, and it flowed the way he asked, and the settlement grew around that first small act of translation.

Maren thinks about him often. She thinks about the long chain of hands holding copper chisels, stretching back to that first old man, stretching forward to whoever will come after her. Corrin, maybe, though he still can't read the moss. Or the child who carries the water pots—she's noticed that the child walks differently than the others, slower, more deliberate, stopping sometimes to watch the way water beads on a leaf. She's only seven or eight, still small enough to fit through the narrowest seams between buildings. Maren has started leaving copper pins where the child will find them.

The *tink tink tink* stops. Maren sets down the chisel and flexes her hand. The new carving is almost done—just a few more hatch-marks, a final spiral-turn. She'll finish it tomorrow, or the day after. Time moves slowly here, in the wet quiet, and the water is patient. It's been patient for thousands of years, asking its questions, waiting for answers. Maren is just the latest in a long line of people who've tried to answer back.

She looks up, and sees you. She's known you were there for a while—the moss told her, a disturbance in the air, a foreign dryness moving through the damp. She doesn't seem surprised. Strangers come, sometimes, climbing over the basalt rim and standing in the narrow paths with their mouths open, trying to understand what they're seeing. Most of them leave. A few stay. Maren doesn't judge either way. She just watches, her eyes pale and wet-looking in the pearlescent light, her hands resting on the warm stone.

"You're dry," she says. Her voice is low, roughened, the voice of someone who speaks more to stones than to people. "Come. There's water."

She gestures to one of the standing stones, the one with the deepest cupped top, full to the brim with clear mineral water. A bead of condensation runs down its side, slow as honey.

"It's safe," she says. "It's young, but it's safe. It's been waiting for you."

And then she picks up her chisel again, and the *tink tink tink* resumes, and the settlement breathes its slow wet breath around her, and everything continues.
