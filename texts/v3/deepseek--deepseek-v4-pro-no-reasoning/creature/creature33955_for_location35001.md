She is known by a sound first, by a rhythm second, by a name only third and then reluctantly. The sound is the knock of her knuckles against pipe flanges as she passes—a percussive habit, testing for hollowness, for resonance, for the subtle change in pitch that means corrosion or sediment or pressure where there shouldn't be. She does it without thinking, the way a priest touches a rosary. The rhythm is her walk: three steps, pause, two steps, pause, shift weight to the left leg. The left knee doesn't bend all the way anymore—an old injury, something that happened topside years ago and never healed right in the constant humidity. The name is Mae, but nobody uses it. The few who work this shift call her "the knocker" or just "she," because there's only one woman down here and no need for specificity.

Mae has been coming down into the sump complex for twenty-three years. She started as a third-shift monitor for the treatment authority, back when there was still an authority, still a shift schedule, still a logbook that someone checked. The authority still exists on paper—she gets a paycheck deposited every two weeks into an account she never looks at—but the logbook hasn't been signed by anyone but her in over a decade. The pages are swollen with humidity now, the ink blurred into grey clouds. She still fills it out. Date. Time. Sump levels. Pump RPM. Unusual sounds. Unusual smells. Amphibian activity. Last entry before this one was always hers. She writes her own name in the "relieved by" column and feels something that might be amusement or might be something else.

Her body has adapted to the place in ways she doesn't think about anymore. Her ears have learned to filter the pump hum, to hear a valve leak or a bearing going bad as clearly as a shouted word. Her eyes parse the dimness without strain; she can read a gauge at ten paces in light a surface-dweller would call darkness. Her lungs have adjusted to the metallic air, the spores, the faint chemical tang that changes character depending on which sumps are active. She doesn't cough anymore. Her skin has developed a relationship with the humidity that isn't quite symbiosis but isn't rejection either—she is always damp, and the dampness has become a second skin, a condition she only registers when she goes topside, once a month, to buy supplies, and the dry air makes her feel flayed.

She lives in a corner of the complex that she has claimed and shaped over decades. It was originally a storage alcove, a recessed bay between two major pipe runs where spare gaskets and valve packing were kept on metal shelving. The shelving is still there, but the gaskets are long gone, and the shelves now hold her things: canned food arranged by type, a hot plate that runs off a tapped electrical line, a collection of paperback books she's read so many times the pages have softened to cloth. She sleeps on a cot with a foam mattress that has molded itself to her shape. The mattress is covered in a waterproof casing that crinkles when she moves. She owns four sets of coveralls, identical, dark blue, patched at the knees and elbows with fabric from older coveralls that had become more patch than original. She washes them in a sump that doesn't carry chemicals and hangs them on a pipe near a warm pump housing; they dry in a few hours and smell faintly of iron.

She is not alone, exactly. There are other people who come down—maintenance crews from the authority, occasionally, though they're always younger, always new, always blinking in the dimness and shouting at each other about things she could have told them if they'd asked. There's a man who descends once a month to check the electrical panels; he brings her mail, which is mostly nothing, and she gives him a list of supplies, and he returns a week later with what he could find. There are drifters sometimes, people who've heard about the complex, who come down looking for shelter or scrap or something they can't name. She lets them stay if they don't touch the valves. Most leave within a few days, driven out by the noise, the damp, the sense that the place is watching them. Some stay longer. One stayed a year, a quiet man who never gave his name, who tended the moss beds with a gentleness that surprised her. One morning he was gone, and she found his cot folded neatly, his coveralls washed and hung, a note that just said "thank you" weighted down with a brass fitting he'd polished to brightness.

Her days follow the rhythm of the pumps, not the sun. She wakes when the large sump's recirculation cycle shifts—a change in the hum's frequency that she feels in her sternum—and she makes coffee on the hot plate, drinks it while sitting on the edge of her cot, watching steam rise from the cup and join the ambient mist. Then she walks the complex. The route never varies: the north pipe gallery first, checking the pressure gauges, tapping each flange with her knuckles, noting which ones sound dull (moisture ingress, monitor) and which ring clear. Then the catwalks, high and low, looking for pooling water, new rust, vegetation encroaching where it shouldn't. She pulls ferns out of the grating by hand when they grow through. She doesn't hate them—she's grateful for anything green—but they'll trip someone eventually. Then the sumps, all of them, checking levels, checking clarity, checking for the things that move in the water. She knows the fish by sight now, the individuals. There's one, a pale carp with a dark spot behind its gill, that has been alive for at least fifteen years. She feeds it bits of bread sometimes, though she knows she shouldn't, and it rises to the surface with a slow dignity when it senses her shadow.

She is fifty-eight years old but looks older the way everyone who lives underground looks older, the skin softened and creased by perpetual moisture like a hand left too long in bathwater. Her hair is grey and cut short with scissors she keeps in a pouch on her belt. Her hands are strong, the knuckles enlarged from decades of turning valve wheels and gripping pipe wrenches and knocking on iron. She has a tattoo on her left forearm, a simple diagram of a centrifugal pump, cross-sectioned, done in blue ink that has faded to grey. She got it when she was twenty-five, newly hired, proud of her job and her knowledge and her place in a system that mattered. She doesn't regret it.

She has a radio, a old handheld unit that picks up a single frequency used by the authority's remaining surface personnel. Most of the time it broadcasts nothing but static and the occasional automated status report from remote monitoring stations. Sometimes, very rarely, a voice comes through—a dispatcher asking if anyone is on channel, a maintenance crew coordinating a repair, a garbled transmission from somewhere far away that might be related to the sump complex or might be from a completely different system, a different network, a different world. She always responds. She presses the transmit button and says "Sump complex, monitoring" and waits. Usually there's no reply. Once, a voice said "Copy that, sump complex" and then nothing else, and she replayed those two words in her mind for weeks, savoring the acknowledgment.

The frogs know her. This is not a figure of speech. They don't startle when she walks past; they continue their chorusing, their small bodies vibrating with call, their throat sacs inflating and deflating in the dim light. She has learned, over years of listening, to distinguish individuals by their calls—the stutter-croak of the big male near sump three, the high piping of the juveniles that cluster on the warm pipe near her alcove. She has learned that when they stop all at once, something has entered the chamber she should probably check. It's usually a rat, or a bird that found its way down through a vent, or a new person who doesn't know they need to move slowly, breathe shallowly, become part of the background before the background will accept them. Once it was a fire, small and electrical, spitting sparks from a junction box, and she put it out with an extinguisher that was older than she was, and the frogs resumed their calling within minutes.

She thinks about leaving sometimes. The thought arrives not as a plan but as a stone dropped into still water, ripples spreading and then fading. She could go topside. She has money—the accumulating paychecks, the account she's never touched. She could find a place with windows, with dry air, with a bed that doesn't crinkle and light that comes from the sky instead of bulbs. She doesn't know what she would do there. The sump complex is the only thing she knows how to do. Her skills—reading the mood of pumps, hearing the distress in a bearing, knowing which stains signal corrosion and which are just stains—have no application in a world she hasn't seen in anything but brief, blinding exposures. She is, in a way she cannot articulate, married to the water. Its cycles are her cycles. The steady thrum of the pumps is her heartbeat. To leave would be to go silent.

There was a moment, years ago, when the pumps almost stopped. A power failure that cascaded through the grid, shutting down the main feed, leaving only the emergency lighting and the drip of water and a silence so profound it felt like pressure. She stood in the dark, listening to the sumps settle, hearing the water stop moving, feeling the humidity begin to drop for the first time in her memory. She had, in that hour, a vision of the complex drained, dried, filled with clean air and quiet, the pipes cooling, the moss withering, the frogs migrating upward into a world that didn't want them. She felt something like grief, something like relief. Then the power returned, and the pumps spun up, and she went back to her rounds, and the feeling passed.

She is not lonely in the way that requires other people. She is lonely in the way that a lighthouse is lonely—functional, necessary, oriented toward a purpose that doesn't require company. She talks to the fish sometimes. She talks to the frogs more often, narrating her rounds, explaining what she's checking and why. She has named the major pumps: Big Henry, the one that rumbles and overheats if she doesn't grease it weekly; the Twins, two identical units that run in tandem and have to be adjusted together or not at all; Old Lady, the small auxiliary that predates all the others and still runs, inefficiently, faithfully, a sound so familiar it's almost silence. She touches their housings when she passes, a palm flat against the warm metal, the way a farmer might pat a draft horse.

At night—or what passes for night, the dimming of the already-dim when she turns off her alcove light—she reads. Her books are a strange library: a repair manual for a pump model that was obsolete before she was born, a romance novel with a cover torn off, a guide to freshwater fish of the region, a thick paperback about the history of municipal water systems. She reads slowly, mouthing the words, and when she finishes a book she starts another, and when she finishes all of them she starts again, and the stories have become so layered in her memory that she can no longer separate the pump specifications from the romance plot from the spawning habits of carp. She doesn't mind. The blurring feels appropriate, a kind of intellectual humidity that matches the physical one.

There is a thing she does not speak about, even to the frogs. In the sump where the water smells of ozone, the one in the far corner, the one where the fish won't go, she has seen something twice now. Not a thing, exactly. A shape. A movement in the water that doesn't correspond to any creature she knows. The first time was eight years ago: a long pale form, sinuous, that broke the surface and then submerged without a sound. The second time was last winter, or what she thinks was winter, the season when the condensation slows and the air grows marginally cooler. She was checking the level gauge and saw it again, the same pale curve, the same silent vanishing. She does not believe in monsters. She believes in infrastructure, in systems, in the tendency of sumps to accumulate things that fall in or swim in or grow in the dark. But she checks that sump last now, and she does not linger, and she does not feed whatever lives in it.

This morning—it is morning, the pump cycle has shifted, the coffee is cooling in its metal cup—she is sitting on the edge of her cot, lacing her boots. The frogs are still calling, which means nothing has entered, nothing is wrong, the complex is breathing as it always breathes. She will walk her route. She will tap her flanges. She will check her gauges. She will fill out the logbook with her own name over and over, a chain of Maes stretching back to when she was young and forward to when she is dust. The pumps will run. The water will move. The rust will spread. And Mae will be here, knocking her way through the dark, married to the machinery, an organ in the body of the sump, beating steadily, patiently, without end.
