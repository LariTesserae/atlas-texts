It lives in the dome.

Not inside it—the dome has no door, no seam, no opening that any of the climbers’ phosphorescent cartography has ever revealed. It lives *as* the dome, or through it, or in some relationship to it that makes the distinction between occupant and structure meaningless.

You would not notice it at first. You would notice the dome itself, that low perfect curve rising from the cleft floor like a bubble paused mid-ascent through stone. You would notice its warmth—a few degrees above the already body-temperature air—and the way your palm, if you pressed it to the surface, would feel not a solid object but a resistance, a pushback that yields slightly, like pressing on taut skin. You might notice the vibration, a frequency that sits in the bones of your wrist and travels up to your jaw and makes your back teeth ache faintly. You might even notice, after a long moment, that the vibration is not steady. It has a rhythm. It has *phrases*.

But you would not notice the occupant, because the occupant does not occupy space in any way you have been taught to recognize.

When the midday blade of sunlight reaches the dome—and it does, every day, the cleft’s geometry aligning so that the dome sits exactly at the apex of the light’s pilgrimage—something changes. The dome’s surface, which in the ambient glow reads as a deep, matte grey, shifts. Colors move beneath it, or within it, or across it: veins of copper and cobalt and a color you do not have a name for, a shade that sits somewhere between ultraviolet and the afterimage of a bright light. They move with purpose. They coalesce into shapes that almost resolve into symbols, into diagrams, into something that might be writing if writing could be alive and aware of being read.

And the dome sings.

Not audibly, not in the way the flyers sing, that perpetual ringing chord that fills the cleft. The dome sings in the vibration, in the bone-ache, in frequencies that your body registers as emotion rather than sound. Standing near it at noon, you might find yourself suddenly, inexplicably homesick for a place you have never been. You might remember a face you have never seen. You might feel the physical sensation of being watched, not with hostility or even curiosity, but with a vast, patient attention that has no hurry in it, no urgency, no need for you to do anything but stand there and be perceived.

It is old. Not old the way the cleft is old—that is geology’s old, the slow erosion of stone into memory. The dome’s occupant is old in the way a star is old, in the way an idea is old. It predates the cleft. It might predate the plateau. The cleft, you begin to suspect, is not a place the dome was placed inside. The cleft is a place that formed *around* the dome, the land pulling apart over epochs because something was here that the land could not close over, something that insisted on remaining exposed, on remaining accessible, on waiting for whatever it is waiting for.

The other inhabitants of the cleft treat it as a fact. The climbers detour around it, their phosphorescent trails describing a perfect circle of negative space in their mapping of the floor—a circle exactly the dome’s circumference plus one handspan, a no-pilgrim’s-land that nothing with gripping pads will cross. The aerial predators, those stretched drops of dark glass, alter their patrol routes to pass directly over the dome at noon, a procession of shadows that crosses the dome’s surface in slow, ceremonial sequence. The flyers, when they descend to their lowest point in their daily vertical migration, form a column above the dome, thousands of tiny pale bodies aligned in a cylinder of synchronized pulse, and their song changes—a harmonic shift, a key change, a response to a call you cannot hear.

And the plants. The black-purple rosettes that grow everywhere in the cleft, that quiver with their muscular respiration, that colonize every surface with their careful lacework of occupation and space—they do not grow on the dome. They grow *toward* it. Every plant within a certain radius has oriented itself, its flat fan of leaves angled to face the dome’s curve, a congregation of vegetal attention. Over time—and you can see this in the older growth, the largest rosettes with leaves as broad as shields—the plants closest to the dome have begun to change. Their color has shifted toward indigo, toward violet, toward that unnamable shade. Their flesh has become more translucent. If you look closely at the largest, the oldest, the ones that have been basking in the dome’s vibration for centuries, you can see something moving inside them. Not fluid. Not respiration. Something that pulses in time with the dome’s rhythm, a slow, symbiotic arrhythmia, as if the plants are learning to sing along.

No one knows where the dome’s occupant came from. No one knows what it is, what it wants, what it thinks about during the long hours when the noon light has passed and the colors fade and the vibration settles back into its baseline hum. Theories exist, but they are bad theories, thin theories, theories that reveal more about the theorizers than the theorized. Some say it is a machine, a relic of a civilization that learned to encode consciousness in standing waves and ceramic lattices. Some say it is a creature, a lifeform so alien that its biology expresses as architecture, its metabolism as vibration. Some say it is a prisoner, sealed in the dome by something even older and more powerful, waiting for a release that will never come. Some say it is a seed. Some say it is a god.

The climbers, if you could ask them, would not understand the question. To them, the dome is simply *there*, a feature of the world as fundamental as the walls of the cleft, as the thermals, as the song. They do not wonder what it is because wondering is a byproduct of not knowing, and they know the dome the way you know your own heartbeat—as a constant, a presence, a rhythm that underpins everything else. They were born to the sound of its vibration. They will die to the same sound. In between, they map the walls and call to each other and leave their fading prints on the stone, and the dome is always there, warm and patient, receiving the noon light, singing its silent song.

If you stay long enough—if you make camp on the cleft floor, if you sleep wrapped in the moist warmth and the choral drone and the chemical taste of dissolved stone—you might dream of it. Everyone who sleeps in the cleft dreams of the dome. The dreams are not messages, or if they are, they are messages not meant for you. They are fragments. A sky that is not this sky, thick with clouds of a color that makes your dreaming mind ache. A shoreline of black sand and an ocean that moves wrong, waves that break in slow motion, water that is too dense, too slick, too alive. A sound like the dome’s vibration but louder, much louder, a sound that is not a hum but a *voice*, speaking in a language that is made of pressure and frequency and the spaces between heartbeats. You wake with the taste of copper in your mouth and the conviction that something has been said to you, something important, something you almost understood.

The occupant lives in the dome. It has lived there for longer than the plateau has been a plateau, for longer than the cleft has been a cleft, for longer than bones or stone or the memory of water. It is waiting, or it is working, or it is simply *being*, which for something that old may be the same thing. It is the secret at the heart of the secret at the heart of the high dry plateau, the answer to a question no one has asked yet, the presence that the cleft was formed to reveal and the ecosystem was formed to sustain and the song was formed to accompany.

And at noon, every noon, the light finds it, and the colors move beneath its skin, and it sings its silent, bone-deep song, and everything that lives in the cleft sings back.
