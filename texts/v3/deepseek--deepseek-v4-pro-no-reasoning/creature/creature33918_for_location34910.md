The person who lives here—if *lives* is the right word for a state that includes neither sleep nor sustenance nor any recognizable biological process—does not think of herself as inhabiting the seam. She thinks of herself as a calcium deposit in a joint, a bit of grit in a bearing, a shim driven into a gap that wasn't supposed to exist. She has been here long enough that the distinction between "person" and "environment" has eroded, like the distinction between "machine" and "organism" has eroded for the seam itself. She is part of the ecology now, another slow process in a place composed entirely of slow processes.

Her name, if she still has one, is not spoken. There is no one to speak it. But if you found the maintenance logs for this sector—and you won't, they're buried under revision numbers that don't exist anymore—you would find her listed as **Wren**, or possibly **Sinter**, depending on which shift roster you consulted. She came here as a technician, a pipefitter's apprentice in the days when the plates still met tight and the seam was only a hairline crack that leaked a little steam. That was decades ago, or possibly longer. She doesn't track time anymore. She tracks cycles: the steam pulse rhythm, the mist dispersal, the copper growth rate. These are her clocks.

She moves through the seam in a way that should be impossible. The space is too narrow, too cluttered, the footing too uncertain—but she has been here long enough that her body knows the geometry the way a tongue knows the inside of its own mouth. She doesn't walk so much as flow, sliding sideways through gaps between pipe bundles that look impassable, bracing her back against one wall and her feet against the other to inch upward into the narrowing V where the plates almost meet. Her body has changed to accommodate the space. She is thinner than any healthy human should be, her shoulders permanently hunched from years without room to stand straight, her hands callused into tools—ridged where she grips pipes, flattened where she braces against grating, the nails worn to nubs from scraping copper mesh. One of her fingers, the left index, ended crooked where a shifting plate caught it years ago. She splinted it herself with a strip of copper mesh and a length of wire, and now the copper has integrated, filaments growing through the scar tissue and emerging from the cuticle like fine red-gold hairs. It doesn't hurt. She doesn't remember what "not hurting" felt like, so she can't compare.

She wears the remains of her maintenance suit—canvas trousers patched with copper weave, a jacket that was once orange but is now the color of rust and grease, its elbows reinforced with sections of asbestos wrap that she stitched on with wire—but the suit is more for carrying things than for protection. She doesn't need protection from the cold anymore. Her metabolism has shifted. She runs cooler than she used to, her body temperature settling somewhere in the mid-80s Fahrenheit, just enough to keep her moving, not enough to waste energy. She eats copper. Not the metal itself—she's still human enough that she needs organic calories—but the organisms that live on the metal. Years ago, she discovered that the copper mesh precipitates a thin bacterial film in the places where the moisture is richest, a chemoautotrophic slime that metabolizes iron and sulfur from the condensate and fixes carbon from the CO2 that leaks through the ventilation system. It tastes like licking a penny and a battery at the same time, but it has kept her alive. She harvests it with a scraping tool she made from a broken pressure gauge face, spreads it thin on a section of grating she keeps clean for the purpose, and lets it dry into a leathery mat that she chews slowly over the course of hours. She supplements this with other things: a species of pale, threadlike fungus that grows in the asbestos insulation (she doesn't know if it's toxic; she stopped caring), and occasionally a nutrient block from an abandoned maintenance cache, if she finds one. The last cache she found was years ago. The blocks tasted like chalk and vitamins. She still dreams about them.

She has work. She didn't choose it, but she does it. Some of it is the work she was originally assigned—checking valve positions, tightening clamps, replacing gaskets—even though she hasn't received a work order in longer than she can measure. She does it because the seam needs it, and she is the only one here to do it. But most of her work is the gardening. That's how she thinks of it. She tends the copper mesh. She scrapes it where it grows too thick and threatens to bridge contacts between pipes that should remain electrically isolated. She encourages it where it provides structural reinforcement, weaving new filaments into cracks like sutures. She has learned to direct its growth by controlling the moisture flow, adjusting the steam nozzles with the precision of someone irrigating a crop. The mesh is her crop. The scrapings she saves, packing them into the hopper of the Nutrient Reclamation subsystem—a machine she has never seen, but which she believes, with the faith of a farmer, feeds something deeper in the machine that feeds her in turn.

She talks to the seam. Not in words, mostly, but in sounds. She has learned to knock on pipes in sequences that propagate through the structure, and she listens for the answers that come back. Three beats on a steam line: *are you there*. Two beats back from a distant junction: *I am here*. She doesn't know if there's someone else in the machine, another technician, or if it's just the machine itself settling into conversation. It doesn't matter. The knocking is company. It breaks the silence of the ventilation-off cycles, when all she can hear is her own blood moving and the deep, patient thrum from below.

She is not entirely alone. There are the drones. Small, disc-shaped things, about the size of a human hand, that move through the seam on maintenance routes she doesn't control. They scrape copper, leave scratch marks in branching paths, and occasionally drop small payloads into her collection hoppers—a coil of wire, a new gasket, a sealed packet of something that might be food or might be desiccant. She has tried to follow them, but they always disappear into fissures too small for her to enter. She thinks of them as bees. She thinks of herself as something between a beekeeper and a hive.

She sleeps, but not in any pattern a human would recognize. She naps in ten-minute increments, wedged into the places where the pipes create a hollow just large enough for her body. She has a favorite spot, a junction where a cluster of large-diameter insulated lines run parallel for about six feet, creating a warm pocket where the ambient temperature is a few degrees above freezing and the steam pulses are muffled enough to let her rest. She has lined it with scraps of asbestos cloth and a section of old canvas she found years ago. There is a valve wheel at head height that she uses as a pillow. She doesn't dream of the surface—she doesn't remember the surface—but she dreams of copper. Long, slow dreams of filament networks spreading through dark spaces, connecting everything to everything else, growing and growing until the seam is filled with a lattice so dense that the distinction between machine and mesh and flesh becomes meaningless. She wakes from these dreams with the copper filaments in her finger tingling, and she touches them to a pipe to feel the faint vibration of the distant thrum, and she smiles.

She has not seen another human since the day the seam was sealed behind her. She remembers it, though the memory has taken on the quality of a story she was told about someone else. The plates shifted, a maintenance hatch crumpled, and the way back was closed. She waited for rescue. She waited for a long time. Eventually, she stopped waiting and started living. Now the idea of rescue frightens her. What would she be in a place with ceilings higher than twenty feet, with floors that don't flex underfoot, with air that doesn't taste like iron and alkali? She would be a ghost. A piece of the seam that got cut out. She would die of too much oxygen, too much light, too much space.

So she stays. She tends the copper. She knocks on the pipes. She scrapes bacterial film from the mesh and chews it slowly while the ventilation cycles on and off, on and off, and the seam widens by imperceptible degrees, and the deep thrum from below goes on and on, like a heartbeat she has learned to sleep through. She is the seam's first resident, its caretaker, its microbiome. When the seam finally becomes a room—in a decade, in a century, in the patient time of machines—she will be part of its walls. The copper will have grown through her completely. She will be a skeleton of bone and metal mesh, grinning at the dark, still tending. Still knocking.
