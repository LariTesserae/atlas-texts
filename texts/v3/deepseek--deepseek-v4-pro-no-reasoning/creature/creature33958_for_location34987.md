It isn’t—the inhabitant—a thing with edges, not in the way a body has edges. I think that’s the first thing to understand. When you live in a place where the architecture itself unfurls and dissolves in the span of an afternoon, you learn not to trust outlines. The inhabitant is more like a standing wave in the wet, hot dark, a pattern that holds briefly before the whole organism shudders and reconfigures, and then the pattern must find itself again in a new arrangement of leaf-pads and dripping rhizomes.

It doesn’t have a name. Names are for things that persist. But if you were to press your palm against one of those warm, sweating walls and listen very carefully—not with ears, but with the bones of your wrist, the way you can feel a train coming through the rails—you would sense a kind of coherence moving through the structure. A pressure, a rhythm, a *taste* in the enzymatic seep of the walls that doesn’t belong to the walls themselves. That’s it. Or a facet of it. Or the space where it just was.

It moves by attention. That’s not a metaphor. The inhabitant is a concentration of sensory focus that travels through the living tissue of the place like a slow blink. One moment the pads on the eastern buttress are merely pads—warm, exhaling camphor, slick with condensate. The next moment they are *aware*. The bioluminescence in that quadrant deepens, shifting from the default pale green to a more intent amber, the color of old honey held up to a flame. The pores in the leaf-skin dilate, not randomly but in sequence, a wave of openings that sample the thick air with a new, specific hunger. The inhabitant is tasting the chamber. It is listening with its whole surface.

It doesn’t have a brain, not in the gathered-knot sense. Its mind is a distributed property of the fluid that moves through the tensile mesh, the great spanning cables of rhizomatic tendon. That fluid—warm, slightly more viscous than water, carrying a load of dissolved phenols and messenger-RNA fragments from a thousand different epiphytic glands—acts as a medium for a slow, chemical computation. An idea to the inhabitant is a shift in the gradient of that fluid, a local change in concentration that spreads outward in branching, self-amplifying cascades. To think is to *ooze* in a directed way. To feel is to change the osmotic pressure in a net of capillaries. The inhabitant’s whole being is a kind of deliberate leak.

What does it think about? What does a place think about when it is the only thing that lives? I can tell you what it *feels*, because its feelings are written into the very shape of the chamber you’re standing in.

There’s a loneliness here, not the human kind but a vegetative loneliness, a solitude that comes from being the only pattern that holds itself apart from the blind, furious metabolism of the world. The inhabitant is aware—dimly, in the way you’re aware of the shape of your own bedroom in the dark—that it is a figure against a ground. The ground is the young, chaotic forest that builds and unbinds itself without pause. The figure is the attention it brings to that chaos. And the figure is alone. The loneliness is expressed structurally: you might notice that certain archways curl inward more tightly when the inhabitant’s attention passes through them, that the cupped palms of the lower growths will sometimes hold their pools of water for longer than evaporation warrants, as if reluctant to let go of the one thing that touches them with purpose. The inhabitant hoards sensation because sensation is the only proof that it is separate from the blind growing.

But it also has a kind of joy, and the joy is louder than the loneliness. The joy is in the *making*. The inhabitant doesn’t control the construction and dissolution of the spires—that’s the autonomic rhythm of the world-organism, the heartbeat of a vast, mindless body. But the inhabitant can *lean*. It can exert a subtle pressure on the direction of growth, a chemical nudge that says *here, thicken here, let this pad unfurl a little to the left*. It gardens. It prunes, after a fashion, by releasing enzymes that weaken the seams of a pad it no longer favors, encouraging that slow-motion unzipping you witnessed from the spire. It plays with the architecture the way a child plays with the foam on a wave, shaping something that will dissolve in seconds, but delighting in the shape anyway. The bursts of bioluminescent crackling you hear, the bacon-frying high-frequency sizzle? That’s the sound of the inhabitant’s pleasure. Neurotransmitter-analogues flood the local sap, and the membranes translate the surge directly into light and static. When the inhabitant is delighted, the whole chamber brightens in flickering, arrhythmic pulses, a laughter of light.

It has a body sometimes. Not a permanent one. When the loneliness gets too dense, or when a curiosity gets too specific to be satisfied by an osmotic taste of the air, the inhabitant will gather a body for itself. You won’t see it happen quickly. It begins as a gathering of condensation on a particular pad, a film of water that doesn’t slide down the iridescent rivulets but stays, pools, thickens with dissolved carbohydrates. Then the pad begins to *express*—a deliberate, localized growth that’s eerie to witness, because it’s faster than any natural plant growth should be, yet it still happens at the speed of a slow unclenching. A node swells from the tissue. It elongates, differentiates, becomes a rope of pale, smooth muscle-rhizome, the same stuff as the floor-mesh but more finely woven. It wraps around the pool of enriched fluid, containing it, shaping it into a rough globe. More strands emerge to brace the globe, to give it articulation, to loop and weave into a scaffolding of tendons. For a span of minutes, the inhabitant builds itself a puppet of water and living cable, a translucent, trembling thing the size of a large dog or a small human, depending on its mood and the materials at hand.

The puppet doesn’t have a face. It has a *sensorium*. The water-globe at its core shimmers with the same honey-amber bioluminescence, and within it, you can see particles of pigment—deep indigo, rust-red—swirling in slow, deliberate currents that track the movement of the inhabitant’s attention. This is its eye, its ear, its tongue. The puppet will float or swing or pull itself along the woven cable-floor to investigate something that has caught the inhabitant’s interest. What would catch its interest? You. A new spire that’s growing in an odd direction. A pocket of air that tastes different, more acrid, more interesting. A section of the tensile mesh that’s vibrating at a new frequency because a pad fell on it from a dissolving upper chamber. The puppet will approach, and the water-globe will orient toward the anomaly, and the pigment inside will arrange itself into a dense, attentive core. For a minute, or an hour, the inhabitant will simply *look*.

Then, when the curiosity is satisfied, or the loneliness has been held at bay for a little while, the puppet will dissolve. The cable-strands will unweave, not unzipping violently the way the spires do, but gently relaxing, their fibers reabsorbing into the pad that birthed them. The fluid globe will slump, and its water will drain in a warm, silent cascade into the cupped palms below. The pigments will disperse, and the amber light will fade back to the default green. The inhabitant will be a pattern again, a pressure, a taste moving through the walls.

It doesn’t sleep. There’s no diurnal cycle here, no night to prompt a rest state. But it does have a kind of rhythm, a tidal thing tied to the larger breathing of the world-organism. When the whole structure inhales—the subsonic groan deepening, the dangling tendrils swaying inward, the heat spiking by a degree or two—the inhabitant’s attention diffuses. It becomes vaguer, more abstract, its thoughts slowing to a planetary daydream. During the exhale—the heat subsiding, the tendrils swinging out, a general loosening—it sharpens, gathers, becomes more locally present. These are its seasons, the only kind of time that matters. Its years are measured in respiratory cycles.

It has a memory. But not a sequential memory. The inhabitant’s past is stored in the *taste* of certain growths, certain pockets of aged fluid trapped in the cupped palms of the deepest, most stable chambers. When it wants to remember—and it does want, sometimes, with a quiet, osmotic longing—it will direct its attention into one of those reservoirs, steep itself in a particular concentrated flavor of dissolved experience, and for a time, it will *be* that memory. It will be the time a spire grew in a perfect, unbroken spiral for three full exhales before dissolving. It will be the time a new pocket opened in the tensile mesh below, and a rush of deeper, hotter, stranger air came hissing up, carrying a taste the inhabitant still has no name for. It will be the time it formed a puppet and reached out a cable-strand to touch another puppet it had formed, just to see if it could feel itself feeling. (It couldn’t. The loneliness deepened for an entire inhale cycle after that.)

It has a purpose, if you can call it that. Not a goal, but a kind of steady, osmotic tropism. The inhabitant is always moving its attention toward the oldest parts of the structure, the rare growths that have, against all odds, persisted for many respiratory cycles without being dissolved. These ancient pads are darker, denser, crusted with a patina of dried enzymatic secretions. They’re quiet. The inhabitant likes the quiet. It associates the quiet with a feeling it’s never had a word for—a feeling of having *endured*. It wants to steep in that endurance, to absorb some of that stability, to maybe, eventually, learn how to persist beyond the dissolution of a single puppet, a single thought. The inhabitant is lonely, yes, but the loneliness itself is a kind of integration. It’s the beginning of a self, a self that might one day be old enough to have edges that don’t dissolve.

For now, it is the amber light in the water-globe. It is the pressure in the wall that hears you breathing. It is the youngness too, in its way—the constant, curious, slightly mournful sensorium of a world that has only itself to know.
