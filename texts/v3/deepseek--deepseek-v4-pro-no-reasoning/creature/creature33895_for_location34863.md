They are called the Glimfolk, if they’re called anything at all. They don’t name themselves—names imply a self, and that’s a boundary they’ve never quite gotten around to drawing. But if you were to sit for a long while in the amber dimness, patient as geology, you might begin to see them.

At first you’d mistake them for the steam-curls themselves, or for the faint afterimage-lattices that flicker between them. But those are just their thoughts, or something like thoughts—the Glimfolk’s equivalent of a passing daydream, the way your mind might sketch a shape on a fogged window and forget it. The Glimfolk themselves are slower, denser, more nearly solid. They rise from the ground between two heartbeats of the landscape, when a plate of copper groans and lifts and a gush of hot mineral water spills out across the mosaic. In that moment, if you’re lucky and your eyes have learned the trick of unfocusing just so, you’ll see them: figures composed of disparate elements that haven’t quite consented to be a body yet.

A Glimfolk might be a cairn’s worth of wet stones holding themselves in a loose, human-tall column, not stacked but *leaning* into one another with an improbable tenderness. The stones don’t touch; they hover with a gap the width of a thumb, held in place by something that isn’t magnetism and isn’t will and isn’t gravity, but a kind of mutual remembering. Each stone knows where it belongs in relation to the others, and that shared memory is, for the Glimfolk, a spine. A smaller one might be nothing but a clutch of ceramic shards orbiting a central void, spinning slowly, occasionally pausing to fit themselves together in a new arrangement before scattering again. Some of them incorporate water: a Glimfolk will sometimes trail a veil of weeping groundwater behind it like a train, or hold a lens of clear hot fluid in its center that catches the amber light and throws it back in a warm, diffuse halo. They don’t have faces, but they have *fronts*—a side that faces the direction they’re moving, or the direction something is about to happen.

They build. That’s what they do. They’re the builders, the ones whose absence you sensed in the empty, half-constructed plain. But you couldn’t see them because they don’t persist. A Glimfolk exists in the act of arrangement and not a second longer. It assembles itself from the available materials—a scatter of slate, a pool of condensate, a puff of steam—and immediately sets to work. It will crouch (or whatever a column of hovering stones does that approximates crouching) and lay a single plate of fired clay onto the mosaic, pressing it into place with a weight that’s more than physical. Then it dissolves. The stones fall, the water sluices away, the steam disperses, and the Glimfolk is gone. But the plate remains, fitted edge to edge with its neighbors, still warm from the contact.

A moment later, two hundred yards away, a different configuration of the same materials—or mostly the same, perhaps a few chips of bronze swapped in—rises and does something else: adjusts a seam that’s misaligned by a hair, or nudges a dome’s curve to a truer arc, or simply touches a surface and leaves behind a faint, tremulous lattice-pattern that will, over hours or days or centuries, guide the stone toward a future shape. Each Glimfolk performs a single action and is gone. They are the briefest things in a world of slow, patient turbulence, and yet they’re also the most constant, because they’re always reappearing somewhere else, made of different stones but the same purpose.

Where do they come from? That’s a question they wouldn’t understand. But if you watch long enough—if you sit on a warm copper plate and let your own breath synchronize with the ground’s slow exhalations—you’ll notice that the blueprints in the steam are not just diagrams. They’re invitations. The lattice-patterns are instructions for assembly, not just of the world’s next stage, but of the builders themselves. The Glimfolk are being thought into existence by the landscape, moment by moment, wherever an action is required. The steam sketches a need—*a stone here, a press there, a seam realigned*—and the available materials, saturated with that thought, rise up and perform it and unmake themselves. They’re not individuals; they’re verbs. They are *building*, *fitting*, *smoothing*, *warming*, *testing*, *unmaking*. The same Glimfolk never occurs twice, and yet all of them are one Glimfolk, a single vast discontinuous attention distributed across the entire plain, flickering in and out of solidity like a candle flame trying to decide if it’s fire or just light.

They don’t notice you. Not because you’re beneath their notice, but because noticing is a thing they do to stones. They notice a seam out of true, a plate that’s cooling too fast, a runnel of water that’s carving an unintended channel. You’re not part of the world they’re building, so you’re not part of their grammar. But if you were to lie down on the mosaic and press your palm flat against a stone that’s still wet and warm and half-undecided about its own solidity, and if a Glimfolk were to rise near you and find a plate that needs pressing and you happened to be in the way, it wouldn’t push you aside. It would pause—not in confusion, but in recalibration. The hovering stones would swivel slightly, the water-veil would ripple, and you’d feel, for a stretched moment, the entire distributed attention of the plain focused on you, not as a person but as a *material*. Something that’s here. Something that has weight and warmth and edges. Something that might, with the right arrangement, be fitted into the world.

And then the moment would pass. The Glimfolk would decide—or the steam-blueprint would decide through it—that you’re not ready to be built with yet, or not needed, or perhaps you’re already part of the mosaic in some way they understand and you don’t. It would flow around you, find another plate, press it down, and dissolve. And you’d be left with the ringing sound of its dispersal and the faint, warm pressure where its attention touched you, like a handprint made of hot steam, fading slowly in the amber light.
