The doorway pulses faintly, a slow heartbeat of green light that matches the rhythm of your own breathing. You've walked this root-bridge a thousand times, and yet tonight—if it is night; the light cycles are subtle here, a dimming of the vascular glow rather than true darkness—the familiar feels strange. The root beneath your feet gives slightly, as it always does, the living fibers compressing and releasing with each step, a sensation so constant that you stopped noticing it years ago. But now you notice it again, because something is waiting in the doorway.

She is small, smaller than she should be for her age, though age is a difficult thing to measure here. The children of the middle strata grow slowly, their bodies taking cues from the plant itself, which has no urgency. She might be seven cycles, or twelve. Her carapace—no, her clothing, you correct yourself, though the distinction has always been blurry—is a woven second skin of silk and shed leaf-sheath, mottled in the grays and pale greens of the inner bark. It covers her shoulders and back in overlapping plates, flexible and tough, leaving her arms and legs bare except for the fine, almost invisible hair that all dwellers have, a soft down that helps sense the slightest shifts in air pressure and humidity. Her face is round, her eyes large and dark, the pupils dilated wide to gather the dim light. There is no white in her eyes; the sclera are a deep amber, almost orange, a trait common among those who live their whole lives in the glow of the symbionts.

She is holding something. A larva? A piece of fruit-flesh? No—it is a knot-cord, a message, and she is working it with her fingers in the way of the younger children, who learn to read by touch before their eyes can focus properly on the intricate braids. But she is not reading. She is looking at you, and her expression is not the calm, slightly distant expression of a child at home in the quiet. It is something else.

You remember, then. You remember that this child is not yours, not exactly. She is a foundling, a gift of the reality breakdown, brought to your family compound three cycles ago by a browser-herd that had no herder. She was sitting among them as if she had always been there, her small hands tangled in the leaf-fur of the largest beast, her carapace-clothing still slick with the birthing fluids of somewhere else. She did not speak for the first cycle, and when she did speak, the words were in a dialect you knew but with an accent that turned the vowels strange, as if she had learned them from someone speaking through water. She had no name. You gave her one: Kethe, after the small, tenacious moss that grows in the driest cracks of the outer bark.

Kethe looks at you now, and her mouth opens. For a moment, no sound comes. Then, in that liquid accent, she says: "The whirr-click stopped."

You listen. The root-bridge creaks softly. The distant water drips. The almost-sounds of the plant hum at the edge of awareness. But she is right. The spinner's weighted spindle, the constant, comforting rhythm from the high gallery, has fallen silent.

You take a step toward the doorway, toward the warm glow of home, and Kethe holds up the knot-cord. Her small fingers have been working it into a new shape, not a message but something else: a representation, crude but unmistakable, of a figure. A figure with too many limbs, or perhaps the right number of limbs but arranged wrong, bent at angles that suggest joints where no joints should be. A figure that is, you realize with a coldness that has nothing to do with temperature, standing exactly where the spinner was working.

"The browsers are moving," Kethe says. "They're moving toward the deep wet. They say something is waking up."

You look past her, into the dim interior of your home, where the hand-smeared cultures of phosphorescent symbionts are beginning their slow night-dimming cycle. The walls weep their constant moisture. The fiber-felt floor is smooth and warm. Everything is as it should be, and yet.

And yet the silence where the spindle should be is a hole in the world, a gap like the one beneath the root-bridge, miles deep and full of mist. You think of the reality breakdown, the old stories, the patterns in the vascular light that almost form faces. You think of the browsers moving with purpose, and the whisper-voice of the plant, and the way the galleries sometimes lead where they did not lead the day before.

You reach for Kethe, to pull her inside, to close the trained-lens aperture of the doorway against the warm, waiting dark. But your hand pauses halfway there, because she has turned the knot-cord figure toward you, and you see now that it is not a figure at all. It is a map. A map of the root-bridge. A map that shows, in careful braided detail, a second bridge growing beneath the first, spiraling down into the abyssal wet, where no one goes, where no one has ever gone.

"The whirr-click," Kethe says again, and this time her voice is not a child's voice. It is something older, something that has been speaking through water for a very long time. "It didn't stop. It moved. Can't you hear it? It's coming up from below."

You listen. You listen with your ears, with the fine hairs on your arms, with the soles of your feet pressed against the living root. And there, so faint it might be the memory of a sound, so deep it might be the vibration of the plant's own vast heart: the *whirr-click*. Rising.
