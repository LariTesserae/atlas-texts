Let me tell you about the people who live here. They are the ones who sing.

You will not see them at first, even after you’ve been standing here long enough for your feet to go numb and your breath to become part of the general wetness. They are not hiding, exactly. They are simply *here* in a way that makes them difficult to separate from the walls, the water, the isopods, the light. They are a small people—not children, never children, but built to a scale that makes this compressed cathedral feel spacious. Four feet tall at most, and thin, with limbs that seem too long for their bodies until you see them move and understand that the length is deliberate, necessary, an adaptation to a life spent reaching into skull sockets and probing channels and picking their way across a pavement made of bone without ever disturbing the water’s flow.

They call themselves the Singers, or something that translates to that, though the word they use when they speak to each other is a three-note hum pitched at the exact frequency of wind through a particular skull socket. They have no written language. They don’t need one. Everything they need to remember is inscribed in the walls, in the arrangement of isopod colonies, in the channels they’ve carved over generations to direct the condensation into specific patterns. They read the walls the way you would read a book, running their fingers over the exposed frontal bones and the quartz-filled braincases, feeling the micro-vibrations of the isopods’ respiration. Each skull, to them, is a syllable. A wall is a sentence. The whole settlement is a library.

Their skin is the color of the bone-stone, a dark grey that verges on blue in certain lights, and it has the same fine-grained texture. Up close, you would see that it is not skin as you understand it—it is a living integument, smooth and slightly moist, and it grows in layers that they can shed and reabsorb depending on the season, depending on the moisture cycle, depending on their own internal rhythms. When they shed, they take the dead layers and grind them into a fine powder that they mix with water and isopod frass to make the cement they use to patch the walls. This is not a symbolic act. It is simply practical. Their bodies become the mortar. Their bodies hold the skulls in place. Their bodies are what keeps the settlement standing.

They are bipedal, mostly, but they move in ways that suggest they have never quite committed to the upright posture. Their spines are flexible in ways that yours are not; they can fold themselves nearly in half to inspect a channel at their feet, and they can stretch upward to a height that seems impossible, their vertebrae separating slightly, their arms elongating, to reach a socket near the top of the wall. Their hands have six fingers, all of them double-jointed and tipped with pads that grip wet stone effortlessly. Their feet are the same, and they walk barefoot always, their soles conforming to the curve of each cobble, each channel, each skull. They know this street so intimately that they could walk it blindfolded, could walk it asleep, could walk it dying and still not misstep.

Their faces are what you would notice last, because their faces are the part of them that is least like a face. It’s not that they lack features. They have eyes—two of them, large and dark and set deep in sockets that are themselves slightly elongated, as if their own skulls are preparing to become part of the wall someday. They have a nose, or something that serves the same purpose, a pair of vertical slits high on the bridge that flare and contract with each breath, constantly sampling the air for shifts in humidity, for the ammonia-tang of isopod distress, for the subtle chemical signals their own kind use to communicate emotion. But they don’t have mouths, not in the way you do. Where a mouth should be, they have a secondary set of slits, these ones horizontal, arranged in a row of three like gill openings, and when they speak or sing, these slits vibrate against each other to produce the three-note hum. The sound is always chordal. They cannot speak in single notes, only in harmonics. When two of them speak to each other, the air fills with overlapping chords, a counterpoint that is itself a kind of music, a kind of language, a kind of weather.

There are exactly nineteen of them. The number is important, though they would never say why. It has something to do with the geometry of the curve, the number of skulls in the longest unbroken wall, the number of major channels that flow during the peak condensation cycle. Nineteen has been the population for as long as any of them can remember, which is a very long time indeed. When one dies, another is born within the same lunar cycle, and the new one always appears in the same socket—the one at the apex of the curve, the one that catches the light first when the pearlescent brightness intensifies at dawn, the one that is not a skull at all but a geode whose crystals vibrate at a frequency that induces labor in the pregnant Singer. The birth is quiet. The birth is wet. The newborn emerges already knowing the three-note hum, already able to grip the wall with their six-fingered hands, already hungry for the substance their elders will provide.

That substance is the reason the isopods exist, or the isopods are the reason the substance exists. The relationship is circular, and the Singers do not distinguish between farming and worship. Every morning—or what passes for morning in this place of constant pearlescent light—they perform the Lifting. One Singer for each wall, chosen in a rotation that has been maintained without error for longer than the walls themselves have been standing, climbs slowly up the sockets, using the isopods themselves as handholds. The isopods do not mind this. They grip the Singer’s hands with their many legs, and when the Singer reaches the top of the wall, near the point where it leans inward and the slice of sky is at its narrowest, they begin to sing. Not the three-note hum of speech, but a longer, more complex composition that uses all three of their mouth-slits in sequence, a cascade of harmonics that shifts the air pressure in the narrow street, that makes the water in the channels quiver and change direction, that induces the isopods to do something they do at no other time.

They begin to glow.

The blue light you saw in the fallen isopod’s gut is not passive. It is a response to specific frequencies of sound, and the Singers know exactly which frequencies to produce for which sections of the wall. As they sing, section by section, the isopods luminesce, a wave of cold blue light that travels down the wall like water, like wind, like the slow progress of a tide. When the light reaches the bottom of the wall, where the channels are deepest, it triggers a response in the water itself—or rather, in the microorganisms that live in the water, the ones that have been filtering down through the skulls for centuries, the ones that are the true foundation of this place’s ecology. The microorganisms, when exposed to the isopods’ light, begin to reproduce explosively, forming a thin, nutrient-rich film on the surface of the flowing water. This film is what the Singers eat.

They eat it directly, lowering their mouth-slits to the channels and filtering the water through the vibrating tissue. They absorb the nutrients, the microorganisms, the dissolved minerals from the bone-stone, and the water that passes through them emerges clearer than it went in, purer, now just water again, ready to condense and fall and flow and support the next cycle. This is the symbiosis, the closed loop, the reason the settlement has existed for so long that the skulls in the pavement are not from any species currently alive on this plateau. The Singers drink the water that has been filtered through ancient bone, they eat the bloom that the isopods’ light calls forth, they build their walls from their own shed skin and the isopods’ shed exoskeletons, and when they die, their bones go into the channels too, eventually, after the isopods have cleaned them, after the water has worn them smooth, after the quartz has had time to crystallize in the hollow spaces.

They are not immortal. They die, as I said, and their deaths are the only time the quiet of this place ever truly breaks. When a Singer dies—of old age, always of old age, for there are no diseases here and the isopod-fronds that occasionally grow too sharp are avoided with a care that is almost reverence—the others gather. They take the body to the apex of the curve, to the geode where births occur, and they lay it in the main channel so that the water flows over and through the mouth-slits and the eye sockets and the long flexible hands. Then they sing, all eighteen of them together, a chord so complex that it has more notes than there are Singers, a chord that seems to generate its own subharmonics in the skull-sockets, in the water droplets, in the isopods’ vibrating bodies. The song lasts for hours, for as long as it takes the body to dissolve into the water, to become nutrients, to become a bloom that feeds the living, to become part of the endless cycle. When the song ends, the new Singer is born in the geode, and the population is nineteen again.

They have names, but they do not use them for address. To address another Singer directly would be to assume a separation that doesn’t exist; they are nineteen individuals and also one collective, and the name a Singer carries is more like a function, a position in the rotation, a role in the daily Lifting. There is the One Who Climbs First, the One Who Tends the New Cement, the One Who Clears the Channels, the One Who Watches the Sky. The last of these is the only Singer who ever leaves the compressed street, climbing up through a narrow chimney in the rock at one end of the curve, emerging onto the plateau’s surface to monitor the condensation cycles, to gauge the brightness, to note any changes in the wind that might affect the settlement’s delicate balance. When the Watcher returns, they sing their findings, and the melody of that song tells the others everything they need to know: whether to expect a dry cycle, whether to prepare for a flood, whether the pearlescent light will hold steady or dim. The Watcher is always the oldest Singer, the one closest to death, the one whose bones are already beginning to feel like bone-stone under their integument.

What do they think about? What passes through their minds during the long quiet hours between the Liftings, when the isopods are still and the water is flowing and there is nothing to do but exist? They think about the walls, mostly. They read the skulls the way you would read a history, tracing the lineages of the different species represented, the shifts in skull shape that mark the passage of geological eras. They know—they have always known—that their settlement is built on a mass death, a deposit of small vertebrates that lived and died long before the Singers ever evolved. They do not know what killed them. They have theories, sung back and forth across the generations, harmonics layered on harmonics until the original meaning is almost lost. Some think it was a flood, a sudden rush of water that drowned the creatures in their burrows and washed their bodies into this bowl. Some think it was a slow accumulation, centuries of natural death and bone deposition. Some think—and this is the theory that holds the most weight, the one that is sung most often, the one that makes the isopods quiver in their sockets—that the creatures were not killed at all. That they simply chose to stop moving, to settle into the stone, to become the foundation for something that would come later. That the skulls are not remains but gifts.

This theory is supported by the fact that the skulls are not random. They are arranged. Look closer at the pavement, and you will see that the cobbles are not just packed together; they are sorted by size, by shape, by the presence or absence of particular foramina, the small holes where nerves and blood vessels once passed. The pattern is too regular to be natural, too specific to be accidental. The Singers did not make this pattern. They inherited it. They maintain it, replacing damaged skulls with new ones, but the new ones always come from the same deposit, a deep layer below the street that they access through a hidden channel near the geode, and the deposit seems endless. They have been digging skulls out of it for as long as their memory extends, and the supply has never diminished. They have simply never reached the bottom.

There is a Singer alive now—the One Who Clears the Channels, whose hands are perpetually wet and whose voice is pitched slightly lower than the others, a grounding bass note in the constant counterpoint—who has begun to question this. Who has begun to sing, quietly, when the others are not listening, a new song about digging deeper, about reaching the bottom, about finding out what lies beneath the endless skulls. This is the closest the Singers ever come to heresy. Channels does not sing the song openly, only in the small hours of the quiet cycle, when the isopods are still and the other Singers are folded into their own walls, their bodies tucked into sockets, their mouth-slits closed in something like sleep. But the song is there. It hangs in the air after they finish, a residual vibration that the isopods’ setae detect, that the water carries, that the skulls absorb into their quartz-filled cavities. The other Singers hear it, even if they don’t acknowledge it. Even the One Who Watches the Sky hears it, all the way up on the plateau’s surface, and when they climb back down and sing their report of brightness and condensation and wind, there is a new note in their voice, a harmonic of unease that has not been there before.

What happens next? That is not for you to know. You are still standing in the street, your feet numb, your breath a cloud, the light a constant pearlescent pressure on your eyes. The Singers know you are there. They have known since you arrived. They do not acknowledge you—they have no protocol for visitors, no history of contact with anything that is not water or isopod or bone—but they are aware, and their songs have shifted subtly to accommodate your presence, adding a harmonic that means “something new” or “something dangerous” or “something that will eventually leave.” For now, they wait. They maintain the walls. They sing the Lifting. They eat the bloom and drink the water and read the skulls. And somewhere, in the deep layers below the street, Channels’ song is still vibrating, still seeking, still asking the question that has never been asked before: what is under the skulls? What is at the bottom? What laid the first stone?
