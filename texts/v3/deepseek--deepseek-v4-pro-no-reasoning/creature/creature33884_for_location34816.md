The ones who live here are called the Attendants.

It is not a title they chose for themselves, but one that stuck — an old word from an old language, spoken now mostly by the city’s infrastructure systems when they announce maintenance cycles or passenger manifests. The Attendants have lived on the plateau long enough that their own name for themselves has drifted into the same sub-audible hum as the city’s vibration: felt, not spoken. If you ask one what they call their people, they will pause. They will look at the damp basalt, or at the wheeling birds, or at their own hands, which are usually gloved in something thin and textured. Then they will say something like “we” or “us” and let the word hang there, as if it were a condensation bead waiting to fall.

They are human. Mostly. Certainly they began that way — colonists, engineers, terraformers, whatever the old records call them — but that was centuries ago, and the plateau does not permit anything to remain unchanged. The Attendants are tall, as a rule, with the kind of elongated bone structure that comes from growing up in gravity that is very slightly less than Earth-standard and air that is very slightly thinner than lungs prefer. Their chests are deep, their breathing slow and deliberate. They move with a careful economy, the way people do when they have learned from childhood that running out of breath at the wrong moment means a long, cold sit on wet stone while the world spins gently around you. Their skin runs toward browns and deep olives, and their hair is universally dark and worn very short or bound tight against the head, because long hair catches condensation and drips into the eyes.

Their faces are difficult to read if you are not one of them. The expressions are there, but muted — not from emotional restraint, but from a kind of physical patience. An Attendant’s smile is a slow thing that arrives over the course of a conversation and departs just as gradually, like the movement of a shadow across basalt. Their anger is cold and quiet and can last for years without anyone realizing it was ever anger at all, until some small decision tips and reveals the shape of the grudge beneath. They do not laugh often, but when they do, it is startlingly warm, a sound that seems too large for their careful bodies.

Their clothing is functional and layered: tight undersuits of some dark, slick material that wicks condensation away from the skin, covered by looser outer wraps of woven fiber that they make themselves from the tough, filamentous stalks of the rock-moss. The fiber is coarse and slightly stiff, dyed in the deep purples and near-blacks of the mats it comes from. The Attendants’ garments are never brightly colored. To wear bright color on the plateau is to be visible for kilometers against the dark stone, and visibility, to them, feels like shouting in a library. They prefer to blend — with the basalt, with the shadowed folds of the towers, with the long, grey light. Their clothing often incorporates small, integrated technologies: heating filaments, pressure sensors, communication meshes that link them to the city’s information systems with a subvocal murmur. But these are old technologies now, inherited and repaired and repaired again until they are as much organic as synthetic, seeded with the same self-assembling ceramics that grow in the towers.

Attendants do not live in family units in any way recognizable from Earth. They live in *rings* — loose, overlapping circles of between five and thirty individuals who share a district, a tower complex, or a transit spine segment. Ring membership shifts slowly over a lifetime. A child grows up in one ring, then migrates to another in adolescence, then perhaps to a third in adulthood, then back to the first in old age. Rings are not defined by blood, though blood relations exist and are acknowledged with a particular quiet tenderness. An Attendant will refer to someone as “my sibling” or “my child” without any of the possessiveness those words carry in other cultures. The relationship is a fact, like the location of a lake or the schedule of the transit pods. It simply is.

Children are rare. The Attendants have long since passed the point where biology alone governs reproduction; their bodies are supplemented and adjusted by the same ambient biotechnologies that maintain the city. Conception is deliberate, slow, and requires the consensus of at least one full ring. A child is raised collectively, passed from arms to arms, taught by whoever happens to be present when a question needs answering. The result is a population that is emotionally self-sufficient but profoundly interconnected. An Attendant alone is perfectly comfortable. An Attendant among others is perfectly comfortable. The distinction barely registers.

Their work — if it can be called work — is maintenance in the broadest sense. They tend the condensation channels, scraping away mineral buildup with tools that are themselves grown from ceramic and shaped to fit the hand that wields them. They monitor the slow migration of the city’s districts, adjusting anchor lines and realigning transit spines with the patience of gardeners training vines. They track the mist-fish populations, harvest them in cycles that have been refined over generations to keep the ecosystem stable, and preserve the meat in cold, dry vaults carved into the basalt. They study the cracks in the stone — the ones the city is cultivating — and make small, precise interventions: a wedge of ice placed here, a heating filament withdrawn there, a chemical wash applied to accelerate or slow the etching. This is their art and their science, their longest-running project. They call it *stone-shaping*, though the word in their own language is closer to *slow conversation*. They speak of the basalt as if it were a partner in a dialogue that will outlast them all.

Their relationship with the city is intimate and unspoken. The Attendants do not build anymore; they have forgotten how, or perhaps they have simply moved past the need. The city grows itself, and the Attendants live within that growth the way symbiotic organisms live within a host body. They clean the ceramic ribs of the towers where they can reach. They clear debris from the involute transit tubes with long, flexible brushes that they make from mustelid hair. They talk to the city’s systems — not with commands, but with the same calm, murmuring tone they use with each other. The city listens. Whether it understands is a question the Attendants no longer ask. The city responds: a door opens, a light adjusts, a condensation trap diverts water to a dry algal mat. That is enough.

They eat simply. Mist-fish, preserved or fresh, sliced thin and eaten raw with a crumble of dried rock-moss that tastes sharply mineral and slightly sweet. A dense, chewy bread made from cultivated yeasts grown in the warm hum of the city’s power cores. Water, always water, drunk from condensation taps that gurgle softly in the walls. They eat alone or together, standing or sitting on the smooth ledges of their towers, facing out toward the plateau. Meals are quiet. The view is enough.

Their recreation is subtle. They walk the long, open stretches of basalt between districts, alone or in small groups, their footfalls soft on the wet stone. They watch the birds — there are Attendants who have spent decades learning the patterns of a single species, mapping the relationship between a particular flock’s flight and the half-degree shifts in lake temperature. They weave. The coarse moss-fiber takes dye in unpredictable ways, and the Attendants have turned this unpredictability into an aesthetic: no two garments alike, each wrap a record of the specific mineral content of the water used in its making. They tell stories, but not around fires — there is no wood to burn, and the towers provide all the warmth they need. Instead, they tell stories in the dark, lying on their sleeping platforms with the city’s sub-audible hum running through their bones. The stories are long and recursive, full of characters who are also landscapes, landscapes who are also weather, weather who speaks in the voice of a long-dead Attendant whose ring still remembers her name.

Their relationship with death is practical. The Attendants die — eventually. Their biotechnologies have extended their lifespans, but not indefinitely. When an Attendant feels the approach of death, a slow and recognizable thing that comes with a particular coolness in the extremities and a particular clarity of thought, they inform their ring. There is a ritual: the dying Attendant walks out onto the basalt, alone or accompanied, and finds a place where the stone is still raw and unshaped. They lie down. The cold does the rest. The body freezes, eventually — the plateau’s temperature dips low enough at night — and the ice crystals work their way through the tissues, preserving the form for a long, slow time. The birds do not touch them. The mustelids, curious, sometimes sniff at the frozen features and then move on. The body becomes part of the stone, slowly, over decades, as the freeze-thaw cycles and the eternal condensation etch it into the basalt. An Attendant grave is a slight depression in the rock, a faint outline of a human shape, visible only in certain light. The rings remember where their dead lie. They visit, sometimes, and pour a little water from their own condensation flasks onto the outline, as if helping the erosion along.

This is not sad to them. It is the slow conversation, continued past the point of speech.

They are not a large population — a few tens of thousands, spread across a plateau the size of a small continent. You can walk for days between districts and see no one. But they are dense in their own small way: the towers are full of them, going about their quiet business, tending and eating and weaving and watching. The Attendants have no government in the traditional sense. Decisions that affect multiple rings are made through a process so slow and layered that it hardly reads as decision-making at all. A question is posed: Should the city’s southern transit spine be realigned to follow a new crack system? It passes from ring to ring, carried in the subvocal meshes and in the mouths of travelers. People discuss it, or they don’t. They think about it while they scrape the channels. They dream about it, sometimes, and share the dreams. After months or years, a consensus emerges — not a vote, not a decision, but a gradual settling of opinion like silt in a still pool. The city’s systems register the shift and act accordingly. No one gives an order. No one needs to.

They are, in their own understanding, a people who have stopped striving. Not stopped living — they are vividly alive, attentive and present and curious about their world. But they have stopped pushing. The plateau is not finished, and neither are they, and neither is the city. The process will take millions of years. There is no hurry. When an Attendant looks out across the raw, wet basalt at the thin grey sky, they feel something that might be called peace, if peace were a more active word — a sense of being exactly where they belong, in exactly the right moment of a story that began long before them and will continue long after. They are small against the plateau. They know this. And the knowing is not a diminishment, but a kind of quiet, profound relief.

Sometimes, very rarely, one of them will look up at the ordinary sky and feel a flicker of something else — a curiosity about where they came from, about the ships that brought their ancestors, about the other worlds that might exist beyond the thin grey light. But the feeling passes. The condensation runs in the walls. The birds wheel and fall silent. The city hums its sub-audible hum. And the Attendant returns to their work, which is not really work, and their life, which is not really separate from the life of the stone and the water and the moss and the birds, but part of it, one more slow voice in the long, quiet conversation of the plateau.
