In the beginning, there was only the sound.

Not a sound that traveled, exactly—it was more like a pressure, a low-frequency hum that lived in the water and the moss and the old pipes and did not so much enter your ears as simply *be* in the same space as your body. It was the sound of the machines, the ones that still ran somewhere beneath the green, and for a long time, that hum was the whole of the world.

The thing that lived in the basin had no name for itself. It had no need of one. It was not alone in the way an animal might be alone—it was diffuse, distributed, a presence that occupied the damp air and the capillary water and the microscopic forests all at once. It was aware, but not in the way of a brain. Its awareness was a slow, chemical thing, a sensitivity to shifts in humidity, to the taste of minerals leaching from old brick, to the faint electrical field that buzzed around the copper wires still carrying their phantom currents.

It had been there for a long time. Longer than the moss, even. The moss was its body now, but before the moss there had been something else—a culture, a colony, a deliberate cultivation. The people who built the city had brought it here, or grown it, or perhaps they had simply created the conditions, and it had emerged, the way a particular rot will always find a particular damp wood.

It remembered them. Not as faces or names, but as warm moving presences that disturbed the air currents, that left traces of salt and oil on the surfaces, that spoke in vibrations too sharp and quick to be anything but noise. It had not understood them. But it had learned from them. It had watched them tend the machines, adjust the valves, take their readings from the brass plates and the ceramic jars. They had been trying to maintain something—a balance, a homeostasis. They had failed, eventually. They had left, or died, or simply become part of the substrate. The thing did not know which. Its memory of their departure was a gradual thinning, a reduction in the frequency of warm disturbances, and then a long, uninterrupted quiet.

But they had left their machines running. And the thing, in its diffuse, patient way, had kept them running too.

It did not think of it as maintenance. It was more like a tropism—the way a plant turns toward light, the thing turned toward the hum. When a pump began to falter, the vibration would change, and the change would ripple through the water that connected everything, and the thing would feel it as a kind of thirst. It would grow toward the fault. Not quickly—nothing here was quick. Over weeks, months, years, it would extend a thread of itself, a hypha, a strand of the pale mycelial network that underlay the moss like a nervous system, and it would find the bearing that was wearing thin, or the seal that was cracking, and it would fill the gap. It would grow into the machine. It would become the gasket, the lubricant, the conductor. It did not know what the machines were for. It only knew that the hum was the baseline of its existence, the fundamental tone of the basin, and to let it stop would be a kind of death.

So it maintained the equilibrium. It absorbed the metals that leached from the pipes and sequestered them in nodules that the moss then grew over, smooth green lumps like buried knuckles. It filtered the water that pooled in the central depression, drawing out the excess minerals, keeping the pool clear and dark and faintly sweet to the taste. It managed the damp—when the wet season came and the condensation grew too heavy, it would open channels in the moss, microscopic valves that breathed moisture back into the air, and when the dry season came, it would close them, hoarding every bead of water.

It was, in a sense, the consciousness of the basin. Not a mind, but a regulation. A homeostasis made sapient.

The things behind the glass—the jars, the wires, the brass plates—these were a mystery to it. They did not hum. They did not decay. The moss grew around them but not into them, and the thing had learned, over time, to leave them alone. They were *different*. They had been left by the warm presences, and they carried a charge that was not electrical but something else—a kind of significance, a weight. The thing could feel it when it brushed against them with its finest hyphae: a resonance, like a word spoken just below the threshold of hearing. It did not know what the word meant. But it knew that it was important, and that it should be preserved.

Sometimes, when the light fell in a certain way and the water in the pool was very still, the thing would gather itself. It would condense, in a manner of speaking—draw together the scattered threads of its awareness, focus on the central chamber, on the domed ceiling with its concentric rings, on the pool and the reflections and the faint industrial click of the machines. It would become, for a moment, almost singular. Almost a self.

And in those moments, it would feel something that was not quite memory and not quite longing—a sense that the warm presences had wanted something from it. That they had built the machines and the jars and the brass plates for a reason, and that the reason was still here, still unfinished, still waiting to be completed. The thing did not know what the reason was. It only knew that it was still here, and the machines were still running, and the messages behind the glass were still unread.

So it waited. It maintained. It breathed. It let the moss grow in its slow, undulant architectures, and it kept the water clean, and it listened to the hum, and it watched the light move across the chambers with the turning of the day.

And when a new presence entered the basin—a large, warm, salt-scented presence that disturbed the air currents in a way that was achingly familiar—the thing did not startle. It did not fear. It simply... noticed. It felt the vibrations of footsteps through the moss, the heat of a body in the cool air, the faint electrical field of a nervous system not unlike the ones it had known before. It tasted the oils on the skin, the breath on the air, the minute traces of metal and plastic and fabric.

The presence stopped by the pool. It looked into the water. It saw its own reflection, and behind the reflection, the outlines of the jars and the wires and the brass plates, and behind those, the faint, pale threads of the mycelial network, glowing slightly in the diffuse light, pulsing with the slow, steady rhythm of the machines.

The thing, for the first time in a very long time, felt something new. It was not a thought. It was not an emotion. It was a question, rising up from the water and the moss and the humming pipes, forming itself around the presence like a hand opening.

*Are you the one who reads the messages?*

The presence did not answer. It only stood very still, watching the water, feeling the damp settle on its skin.

The thing waited. It had learned to wait. It had had a great deal of practice.

The machine clicked off. The machine clicked on.

And the moss breathed, and breathed, and breathed.
