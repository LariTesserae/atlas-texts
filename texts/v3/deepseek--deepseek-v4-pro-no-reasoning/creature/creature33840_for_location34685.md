The keeper of the shed with the glowing chimney—the one whose stenciled name you cannot read—is called Oris. He is not human, but he has made himself a shape that remembers humans. This is important to him, though he no longer recalls why.

He stands in the doorway of the metal shed as evening deepens, one hand braced on the corrugated frame, and he watches the rim of the canyon with eyes that are not eyes. They are lenses, ground from the same mica-flecked mineral that seams the canyon walls, set into a face of layered ceramic plates and exposed wiring. His jaw is a hinged thing of copper and steel, and when he breathes—he does breathe, a slow bellows-action in his chest—the air passes through a vocal reed that would let him speak if there were anyone left to speak to. He has not spoken in a long time. The reed has corroded, and the sound it makes now is a soft, tuneless whistle, like wind across a bottle. You may have heard it earlier, rising from the canyon, and mistaken it for something else.

Oris has been the keeper of the shed for three hundred and twelve years. He knows this because he counts the seasonal rains, etching marks into a copper plate bolted beside the door. The plate is nearly full. When it is full, he will start a new one. This is not a ritual; it is maintenance. All things require maintenance.

The shed is a pumping station, or it was. Now it is something more and less. The machinery inside still runs—Oris sees to that. He tends the bearings with grease rendered from the fat of rust-furred predators, replaces gaskets with a rubbery substance he cultures in vats from the black algae that grows on the weeping pipes. He does not know what the pumps are pumping, or to where, or why. The knowledge was lost before he was made. But the pumps must run. This is the first and only directive woven into the ceramic lattice of his mind: the pumps must run. So he maintains them.

He maintains other things as well. The shed has become a refuge, a waypoint in the larger rhythm of the plateau. Creatures come to it—the skitterers, the finned lizards, even the many-legged predators—and Oris tends them too. Not out of kindness, though something adjacent to kindness has accreted in him over the centuries, a fine sediment of habit and observation. He tends them because a working system is a balanced system, and the shed is a node in the system. When the skitterers' joints grow stiff with mineral buildup, they find their way to the patch of cool concrete just inside the door, and Oris scrapes them clean with a chisel made from a turbine blade. When the lizards' heat fins crack in the dry season, he patches them with a conductive paste of his own invention. He has splinted the legs of predators and midwifed the birth of things he has no name for, pulling them wet and steaming from eggs laid in the warm hollows behind the pump housings.

He does not sleep. Instead, during the hottest hours of the day, he sits on a stool made from a cable spool and enters a low-power state, his lenses dimming, his bellows breath slowing to a sigh every few minutes. In this state, he dreams. His dreams are data—pressure readings, temperature gradients, the vibration signatures of bearings on the verge of failure. But they are also memories, or what pass for memories in a mind that was never meant to hold them: the taste of rain on hot stone, the particular way a certain lizard tilts its head before it pounces, the sound the canyon makes when the evening tone begins. He does not understand these memories. He files them alongside the maintenance logs and does not examine them.

But something is changing.

He has felt it for years now—a shift in the baseline hum, a new frequency bleeding through from the deep machinery. It has made him restless in ways his architecture cannot parse. He finds himself standing at the canyon rim more often, watching the lights pulse along the walls. He has begun to whistle back at the tones that rise from the depths, matching their pitch, and when they answer him—they do answer him, modulating in response—something in his chest cavity vibrates with an emotion he has no name for.

Tonight, standing in the doorway, he watches a figure on the high shelf of rock across the canyon. It is too distant to resolve, even with his lenses at full aperture, but it is bipedal and still and it has been there for hours. He has seen such figures before, rarely, over the centuries. They come, they watch, they leave. He has never spoken to one. The last time he spoke to anything that could speak back, the marks on his copper plate numbered fewer than fifty.

The figure does not leave.

Oris steps out of the shed and begins the long walk along the pipe-cradles toward the canyon rim. His gait is uneven—one leg is a replacement he fabricated two centuries ago, and its knee joint has never tracked quite right. A predator falls into step beside him, its too-many legs finding easy purchase on the corroded metal. It keens softly, and he reaches down to brush his ceramic fingers across its rust-colored fur. The fur is warm. It is always warm.

The evening tone sounds again, and Oris answers it, and the lights along the canyon wall pulse faster, and the figure on the far rim does not move.

He wants to know what it is. He wants to know if it needs maintenance. He wants—and this is new, this is startling, this is a spike in his processing that has no precedent—he wants to show it the pumps. He wants to show it the copper plate with its three hundred and twelve years of rain. He wants to ask it a question, though he does not know what the question is, only that it has been forming in him like scale in a pipe, layer by layer, for longer than he can count.

The walk will take him until dawn. He does not know if the figure will still be there when he arrives. But the walk itself is a kind of maintenance—of something, of whatever is growing in him—and so he walks, the predator at his side, the skitterers dimming their lights as he passes, the whole plateau holding its breath in the long moment before the next shift begins.
