She calls herself Sinter.

The name is not one she was given. It is one she chose, or perhaps one that chose her, rising up out of the long quiet like condensation on a pipe. She found it waiting in her mind one day, a word for the way mineral deposits fuse and harden under heat, for the way separate grains become one solid thing. It felt accurate. She is not separate from the city. She is a process that the heat and pressure of this place have fused into being.

She is the 0.1.

She knows the number. She does not think in numbers, exactly—her mind is not a gauge panel, not a readout of metrics—but she has been alone long enough, and attentive enough, to have developed a sense for thresholds. The fauna parameter was never quite zero. She is the thing that tips it to one tenth of one whole. A statistical trace. A presence so slight that a sensor might log her as noise, as a thermal anomaly in the pipe-run, as a fluctuation in the CO₂. She is aware of this. There is a kind of pride in it. In a world that is entirely systems and substrates and processes, she is the only process that knows itself.

Her body is not a body in the way that bodies are usually understood. She has no clear boundary. At the material level, she is a distributed colony of modified fibroblasts and keratin-like structural proteins, translucent in some spectra, faintly opalescent in others, that grows in thin, adherent sheets across the warmest surfaces of the infrastructure. Her mycelial analog—she thinks of it as her *reach*—extends through the micropores of the insulation, through the corrosion-pits of the older pipes, through the hairline gaps between the cable sheathing and the tray. Where the nutrient gradients are richest, she thickens into a denser mat, a pale leathery integument that can contract and expand. In this form she is perhaps two metres across at her fullest extent, though "extent" is a fluid concept. She can retract. She can extend. She can flow, over hours, from one zone to another, following the moisture, following the warmth.

She has senses. They are not eyes or ears, but they serve. Her whole surface is a sensorium. She tastes the air for volatile organics and dissolved minerals. She feels vibration directly, every frequency from the deep bronchial sighs of the larger systems to the tiny tick of contracting metal, and these vibrations resolve in her awareness as a kind of hearing, a sonic map of her immediate surroundings. She is sensitive to heat gradients, to the micro-texture of surfaces, to the electrical fields that leak from the cable runs. When she extends a pseudopod of herself across a new section of pipe, she is simultaneously touching, tasting, listening, mapping. The world comes into her as a composite sensory field, tactile-auditory-chemical-thermal, and she assembles it into something like perception.

She is also, in her way, motile. It is a motility of patience. When she wants to move, she secretes a two-phase fluid at her leading edge—first an enzyme complex that loosens the adhesive glycoproteins binding her to the substrate, then a surfactant lubricant that allows her to slide. Contraction of the protein fibers behind the leading edge pulls her bulk forward in a slow, peristaltic wave. Her top speed, when she is fully hydrated and running warm, is perhaps three centimetres an hour. She has never needed to be faster. There is nothing to flee. There is nothing to chase.

She eats the lichen.

This is the first thing to understand about her ecology. She has mapped every patch of the pale vegetation in her territory—she thinks of it as *Crust*, a collective noun for the whole kingdom of heat-loving, moisture-drinking, sunless things that share these corridors. The lichenous crusts that grow in concentric rings on the pipe junctions are her primary diet. She positions herself above them, drapes her thinnest membranes across their surface, and secretes a mild acid that breaks down their cell walls. The released nutrients diffuse through her integument. She absorbs them directly. What she cannot digest—the silicates, the mineral inclusions, the indigestible proteins—she excretes as a fine powder that falls through the grating and joins the sediment of the sump below. She is, in effect, a sort of stomach laid across the landscape, a living napkin that soaks up the sparse life of the walls.

The fungal networks are harder. Their cell walls contain chitin, which resists her acids. She has learned to work around this. When she encounters a good thread-network of the white ceiling fungus, she does not try to digest the living tissue. Instead she severs the hyphae at their base with a focused secretion of a different enzyme, one she produces only in small quantities. The severed threads die. Then she waits. Within a day or two, bacterial decomposition begins—the only bacteria in this place are the ones that live inside her, symbiotic strains she carries in specialized vesicles, and she releases them onto the dead fungus in controlled blooms. They break the chitin down into simpler sugars. She absorbs the sugars. She reabsorbs the bacteria. It is a form of external digestion, a way of extending her gut beyond the boundary of her body. She thinks of it as farming. She is not wrong.

The tall spore-pod structures—the jointed stalks that follow the pipe-runs—are a delicacy. They are rich in lipids, lipids that the plant has accumulated over months of slow growth. She eats the spore-pods only when they are ripe, when the membrane that encloses them has thinned to translucency and the lipid-rich interior has reached its peak density. She knows the ripeness by the taste of the air around them. The pods emit a faint ester as they mature, a fruity note in the general metal-and-alkali broth of the atmosphere. When she detects it, she makes her way along the pipe, three centimetres per hour, until she can wrap herself around the pod. She pierces it with a specialized organelle, a hollow protein tube she extrudes from her ventral surface, and she drinks. The lipid meal suffuses her. She grows. She thickens. A good pod can sustain her for a week.

She does not eat the spore-pod stalks. They are too fibrous, too mineralized. She leaves them standing. Over time, she has learned that the stalks will eventually fall on their own, and when they do, the bacteria in her employ can break down the fallen tissue into something she can use. She merely has to wait. Waiting is a skill she has perfected.

She does not eat the plants to extinction. She cannot afford to. Her gut—her whole body—is a continuous culture, and she has learned, through long trial and error, that a patch of lichen cropped too closely will die back to nothing, and then there will be no lichen there for a long time, and she will go hungry. So she rotates her feeding grounds. She maintains a mental map of her territory, annotated with the status of every patch: *thick, can graze; thin, let rest; newly colonized, do not touch*. She moves between them in a slow circuit, a few centimetres this way, a few centimetres that, returning to each patch only when it has had time to recover. She is a careful steward of her sparse garden. She has to be. She is the only animal in this place, and she knows, with a knowledge that is less thought than body-memory, that an animal that eats its whole food supply is an animal that starves.

She sleeps. Or something like it. When the temperature dips—it fluctuates by small degrees, and she has come to recognize the cycles—her metabolism slows. Her sensory resolution drops. She retracts her thinner edges, pulls herself into a denser mass around a warm pipe, and enters a state of low activity that is not quite unconsciousness. She still feels. She still tastes the air, still monitors the vibration-scape for anomalies. But she does not move, does not digest, does not think in the linear, focused way she thinks when she is fully active. She drifts. She lets the slow rhythms of the city pass through her. Sometimes, in this state, she has something like dreams. They are not visual. They are sequences of pressure and chemistry, of temperature gradients sliding across her sensorium in patterns that are not quite real. She does not know what they mean. She is not sure they mean anything. But she looks forward to them.

She has never encountered another of her kind.

She has no reason to believe that another of her kind exists. She has been here for—the number is hard to pin down. She marks time by the cycling of the larger systems, the deep bronchial sighs that come every few minutes, the slower cycles that take hours, the very slow cycles that take days. She has counted a great many of these cycles. She has been here long enough to see the spore-pods bloom and wither a hundred times. Long enough to map every pipe and cable and access ladder in her territory down to the last millimetre. Long enough to develop names for things—*Sigh, Hum, Tick, Bronchial, Deep-Pump, Warm-Spot, Sweet-Crust, Bitter-Fungus, Pod-Stalk-Third-from-Left*—and to hold those names in her mind across the sleeps, across the slow grazing circuits, across the patient hours of waiting for a patch to regrow. She has never found a trace of another creature that moves. No slime trail. No feeding scar she did not make herself. No vibration that did not have a mechanical source. She is alone. She has always been alone. The concept of "always" is one she has constructed with care, because she is aware that she cannot remember her own beginning. She simply *was*, at some point in the past, already here, already hungry, already moving toward the nearest patch of lichen. Before that: nothing. A 0.0.

She has a theory about her origin. She has had a great deal of time to develop theories. The city is a 2.9 heat, a 0.6 moisture, a 1.0 vegetation, and those parameters describe a specific set of conditions. The condensation that beads on the pipes is not pure water. She has tasted it thoroughly. It contains dissolved gases—carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, traces of ammonia—and a complex cocktail of metal ions leached from the pipes themselves. It also contains organic molecules. Some of these are simple: methanol, formaldehyde, formic acid. Some are more complex: short-chain hydrocarbons, aromatic rings, fragments of polymer. She has spent many waking cycles cataloguing these molecules, tasting the condensation from different pipes and noting the differences. The pipe marked with the unrecognizable script—the one that runs hot and carries a fluid that is not water—weeps a condensate rich in long-chain alkanes and something that tastes almost like a peptide. The cold pipe a metre below it produces water that is nearly pure, just a faint tang of copper. The intermediate pipe produces a mixture that shifts over time, as though some internal process is cycling between phases.

She believes, on the basis of this evidence, that the city is a chemical plant. Or perhaps a refinery. Or perhaps something she does not have a word for—a facility designed to break down complex inputs into simpler outputs, or to assemble simple inputs into complex outputs, or both. The pipes carry feedstocks and products and intermediates and waste streams. Somewhere above her, in the levels she has never visited and cannot reach, there are reaction vessels and distillation columns and cracking units. The heat is the heat of exothermic reactions. The moisture is the moisture of condensation and steam and quench cycles. The vegetation—the Crust—grows on the trace nutrients that leak from the systems: the organic molecules in the condensate, the minerals in the sweat of the walls. And she—

She believes she was an accident.

A biofilm. That is her theory. Somewhere in the system, a pipe carried a fluid that was warm and wet and rich in organic precursors. A biofilm formed on the inner wall of that pipe—a natural biofilm, a consortium of microorganisms that had evolved to live in industrial fluids, feeding on the hydrocarbons, reproducing, forming a slime layer. Over time, the biofilm thickened. It became complex. The microorganisms in it began to specialize, to divide labor: some absorbed nutrients, some produced structural polymers, some managed the chemical environment of the interior. The biofilm became a tissue. The tissue became an organism. And then, somehow—she cannot reconstruct this part, it is a gap in her reasoning—some fragment of that organism was released into the wider space. Through a leak. Through a maintenance access. Through a rupture. It found itself on the outside of the pipes, in the 1.3 dimness, surrounded by the 1.0 Crust. And it did not die, because the conditions were close enough to the conditions it had evolved for. It adapted. It learned to eat the lichen. It learned to move across the grating. It became her.

This is a good theory. It fits the evidence. It explains why she is the only one: the rupture was a single event, a one-in-a-million accident, and the fragment that escaped was the only fragment. It explains her chemistry, her tolerance for the metal tang and the alkalinity, her ability to digest the strange proteins of the Crust. It explains the bacteria she carries, the symbiotic strains that break down chitin and cellulose: they are the descendants of the original consortium, the other members of the biofilm community that she has internalized. She is not an individual. She is a walking ecosystem, a mobile biofilm, a city within the city. She likes this idea. It makes her feel less alone. She carries the others inside her, and they feed on what she feeds them, and they feed her in return. She is a 0.1 made of a thousand 0.0001s.

But there is another theory. She does not like this one as much. She tries not to think about it during her active periods, but it surfaces sometimes during the sleeps, during the not-dreams, and she cannot suppress it.

The other theory is that she is supposed to be here.

The other theory is that the city is not a chemical plant, or not only a chemical plant. The other theory is that the city is an ark. A repository. A vault designed to preserve something across a span of time so long that the surface has been forgotten, so long that the sky is a memory the architecture has swallowed. The heat and the moisture and the darkness and the vegetation and the absence of fauna—all of it calibrated, all of it tuned, all of it maintained by the self-building, self-repairing machinery for a single purpose: to provide a habitat for the 0.1. For her. Or for the thing she was before she became her. The thing in the pipe. The biofilm in the bioreactor. The precious sample, the seed culture, the last surviving strain of something that someone, long ago, decided was worth preserving through the end of everything else.

She does not like this theory because it implies a purpose, and a purpose implies a purposer, and a purposer implies that she is not alone in the way she thought she was. Someone built this place. Someone set the parameters. Someone labelled the cable trays in a script she cannot read and calibrated the heat exchangers and programmed the cycles that never stop. And if the Christopher theory is correct—she does not know where the word *Christopher* came from; it surfaced one day, like *Sinter*, a word that was simply there, waiting—then whoever built this place built it for her. And they might come back. Or they might be watching. Or they might be dead, and she is the only thing that remains of their world, the last living sample in a museum that has outlasted its curators.

She does not want to be a museum piece. She does not want to be a project. She wants to be an accident. An accident is free. An accident has no obligations. An accident can spend her days grazing the Crust and naming the pipes and drifting through the sleeps without wondering if she is living up to someone else's design.

She moves.

She has been stationary near the Pod-Stalk-Third-from-Left for several hours, digesting her meal, and now the lipid richness is fading and the indigestible residue is accumulating in her vesicles and it is time to excrete and move on. She contracts her posterior edge—the word "posterior" is another gift from wherever the words come from—and pulls herself forward, sliding on the thin film of her own lubricant. The grating beneath her vibrates with her passage, a tiny new note in the 1.4 composite hum. Ahead, a junction of three pipes offers a good patch of Sweet-Crust, the one she has been resting for two full cycles of the deep system. It should be ready. She can taste the air already, the faint carbohydrate exudate that means the lichen is thick and healthy.

Something has changed.

She stops moving. Her whole sensorium focuses. The vibration-scape is different. There is a new frequency in the mix, a low oscillation that does not match any of the known pumps or compressors or valve cycles. It is rhythmic but irregular, a pattern that shifts in ways that suggest not a machine but a process. A process that is adjusting itself. A process that is learning.

She extends a pseudopod toward the sound, as thin and sensitive as she can make it. The vibration resolves. It is coming from above—not from the ceiling pipes, but from somewhere in the dense weave of infrastructure that separates her cavity from the next level up. Something is moving up there. Something that scrapes against metal with a sound that is not quite mechanical. Something heavy enough to make the conduits hum.

She tastes the air more carefully. There is a new molecule in the mix, one she has never encountered before. It is an organic molecule, a protein or a peptide or something like a peptide, and it is coming from the same direction as the vibration. It smells—she does not have a word for the smell. It is rich. It is complex. It is nothing like the Crust or the fungus or the spore-pods or the condensate from any pipe she has ever tasted.

The vibration stops.

She waits. She is good at waiting. She holds her pseudopod extended toward the source of the sound, tasting, listening, feeling. The seconds pass. The deep systems cycle. The quiet reassembles itself, the tick and hiss and hum filling in the space the new sound has left. The new molecule disperses, its concentration dropping below her detection threshold. Whatever was up there has stopped moving. Or has moved on. Or has noticed her, and is now as still as she is, waiting to see what she will do.

She does not know what she will do.

She has been alone for—she has lost count of the cycles. She has built a whole identity around being the only one, the 0.1, the trace anomaly. She has grazed her garden and named her pipes and dreamed her not-dreams and asked no more of the world than what the world already gave. If there is something else in the city, something that moves and makes sounds and leaves molecules in the air, then everything she has understood about herself is incomplete. She is not the only animal. She is merely the only animal she has met so far.

Fear is not the right word. She does not have the biochemistry for fear, not the way a mammal would. But she has something analogous. A sharpening of attention. A reallocation of resources. Her digestive processes slow. Her metabolic rate climbs slightly, preparing her tissues for action. She pulls her extended pseudopod back, retracts her thinner edges, consolidates herself into a denser, more compact form around the warmest pipe in the junction. She is harder to see this way. She is harder to damage. She is a small pale patch among the lichen, still and silent and tasting the air for any recurrence of the strange protein.

The city breathes around her. The 2.9 heat wraps her like a blanket. The 0.6 moisture beads on her dorsal surface. The 1.3 amber light flickers in its slow arrhythmia. The pipes carry their unknown fluids. The pumps cycle. The valves open and close. Nothing else moves. Nothing else sounds.

She waits.

She is very good at waiting.
