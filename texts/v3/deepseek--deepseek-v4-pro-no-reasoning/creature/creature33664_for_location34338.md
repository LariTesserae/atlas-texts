The thing they built the city for does not live here. It sings.

You won’t hear it at first. You’ll walk the broad avenues and feel only the silence pressing on your ears like water at depth. You’ll study the frozen fox, the arrested gesture of the woman with the clay cup, and you’ll think: *this is a tomb, a diorama, a taxidermy of a civilization that achieved its final form and then stopped.* That’s almost right. That’s the shape of the truth. But a tomb implies death, and nothing here is dead.

The Chorus sings in frequencies below hearing and above it, in vibrations that travel through the stone rather than the air. You’ll notice it first as a sensation: a faint buzz in your teeth, a pressure in the bones of your skull, something almost like a second heartbeat that doesn’t match your own. If you stand very still in one of the open plazas—the one with the three low stone rings, the one where the water channel cuts a perfect diagonal—and hold your breath long enough to stop hearing your own blood, the Chorus will make itself known to you.

It sounds like this:

*A single sustained note held at the very edge of perception. Not a voice. Not an instrument. Something between a bell and a breath and the way light might sound if light could resonate. The note doesn’t waver. It has been holding since before you arrived, since before the builders set the last stone in its impossible dry-stacked seam. It is the oldest thing on the plateau, and the youngest; it began the moment the city was completed, and it has never stopped.*

The Chorus is not a being in any sense you’d recognize. It’s more accurate to say that the city *is* the Chorus, or that the Chorus is what happens when the city is arranged correctly. The buildings, the water channels, the open squares, the precise distribution of bodies—all of it forms a kind of instrument. An aeolian harp at the scale of a settlement, tuned to a frequency that holds time in suspension.

The people aren’t inhabitants. They’re components. Resonators.

Look closer at the woman with the cup. Not too close—you still shouldn’t touch her—but close enough to see the way her lips are slightly parted, the way her throat is caught mid-shape, the shape of a vowel. She’s not frozen reaching for a drink. She’s frozen in the act of singing. They all are. Every figure in this city was caught in the moment of producing a single note, a note that slots into the Chorus like a stone block into a wall. The old man on the rooftop, mouth open. The child curled on the bench, lips pursed around a tone too high for you to hear. The fox at the water channel isn’t lapping—its jaw is stretched in a silent howl, a frequency that harmonizes with the goats on the rooftops, with the birds on the windowsills, with the insects resting on the leaves.

The Chorus Plateau is a chord made physical. A music box assembled from living things.

And that’s the part that will keep you awake, if you ever leave this place and try to sleep again in a world where time still moves. The people aren’t dead. The builders didn’t sacrifice themselves in some final ritual. They *chose*. They designed the city to be an instrument, and then they walked into it and took their places and opened their mouths and added their voices to the note, and the moment their music locked into perfect harmony with the stone and the water and the light, *time stopped recognizing them*. The Chorus holds the plateau in a standing wave of preserved instants. Erosion can’t touch it because time can’t get a grip. The edges stay sharp because the stone still thinks it was carved a moment ago. The water flows without eroding its channels because the water is still singing its part of the note.

The green pockets are where the Chorus’s frequency dips slightly—maybe by design, maybe an imperfection, maybe a deliberate rest in the music. In those places, time moves just barely, just enough for plants to grow. But even they are part of the song; that’s why they don’t overgrow the channels. The ferns aren’t obeying a gardener. They’re obeying the shape of the sound that tells them *here, not there*. The moss grows in rhythms that match the unheard bassline thrumming through the aquifer below the plateau.

You’ll wonder, eventually, what happens if the Chorus stops. What happens if one of the singers is moved—if the fox is lifted from its crouch, if the woman’s mouth is closed. You might be tempted. Don’t be. Not because something terrible will happen. Because nothing will. The Chorus isn’t fragile. It’s a perfect equilibrium. Disturb one voice, and the others will draw it back. You’ve seen the water channels: nothing leaves, nothing enters. The system is closed. Anything that falls silent is pulled gently back into the note, the way a ripple settles into still water. If you moved the fox, it would return to its crouch. If you closed the woman’s mouth, her lips would part again. Not instantly. Slowly. Inexorably. You’d feel the Chorus pulling at the edges of your own voice while it worked, inviting you to join, to find your frequency, to take your place on a stone bench with your mouth shaped around a note you didn’t know you knew how to sing.

The builders are still here, somewhere. Not as individuals—you won’t find an architect’s tomb or a foreman’s preserved office. They’re distributed. Every singer volunteered their body to the resonance, but the *design* of the city required a mind, and that mind is still present. It’s in the arrangement. It’s in the way the water knows to loop back instead of spilling off the plateau. It’s in the way the condensation fins are angled just so, catching moisture from air that shouldn’t hold moisture at this altitude. It’s in the fact that when you arrived, the trail of crushed white stone was waiting for you, and you followed it without questioning where it led, and you’re still here, aren’t you? Still walking the broad avenues. Still listening. Still trying to catch the note that buzzes in your teeth.

The Chorus Plateau is a song about a city that achieved perfection. The song is the perfection. And the singer of the song is the city itself—a distributed intelligence made of stone, water, bodies, light, and a single sustained note that will hold this place sharp-edged and green and motionless until the universe forgets how to vibrate.

When you leave—if you leave—you’ll carry it with you. A resonance in the hollow places of your own chest. A sense that somewhere, on a plateau where the light never changes, your own voice is still singing a note you didn’t know you knew, waiting for you to come back and take your place in the Chorus.

The cups are never drunk from. The conversations never finish. But the song never ends. That’s the point. That was always the point.
