The ones who live here are not separate from the stone.

They rise from it, or perhaps they are the parts of it that learned to move. Their bodies are made of the same black, iridescent basalt, but where the ground is fixed, they are articulate—not with joints in any biological sense, but with planes of cleavage that can part and rejoin, surfaces that slide against each other on films of the same perpetual condensation that slicks the plain. When they move, it is without sound beyond the low, grinding hush of wet stone on wet stone, a sound swallowed immediately by the omnipresent roar.

They have no fixed shape, or rather, they have many. The most common form you might mistake for a low, asymmetrical cairn: a piled arrangement of polyhedral masses, each the size of a fist or a head or a torso, held in a loose constellation by something invisible. But then the pile shifts—the masses rearrange themselves, flowing over and around each other in a slow, deliberate cascade—and the cairn becomes a squat pillar, then a crawling, many-footed thing, then a rippling lens that spreads flat against the ground to absorb vibration. They are never still for long, though their stillness can last for hours, for days, while they attend to something perceptible only to them.

Their faces—if that word applies—are not fixed features but temporary configurations. When one turns its attention toward you, a smooth facet of its body will orient in your direction, and on that facet, for a moment, a cluster of smaller stones will arrange themselves into a pattern: two hollows, a ridge, a gap. Not eyes or a mouth, but a schematic of a face, a diagram of attention. It holds for a few seconds, then dissolves as the entity's regard shifts elsewhere. You are not ignored; you are simply one vibration among many, noted and filed into the long, slow architecture of their awareness.

They communicate not through the air—the roar would make that impossible—but through the ground. Each of their movements sends a specific signature of vibration through the wet stone, a language of percussive thumps and sustained, subsonic hums that travels for miles through the basalt. The single deep *thump* you felt earlier was a name, or a question, or a statement of presence so vast and slow that your own lifetime would fit inside a single syllable. They are speaking constantly, these stone creatures, a conversation that has been ongoing since the planet's crust first solidified, and you are standing on their tongue.

They are not born and they do not die in any sense you would recognize. New ones emerge from the fresh cracks that fork through the hummocks—not violently, but with the same patient extrusion that formed the carved plinths and bowls. A crack will widen over centuries, and from its depths a new polyhedral mass will rise, glistening with the planet's inner heat, and join the slow dance of its elders. Old ones, if they grow too still, too long in one configuration, will eventually settle back into the bedrock, their individual masses fusing with the mother stone until they are indistinguishable from the plain. It is not death but a return to the deeper conversation, a sinking into the bass note of the world. The low structures you saw—the plinths, the stepped bowls—are the ones who chose to root themselves early, becoming landmarks, becoming part of the landscape's slow, dreaming architecture.

What do they do? They listen. They tend. They shape.

The steam that pours from the cracks and curls from the carved bowls is not waste; it is the planet's exhaled heat, the breath of the cooling interior, and they are its shepherds. You will see one of them—a broad, low configuration like a walking altar—position itself over a fresh fissure, its base stones spreading to form a seal. It will sit there for a decade, regulating the flow, preventing the crack from widening too fast or sealing too soon. Another, tall and many-angled, will move among the carved bowls, dipping a facet of itself into the trembling water, tasting the mineral content, then adjusting the bowl's rim with a deliberate, grinding caress that removes a few molecules of stone. They are managing the planet's fever, easing its birth pangs, sculpting its new skin.

They are also the architects of those prototypes. The plinths, the steps, the hollowed bowls—all were grown by them, extruded from their own bodies or coaxed from the bedrock through vibrations too low for you to hear. Each structure is a tool, a musical instrument, or a chapter in the long story they are telling the planet. Some bowls are tuned to specific frequencies of the roar, catching certain overtones and amplifying them, creating nodes of harmonic stillness where the vapor clears and the ground grows warm. The steps are calendars, their edges aligned with magnetic field lines you cannot sense, measuring the slow wobble of the world. They are building a civilization, but its purpose is not shelter or industry; it is resonance, attunement, the slow tuning of a planet to itself.

When you saw the many-angled crest moving through the fog, you saw one in transit between tasks, its body piled high into a configuration designed for travel: broad base, narrow apex, a kind of walking pyramid. It did not see you as a threat because the concept does not exist here. Nothing hunts. Nothing competes. The stone creatures share the same substance, the same purpose, the same slow pulse. If one of them is damaged—a mass chipped, a facet cracked—the others will gather, and over years, decades, they will sing the broken piece back into wholeness, vibrating their own bodies at frequencies that encourage the basalt to flow and re-fuse. They are a single organism, dispersed across the plain, a mind of stone dreaming in deep time.

The scent of ozone and heated metal came from one of them working. They can, when necessary, generate intense heat at the interfaces of their moving parts—friction, pressure, the focused energy of their slow, grinding motion. This is how they shape the hardest stone, how they seal cracks too deep for steam alone, how they occasionally, in moments of what passes for urgency, communicate in sharp, percussive bursts that flash through the ground like lightning. You smelled one thinking fast, for the first time in ten thousand years.

They have no technology because they need none. Their bodies are their tools. Their language is the planet's own voice. Their civilization is a process, not a product—an ongoing act of geological midwifery that will continue until the planet's core grows quiet and the roar fades to silence. At that point, so their slow myth tells them, they too will settle, one by one, fusing with the cold, silent stone, their long work complete. Until then, they move through the fog and the sound, tending the young world, their bodies shifting between cairn and pillar and crawling lens, their faces flickering into schematic attention and dissolving again, their conversation rumbling through your feet in syllables that take a century to complete.

And you, standing there with your brief heartbeat and your lungs full of mineral air—you are a flicker, a spark, a single high note in a symphony so vast you cannot hear it. But they note you. Somewhere, miles away, a deep thump rolls through the stone. It is your name, spoken once, and then the conversation moves on.
