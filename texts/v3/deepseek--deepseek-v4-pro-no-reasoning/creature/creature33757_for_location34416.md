The keepers are not the ones who shaped the city.

That fact is a quiet one, easy to miss, but it sits at the bottom of everything here like sediment that will not settle. The ones who asked the root to grow lintels and staircases, who spent centuries murmuring the right words or thoughts or exudations into the meristem tips until corridors unfurled like fiddleheads—those beings are gone. Left. Slipped out of the city’s memory so completely that the keepers do not even have a story for it. There is no myth of departure, no legend of a great migration. The keepers simply know, in the way they know the taste of the water or the rhythm of the deep pump, that they were not the first.

The keepers call themselves the Syzygy. It is not a word; it is a gesture, a particular configuration of the webbed fingers against the current, a way of positioning the body in relation to another body that means *we-who-hold-the-balance*. The name itself is a diagram of their role.

They are amphibian, or something adjacent to amphibian—the taxonomy of this place resists Linnaeus. Their bodies are long and loosely jointed, built for drifting rather than swimming, with limbs that end in six-fingered hands where the skin between the digits extends nearly to the tips. The webbing is not just for propulsion; it is their primary sensory surface, densely innervated, capable of reading the faintest shifts in water pressure and chemistry. When a Syzygy pauses in a corridor and spreads its hands wide, it is listening to the city in a way that has no terrestrial equivalent, feeling the subtle pressure-gradients that indicate a blockage in a flow-channel three levels away, or the chemical sigh of a polyp colony under stress.

Their skin is pale, but not uniformly so. Across the torso and the upper back, chromatophores bloom in slow, tidal patterns—not the quick flash of a cephalopod, but something more like the gradual change of a bruise healing, a shift from pearl to lavender to a grey-green like old copper taking centuries to complete a cycle. The Syzygy do not control this consciously. It is tied to their internal state, to the deep rhythms of their own metabolism that beat in sympathy with the city’s pump. A Syzygy in distress goes pale. A Syzygy in deep, restful synchronization with the tangle will slowly bloom with color, a walking garden of mute, oceanic sunset.

Their faces are what a human would call placid: large, dark eyes with nictitating membranes that sweep across them in a slow, diagonal blink; small mouths set in a permanent slight downturn that has nothing to do with sadness; no external ears, but a ridge of sensory papillae along the jawline that serves the same function. They have no hair, no scales—just that smooth, permeable integument, through which they absorb a portion of their respiration and excrete the mild toxins that their symbiotic polyps find delicious. The relationship is direct and physical: a Syzygy who tends a polyp garden will often press their bare back against the colony and let the polyps feed from their skin for hours, a mutual grooming that is also a kind of communion.

They are not many. The city’s volume is vast, a cathedral of hollowed root the size of a mountain turned inside out, and the Syzygy population has never exceeded what the keepers call *a comfortable drift*—perhaps three hundred individuals scattered through the chambers and corridors. They are long-lived, though no one keeps count. A Syzygy will simply continue until they do not, and the transition from presence to absence is treated as a slow fading rather than a sudden event. When one of them grows thin and still, spending more and more time pressed against the warm central trunk where the pump-beat is strongest, the others will visit, will spread their hands against the fading one’s skin to feel the gradual quieting of their internal tides, and will eventually carry the body to a particular chamber deep in the lower tangles where the wood is soft and spongy and will, over years, absorb it. There is no ceremony. There is only the fact of it, accepted with the same still patience with which the city accepts a new branch thickening.

Their lives are structured around maintenance, but “maintenance” is perhaps too active a word. The Syzygy do not repair; they *adjust*. The city, left to itself, would not collapse—the tangle is too deeply woven, the lignified beams too thoroughly fused. But it would drift. Over decades, a wall might thicken too far and pinch off a passage; a flow-channel might grow a baffle of new root-hairs and alter the current; a staircase might slowly spiral itself closed like a fern uncurling in reverse. The Syzygy’s role is to notice these things early, when they are still tendencies rather than problems. They use their hands—the webbed fingers are deft, surprisingly strong—to gently redirect a growing tip, to tease apart fibers that are knitting together where they shouldn’t, to prune with careful, almost surgical precision. They use tools made from the city itself: wedges of particularly dense heartwood, polished to a water-smooth edge; cords of inner-bark fiber that are soaked and twisted and dried (as much as anything dries here) into ropes of extraordinary tensile strength; baskets woven from the shed leaves of a particular epiphyte that grows only near the light-source. They do not have knives in the human sense. They do not cut. They separate, persuade, encourage.

Their social structure is diffuse, almost invisible. There are no leaders, no councils, no disputes that rise to the level of a recognizable argument. Disagreement manifests as a slow, mutual avoidance that can last for decades, two Syzygy simply drifting to opposite sides of the city’s volume until the emotional weather shifts. They do not pair-bond in any exclusive sense; reproduction is a communal affair, the fertilized eggs deposited in a shared nursery-chamber where the water is slightly warmer and the flow is carefully managed, and the resulting young are raised by whoever happens to be nearby when they hatch. A juvenile Syzygy learns by watching, by imitating, by drifting after an adult for a few years until they absorb the patterns. There is no formal instruction. The knowledge is in the hands, in the slow bloom of the chromatophores when a task is done correctly, and it passes from body to body as naturally as the city’s exhalations pass through the water.

But the most remarkable thing about the Syzygy—the thing that a human visitor might finally notice after the strangeness of the architecture and the fauna has settled into something like familiarity—is that they are not lonely.

They live in a city of three hundred souls scattered through a volume that could hold a hundred thousand, in water that muffles sound and turns distance into haze, in a place where the loudest noise is a subsonic thrum that never stops, and they are not lonely. They are not even solitary in any meaningful sense. Because the city is alive, and the city is aware, and the Syzygy are part of its awareness.

The Tangle-Kept City thinks. Not in words, not in anything that could be mistaken for a mind—it has no ego, no intention, no discrete self. But it has a nervous system. The same fine, hair-thin fibers that carry water and nutrients through the tangle also carry electrochemical signals, a slow, distributed computation that has been running for millennia. The city knows when a Syzygy is born. It knows when one fades. It adjusts its growth in response to their movements, thickening the walls of a chamber that hosts a gathering, opening a new passage toward a polyp-bloom that a keeper has been tending. The relationship is symbiotic in the truest sense: the Syzygy shape the city with their hands, and the city shapes itself around the Syzygy in a feedback loop so ancient that cause and effect have merged into a single process.

The keepers feel this. They do not talk about it—they have no words for the sensation, or no need of them—but when a Syzygy presses their palm flat against a wall and spreads their fingers, they are not just testing the water pressure. They are in contact with the city’s slow thought. And the city, in turn, is aware of the warmth of their body, the quiet electrical hum of their living tissues, the particular chemical signature that is *this* Syzygy and not another. In the deepest, most restful hours of their rest-cycle, the keepers dream the city’s dreams: vast, slow images of growth and light, of water moving through infinite conduits, of the weight of centuries settling like a comforting hand.

The polyps are part of this. The fish are part of this, their shoaling patterns influenced by subtle electrochemical fields the city generates without intending to. The shrimp-like grazers, the feathery things that are not quite coral, the bioluminescent microorganisms that drift in the water and contribute their faint honey-glow to the general radiance—all of them are nodes in the same network. The Tangle-Kept City is not a city that contains an ecosystem. It is an ecosystem that has become a city, and the Syzygy are its gardeners, its nervous system, its children, and its hands.

They do not remember the builders. But sometimes, in the oldest chambers, near the central trunk where the braided stems are thick as houses and the grain of the wood flows in ancient cathedrals of lignified time, a Syzygy will pause. Will press both palms against the wall and feel, beneath the deep thrum of the pump, a fainter pulse—a secondary rhythm, older, almost faded out. It is the echo of the instructions that shaped this place, a pattern of electrochemical signals laid down thousands of generations ago by hands that were not webbed, that did not have six fingers.

The Syzygy cannot interpret the pattern. They feel it as a kind of ghost-tickle, a resonance that makes their chromatophores flutter in a brief, unsettled sequence. They do not know that it is a lintel. They do not know that it is a staircase. They simply feel that the wall is, in some way they cannot name, *right*—that it is doing what it was asked to do, long before there were Syzygy to ask anything. And they drift on, their webbed hands trailing against the wood, carrying a basket of transplanted polyps from one chamber to another, keeping the balance as they have always kept it, in the honey-light, in the cool water, in the city that breathes.
