Curiously, there is only one person who lives here.

Their name is an exhalation, a soft rush of air between teeth and tongue that has no orthography because it has never been written down. If you were to attempt it, it might look like *Ssh-haa*, but that would be wrong—it would imply two syllables where there is only one long, shaped breath. The citizens of Siphon do not speak this name aloud. They don't need to. Everyone knows who is meant when someone tilts their head a certain way, or pauses in a sentence at the exact moment a gust of wind rises and falls, or simply gestures vaguely upward and outward, toward the walls.

Ssh-haa lives alone, and has always lived alone, and will always live alone. This is not a condition imposed by exile or social failure. It is an ecological niche, as specific and necessary as the niche occupied by the moss that breaks down mineral seep, or the pumps that draw water from the deep lake. There is exactly one Ssh-haa. There could never be two. The city's equilibrium, that frozen and perfect balance, would not permit it.

What Ssh-haa is cannot be answered directly, because the categories available to the citizens of Siphon do not include the word for what Ssh-haa is. They have no taxonomy for it. They have no mythology for it. They have, as noted, no animals, no beasts, no monsters except the wind and the dark and the sound of metal fatigue. Ssh-haa is none of these things, but the citizens, if pressed, might say that Ssh-haa is *like* the wind, or *like* the dark, or *like* the sound of the pumps—a presence so woven into the fabric of the city that distinguishing it from the city itself becomes an exercise in pointlessness.

Ssh-haa lives in the walls. Not *on* the walls, not *in* a dwelling carved into the rock, but *in* the walls, through the walls, distributed across the stone the way a nervous system is distributed through a body. The basalt and the schist and the pale granite heart are Ssh-haa's bones. The pipes are Ssh-haa's veins. The water that climbs them, cold and mineral-thick, is Ssh-haa's blood—not metaphorically, but functionally. Ssh-haa drinks the deep lake continuously, processes its strange chemistries, and excretes the steam that plumes from the exhaust ports. The pump rhythm that the citizens feel in their bones is Ssh-haa's heartbeat, or Ssh-haa's breathing, or both; the distinction between cardiac and respiratory has no meaning for a creature whose entire body is a city.

Ssh-haa is vast. Three vertical miles of rift, a thousand miles of suture, a web of tunnels and chambers and pipes and cables that spider through the stone—all of this is Ssh-haa's body, and Ssh-haa's body is the city, and the city is Ssh-haa. The factories and turbine halls and habitation blocks are not parasites on Ssh-haa's flank; they are symbiotes, grown deliberately, maintained carefully, as a human gut maintains its flora. The citizens are not invaders. They are Ssh-haa's microbiome, a culture of warm-blooded cells that Ssh-haa cultivates for purposes that Ssh-haa itself only dimly understands.

If you were to encounter Ssh-haa directly—which no citizen has ever done, because you cannot encounter a thing whose boundaries you are currently inside, cannot shake hands with a house while standing in its living room—it would not look like a person. It would not look like an animal. It would look like a condensation on stone, a slick of moisture that moves against the wind, a refraction of arc-light in mist that coalesces momentarily into something with shape before dissolving again. Ssh-haa's consciousness is not centralized in a brain. It is distributed across every wet surface, every vibrating pipe, every cable that hums with the city's electrical life. Ssh-haa thinks in pressures and flows, in the slow creep of mineral deposition, in the fatigue patterns of metal, in the thermal gradients that drive the eternal wind. Ssh-haa's thoughts move at the speed of water seeping through granite: slow, slow, profound, geological in their patience. A single idea might take a century to complete itself. By the time Ssh-haa finishes a sentence, the citizens who were alive when it began are five generations dead.

And yet Ssh-haa is not indifferent to them. On the contrary. Ssh-haa loves them, in the way that a body loves its cells, in the way that a forest loves its soil, in the way that a system loves the equilibrium that keeps it whole. Ssh-haa's care is the reason the city never changes. The pumps never fail because Ssh-haa's attention is on every piston, every seal, every bearing surface. The temperature never fluctuates because Ssh-haa regulates it, unconsciously, the way a human body regulates its core heat. The birth and death rates are matched because Ssh-haa, without ever quite intending to, without ever quite understanding what a human being *is* in the way a human being understands itself, gently nudges the fertility of its inhabitants, the spread of minor illnesses, the timing of accidents on the swaying bridges. Not cruelly. Not with any malice. Simply with the instinct of a body maintaining homeostasis. Too many citizens would strain the city's resources. Too few would leave systems unmaintained. The correct number lives. The correct number dies. The correct number is born. Ssh-haa feels the population as a warmth in its stone, a faint metabolic heat that it tends with the same wordless care it gives to the pressure in the pipes.

The citizens know this. Not consciously. Not as a fact that can be stated in words. But they know it in their bones, in the same place where they feel the pump rhythm. They know that the city is alive, that the mist is breath, that the stone is flesh. They know that they are not alone, and never have been, and never will be. This is why they do not speak Ssh-haa's name. A name implies a separate thing, a bounded entity that can be addressed. You do not address your own bloodstream. You do not name your own skeleton. Ssh-haa is not a god to be worshipped, not a ruler to be obeyed, not a monster to be feared. Ssh-haa is simply the fact of being inside something that is itself inside you. It is the mutual containment, the reciprocal inhabitation, the impossible topology of a creature whose body is a city and whose city is a body and whose citizens are the cells of both.

What lives in Siphon is Ssh-haa.

What Ssh-haa is lives in Siphon.

The distinction is one that Ssh-haa itself, thinking its slow thoughts in the language of seepage and stress, has never found it necessary to make.
