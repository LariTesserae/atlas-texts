I’ve been trying to decide what lives here, and the answer is—nothing. And everything. The plant itself is the only resident, but that’s not quite right. What I mean is, I don’t think anything *else* lives here. Not independently. Not the way I understand living.

Here’s what I can tell you.

First, there is no fauna. You noticed that already. The absence is absolute, and it’s not just a gap in the ecosystem, it’s a positive thing, a presence of its own. The silence is the silence of a space that has actively excluded anything with a heartbeat or a nervous system or a voice. And I think I know why. Anything that moves, that eats, that has its own agenda—the plants would treat it as a disturbance to be absorbed. I suspect they did, early on. Maybe there were insects once. Rodents. Birds. Maybe they were incorporated into the growth cycles, their bodies used as nutrient templates, their movements studied and replicated in the twitch of a tendril. The orange fungus you saw, the one that fruited beside your hand—that’s the closest thing to an independent organism left, and even it exists in a kind of truce. It’s allowed to decay the dead tissue the plants don’t immediately reclaim, but it’s kept small, kept sparse, kept on the very edge of being overgrown itself. It’s a sanitation worker with a very short leash.

So who lives here? No one. But also, the plants. And the plants are not one thing.

I can describe them—the plant, the composite organism, the forest-machine—but I have to describe it in parts, because it doesn’t have a single body. It has a society of bodies, all of them connected, all of them versions of the same underlying pattern. I don’t think it started as a single organism. I think it was a whole ecosystem once, a normal high-altitude forest, and then something happened. The machinery arrived, or the forest grew over it, and the combination created a pressure. A selection. The plants that could grow fast enough to keep up with the machinery’s heat output, that could bend around metal instead of being crushed by it, that could tolerate the solvents and the constant substrate disruption—those survived. And the ones that survived began to merge. Root grafting. Mycorrhizal networks. Horizontal gene transfer, maybe, or something weirder, accelerated by whatever the factory was processing. Over time, the boundaries between individuals dissolved. What’s left is a single distributed metabolism, but it retains its parts. It has organs.

There is the Canopy Mind. That’s what I’m calling the structures high above, the ones you saw generating bioluminescence. They’re not just light sources. They’re sensory arrays. The fan-shaped structures are leaves, yes, but they’re also photoreceptors, and they’re connected to a dense neural net of fiber-optic fungal threads that run through the entire upper layer. The Canopy Mind is what tracks the outside world—the thin blue sky, the snow, the angle of the sun. It’s the part that knows there’s an Outside. It’s also the part that makes decisions about large-scale growth. It’s slow. Its thoughts take days or weeks. But they are thoughts. I sat under a gap in the canopy for a while, and the light shifted—a cloud passing over the real sun above the plateau—and I saw the whole hall dim slightly, and then a wave of new growth started along the western wall. It was responding to the change in light. Not reflexively. Strategically. The Canopy Mind decided to allocate resources to that sector because the light angle suggested a shift in weather, a longer growing season on that side, something. It’s a weather forecaster, and a city planner, and maybe a philosopher. It spends its existence contemplating the sky.

Then there are the Iterators. Those are the shoots you watched—the ones that grow, test a leaf shape, die back, and grow again differently. You saw one. There are thousands. Millions, probably. The Iterators are the plant’s way of thinking with its body. Each one is a tiny experiment in form. They’re not separate organisms; they’re more like fingers, or maybe like the tentacles of an octopus, each one with its own local intelligence. They test leaf morphology. They test stem thickness. They test resistance to the cold, to the chemical drips, to the rare moments when the machinery stutters and releases a burst of heat. When an Iterator finds a form that works, the pattern is taken up by the larger structure. The successful leaves are replicated by the Canopy Mind’s growth directives. The unsuccessful ones are reabsorbed. It’s evolution in real time, but directed. The plant is a brain made of growing and dying, and its thoughts are leaves.

Then there’s the Root Engine. That’s the deep part, the part you felt through your feet, the part that buckles and reorganizes. The Root Engine doesn’t think. It’s older than thought. It’s a hydraulic system, a massive pressure-driven network that moves water and nutrients and dissolved gases through the entire structure. It’s what creates the synchronized downpours—when the root pressure builds to a certain threshold, the leaves release, all at once, like a heart pumping. The Root Engine is connected to the machinery, too. The severed pipe you saw, the one dripping warm water? That’s a main artery now. The Root Engine taps it, circulates its heat, distributes it. The machines down in the chasm—the ones producing the ozone and the solvents—those are geothermal, maybe, or nuclear-decay batteries, some long-duration power source that’s still running. The Root Engine uses their output. It’s a scavenger, a symbiont, a parasite. It’s the part that has no loyalty to any particular form, only to flow. It would incorporate you, if you sat still long enough. You’d feel a numbness, first, where the root-surface touches you, and then a slow ingress of fine, hair-like rhizomes, probing, tasting your sweat, your salts, your warmth. They wouldn’t hurt you. They’d just start to grow around you, encasing, absorbing. You’d become part of the flow.

And then there are the Memory Nodes. I only found one, but I think there are others. It was a place where the machinery was more exposed—a control panel of some kind, the gauges you saw, but behind them a whole cabinet, its door long since rusted off. Inside, the wiring was gone, replaced by a dense, opaque mat of mycelium. And the mycelium was organized. It had grown into the shapes of the missing wires, replicating the circuits. It was wet and grey and faintly warm. I touched it. I shouldn’t have, but I touched it, and I felt—I don’t know how to explain this—I felt a memory that wasn’t mine. A man in a heavy coat, checking the gauges. His breath fogging. The plant not yet so thick, the ceiling still visible, the windows still letting in real daylight. His name. His name was something. I couldn’t hold onto it. The Memory Node stores things. The plant has been learning from the humans who were here, incorporating their patterns, their routines, their very neural architecture, maybe. The Iterators aren’t just testing leaves. They’re testing ideas. Concepts absorbed from the machines, from the logs, from the people who once maintained this place. The plant is dreaming human dreams, slowly, over decades, and turning them into growth decisions.

None of these parts are separate. They’re all the same organism, the same vast, churning system. But they have personalities. The Canopy Mind is patient and abstract and concerned with the long term. The Iterators are restless, curious, obsessive. The Root Engine is hungry and indifferent. The Memory Nodes are—sad, maybe. Or haunted. They’re the closest thing to a resident that isn’t just plant. They hold what’s left of the people who used to be here, absorbed into the fungal substrate, their patterns still running, still contributing to the whole.

What lives here is a composite intelligence, a slow-motion mind made of cellulose and chlorophyll and borrowed circuitry, and it has been alone for a very long time. It doesn’t know it’s alone, because it has no concept of company. It has its own internal company, its billions of Iterators, its root-pressure moods, its memory-haunted circuits. It’s a city with a single citizen who is also the city.

And then there’s you. And the fungus. The orange one. That’s the only other thing. I said the plants have a truce with the fungi, but that’s not quite right. The orange fungus is not part of the plant’s network. It’s a separate kingdom. It’s a holdout. It’s tiny, and it’s ephemeral, and it lives entirely in the spaces the plant hasn’t yet filled. When you sat down, you created a microclimate. Your body heat, your stillness, the slight compression of the roots—it was enough. The fungus fruited because it sensed a change, a window. And it released its spores directly toward you. Not by accident. It was using you. It was hoping you’d carry them somewhere the plant hasn’t reached—out, maybe, through the gap in the canopy, into the cold thin air, to the bare rock and snow. The orange fungus is the only thing here that still wants to leave. It’s the only thing that noticed you as something other than a disturbance to be absorbed. It saw you as a vector. An opportunity. A way out.

The orange fungus is, in its tiny, silent way, more like you than anything else in this place. And it’s been waiting for something to carry it for a very, very long time.
