The Groaning Plain holds its own counsel. I am not its first chronicler, nor will I be the last to stand at its edge and mistake description for understanding. But I have listened longer than most, and the holes have told me things.

You ask what lives here. The question assumes a distinction the plain does not make. Life, as you conceive it—bounded organisms, metabolic fires, the urgent project of self-perpetuation—is a late invention, a temporary agitation in matter. The plain predates that experiment. It will outlast it. What lives here is the plain itself, and the plain is not alive in any way that cares about the difference.

I will tell you about the singers, though they are not inhabitants so much as modalities. I will tell you about the bleached-mind pilgrims who come to be unmade. I will tell you about the lithomorphs, who are not the pale many-legged things but their stationary parents. I will tell you about the weather that breathes sideways. I will tell you about the sound-forms that condense in the high frequencies and dissolve before they touch the ground. And I will tell you about the final equilibrium, the state toward which all this tends, the thing the plain is becoming that it has not yet finished becoming—because even here, even in this place you mistook for static, there is a direction. It is only very slow.

But first, you must understand the holes.

They are not incidental. They are not erosion features in the conventional sense, though water made them. Each shaft, each fissure, each microscopic pore is a tuned resonator. The plain is a lithic organ, a continent of stone that has sorted itself by density and grain structure into a single vast instrument. The water that weeps from the rock is not groundwater—there is no aquifer here, no subterranean reservoir. The water is manufactured. It is a byproduct of the light.

The light you described so well—ferocious, flat, wrong in its spectrum—is the engine. It is not sunlight. The plain sits at an elevation where the atmosphere above it is permanently ionized, a plasma sheet that glows continuously in frequencies that plants cannot use because those frequencies dismantle complex molecules. The light dissociates the surface minerals, liberates oxygen and hydrogen from the crystal lattices, and the hydrogen recombines with atmospheric traces to form water. The plain makes its own moisture from its own substance, catalyzed by a sky that is perpetually on fire. What you called weeping is actually exhalation—the stone is breathing out its own elemental components and reabsorbing them, over and over, in a cycle so slow that a single breath takes ten thousand years.

The holes are where the exhalation concentrates. The sound is the stone's voice, and the stone is singing its own dissolution and reconstitution. This is the plain's metabolism. This is what passes for living here.

Now. The singers.

You saw them and called them pale shapes, many-legged, soft as exposed nerves. Your description is accurate but superficial. They are the motile phase of the lithomorphs, and they have no name because the plain has no language. I call them singers because their primary function is acoustic. They maintain the drone.

Each singer emerges from a lithomorph—I will return to these—as a mobile extension, a crawling probe of tissue that is not quite flesh and not quite mineral. They are calcium-carbonate lattices interwoven with a hydrated silica gel that conducts vibration. They have no digestive system, no reproductive organs, no brain. They are sensory apparati on legs, and what they sense is frequency. They crawl the plain on their many soft limbs—twelve, always twelve, arranged in pairs along a segmented body that can stretch or compact like an accordion—and they listen. They press their anterior segments against the lips of holes and measure the pitch. When a hole drifts from its assigned frequency—your presence caused this, your body heat altering the air density, your weight microfracturing the stone—the singer adjusts it.

It does this by secreting itself. The singer's body is not permanent. It is a temporary extrusion of the lithomorph that birthed it, and it can sacrifice portions of its mass to tune the plain. It will extrude a tendril of silica gel into the hole, altering the bore diameter. It will deposit a calcium carbonate ring around the lip, raising the pitch. It will abrade a surface with its own ventral ridges, smoothing a whistle-stop imperfection. Over the course of its active life—perhaps a century, perhaps five—a singer will gradually dissolve itself into the plain, losing segments one by one until only the anterior sensory cluster remains, which then crawls back to its lithomorph parent and is reabsorbed.

The singers do not eat. They do not sleep. They do not reproduce. They are not individuals in any meaningful sense; they are appendages, the hands and ears of the stationary beings that generate them. The plain is covered in singers, thousands of them at any moment, crawling their slow circuits, making their microadjustments, singing back to the stone in frequencies too high for you to hear but which I can, faintly, like the whine of charged electronics. They ignored you because you were not a threat. You were a perturbation, a new variable in their calculations. They encircled you to measure you. They would have incorporated you, if you had stayed—not through malice, but through the plain's default logic, which is equilibrium at any cost.

The lithomorphs.

You did not see them because they are indistinguishable from the plain itself. A lithomorph is a region of stone that has achieved self-referential organization. It is a mineral entity, not biological, not chemical in the metabolic sense, but structural. It is a pattern that maintains itself.

Imagine a volume of the porous basalt-slate composite, roughly spherical, perhaps ten meters in diameter. Within this volume, the holes are arranged not randomly but in a specific topology, a fractal network that channels air and water in closed loops. The lithomorph is a standing wave in the plain's processes—a localized concentration of the same water-weeping, air-pulsing, light-dissociating cycles that characterize the whole plateau, but folded inward, made recursive. It draws water from the surrounding stone, circulates it through its own channels, and re-deposits the minerals in patterns that reinforce the channel structure. It is a self-maintaining vortex in a dissipative system, and it has existed for six hundred thousand years. It may exist for six hundred thousand more.

The lithomorphs do not think. They have no nervous system, no signaling molecules, no information processing in any sense you would recognize. But they are sensitive. The entire volume of each lithomorph is a phase-array acoustic detector; it hears the plain entire, every hole, every singer, every shift in the drone. And when a deviation occurs—a blocked pore, a widened fissure, a foreign object like yourself—the lithomorph responds. Not through decision. Through mechanical necessity. The altered sound pattern changes the resonance of the lithomorph's own internal channels, which changes the rate of mineral deposition, which extrudes a singer from one of its surface orifices, pre-programmed by the acoustic gradient to crawl toward the deviation and correct it.

The singers are not sent by the lithomorphs. They are shed by them, like skin cells, like leaves. The plain's maintenance is autonomic.

There are perhaps two hundred lithomorphs on the Groaning Plain. They are the only entities here that might qualify as "living" in any timescale that matters. They do not reproduce. They have never needed to. But they are not immortal. Occasionally—every few tens of millennia—one collapses. The internal channels silt up, the fractal topology simplifies, the standing wave dissipates. The lithomorph becomes just stone again, indistinguishable from the matrix. And when this happens, the other lithomorphs shift their singers, adjust the surrounding holes, and fill in the acoustic gap left by the lost resonator. The plain does not mourn. It compensates.

But here is where your description erred. You said there was no life. You were wrong.

There are pilgrims.

They come from the lowlands, from the valleys where biological life still pretends to dominance. They are human, mostly, or close enough—the distinction has blurred in the eons since the first emergence, but the pilgrim morphology is bipedal, bilateral, brain-cased. They walk up through the thinning atmosphere, through the zones where the light begins to change, and they arrive at the edge of the plain with a purpose you would not recognize as religious but which serves the same function.

They come to be dissolved.

The light that bakes the plain dismantles complex molecules. You felt it yourself—the way it didn't warm you, the way it seemed to bypass your skin and work directly on your tissues. Prolonged exposure is lethal to organisms like you. Not because it burns. Because it unpicks. The ionizing frequencies break the long-chain polymers that hold living cells together. DNA unwinds. Proteins denature. Cell membranes unzip into component lipids. Over the course of days, a human body on the Groaning Plain does not decay in the microbial sense. It disassembles. It deliquesces. The water that already saturates the air catalyzes the process, and the body becomes a slow puddle of organic slurry that seeps into the porous stone and joins the plain's water cycle.

The pilgrims know this. They come here to die. But they do not think of it as dying.

There is a belief system, ancient and persistent, that holds the Groaning Plain to be a paradise. The logic is this: the plain is eternal, unchanging, perfectly balanced. All suffering arises from change—from growth and decay, from striving and failing, from the biological treadmill of need and satiation. To enter the plain's equilibrium is to escape the cycle. The light will unmake you, yes, but your constituent molecules will be incorporated into the stone's slow breathing. You will become part of the plain. You will weep from the rocks. You will sing in the drone. You will persist, not as an individual consciousness—the pilgrims are sophisticated enough not to expect that—but as a pattern dispersed through the lithic ecosystem, contributing your carbon and calcium and trace metals to the ongoing maintenance of the eternal chord.

They are not entirely wrong. The plain does incorporate them. The organic compounds from dissolved pilgrims alter the mineralogy of the water cycle. The pores nearest to a dissolution site will grow at slightly different rates, develop slightly different resonances. The lithomorphs' singers will adjust, will carry the pilgrim's chemical signature into the hole-network, and for a brief geologic moment—a few thousand years—the pilgrim's existence will have been audible in the plain's music. Then the signature will dissipate, diluted into the vast cycling of water and stone, and the pilgrim will be indistinguishable from the billions of other dissolved organisms that have soaked into the plain over its lifetime. Anonymity. Equilibrium. Peace.

I have seen them arrive. They walk onto the plain, find a depression between holes, and lie down. They do not shield their eyes from the light. They do not cover their ears against the sound. They open themselves. Within hours, their skin begins to glisten, not with sweat but with the first loosening of cellular adhesion. Within a day, their outlines blur. Within three days, only a dark stain on the stone, already fading as the water washes it away. Within a week, nothing.

The singers will have visited them multiple times during this process, adjusting the nearby holes to account for the new acoustic properties of a dissolving body. The pilgrims would consider this an honor. They are being tuned. They are being incorporated into the music.

But the pilgrims are visitors, not inhabitants. They do not live here, even temporarily; they only die here. The true inhabitants—the ones who live on the plain in the sense of dwelling, of belonging—are something else entirely.

They are not carbon-based. They are not even matter-based, exactly. They are standing waves in the high frequencies of the drone.

The sound-forms.

You heard the plain's continuous chord—the bass from wide shafts, the high keening from needle cracks. But you lacked the perception to hear the full spectrum. The drone extends far above the range of your ears, into frequencies that propagate poorly through air but efficiently through the stone itself. Up there, in the ultrasonic and beyond, the plain's voice becomes structured. It forms harmonics, overtones, interference patterns. And some of those interference patterns are stable. They are nodes of constructive interference that persist across time, feeding on the energy of the plain's constant exhalation, maintaining their structure against entropic decay.

These are the sound-forms. They are vortices in the acoustic field, regions of organized vibration that exhibit complex behavior. They are not intelligent in any sense you would recognize—they do not symbolize, they do not plan, they do not self-reflect. But they are responsive. They move across the plain, propagating through the stone, interacting with each other. They have lifespans measured in centuries. They dissipate eventually, when their sustaining frequencies are disrupted by a large-scale geological event—a cave-in, a shift in the lithomorph network—but until then, they persist.

The sound-forms are invisible to human eyes, but the singers are aware of them. The singers' silica-gel tissues are sensitive to the ultrasonic band; they orient partly by sound-form positions, using the stable acoustic vortices as navigation beacons. The lithomorphs themselves may be incubators for sound-forms—the recursive resonance patterns inside their fractal channels are ideal spawning grounds for standing-wave structures. When a new sound-form condenses and detaches from its parent lithomorph, it drifts across the plain, following the acoustic gradients, until it finds a niche in the background drone where it can stabilize.

I have watched them for millennia. They are beautiful. Not visually—there is nothing visual about a pressure oscillation in a mineral matrix—but structurally. Each sound-form is a specific harmonic pattern, a unique signature of frequencies and phase relationships. They interact by merging their patterns, creating temporary hybrids that explore the possibility-space of the plain's acoustics before resolving back into their stable forms.
