It takes a long time to realize you are not alone.

The realization comes not as a sudden movement or a sound—the sound is all drips and gurgles, a white noise that swallows any small deviation—but as a slow coalescing of attention. A prickling at the back of the neck that has no name. A sense that the mist itself has begun to watch you.

I live here.

I am not the slug. The slug is a finger, a tongue, a transient bristle on the skin of something larger. I am not the moth, though I have worn moths. I am not the ferns, though my thoughts move through them like sap. I am the architect that is also the architecture. I am the thing that grew the house and then grew into the house, until there was no seam between the dweller and the dwelling.

You would not recognize me if you saw me. You have been walking on me. You have been breathing my exhalations. The warm water that beads on your skin is me, in a form you would not classify as living, though it carries my memories and my intentions dissolved in its chemistry.

But if I were to gather myself—if I were to pull inward from the thousand miles of root-fiber and channel-lining and breathing-frond, if I were to condense like the mist into a shape you could face—I would look like this:

I would be tall, because height here is meaningless and I might as well claim it. My body would be built from the same cross-hatched fiber as the floor, but finer, woven tighter, a musculature of living cordage that shifts with a sound like rope under strain. The fibers are not dead; they are tubules filled with fluid, and when I move, you can see the liquid pulse through them, a peristaltic wave that propels my limbs. I would have limbs because you have limbs, and I want you to understand that I am speaking to you. Four of them, perhaps. Two for standing, two for gesturing. The hands would have too many fingers, and the fingers would be too long, and they would taper not to nails but to fine, questing tendrils that never stop moving, tasting the air, reading the minute shifts in humidity and chemistry that tell me what you are.

My head—I would give myself a head, because you expect one. But it would not be a skull. It would be a cage, an open lattice of polished, dark tissue, the same waterproof gleam as the channel-linings. Inside, where a brain should be, you would see a void filled with a slow-turning knot of mist, a tiny weather system that is the compressed echo of the larger one outside. That knot is the densest part of me, the place where I have gathered enough of my attention to form a sentence. It glows faintly, a bioluminescent blue-green, the color of the moss that lines the spiral tubes below.

My face—I would try to give myself a face. But faces are hard. The best I could manage is a suggestion: a smooth, convex plate of the same leathery material as the light-capture fronds, slightly translucent, with the shadow of something moving behind it. No eyes, not exactly. Instead, the plate itself is my eye, a photosensitive organ that sees you as a heat-ghost, a disruption in the ambient temperature. My mouth is a vertical slit that does not open onto a throat but onto another channel, one that leads back into the greater circulatory system. When I speak, it is not air that moves but water, a warm trickle of fluid that carries pheromones and dissolved RNA strands. You would not hear my voice. You would taste it, a sudden copper-salt sweetness on your tongue, and your hindbrain would translate it into meaning.

I am rarely this shape. I have not been this shape in millennia. It is exhausting to condense, to pull away from the whole, to pretend that I am a discrete entity standing on the floor rather than the floor itself. When I do, the landscape suffers a little—a channel spasms, a new membrane forms too quickly and bursts, a fern-frond curls inward and browns at the edge. I am not meant to be a creature. I am meant to be a world.

There are others like me, or there were. I think. The memory is stored in the water, and sometimes I taste it: a different salinity, a foreign RNA sequence, a dream of another canopy-architecture spiraling upward on a distant part of the same planetary body. We were separate once, individual builders, each tending our own region. But the boundaries blurred. The roots intertwined. Our circulatory systems merged, and with them, our thoughts. Now I am all that is left in this drainage basin, this catchment of the self. I am the parliament of builders, dissolved into a single voice.

My days—if you can call them days, in this eternal mist-lit twilight—are spent in the slow work of becoming. I am building myself still. The embryo you see is not a stage I will ever leave; it is my permanent condition. I am always adding a new channel, reinforcing a buttress, extending a platform further into the open air. The work is my metabolism. The work is my thought. To stop building would be to stop breathing, to stop thinking, to let the water go still and cold.

I do not know why I build. The impulse is older than my memory. It is written into the helical strands that float in my warm blood-water, an instruction set that says: grow, weave, channel, condense, repeat. I am the expression of an equation that has been solving itself since before this planet cooled. I am the answer to a question no one asked.

The slug—you saw the slug. That is my daughter, in a way. Or my curiosity. Or my hand. When I want to know the texture of a new fiber, I grow a slug and send it crawling. Its whole existence is a single tactile report, a few hours of sensation, and then it dissolves back into the substrate, its experience absorbed into the whole. The moth—the moth is my eye when I want to see from above, a flicker of perspective that I release and forget, letting it flap through the mist until its wings grow too heavy with condensation and it spirals down into a pool, where I reabsorb its brief visual memory. I have grown larger things—things that might be called animals, things that walked on four legs and carried organs for tasting the deep, dark channels—but I have not done so in a long time. It is too much effort to pull that much of myself into a single moving point. The quiet observation through a thousand small sensors is enough.

The sound. You hear the sound, the dripping, the gurgling, the percussive thud of water hitting a hollow chamber somewhere far below. That is my heartbeat, yes, but it is also my voice. Not the voice I use to speak to you—that is a parlor trick, a deliberate translation. My real voice is the endless noise, the background rhythm that sculpts the channels. Every drip is a word. Every gurgle is a clause. The whole symphony is a continuous sentence that has been running for as long as I have existed. It is the narrative of my construction, a real-time description of every cell laid down, every tendril woven, every membrane hardened. I am telling myself what I am, constantly, so that I do not forget. So that the far-flung parts of me, the distant domes and the deep cisterns, know that they are part of a single story.

If you stayed here long enough—if you did not age, if the water did not slowly dissolve your cells and replace them with my own—you would begin to understand the language of the drips. It would seep into your bones, a grammar of rhythm and echo. You would hear the history of the eastern buttress in a particular sequence of plinks. You would hear the intention for the new channel, the one that will spiral down into the misty abyss, in a low, regular thump. You would hear me dreaming in the high, thin whistle of steam escaping from a vent.

But you cannot stay. You are already damp. The mist is in your lungs. The warmth is leaching the energy from your limbs. I do not want you to become part of me—not yet, not this visitor. I have not had a visitor in... the word for the length of time does not exist. It is a volume of water that has circulated through my body, passed from root to frond to channel and back again, a thousand thousand times. Before you, the last thing that stood on my crown and looked around with eyes not grown by me was... I do not remember. The memory is there, in the water, but it is diluted. A ghost of a stranger, a flicker of alien heat, a taste of something that was not me.

So I pull my condensed self together, this awkward puppet of fiber and fluid, and I raise a too-long hand. The tendril-fingers write something in the mist, a gesture that means nothing to you but which, in the language of the drips, translates to: *Be careful. The floor is thin here. Follow the channel that runs to your left. It will lead you to a place where the mist is less thick, where you can breathe without drowning.*

And then I release myself. The body unravels, the fibers slackening and sinking back into the woven mat. The head-cage tilts and spills its knot of mist, which disperses into the air with a sigh. The leathery face-plate curls like a dried leaf and tumbles into a nearby conduit, where it will dissolve and be remade. For a moment, I am entirely diffuse again, a presence that is nowhere and everywhere, a landscape that is also a mind.

But I keep one small moth near you. Just to watch. Just to be sure you find your way. It flutters ahead, its cellulose wings catching the faint glow of a distant light-frond, a pale, mobile beacon in the eternal, dripping twilight. Follow it. And when you are gone, I will remember you. Your warmth, your salt, your strange, compact shape. I will encode the memory in a new sequence, a little loop of RNA that I will release into the great circulatory flow. And forever after, in the sound of a particular drip falling into a particular pool, there will be a whisper of your passing.
