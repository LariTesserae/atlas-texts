The people of the column city call themselves the Hands, and they name their home the Keening, after the sound the wind makes in winter.

They are a small people—not dwarfed, not stunted, but proportioned for their vertical world. Their bodies have answered the demands of the stone over generations. Their hands are long-fingered and astonishingly strong, with thumbs set slightly lower than yours, and the tendons on the backs of their palms stand out like bundled twine even at rest. A Hand child can dead-hang from a two-finger ledge by the age of four. Their feet have adapted in parallel: toes lengthened and grown prehensile, the arch become a second grasping surface. They wear no shoes. The soles of their feet are a dark, thick leather of callus, ridged like the pads of a gecko, and when they walk on wet stone they do so with absolute, unthinking certainty.

Their skin is a muted palette of the city itself—charcoal grey and deep brown, with occasional undertones of russet or umber that emerge in certain light. Dappled patterns of darker pigment freckle their shoulders and backs, resembling nothing so much as the lichen colonies they share their walls with, and this is no accident. The Hands are slowly, generation by generation, being written upon by their world. The patterns are unique to each lineage, a heredity of camouflage that elders can read like a genealogy. “She has her grandmother’s colonies,” they say, meaning the constellation of spots across a girl’s shoulder blades is the same.

They wear little clothing in the traditional sense, for loose fabric is a hazard on the climbs. Instead, they drape themselves in belts and harnesses of braided sinew and soft, supple hide, worked until it feels like oiled silk against the skin. These are more toolbelt than garment, studded with loops for tools, pouches for small treasures, attachment points for safety lines. The older a Hand becomes, the more complex their harness grows, until an elder is wrapped in a dense webwork of knots and cords, a living archive of every climb they’ve ever made, every life they’ve ever tethered themselves to. Children begin with a single waist cord, often dyed with a pigment made from chewed river clay—a rusty red that stands out against the grey stone so parents can spot them across a chasm.

Their faces are, by your standards, difficult to read at first. The expressions are there, but they play out across features adapted to the dimness of the fissures. Their eyes are large and dark, pupils wide and slow to constrict, and the sclera has a faint bluish tint that helps them gather what little light falls into the deep streets. They blink rarely. Their ears are shell-like and slightly mobile, capable of swiveling toward a sound with an almost animal precision; they can hear the click of a four-wing’s sonar from a hundred feet away and know whether it’s hunting or courting. Their noses are narrow and long-bridged, and their sense of smell is their true language of place. Every level of the Keening has a distinct scent signature—the wet-iron tang of a fissure near the river, the dry-dust smell of the upper columns, the mushroomy richness of a midden chamber. A Hand can tell you where they are with their eyes closed, just by breathing.

One Hand, or many: the language prefers the singular to represent the whole. So let us say, here is a woman. Her name is Mist-Caller, a name she earned, not given. She is perhaps forty seasons old, her hair a thick, wiry black streaked with silver at the temples, pulled back and bound with a cord of braided fish-leather into a tight tail that hangs between her shoulder blades, out of the way. The colonies on her back form a pattern the elders call Two Rivers Converging, a double line of dark spots flowing down her spine and branching over her ribs. She is a knapper, one of the best, and her forearms are cross-hatched with fine white scars from a thousand flying shards of volcanic glass. She wears a harness of thirty-seven cords, each one representing a significant climb—her first solo descent to the river, the rescue of a fallen cousin, the ascent of the Needle, a column so thin and tall it hums audibly in a strong wind.

She is mother to a boy named Keeps-His-Grip, who is eight and already has his own waist cord, which he earned by climbing the eastern face of their home column without a safety line, a feat that earned him a scolding as much as a celebration. He is small and quick and perpetually damp, as all children are, and he is currently crouched on a hide bridge three levels above his mother, dangling a line baited with a bit of fish gut into a fissure, hoping to catch a mist moth for its glowing abdomen, which the children collect in little mesh cages and use as living lanterns.

The Hands do not live alone in their bodies. They live also in their dead.

When a Hand dies, the body is not buried; there is no soil to dig. It is tenderly wrapped in a shroud of woven lichen-fiber and carried, by the family, on a long and silent procession down into the deepest fissure their lineage has access to—the family’s own particular rent in the stone. The procession is vertical, the body passed from hand to hand, belayed with infinite care, lowered into the permanent mist. The destination is a chamber carved into the wall just above the river, a place the Hands call the Still House. Here, the dead are laid in rows on carved shelves, and the constant cold mist and mineral-rich air slowly, over years, petrifies them. Their flesh is replaced by a fine white stone, their features preserved in exquisite, eerie detail. They become part of the geology. The oldest Hands in the Still House are so fully mineralized that they ring faintly if tapped, like a ceramic bowl.

Families visit their Still House on significant days—births, namings, the first solo climb of a child. They speak to the petrified ancestors, not in prayer exactly, but in conversation. They tell them the news of the Keening. The Hands do not believe the dead can hear them. They do it because, as they say, “the stone remembers being spoken to.” The dead are becoming the mountain, and the mountain, in its slow, patient way, is becoming a witness.

Their social structure is not a hierarchy but a network, mirroring the city itself. There are no chiefs, no kings. Decisions are made by resonance—a proposal is spoken in the common chambers, and from there it moves through the vertical streets, gathering voices or fading into silence. A thing becomes true when enough Hands have repeated it. This requires a culture of extraordinary listening. Disputes are rare and, when they arise, are settled by a ritual called the Tying. The two parties are physically tethered together by a single cord of rawhide and must spend a full cycle of the fog—sometimes a day, sometimes a week—bound to one another in a space too narrow to sit down in. They must coordinate every movement, every shift of weight, or both will fall. By the time the cord is cut, the dispute has usually dissolved into the simple, shared fact of survival.

Their art is in what they make and what they name. The city is full of names: Every column of significance has one—Wind-Whistler, Grandfather’s Tooth, The Leaning Sister, She-Who-Hums. Every bridge is named for how it was first crossed or who fell from it. Every chamber holds a story, pecked into the wall in a dense, spiraling script of glyphs—not writing as you know it, but a kind of mnemonic topography, lines that map the sound of the story onto the surface of the stone. A Hand can run their fingers over the incised curves and feel the voice of the teller, the cadence and the pitch, encoded in the depth and angle of the cuts. A story is not a text. It is a shape.

Mist-Caller, the knapper, is also a reader of these glyphs. She is working now on a new blade, a long, leaf-shaped knife of obsidian so black it seems to drink the dim light. She is knapping it for her son, who will soon make his first descent to the Still House, the family pilgrimage. She works on the ledge outside her door, thirty feet above the unseen river, her legs swinging in the void, and with each tick of the antler flaker she is not just shaping a tool. She is leaving a story in the stone. The ripples of the pressure flakes will be readable by her son, by his children, by the petrified ancestors. The tool will, in time, be a text, and a text that can also cut.

The Keening is a city. It is also a conversation between the living and the stone that holds them, a conversation spoken in the tick of flint, the hum of the river, the click of wings, and the long, slow breathing of a world that is still becoming itself. And the Hands, the small people with their large eyes and prehensile toes, are simply the most recent syllables of that conversation, carved into the columns for now, patient in the damp air, waiting to see what the next word will be.
