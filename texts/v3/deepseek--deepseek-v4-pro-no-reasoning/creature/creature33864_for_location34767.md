I notice them first by their wake—a faint disruption in the phosphorescent mats, a corridor of dimmer water where something has passed recently and often. The guide-ropes near the eastern wall are worn smoother than elsewhere, the braided gut polished to a shine that catches the bacterial glow and throws it back in attenuated ribbons. Whatever uses these ropes does so regularly, and with a grip that leaves no slime, no residue. Clean. Deliberate.

They live in a pod that isn't a pod. It's a whalefall tree bole that has been hollowed rather than hung from—the tree itself is the dwelling, a swelling in one of the oldest trunks about forty feet below the ceiling, where the bark has grown around and through the remnants of an earlier structure, a copper armature now embedded in living tissue like a suture left too long. The entrance is not a hatch but a slit in the bark that pulses faintly, a slow peristaltic rhythm, one contraction every three minutes. The tree has been trained to this. Or it has been persuaded. The distinction is academic after a century or two.

The dweller has no name, or rather has a name that is not a sound. It's a chemical signature, a specific ratio of amino acids that the finger-fish recognize and that the bacterial mats have learned not to colonize. The market's human inhabitants—there are perhaps forty of them, spread across the generations and the hanging pods—call them the Keeper, though the word is only approximate. What they keep is the tree. Specifically, the tree's memory.

The whalefall trees have a form of distributed cognition. Not intelligence in any vertebrate sense, but a chemical record-keeping, a slow inscription of environmental conditions into the layers of their heartwood. Each season of mineral upwelling, each shift in the geothermal vent's cycle, each new substance introduced into the root-clefts—the tree records these as variations in lignin density, in the isotopic ratios of the metals it sequesters, in the branching patterns it chooses over decades. The record is legible, if you know how to read it, and if you have been reading it continuously for long enough to understand what a change signifies.

The Keeper has been reading this particular tree for a very long time. Longer than the turbine has been turning. Longer, perhaps, than the copper sphere was hung, though that is difficult to verify because the Keeper does not keep records in any medium the other inhabitants can access. They simply know. They know that a particular micronutrient ratio in the new branch-tips means the deep vents are shifting toward sulfur, which will require adjusting the electrolysis membranes within the next decade. They know that a certain stress-pattern in the trunk's girth-growth corresponds to a pressure-anomaly that preceded the last turbine bearing failure, two bearing-failures ago, in a generation no one living remembers. They are, in effect, the market's institutional memory, its archive, its slow-motion disaster prediction system.

The Keeper's body is adapted to the tree in ways that blur the boundary between symbiont and parasite. They are human—or were human, or are descended from humans who were—but the long residence inside the trunk has changed them. Their skin is pale as the tree's blind branches, and it carries the same faint phosphorescence, a blue-green that pulses in time with the entrance-slit's contractions. Their limbs are elongated, fingers and toes lengthened to grip the interior bark, and their joints have developed a secondary articulation, a kind of partial dislocation that lets them move through the bole's cramped interior spaces without ever losing contact with the living wood. They wear no suit. They breathe water directly, through a dense net of capillaries that have opened along their ribcage, a self-modified gill system that the tree's own nutrient-exchange vessels have grown into and around. The oxygen comes from the tree, or the tree mediates it from the water, or the distinction has ceased to be meaningful. The Keeper is vascularly connected to something that is itself connected to the entire cavern's thermal and chemical cycles. They are an endosymbiont of the infrastructure.

Their diet is the tree's exudates and, occasionally, the finger-fish that venture too close to the entrance slit. They eat them raw, whole, swallowing them head-first in a single slow motion, and the finger-fish do not resist. They may not be capable of resistance. The chemical signature that serves as the Keeper's name may also serve as a pacifying agent, a pheromonal override that the tree produces and the Keeper exudes. The relationship between the Keeper and the tree's other symbiotes is not fully understood by the market's human inhabitants, who have learned not to ask. The Keeper is necessary. The Keeper is, in some sense, sacred. The Keeper is also disturbing in a way that the inhabitants have learned to live with, the way they have learned to live with the silence and the dimness and the slow, patient knowledge that the entire cavern is one bearing-failure away from a crisis none of them are young enough to survive.

What the Keeper knows that no one else knows: the tree is dying. Not quickly. The rate of heartwood deposition has been declining for eleven decades. The root-tips in the limestone cracks are reaching into chemistries that are increasingly barren; whatever the surface once provided has been tapering off, either because the surface itself has changed or because the cracks have been occluded by slow mineral precipitation. The tree is not starving. It has reserves stored in the bole, in the thickened bark, in the symbiotic bacterial mats that have evolved to feed it waste metabolites. But reserves are finite. The Keeper has calculated, in a slow, recursive way that is more like meditation than mathematics, that the tree will enter a terminal decline within another two centuries. Less, if the vent cycle shifts faster than projected.

The Keeper has not told anyone this. It is not secrecy, exactly. It is that no one has asked, and the Keeper's mode of communication is not suited to volunteering information. They speak through the tree, in a sense—their presence in the bole triggers chemical gradients that propagate through the root network and emerge in the other trees as subtle shifts in growth patterns, which the market's chemists can read if they know where to look. The market's chemists have noticed that the eastern tree's branches have been growing more slowly. They have noted it in their own records. They have not yet connected it to the Keeper. They will, eventually, perhaps in another generation, and the connection will come as a revelation and a terror, and a new accommodation will be made, a new equilibrium found. That is how the Hollowmarket works. No crisis is sudden enough to be a catastrophe. Everything decays slowly enough to be adapted to.

The Keeper is not lonely. Loneliness requires a self that is separate from its environment, and the Keeper's self diffuses along the tree's vascular channels, into the bacterial mats, into the finger-fish, into the water itself. They experience the market's density not as crowding but as a continuous texture of presence. The guide-rope traffic. The distant chime of the workshops. The subsonic hum of the turbine, which they feel as a vibration in their own bones, which are also the tree's bones, the copper armature's bones, the bones of the cavern. They are old enough that memory has become indistinguishable from physiology. They do not recall the bearing failure of two centuries ago; they remember it as a twinge in a branch that has since been pruned, a faint scar in the bark that still weeps a little more sap in the warm season, which is all seasons.

When the surface divers come—those rare 1.3 visitations—the Keeper notices them as a pressure-anomaly, a taste of foreign metals, a disturbance in the finger-fish schools. They do not emerge from the bole. They have not emerged in what the human inhabitants would call living memory. But they extend a chemical tendril through the root network, a query without words, a kind of long, slow sniff of the stranger's thermocline. What they learn is filed in the heartwood. What they do not learn becomes a new thread of inquiry, a new direction for the next decade's root growth. The divers never know they have been assessed. They leave with their trade goods and their samples and their surface-world urgency, and the market returns to its equilibrium, its held breath, its permanent dusk. The Keeper remains. The tree grows, or fails to grow. The centuries pass like silt settling, one particle at a time, until the weight of accumulated time becomes indistinguishable from the stone itself.
