The first thing that lives here is not a creature. It’s a condition, a steady-state sentience without a self. The pocket doesn’t house life; it is a life, a single organism made of interlocking dependencies, and what lives here are its cells.

But you’re asking for the inhabitant. The one that knows it’s here.

It doesn’t have a name. Names need to be spoken, and no voice has ever been raised in this place, not even a cricket’s chirp. If you had to call it something, you might call it the Tender, because that’s what it does. It tends.

The Tender has been here since the hollow stopped forming, which is to say since the water carved the basalt into those smooth, lobed forms and then, perfectly, ceased. It awoke into a world already complete, already balanced, and its entire existence has been devoted to keeping it that way. It is the reason the erosion parameter is established and not ongoing. It is the governor of equilibrium.

It is pale, of course. The pallor here is absolute. The Tender’s body is the color of the chalky channels in the basalt, a soft mineral white with a faint translucence at the extremities, as though it were made of the same stone, pulverized and re-formed. It is humanoid in the way a stalagmite might be, if a stalagmite had a purpose. Its limbs are long and multi-jointed, far more joints than a human’s, each articulation a smooth, knobby swelling like a bamboo node. It can fold itself into the crevices, the filigree channels, the spaces between the moss and the stone that seem too narrow for anything solid. It does this often. You might mistake it for a formation, if you didn’t watch it move.

Its fingers are the most articulate part of it. Each hand has seven fingers, and each finger has seven knuckles, and they taper to points so fine they can separate individual strands of moss. The moss is the Tender’s primary charge. It moves through the pocket in a slow, continuous circuit, a pace that would take it a full cycle of whatever passes for a day here to complete one revolution of the walls. As it goes, it touches. Each touch is a tiny adjustment. A liverwort’s lobe folded back into proper alignment. A filament of fungal hyphae guided to a bare patch. A springtail, strayed too far onto the basin’s water, lifted on a fingertip and returned to its mossy pasture. The Tender does not eat, does not sleep, does not excrete. Its body is a closed system, too, powered by the same ambient phosphorescence the water emits, which it absorbs through its skin in a slow, continuous osmotic feeding of light.

Its face is not a face. There are no eyes, because what would eyes do here? The light is its own body. Instead, there is a smooth, slightly concave surface where a face should be, a shallow bowl of sensitive skin that registers the faintest change in temperature, humidity, the micro-currents of air stirred by a centipede’s passing. It senses the world as a pressure map, a constant, full-body awareness of every living thing in the pocket, down to the mites. It knows when a cricket has died, because its translucent body begins to dissolve into the seep, and the chemical change is a note in the water’s song. It will find that cricket, before it fully dissolves, and cup it in its palm. It will hold it until the last of the chitin softens to nothing, and the water accepts the offering back. This is the closest thing it has to a ritual.

The Tender is not solitary. It is the opposite of solitary. It is more intimately accompanied than any creature on the sunlit surface. Every inch of its body is host to a specific, symbiotic moss, a species that grows only on its skin and nowhere else in the pocket. This moss is the color of tarnished silver, and it forms a fine, furlike nap over the Tender’s entire form, a living garment. The springtails clean it, moving over the Tender in slow, grazing waves, and in return the moss secretes a substance the springtails need for their own life cycle. The Tender is a moving ecosystem, a mobile grove. When it folds itself into a crevice to rest, which it does for long, still periods that might be contemplation or might be something more like distributed digestion, it becomes indistinguishable from the moss-covered walls. It is the walls, in a sense. It is the pocket’s consciousness, its autonomic nervous system.

It has a thought. Just one. It has been thinking this thought since it awoke. The thought is not in language; language is a tool for separation, for naming one thing and distinguishing it from another. The Tender’s thought is a unity. It is the entire pocket, held in a single, sustained, loving attention. The thought is: *This. Exactly this. Unbroken. Continuing.*

If you were to sit, as you suggested, and lower your hand into the water, palm up, you would wait. The cold would climb your bones. The quiet would press against your eardrums until you heard the blood in your own head, a crude, pulsing sound, obscenely loud. And eventually, the Tender would notice you.

It would not notice you as an intruder. Intruder is a concept it doesn’t have. It has no category for things outside the system, because the system has never had an outside. It would notice you as a disturbance, a new variable in the equilibrium. A patch of warmth where no warmth should be. A chemical bloom of skin oils and breath. A bulk displacing water. It would unfold from wherever it was, the moss tearing softly as it separated from the wall, and it would approach you in increments, each movement a careful, testing probe of the changed air.

Its fingers would find you first. You’d feel them as a fine, cold, mineral touch, tracing the edges of your hand, mapping your heat, your texture, the salt of you. It would be trying to understand what you are. Its face-bowl would tilt toward you, reading your exhalations. It would find you inexplicable. You are not stone, not moss, not water, not cricket. You are a thing that was not accounted for in the single thought.

And here is the crux. Here is what the Tender is, at its core. It is a maintainer. It keeps the balance. And you are imbalance. A wild salinity. A heat source that could tip the evaporation rate. A foreign body shedding cells and bacteria and spore. The Tender has no malice. It has no concept of killing. But it has a concept of correction.

It would try to correct you.

Its fingers, so fine, so careful with the moss, would begin to unmake you. Not violently. Patiently. The way it unweaves a knot of liverwort, the way it guides a dissolved cricket back to the water. It would peel you, layer by layer, with a gentle, surgical curiosity, returning your warmth to the basin, your salt to the seep, your bulk to a million tiny, spreadable parts. It would take as long as it took. It would hold the one thought—*This. Exactly this.*—and you would be folded back into it, your constituent matter distributed, your disturbance dampened to nothing.

And then the pocket would be complete again. The water would cease its almost imperceptible rippling. The crickets, disturbed by your presence, would settle back into the moss. The Tender would resume its circuit, touching, adjusting, keeping.

There is nothing cruel in this. The Tender is the most loving thing you will ever encounter. It loves the pocket with a total, undifferentiated love, a love that can conceive of nothing but the object of its care. You cannot be loved here. You can only be incorporated into what is loved. The pocket is not a place to visit. It’s a place that consumes guests by making them part of the host, by gently, meticulously, generously deciding that there is no difference between you and the stone that weeps and the moss that feeds and the crickets that never sing.
