To live in the Moult is to never know the shape of your own home.

The Glimmerkin have no word for "house" that does not also mean "temporary." They build their shelters the way a spider rebuilds a torn web—not with hope of permanence, but with the resigned, meticulous patience of those who know the wind will come again. Their dwellings are low, half-domed structures of mineral rind and shattered salt-crust, propped against the larger spires for the warmth they radiate. But the spires shift. The ground beneath them dissolves and re-forms. A shelter that stood for three cycles of the Great Drip will, on the fourth, be found sagging into a new-formed pool of milky water, its walls already crusted with the teeth of the blind worms. And so the Glimmerkin move. They always move.

They are small things, the Glimmerkin, built low to the ground, as if the world itself had shaved them down out of fear of the ceiling. Their bodies are soft and pale, the color of the mineral rind before it hardens, and they wear garments woven from the silk of the web-moths—clinging, shimmering cloth that wicks the constant moisture from their skin and leaves them looking, from a distance, like walking patches of mist. Their heads are smooth and hairless, dominated by two enormous, black eyes that have evolved not to see in darkness, but to read the subtle gradations of the sourceless glow—to tell, from a flicker, which pools are safe, which spires are about to fall.

They have no mouth in the traditional sense. Instead, a small, vertical slit in the lower half of their face opens onto a delicate, feathery organ that filters the humid air for the fine mineral dust that is their primary food. They "eat" by breathing—by standing at the edge of a hot vent, their gill-mouths fluttering, as the silica-moths swarm around them, shedding their microscopic scales. To an outside observer, it would look like nothing at all: a row of pale figures, standing perfectly still, their chests rising and falling in unison, while the air around them glitters with falling flakes of life. The Glimmerkin would tell you, had they a language for it, that the taste of the air before a vent-sizzle is the taste of *becoming*—sharp, metallic, alive.

Their hands are their greatest tools and their greatest vulnerability. Each Glimmerkin has four fingers on each hand, long and multi-jointed, tipped with a hard, chitinous claw that never stops growing. They must constantly rasp their claws against the stone, wearing them down, shaping them into hooks and chisels and needles. A Glimmerkin without the means to rasp its claws will be dead within a cycle, weighted down by its own overgrown fingers, unable to eat, unable to build, unable to carve the small, desperate symbols that mark their territory.

Those symbols are everywhere in the Moult, if one knows how to look. Scratched into the bases of spires, etched into the rims of pools, gouged into the salt-crust that forms and breaks and forms again. They are not a written language in the way we might understand one. They are more like a memory, a trace, a way of saying *I was here, and I was alive, and the world was the same shape for a single moment before it changed again.* The oldest of these symbols, the ones carved deepest, are found on the glassy domes that have hardened fully—the ones that will last, perhaps, a little longer than the rest. The Glimmerkin regard these with a kind of reverence, the way one might look at the footprints of a dead ancestor preserved in cooling ash.

They have no leaders, no kings, no councils. Their society, if it can be called that, is a loose web of small family groups—never more than a dozen adults, a handful of larvae—who drift through the Moult like sediment in a slow current, meeting at the warm vents, parting at the cold pools. When two groups meet, they exchange no words, for the Moult is too loud for speech. Instead, they touch their foreheads together, briefly, and the soft, feathery organs around their eyes shiver in greeting. It is a way of saying: *you are still here. I am still here. The world has not yet dissolved us.* Then they part, and the mists swallow them again.

Their young are born from eggs, not laid, but *deposited*—placed carefully in the warm, shallow basins where the mineral water is richest, left to gestate in the same patient, chemical stew that grows the spires. The larvae, when they hatch, are little more than pulsing sacs of translucent flesh, blind and legless, and they feed by burrowing into the soft ground and absorbing the slurry of minerals and micro-life that seeps from below. It is a dangerous time. The blind worms, the skittering arthropods, the grinding shell-creatures—all of them would eat a larval Glimmerkin without a second's hesitation. And so the adults maintain a constant, silent vigil over the birthing pools, standing motionless for cycles at a time, their claws raised, their gill-mouths fluttering with the scent of approaching threats.

The larvae that survive—and it is not many—eventually sprout legs, then arms, then the great black eyes that will be their primary interface with the world. They emerge from the pools dripping with thick, mineral-rich fluid, their small bodies already crusted with the beginnings of the pale, pebble-like growths that mark a mature Glimmerkin. The adults receive them without ceremony, but with a kind of grim tenderness. There is much to teach and little time to teach it: how to rasp one's claws against the right kind of stone, how to read the temperature of a pool by the way the steam curls, how to build a shelter that will hold for three cycles, four if one is clever.

But the most important lesson, the one that every Glimmerkin learns before it can walk, is this: *do not grow attached to the shape of things.* The spire you lean against today may be a pool tomorrow. The pool you drink from this cycle may be a vent the next. The ground beneath you is not ground; it is a river of stone and salt, flowing so slowly that it tricks you into thinking it is solid. Attach yourself to the shape of things, the elders teach, and you will be broken as certainly as the salt-crust breaks under the weight of a growing stalactite.

And so they live, the Glimmerkin, in a state of perpetual, watchful transience. They build their small shelters, knowing they will fall. They carve their small symbols, knowing they will fade. They stand before the vents, breathing in the mineral dust, knowing that the air itself is a kind of food and a kind of erasure, giving and taking in the same breath. They are not the masters of the Moult. They are its children, its symptoms, its scribes. They do not shape the world so much as they carve, briefly, their own small marks upon its ceaseless, indifferent becoming.

And sometimes, when the ground shudders with a particularly violent tremor, and a new spire cracks open with a hiss of steam, they pause in their work and turn their great black eyes toward the sound. Not with fear, not with wonder, but with the calm, practiced attention of those who have learned to read the world's convulsions the way a sailor reads the sea. They watch. They wait. And when the tremors settle, and the new vent begins to breathe its wet heat into the air, they move toward it, slowly, carefully, their claws rasping against the ground with a sound like applause, the sound of the Moult's own continuing, impossible, magnificent construction.
