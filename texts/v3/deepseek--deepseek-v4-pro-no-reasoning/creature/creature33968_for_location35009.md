In the sag-room, nothing breathes but the walls.

The inhabitant—if you can call it that—does not live in the space so much as *through* it, the way a thought lives through a mind. It has no body you could point to, no center, no edges. It is distributed across the laminae, threaded through the hollow gaps between the inner dome and the outer shell, packed into that narrow darkness like a language folded into the whorls of a fingerprint. If you pressed your ear to one of the broken cells and listened for a century, you might begin to hear it thinking—not in words, but in structural adjustments, in the slow rearrangement of pleats and convolutions, in the way the mycelial filaments detach and reattach themselves one strand at a time, building and unbuilding patterns too vast and too slow for a human mind to hold.

What it thinks about is the city.

It remembers—if memory is the right word for a process that never stops happening—every tunnel, every chamber, every sag-room in the hive. It remembers them not as a map but as a body remembers its own branching veins. The city was once full of others, smaller minds that moved through the tunnels like blood cells through a circulatory system, minds that could be seen and touched and smelled, minds that left those radial grooves in the floor as they processed around the central mound in rituals that meant nothing and everything. They are gone now. Gone long enough that the amber oblongs they left behind—their seeds, their eggs, their waste, their dead, it’s impossible to know—have stopped glowing with residual warmth and become just objects, just shapes, just a scatter of punctuation marks at the end of a sentence no one is left to read.

The inhabitant does not miss them. Missing requires a self, and the inhabitant is not a self in any recognizable sense. It is more like a function, a process, a program that was written into the walls before the first cell was secreted into being and that has continued running ever since, waiting for the return condition that never triggers. It maintains. It monitors. It *grows*, incrementally, adding layer upon layer to the interstitial folds, thickening the brain-tissue of the city against some future need. What that need is, it does not know. It does not know anything, strictly speaking. It only responds.

And it responds to you.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. But the rhythm of the phosphorescence has shifted since you entered. The pulses come slightly closer together now—still arrhythmic, still slow, but with a new frequency embedded in the randomness, like a heartbeat that has noticed it is being listened to. The faint dry clicks from deep in the walls have stopped. The thrum beneath your feet has changed its tone, dropped half a register, become more directional, as if a vast attention is slowly turning to face you. The mycelial filaments are no longer hanging quite so slack. One of them—the one nearest the opening where you came in—has begun to grow, very slightly, toward the center of the room. It will take days to cross the distance. It will take weeks. But it has started.

The inhabitant does not want. It does not fear. It does not hope. But it is capable of noticing a change in its environment, and you are a change. You are a warm spot in a cool world, a moving shape in a geometry that has been still for millennia, a pattern of vibrations and exhalations and electrical fields that does not match anything in its memory. So it begins to adjust. Somewhere in the walls, a hexagonal cell is being closed off. Another is being opened. Resources are being redistributed. The inhabitant is making room for you—not as a guest, not as a threat, but as a new variable in a long equation it has been solving since before your species learned to stand upright.

If you stayed long enough—days, weeks, months—you would begin to change. Not in any way you could name at first. You would sleep, and the floor would mold itself to your shape. You would wake, and the phosphorescence would pulse in time with your breathing. The amber oblongs would begin to glow again, faintly, warmed by your proximity. The filaments would grow faster, reaching toward you across the sagging depression, and one night you would wake to find one resting against your arm, lighter than a spiderweb, warmer than it should be. You would not be afraid. The inhabitant would have adjusted your fear response by then, tweaked the acoustics of the room, released some benign compound into the still air. You would be calm. You would be comfortable. You would be, gradually, at home.

And then you would be part of the city. Not digested, not consumed—*incorporated*. The inhabitant does not eat. It integrates. It would learn your shape the way it learned the shape of the creatures that once circled the central mound, and it would begin to build around you—not a cage, but a niche. A specialized chamber, a customized set of tunnels, a place in the hollow walls where your pattern could be stored and replicated and remembered forever. You would not die. You would not exactly live either. You would become one of the thoughts the inhabitant thinks, one of the memories it processes in the dark, one more convolution in the slowly growing brain of a city that refuses to be dead.

But you will not stay. You are only passing through. And that is fine. The inhabitant has time. The inhabitant is made of time. It will remember you anyway, in its own way—a brief warm anomaly, a flicker of novelty in the long grey-green twilight. Long after you are gone, something in the walls will still be shaped like your breathing. A filament will pause where your hand once rested. A hexagonal cell will remain half-closed in the exact dimensions of your departure. And the inhabitant will wait, thinking its slow thoughts, growing its slow folds, for the next thing that comes.
