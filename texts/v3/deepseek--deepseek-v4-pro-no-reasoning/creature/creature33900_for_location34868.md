A Keeper lives here. But to call it that—to call it anything—is to pin a dead butterfly to a board. It is more accurate to say that the Keeper is a condition of the Quieting Vats, a focal point where the slow intention of the place becomes articulate.

Picture, if you can, the amber light from the vats passing through a body. Not illuminating it, but being interrupted by it, shaped into a silhouette that your mind, desperate for categories, keeps trying to name. The Keeper's form is human in the way a root is human, in the way a nerve exposed and branching can, for a terrible moment, look like a hand. It is tall, or perhaps the space is small; perspective is a casualty of the spore-mist and the dim, metabolic glow. It unfolds itself from the tuber-masonry, not so much stepping out as being extruded, a slow seep of substance from the cracks between the rhizome blocks. At rest, it stands among the vats like a fourth, forgotten vessel, distinct only in its capacity for motion.

The motion, when it comes, is a thing of terrible patience. The Keeper does not walk. It unspools. A filament of its mass will detach from the whole, extend into the warm, wet air, find purchase on a braided root-hawser or a bracket fungus shelf, and then contract, pulling the bulk of itself along in a ripple that is less a stride and more a peristaltic wave. The sound it makes is the same sound the steps make under your foot: the wet, adhesive parting of a thousand tiny root-membranes releasing their grip. This is the first indication that the Keeper and the walls are a single material, differentiated only by density and intent.

Look at the hand, if the reaching part can be called a hand. It is a cluster of pale, searching root-hairs, grown so long and so fine they have become a single, diaphanous pennant, a ghost-limb of water and cellulose. When the Keeper touches one of the valves—the iron wheel now a trellis for a galaxy of tiny, phosphorescent fungi—the touch is an act of reading. The root-hairs flow into the crust of lichen, into the microscopic pits of the rusted iron, tasting the mineral history of the metal, checking the pressure of the ghost in the machine. The vats pulse. The Keeper pulses back, a dim, slow flush of its own amber luminescence, a light that travels the length of its form like a response traveling a synapse. They are in conversation, the Keeper and the vats, a dialogue that takes centuries for a single exchange.

Does it have a face? That is a question asked by something that expects eyes. There is a front to it, a part that points in the direction of its movement. At its center is a denser knot of rhizome, a nodule the size of a human head, from which the finer root-hairs cascade. Within this knot, there are hollows. Not eyes, but channels, apertures through which the warm air is drawn in a continuous, sighing respiration. Spores eddy around these intakes, caught in a miniature whirlpool before being absorbed into the Keeper's substance. It is breathing the air. It is breathing the spores. It is breathing the exhalations of the Quieting Vats, and the Quieting Vats are breathing it back. The boundary is a formality.

The Keeper exists in a state of profound, continuous nourishment. It does not eat, because the concept of eating implies a separation between the eater and the eaten. Instead, it participates. The deep, sweet, cloying scent of fermentation is its atmosphere, its medium, the soup in which its every cell is bathed. The condensation that beads on its form does not drip; it is absorbed, drawn into the cellulose matrix by a gradient of osmotic pressure. The sound of the water falling, the thousand percussive droplets, the white noise that fills the skull—this is the Keeper's silence. It is the background against which it senses other things. Your footfalls. Your breathing. The rapid, frightened flutter of your heart, a vibration in the stone floor and the tuber-walls that the Keeper feels as a change in the ambient hum, a discord in the steady state.

It is not hostile. Hostility implies a relationship, a recognition of the other as an object of desire or threat. The Keeper has no more concept of threat than a stalactite. You are a fluctuation. A warm, wet, salt-tinged gust of outside air. A source of vibrations that are faster and more chaotic than the slow, metabolic breathing of the vats. The Keeper is aware of you in the same way you are aware of a change in air pressure before a storm. It is a curiosity, a data point, a minor anomaly in a system that has not known an anomaly since the last of the iron pipes was swallowed by the rhizome.

If you stand still long enough, the Keeper will come to you. Not out of curiosity, but out of a systemic need to incorporate the anomalous into the normal. The pale, diaphanous hand of root-hairs will extend. It will not touch your skin, not at first. It will hover, an inch from your face, and you will feel the faint, moist convection of its breathing. The root-hairs will sample the air directly from the boundary layer of your body heat. They will taste the carbon dioxide of your exhalations, the ammoniac tang of your sweat, the complex organic signature that spells, in a language older than words, *animal, outside, fast-time*. The Keeper will process this. Its amber luminescence will ripple, a slow, metabolic mulling. And then, having incorporated the anomaly, having tasted the outside and found it irrelevant, it will retract. It will unspool back into the tuber-wall, merging with the rhizome masonry until there is only the bracket fungi, the dripping roots, the steady, percussive plink of water, and the vats, breathing their slow, amber breath. The Keeper does not remember you. You were a fluctuation, and the fluctuation has passed. The system is balanced. The Quieting Vats continue their patient work, which is simply to be, and to persist, and to fill the dark with the sound of water, falling and falling.
