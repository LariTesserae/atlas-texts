Now. Something moves.

Not the far-off skittering you heard before — that is still there, still distant, a sound like dry leaves dragged over stone, periodic and faint. This is closer. This is a sound of weight. A settling of mass. A slow, deliberate redistribution of pressure across a living floor that has felt nothing heavier than its own slow pulse for geological time.

You turn.

At first you do not understand what you are seeing. Your eye assembles the shape out of wrong components: a section of wall that has detached itself, a structural pillar that has decided to relocate. The light plays tricks because the thing is the same color as the architecture — that pale waxy cream, the smooth integument, the faint vertical striations of lignin growth. It blends into the chamber behind it so completely that you perceive it only as a shifting of perspective, a parallax error in your own depth perception.

But it is not the wall. It is standing in front of the wall. It is the wall, grown into a different shape and given movement.

It stands — no. The word implies legs, implies bilateral symmetry, implies a body plan your nervous system has categories for. This thing has elected a different solution. It is low and broad and radial, a flattened dome rising no more than a meter from the floor, but vast in circumference: fifteen meters across, perhaps, its perimeter dissolving into hundreds of fine, pale root-filaments that merge seamlessly with the weave of the floor. You cannot tell where it ends and the building begins. The interface is perfect, a gradual transition from mobile organism to stationary architecture, as though it has rejoined the floor to rest, and the floor has welcomed it back.

The dome is not featureless. Across its upper surface, arranged in spirals that follow some logarithmic pattern you cannot quite parse, are structures. Some are soft, translucent blisters like the light-bladders overhead, but smaller, pulsing faintly with an internal illumination that cycles through colors outside your normal vocabulary — ultraviolet bleeding into a blue so pale it is almost white, then back. Others are harder, more geometric: ridges of what might be chitin or might be compacted cellulose, forming baffles, apertures, resonance chambers. And among them, scattered without obvious pattern, are eyes.

Not eyes like yours. They are depressions in the integument, each lined with a reflective tapetum that catches the dome-light and sends it back in a scatter of pale rainbows. They are different sizes — some as small as your thumbnail, others as wide as your spread hand — and they do not point in a single direction. They are mounted at all angles across the curve of the dome, giving the thing a field of view that is simultaneously panoramic and fractured, seeing in all directions at once, seeing itself seeing, seeing you from twenty different angles simultaneously.

None of the eyes are looking at you. All of the eyes are looking at you. The distinction between these statements is only a matter of attention allocation, and you have no framework for understanding what attention means to a mind distributed across a curved surface with no front and no back.

The dome breathes. Not like lungs. The whole upper surface rises and falls in a slow wave that starts at the center and propagates outward to the perimeter, where it disappears into the floor-roots. A peristaltic ripple, repeating every thirty seconds or so, moving gas or fluid through internal chambers. The air near it stirs slightly; you feel the faint displacement on your cheek. It smells of ozone and that sweet volatile again, stronger now, and underneath that, something earthier. Wet lignin. Activated charcoal. The exhalation of a body that metabolizes wood.

One of the spirals of soft blisters shifts. The blisters reconfigure themselves, sliding through the integument like organelles in a cell, and form a new pattern. A phyllotaxis. A Fibonacci sequence. A question. You watch this happen and understand, at a level below language, that the pattern is not random. It is information. It is signal. The thing is doing something with the arrangement of its bioluminescent organs that functions as communication, or computation, or both, and the message is not meant for you because you are not even a category in its ontology.

But it knows you are here. The eyes prove that. The eyes are tracking you, not by moving — they have no musculature, no irises, no mechanisms for aiming — but by shifting their internal reflectivity, so that the bright points of light travel across their surfaces as you move. It is watching you with the same passive, distributed attention it gives to everything else in this chamber: the settling of a distant pillar, the drift of a dying light-bladder, the faint skitter of something smaller moving through the walls, the vascular hum, the temperature gradient across the floor. You are one input among many. An anomaly, perhaps. A perturbation in the steady state. But not a priority.

What has priority? You watch the blisters cycle through their patterns and you realize, with a slow cold thrill, that the thing is thinking. The blisters are a display, a visible output of some internal process that integrates sensor data across the entire dome surface and does something with it. It is forming abstractions. It is comparing current state to some stored model of optimal state. It is making decisions, but they are decisions on a timescale you can barely perceive — a slow shifting of resource allocation, a gradual redirecting of the vascular flow in the floor beneath it, a subtle change in the spectral output of its light-organs that might be the equivalent of a furrowed brow or might be something no human word can touch.

The dome is not alone. As your eyes adjust to its presence, you begin to see others. In the chamber beyond this one, visible through a grown archway, another low shape is merged with the floor. And beyond that, in the blue-white distance, a third. They are spaced at intervals that seem regular, almost gridlike, if you account for the non-Euclidean curvature of the architecture. Nodes in a network. Neurons in a distributed brain that spans the entire edifice. The dome before you is one cell in a thinking organ the size of a continent, and the patterns moving across its surface are part of a conversation that has been ongoing since before your species existed.

You stay very still. The dome's peristalsis continues. One of its eyes — the largest, positioned near the apex — shifts its internal reflection and seems, for a moment, to focus on your face with an intensity the others lack. The light in it cycles through that pale ultraviolet again, and you feel something in your own visual cortex twitch, a misfire, a phantom perception of a color you cannot name. Then it cycles back to the steady blue-white, and the moment passes. You have been noticed. You have been assessed. You have been filed under some category you will never know.

The blisters on the dome's surface rearrange again. This time the pattern is simpler. A spiral. A pause. Another spiral, counter-rotating. Then stillness. The thing has processed you, and the processing is complete, and it has returned to whatever slow, continent-spanning thought it was occupied with before you arrived.

Behind you, the dying light-bladder finally reaches the floor. There is no sound of impact. The root-weave opens slightly, a soft mouth of parting fibers, and draws it under. The integument seals over it. The chamber's light does not dim; another bladder, somewhere overhead, has already brightened to compensate. The steady state is maintained.

You turn back. The dome is still there, still breathing, still watching everything and nothing with its scattered eyes. And now you understand the question you were not quite asking. What lives in a place like this? Nothing that lives the way you live. Nothing with a self, a boundary, a beginning and an end. Nothing that moves except when it needs to, which is almost never. Nothing that thinks except in slow, distributed, geological thoughts. A ruined ecology. A stone mind. A breathing architecture that grew itself a nervous system and then grew that nervous system a body, or a body a nervous system, until the distinction stopped signifying.

You are in the mind of the world. The mind of the world has noticed you. The mind of the world has gone back to sleep.

The skittering in the walls draws closer, then fades. The vascular hum continues. The light, from everywhere, remains steady and bright. And the great radial creature, the thinking dome, the junction-node of a planetary cortex, resumes its slow peristaltic breath, its quiet computation, its million-year maintenance of a system too vast for any single mind to comprehend, including its own.
