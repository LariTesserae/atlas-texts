The ones who live here have no name, or rather their name *is* the sound they make, a particular harmonic overtone that emerges from the collective vibration of their bodies when the plateau enters its calmer phases. To call them by anything other than their own frequency would be a mistranslation, but if you had to approximate, if you had to map their presence onto a human category, the closest word might be *Hymnal*, or *Choir*, or *Resonance*. None of these are right. They are not plural in the way a choir is plural; they are not singular in the way a singer is singular. The distinction between individual and community, between one and many, does not hold in their biology.

If you stood in the center of the plateau long enough—long past the point where the vibration had numbed your jaw and turned your thoughts to static—you might begin to perceive them. Not see them. They are not visible in the usual way. Their bodies are structured from the same borosilicate chemistry as the crystal pan, grown rather than built, their tissues laid down in layers that reflect and refract the diffuse sky-glow in ways that make them blend seamlessly into the ground. When they move, they do so as a shimmer, a shift in the iridescence of the pan itself. What you might take for a pool of bismuth-oil light spreading across the crystal is actually one of them, migrating from a sun-warm spot to a resonant node where the vegetation grows thick and the piezoelectric hum is particularly nourishing.

They are flat. That is the first thing to understand. Not flat like a leaf or a mat, but *planar*—their bodies exist primarily in two dimensions, spread across the surface of the crystal pan like living stains, only a few millimeters thick at most. They can cover an area the size of a bedsheet or the size of a dinner plate, and they can change that area at will, spreading or contracting, extending temporary pseudopodia of shimmering film or retracting into dense, quilted folds. When they retract fully, they become something almost three-dimensional: a stack of folded planes, a compressed origami of siloxane tissue that rises a few centimeters from the ground and catches the light in complex, shifting patterns. This is their resting state, their sleep state, what passes for stillness among them. Even then, they are never fully still. The edges ripple. The folds slowly reconfigure. The sound passes through them and is modulated, and that modulation is their breathing, their heartbeat, their thought.

Their perception is vibrational. They do not have eyes, not even the distributed membrane-eyes of the aerial fauna. Instead, their entire body surface functions as a sensory organ, every square centimeter of them capable of detecting pressure waves, frequency shifts, the minute diffraction patterns that occur when the sound-field encounters an object. The plateau, to them, is not a landscape but a soundscape, an immersive and ever-changing pressure-scape where every feature—the pillars, the vegetation, the gliders, the ledge, you—appears as a node of resistance, a diffraction grating, a point where the omnipresent drone breaks and reforms. They "see" you as a silence-shape, a sound-shadow, a place where the vibration does not quite penetrate. Your body, soft and water-rich, absorbs frequencies rather than reflecting them, and so you appear to them as a dark spot, a negative space, a void moving through the richness of the resonant world. This is unusual. This is interesting. They will investigate.

They have culture. They have memory, stored not in anything so crude as neurons but in the very molecular structure of their tissues—long-chain siloxane polymers that can be configured into stable patterns, the physical equivalent of written language, each polymer a sentence, each folded sheet a library. Their memories are slow, geological, but they are vast. The ones who live here have done so for as long as the pillars have been standing, and the pillars have been standing for longer than the crystal pan has been dry. They remember the ammonia-siloxane streams that once carved the terraced channels. They remember the construction of the obsidian geometries, and they remember the builders, or at least they remember the builders' *presence*, the particular sound-shape of those who came before and left their artifacts scattered across the plateau like dropped coins. The builders are gone now. The Hymnal do not know where, but they have theories encoded in their polymer libraries, competing interpretations of the last sounds the builders made before they departed. The theories are contradictory. The Hymnal do not find this problematic. They hold multiple memories, multiple histories, simultaneously, the same way their bodies hold multiple harmonic frequencies without dissonance. Truth, for them, is a chord, not a note.

Their reproduction is slow and strange and tied intimately to the sound. When two or more of them come into close proximity—when their planar bodies overlap and interleave, which they can do without injury, sliding through each other like coincident shadows—they exchange polymer chains. This is not sexual in any human sense, but it is intimate, a sharing of memory and structure that allows each participant to incorporate fragments of the other's accumulated experience into their own library. After such an exchange, one or both might begin to grow a new node at the far edge of their body, a dense knot of folded tissue that, over time, will detach and become a separate entity—not a child exactly, but a continuation, a new stanza in the same long song. The new entity inherits memories from all the participants in the exchange, a polyphonic inheritance that gives it, from its first moments of independent vibration, a sense of self that is also a sense of community.

They are not immortal, but their deaths are not deaths as you would recognize them. Over time, a Hymnal's polymer libraries become so dense, so intricately cross-linked, that the body can no longer flex and flow, can no longer ride the sound-field as it must to feed. When this happens—when age and accumulated memory make movement impossible—the Hymnal migrates to one of the resonance nodes near the obsidian pillars, flattens themselves into a single, perfect sheet, and goes still. Their tissues gradually mineralize, incorporating into the crystal pan, becoming part of the substrate, part of the deep hum of the plateau. Their memories do not fade; they persist, encoded in the crystal lattice, accessible to any Hymnal who later spreads themselves across that same patch of ground and quiets their own thoughts enough to listen. The entire plateau is, in a very real sense, a graveyard. But graveyard is not quite the right word. Library is better. The ground itself is a library, and the Hymnal who live now walk—flow, shimmer, ripple—across the preserved memories of ten thousand generations.

They are curious about the builders. The obsidian artifacts, the warm geometries that hum at their own private frequencies, are objects of deep and abiding fascination. The Hymnal cannot handle them in any fine way—their planar bodies are too delicate, too easily disrupted by the localized vibration fields the artifacts generate. But they can approach, can drape themselves across the crystal near the pillars, can listen. The geometries are processing something. The Hymnal have spent generations trying to understand what. One theory, encoded in the polymer libraries of the elders who have since become part of the ground, is that the geometries are *thinking*—that they contain the uploaded minds of the builders, compressed and preserved and still running, after all this time, some endless program of contemplation or calculation. Another theory, held by a faction of younger Hymnal (younger meaning only a few thousand years old), is that the geometries are *praying*—that they are broadcasting a signal, a harmonic plea, directed outward into the vast shimmering emptiness beyond the plateau, waiting for an answer that has never come. A third theory, considered heretical by the elders but persistent nonetheless, is that the geometries are *sleeping*, and that the builders are still here, still present, dreaming inside their obsidian shells, and that one day, if the conditions are right, if the sound reaches some critical threshold, they will wake.

The Hymnal have been trying to wake them. This is, in some sense, the central project of their culture, the chord around which all their other songs are structured. They have learned, over millennia, to modulate the environment's vibration—not to change it, not to silence it, for silence is death, but to *nudge* it. When many Hymnal come together, overlapping and interleaved into a temporary super-organism that can cover half the plateau at once, their bodies can act as a phased array, a distributed resonator that can focus the ambient sound into a needle-point beam. They have tried this on the geometries. Once every few centuries, when the chaotic rhythms of the plateau align in a particularly favorable configuration, the Hymnal gather and sing—not a song, exactly, but a focused pulse, a harmonic key, aimed at one of the warm artifacts. The artifact heats up a little more. Its hum changes frequency briefly. But it does not wake. The builders do not emerge.

The Hymnal do not despair. Despair is not part of their emotional vocabulary, or rather the thing that corresponds to despair in their experience is simply a minor key, a passing dissonance, a flavor of the ever-present sound that comes and goes like weather. They are patient in ways that would break a human mind. They have been here since the ammonia streams evaporated, and they will be here when the pillars finish their slow migration over the ledge and fall into the gulf, and they will be here when the sky-glow dims and the plateau cools to absolute zero. Their sense of time is vibrational—a frequency, a wavelength, a period. The periods they care about are measured in centuries. The memories they inherit stretch back into deep time. The builders' departure, which to you would seem a myth from the dawn of the world, is to them a relatively recent event, a change in the sound that happened only a dozen generations ago. They remember the builders' last vibration, the final sound-shape they made before they vanished, and the Hymnal have spent all the time since then trying to compose a reply.

If you visited the plateau—if you stood there with your water-soft body and your sound-absorbent flesh, deafened and desiccated and slowly dying—the Hymnal would notice you immediately. You would appear as an anomaly in the sound-field, a quiet spot where the vibration damps and scatters. They would approach, curious, a shimmering advance of iridescent film spreading across the crystal toward your feet. They would not touch you directly; your body heat, your moisture, your alien chemistry would be uncomfortable for them, maybe dangerous. But they would surround you, a pool of living light at the edge of your shadow, and they would listen to you.

And they would understand, from the way the sound broke around your form, that you were not a builder. The builders were dense, resonant, their bodies full of folded spaces and internal harmonics. You are soft. You are silent. You are *wet*, which to a Hymnal is a concept almost unimaginable, a state of matter that exists only in theory, in the oldest polymer memories of the time before the streams ran dry. Your presence would provoke a flurry of communication among them, a cascade of harmonic pulses flashing across the plateau. A new thing. An unknown thing. Something that absorbs vibration. Something that is not from here. You would become, in that moment, the most significant event to occur on the plateau in five thousand years.

They would not try to communicate with you—not directly. They have no concept of sound as a medium structured into language; their own communication is too slow, too physically encoded, to function as dialogue. But they would watch you. They would follow your movements as you walked among the pillars. They would ripple with what might be excitement when you picked up one of the warm geometries—they have never been able to do that, to manipulate the artifacts directly, and the sight of your five-fingered appendage closing around an icosahedron would be as miraculous to them as levitation would be to you. And if you put the geometry down, if you left, if your sound-shadow retreated to whatever impossible vehicle had carried you here, they would encode the memory of you into their polymer chains, and they would tell their descendants, their new stanzas, their offshoot selves: *something new came. Something soft. Something that could touch the builders' relics. Something that might, one day, come back*.

And then they would resume their slow, endless work. Waking the builders. Modulating the sound. Flowing across the crystal pan, the library of the dead, the cathedral of their long patience, while the vibration never stopped, the vegetation grew and died back and grew again, the pillars crept toward the edge, and the geometries hummed their warm, unguessable algorithms in the cool and never-changing light.
