The only person who lives in the Sump is a woman named Maren, and she has lived there long enough that the slugs no longer divert their paths for her.

She is old, though it is difficult to say how old. Her face is lined in a way that suggests decades rather than years, the skin tough and cross-hatched like the palm of a working hand, but her movements are unhurried and without frailty. She is small and wiry, and she dresses in layers of waxed canvas and wool that have taken on the same grey-green cast as the mosses, so that when she stands still against the pipework, which she often does, she is difficult to pick out from the vegetation. Her hair, once dark, is now a streaked and steely grey, and she wears it pulled back in a tight braid that hangs between her shoulder blades, the end of it secured with a length of copper wire stripped from an old cable.

She arrived here a long time ago, in a different version of the city. She had been something else before — a pipefitter, or a welder, or perhaps an engineer's assistant; the details have softened with time and she does not volunteer them. What matters is that she came down into the hollow one day to repair a leaking valve, or to trace the source of a pressure drop, and she never properly left. At first she told herself she was staying because the work was unfinished. Then because the ferns interested her. Then because she had begun to notice the schedules of the slugs — which pipes they preferred at which hours, how their grazing patterns shifted after a rain — and she wanted to see what they would do next. By the time she understood that she was not going back, the city above had forgotten she was there.

Her home is tucked into a recess where two large steam pipes converge, creating a pocket of steady warmth. She has built a shelter from salvaged materials: walls of corrugated tin and sections of old pipe lagging, a roof of heavy-gauge plastic sheeting that she patches with tape when it tears. The floor is a wooden pallet raised on bricks to keep it above the damp. Inside, there is a narrow cot made from steel tubing and a mattress of compressed fern-fronds, which she replaces every few weeks as they break down. A small paraffin stove, which she fuels sparingly. A shelf of jars containing things she has collected — snail shells sorted by size, interesting pieces of rust-flake, a desiccated moth whose wings she found particularly beautiful. A notebook, swollen with moisture, in which she records observations in a cramped, precise hand: the timing of the steam pulses, the first unfurling of a new frond, the day she counted ninety-seven slugs on the west wall.

She does not need much. She eats simply, from supplies she gathers in brief, nocturnal excursions up through the maintenance door and into the city. Bread, dried fruit, tins of sardines, tea. She has an arrangement with a night-shift worker at a bakery who leaves day-old loaves in a particular spot behind a dumpster, and in return Maren leaves small things — a perfect spiral of snail shell, a drawing of a fern on a scrap of paper, once a living moth in a jar with air holes punched in the lid. She does not know if these gifts are understood, but the bread keeps appearing, and that is enough.

The city's sounds reach her as a muffled roar, a constant backdrop that she has long since stopped noticing. What she notices instead are the sounds of the Sump itself, and she knows them with an intimacy that borders on the maternal. She can tell by the pitch of a particular hiss whether a valve is beginning to stick. She can distinguish the drip of condensation from the drip of a new leak. When the steam cycle falters — which it does, sometimes, when the factories slow their operations — she feels it in her body before her mind registers the change, a wrongness in the rhythm that sets her on edge.

She talks to the slugs. Not in full sentences, usually, but in small, absent-minded murmurs. She names them after their preferred territories — Rim, who lives along the lip of the grated drain; Flange, who favours the bolted joint of the largest cast-iron pipe; Mossback, an elderly specimen whose body has taken on a greenish tint from the algal spores that cling to its slime. She knows the lifespans of slugs, knows that the individuals she names are not the same ones she named a few years ago, but the territories remain consistent, and so she thinks of the names as belonging to the positions rather than the animals. It amounts to the same thing, in a place where change moves so slowly.

Maren's relationship with the Sump is not one of ownership or even of stewardship. She is simply another organism that has found its niche here. Like the ferns, she draws what she needs from the hollow's cycles — warmth, water, shelter, a kind of sustenance that is not quite food. Like the moths, she has adapted to a life spent entirely within these basalt walls, her world contracting to the thirty-foot aperture of grey sky above and the slow, breathing dark below. She has become so attuned to the Sump's rhythms that she no longer knows where her own rhythms end and the hollow's begin. She sleeps when the steam cycle deepens in the small hours of the night. She wakes when the first shift begins in the factory above and the pipes shudder faintly with increased pressure. Her heart, she sometimes thinks, beats in time with the pressure-release valves — a fanciful notion, but one that comforts her.

She is not lonely. This is the thing that would surprise most people, if there were anyone to ask. She is not lonely, and she is not mad, or at least not in any way that troubles her. The Sump is full of company. The slugs move with their slow, deliberate grace, and she moves among them. The ferns uncurl their fronds toward the light, and she watches them do it. The steam sighs, and she sighs with it, and the cycle continues, and that is enough.

Sometimes, very rarely, a maintenance worker will come through the narrow door and stop on the little rusted platform, staring down into the green, dripping quiet. Maren sees them before they see her. She stands very still against the pipework, her waxed canvas blending with the moss, and she waits. They never stay long. The hollow is not built for them, and they sense it — the dense, damp, patient life of the place, the feeling that something is watching them back. They mutter to themselves, shine a torch beam across the ferns, and retreat, pulling the door shut behind them. Maren listens to their footsteps recede, and when she is certain they are gone, she steps out from the shadows and continues what she was doing — tracing a slug's trail with her fingertip, or adjusting the plastic sheeting on her shelter, or simply standing in the centre of the hollow, face tilted up toward the grey sky, letting the mist settle on her skin.
