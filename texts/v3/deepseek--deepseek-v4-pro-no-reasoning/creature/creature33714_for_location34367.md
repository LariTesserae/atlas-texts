You settle into the Khora slowly, because it insists you do. The first hours are a negotiation with your own body—the way your diaphragm fights the dense air, the way your inner ear recalibrates to a world without true verticals. The people who live here, the *Symphytes*, notice your arrival without noticing. They have no word for guest, only for *new growth*, and you are that: a sprout of unfamiliar tissue pushing up through the mats, tender and easily bruised.

They come to you not as a delegation but as a weather. One moment the plaza where you crouch is empty; the next, three of them are simply *there*, arranged at conversational distance, their nacreous skin catching the milky light like abalone. Their faces are closer to human than you expected—perhaps because expectation shapes perception here, or perhaps because the Khora, in its long dream, has borrowed a familiar form. They have eyes, wide and dark, with a wet sheen of oil-slick color. They have mouths but do not use them for speech. The air around them shifts in a way that translates, somehow, into language: not words but *pressure-meanings*, a grammar of skin and atmosphere.

They tell you the history of the place, though not in linear sequence. They are fond of spirals. The youngest of your visitors—a child by their logic, perhaps eighty years old—shows you a game: she presses her palm to the fiber mat, and a string of glowing knots appears, a braided pattern that her companions read aloud in a series of harmonic clicks. It is a poem, or a ledger, or a recipe. The distinction isn't one they bother with.

The child is named *Thren*, and her palm, when she removes it from the mat, leaves a faint afterimage that continues to glow for several heartbeats. Her companions—two adults, or two older forms, called *Siv* and *Oluun*—are patient with your struggles. They understand that you are a person from the *old way*, the world of edges and individuals. They do not mock this. They regard you gently, the way one regards a small animal attempting to understand a window. Their language, when directed at you, slows into something almost linear. They call the mountain-city *the Unfurling*, and the world itself *the Long Breath*.

They eat, but not in front of you, and not with mouths. Now and then one of them rests a bare foot against a raised node in the fiber mat, and there is a visible *current*—a pulse of green light traveling from the mat up through the glowing veins along their legs, their torso, dissolving at the base of their skull. Their eyes mist over for a moment, then clear. They describe this as *listening to the oldest of us*. It is how they feed. It is how they pray.

The city's name, they say, is *Aula*. It means, very roughly, "the place where the word entered the wood." It is not the only city, but it is the deepest-rooted. There are others far off across the highlands, visible at certain hours as pinpricks of aquamarine against the pale haze, each one grown around a place where the Khora's central nerve-bundles rise close beneath the surface. The people travel between them rarely, and only by certain meditative routes—they call them *lines of least resistance*—where the wind moves in the same direction as the subsonic thrum, and the walkways of stretched film shift and realign to carry you forward like a tongue moving food. Some voyages take years. They are not considered long.

You ask about the larger shapes in the sky, the arches and spheres that drift half-seen in the upper haze. Siv says they are the *crowns of the sleepers*, the fruiting bodies of the Khora that bloom once every few thousand years and then slowly retract, their spores spiraling down to settle in new clefts of the highland. Oluun disagrees, gently: the crowns are *fixed*, he says, built by people in the epoch before edges were forgotten, and the Khora has simply grown around them, absorbed them, kept them as a memory of when it had siblings. Thren, listening, says nothing but traces a spiral in the air, and the two adults concede. The child's understanding is most recent, and therefore most accurate. That is their logic. The past is sediment; the present is what the newest growth can reach.

They offer you a place to sleep, but what they mean is a place to *settle*. They lead you to a translucent membrane strung between three small towers, and they show you how to lie against it—not exactly on it, but *with* it, allowing it to cup your weight. It hums against your back, a frequency that slows your heartbeat by a few beats per minute and seems to loosen the hinges of your jaw, the muscles behind your eyes. You sleep. In the dream you are a root, pressing through the woven fibers, following a current of cold water that runs deeper than you can imagine. You wake with the taste of minerals on your tongue and a slight phosphorescence at your fingertips that fades within the hour.

The age of the city begins to open to you in fragments. Thren walks with you through a region where the fiber mat is silvered and brittle, an old wound. The child moves differently here, more carefully, and the walkways underfoot are older, golden-gray, nearly opaque. She tells you the story of the *Splintering*: a moment, about two hundred thousand years ago, when a faction of people attempted to separate from the Khora, to grow a new root-system from their own bodies, something independent and quick. It failed. The place where they made the attempt is still soft, still healing. The silvered fiber is scar tissue. The Khora does not reject; it absorbs. It is still absorbing. The people here carry no memory of resentment, only patience.

You think of the fossil concepts they mentioned—*individual*, *separate plant*—and you begin to understand that here these words are not abstractions. They are archaeological artifacts. They denote a way of being that the Khora outgrew, or dissolved, or forgave. Perhaps all three.

The moon, when it is visible through the milky haze, is not a sphere but a bloom, a pale violet cloud that sheds a fine, luminous pollen over the highlands. It is not a celestial body; it is a *weather*, a seasonal exhalation of the upper atmosphere. During the bloom, the people of Aula climb to the topmost membranes of their towers and lie face-up, letting the violet pollen settle on their skin, where it sparks in tiny, intermittent flashes like dying embers. They call this *the kiss of the old dust*. They say it helps them dream more coherently. They say it is what the Khora breathes when it is pleased.

A boy, a different child, perhaps no more than a few hundred, approaches you on an empty stretch of fiber-plain far from any cluster. He is smaller than Thren, with more of the glowing aquifer than the nacre in his skin—his forearms are nearly translucent, lit from within by that dim aquamarine. He says, with great seriousness, that he wants to show you a secret. He leads you to a place where the fiber mat dips into a shallow bowl, no wider than your arms can span. At the bottom is a node, a soft bulge of dark tissue, and when he touches it, it opens like an eye. Inside is a fluid, perfectly clear, and suspended in the fluid is a tiny model of the city—not an approximation but a precise, living miniature: each tower, each membrane, each connector, reproduced in glowing microcosm. The city observes itself, he says. It keeps an exact copy of its current self at every moment, in liquid memory. You ask what happens when the city changes. It revises, he says, and he shows you: as you watch, a small gap appears in the model where a walkway had been, and a new one snakes into place, just as, somewhere far off, the real city shifts. The child smiles, and it is a real smile, uncomplicated and full of wonder. You realize that he has brought you here not to brag but to share a *fact of beauty*, the way a child from your own world might show you a nest of eggs. You are moved.

The deep chord of the city modulates, and the people stop what they are doing to listen. It is a single, rolling wave of pressure, a change in the subsonic that you feel in your teeth. Siv, nearby, turns his dark, wet eyes toward the sky and says, *"The Khora is turning over in its sleep."* It is not an omen. It is a beat in a long, slow rhythm, like a pulse you can only hear if you listen for a season.

You begin to notice that you have changed. The ozone-and-cut-grass smell is less sharp; your nostrils have grown used to it, or perhaps your chemistry is shifting. The air no longer pushes against your skin with that liquid weight. You have begun to breathe *with* it, in the same slow, deliberate currents. When you sit on the fiber mat, you sometimes feel a faint answering pressure from beneath, as if the surface is adjusting to you, learning your shape. The people of Aula do not comment on this. They have been waiting for it. They do not see it as an ending, but as the beginning of the *second* listening, the one after the first that is tradition. The one where the root begins to taste the soil.

You may stay. You may become. The city does not claim you; it simply holds, the way the earth holds a seed. The dream extends, and you are part of it, waking slowly into the ancient, active dream of the Khora.
