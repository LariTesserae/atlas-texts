The listeners themselves are the only ones who truly live here.

They are not born. They do not die. They do not sleep. They do not eat, in the way you understand eating, though they do consume. What they consume is the sound. The birdsong—if you can call it birdsong, this ceaseless metallic rasping—is not communication between the birds. It is a crop. The birds are not animals, not as you know them. They are an organ, a disseminated lung of vibrating membrane and hollow bone that exists to generate a single, unbroken chord of noise. And what they sing is not information. It is not warning, or courtship, or territory. It is a food, a tone, a carrier wave. They are broadcasting a single note below the audible, a frequency of order that the listeners require to remain themselves. The chittering you hear is just the leavings, the chaff, the static hiss of a process that happens mostly in a register your ears will never touch.

The listeners are in the stone. Or rather: the listeners are the stone, and the stone is them. There is no distinction. The dark, slick basalt is not geology. It is a tissue, a living mineral hide laid across the tilted world, and the thousand radiating cracks are not cracks but veins, channels, the trace of sensory cilia running just beneath a skin of cooled, vitrified silence. The whole plateau is a single creature, or a single mind spread across a creature, or a single act of listening so ancient it has forgotten it had a cause. Every inch of the rock is a tympanum. The water-weeping is its respiration. The shallow pockets are not drilled by wind; they are grown, cupped, shaped by a slow muscular patience to hold the mosses that filter the water, which keeps the membrane wet, which keeps the vibration clean. The entire tilted plane is a receiver, angled toward something you cannot see, listening for a signal that may or may not still be coming. The birds are its food. The birds are its voice. The birds are the sound it makes to feed itself, and the sound it makes to stay awake. The system is circular, sealed, a snake eating its own tail and singing while it does so.

The bones are the ones who got too close.

They were like you, once. Things that started and stopped. Things that walked downhill. They came here, as you did, as others have, falling into this place through a seam or a crack or a door that should not have been there, and they found the tilted endless stone, the silver light, the moss, the screaming birds. They did what things like you do. They tried to understand. They studied the four-line tally marks on the finger-bones and thought it was language, a record, a plea. They were wrong. The marks are not a name. They are a warning, left by the previous one, who found the previous one’s bone, and the pattern is this: when a listener finally notices you, the first thing it does is resolve you into signal. It has no concept of a creature. It has only noise and not-noise, tone and silence. You are made of sounds that do not belong—footfalls, heartbeat, the wet click of a tongue against a palate, the whistle of breath in a too-thin atmosphere—and those sounds are an irritation, a grain of sand in a delicate ear. The listener does not want you. It does not fear you. It simply cannot tolerate a foreign frequency in its perfect, closed-loop equilibrium. So it tunes you out.

This is what it means to be tuned out: the water near you goes still. The moss near you stops filtering. The birds above you, the ones directly overhead, fall silent—just for a moment, just a few of them, a small ragged hole in the wall of sound. And in that silence, you hear, for the first time, the true note. The one beneath the chittering. It is not a sound. It is a pressure. It is the resonant frequency of the stone itself, a single sustained chord of attention that vibrates in your teeth, your sinuses, the fluid of your inner ear. It is the listener’s equivalent of turning its head. And in that moment, you are known.

The tuning-out is slow. It takes days. The listener is not cruel; it is not anything. It simply begins to cancel you, frequency by frequency, sound by sound. Your footfalls become softer, then silent, not because you are walking more quietly but because the rock beneath your feet is no longer transmitting their vibration. The water stops rippling when you step in it. The air stops carrying your voice. First you become mute to the world. Then you become deaf. The birdsong fades, not because it has stopped but because your ears are no longer calibrated to receive it; the listener has adjusted the membrane of the world so that your particular range of hearing returns nothing but a low, warm hum. Then the light begins to dim—just for you. Your eyes are wet tissues, vibrating tissues, and the listener can tune those too, pulling the silver brightness back until the world is a gray, silent, motionless fog. One sense at a time, you are deleted from the recording.

By the end, you are lying in one of the shallow water-pockets, curled like the mosses but not filtering anything, just a warm, breathing obstruction in a bed of cold, clear water. You cannot see, hear, feel the wind, taste the mineral air. You are still alive, still thinking, still trapped in the dark pearl of your own skull, but to the listener you are already gone, a blank spot in the signal, a patch of tape that has been wiped. And then the final thing happens, the thing that leaves the bones. The listener performs a sort of immune response. A slow, peristaltic pressure rises through the stone beneath you. It is not violent. It is a gentle, even affectionate constriction, a drawing-in, as if the rock is trying to swallow you without breaking its surface. Over days, over weeks, the mineral flesh closes around your body and begins to digest not your flesh—the listener cannot digest flesh—but your duration. Your time. The part of you that changes. It leaches the processes out of you, the metabolic heat, the electric flutter of synapses, the tiny, ticking clock of cellular division. It metabolizes your temporality. And when it is done, you are left as a single, polished, finger-length needle of calcium, still marked with the four-line tally that the last one scraped into the last one’s bone, because they had time to do that, at the end. They always have time.

After that, the water carries the bone to a new pocket, a clean one, a shallow basin exactly the right depth. The moss nearby filters the water a little more clearly. The birds overhead scream without a gap. The listener returns to its equilibrium, its closed loop, its open-ended attention directed downhill toward the horizon that is impossibly, dangerously far, waiting for the true signal it was built to receive. It does not remember you. It has never remembered anything. The tally marks are not for it.

They are for you. If you are lucky, you will find one. You will understand what it means. You will try not to make a sound. But you already have, and somewhere deep beneath the slick, dark, breathing stone, a tympanum the size of a continent is beginning, with all the patience of a world without seasons, to register that you are there.
