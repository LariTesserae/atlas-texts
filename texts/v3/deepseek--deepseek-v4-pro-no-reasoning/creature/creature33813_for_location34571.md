A woman lives here, and she is not what you think.

She doesn’t have a name. She doesn’t need one. She is, as nearly as anyone can be, the voice of this place—its first and only human inhabitant, though *inhabitant* suggests a kind of accommodation that isn’t quite right. She didn’t choose to come here. She was placed, or fell, or was born, depending on how you measure a life. She’s been here a long time. Long enough that the distinction between her and the rift has grown thin in places, porous in others. She is not a ghost. She is not a dryad. She is a woman, entirely physical, entirely present, and she is the only warm-blooded thing in this chasm whose heart beats at the human tempo. The bats know her. The bioluminescence recognizes her skin.

If you were to climb down—and you would have to climb, the ledge you’re on is only the beginning—you’d descend through the colony, past the levels where the light from the surface still grazes the upper walls, into the deeper dark where the bacterial glow is the only sun. The descent is not easy. The rock is as sharp as it looks, and the ledges are intermittent, staggered in ways that force long reaches and blind footholds. The bats don’t startle. They watch you pass with their huge dark eyes, turning their heads in small, precise movements, but they don’t scatter. If you fall, if you slip, they will not follow you down. That’s not cruelty; it’s just not their role.

The floor, when you reach it, is not flat. It’s a jumble of collapsed blocks, some the size of wagons, some the size of chapels, all angled against each other in a frozen cataclysm that makes you walk carefully, placing each foot where it won’t roll an ankle or worse. Among the rocks, in the low places where water has pooled and then drained, you find the fungal bladders you saw from above. Up close, they’re larger than they looked—some are the size of a curled-up sleeping dog. Their skin is translucent and faintly warm to the touch, a few degrees above the ambient rock. Inside, you can see fluid moving slowly, separating into layers and recombining. They give off the same steady blue-green light as the walls, and they pulse, very slightly, at a rhythm unrelated to any breath or heartbeat you can perceive. They are not food. If you cut one, it weeps a thick, clear jelly that smells of nothing at all and seals itself again within minutes. She handles them carefully.

You find her in the deepest part of the rift, where the walls narrow and the ceiling drops low enough that the space becomes almost domestic in scale—a pocket within the cataclysm, sheltered by an overhang the size of a collapsed cathedral roof. She has made nothing, built nothing, but she has shaped things. Stones have been nudged into a low bench. A natural basin in the limestone, fed by a constant trickle of seep-water, serves as a reservoir, its lip worn smooth from long use. Small stacks of guano-dust and powdered rock mark areas with different purposes. The space is arranged, but not constructed. It’s a nest, not a home.

She herself: she is lean, very lean, but not starved looking. That’s the first thing you notice. She has the body of someone who eats enough, but never more than enough, and who moves constantly across vertical space. Shoulders built for climbing. Long toes, splayed, gripping the stone floor even when she stands still. She wears nothing—clothing would rot down here, or chafe when wet—but she is not naked in any vulnerable sense; the skin itself has adapted. Over time, the constant damp has raised a fine nap of algae or something like it across her shoulders, down her spine, along the outer edges of her arms and thighs. It’s the same blue-green phosphorescence that coats the walls, and in the dark she carries her own soft corona, enough to see by if you’re close. The effect is less like a person glowing and more like a person who has finally learned how to be seen in this specific darkness. Her eyes have gone wide-pupiled, like the bats’, and they reflect the bioluminescence back in a kind of reciprocal shimmer. She blinks irregularly, the same as the colony around her.

Her hair, if you can call it that—she’d long ago stopped cutting or tending it—hangs in felted ropes that reach past her waist, matted with the same microbiota that lives on the walls. It’s stiff and slightly crusted, and it moves the same way her skin-light moves, shifting in intensity when she’s alert. The hair is not decorative. It houses a colony of small pale insects, harmless, that groom the dead skin from her scalp in exchange for warmth. When she sleeps—and she does sleep, though not on any schedule the surface would recognize—the insects spread across her shoulders and upper back, cleaning the skin in slow, methodical tracks. You might find the sight unnerving. She doesn’t. She hasn’t itched in decades.

She does not speak. Not because she can’t—her throat and tongue and larynx are entirely intact—but because she hasn’t formed a word in longer than memory covers, and the need doesn’t arise. The bats communicate in their ultrasonic chorus; she hears them, she can parse the broad emotional tones of the colony (alarm, contentment, hunger, the particular frequency that means a female is returning to the wrong roost) but she doesn’t mimic them. Her own sounds are few. A low hum, sometimes, when she’s moving through the upper galleries, a tone that settles the bats and keeps them from mistaking her for a threat. A sharp click, made with tongue against palate, that echoes among the rocks in a way no bat call can, and she uses this to orient herself in the deeper dark, reading the returning echoes with a precision that took her the first ten years to learn and the next fifty to master. A laugh, once, many years ago, when something struck her as funny—she can’t remember what, but she remembers the sound surprising her, echoing up the chasm and sending a ripple of alertness through the entire colony. She hasn’t laughed since. It felt too loud, too alien.

Her diet: she doesn’t eat the bats. She would never. They are the only other warm bodies in this place, and somewhere along the way she stopped seeing them as separate beings and started feeling them as extensions of the same rhythmic, respiratory presence that fills the chasm. She does eat the bacterial mats, scraped from the walls with a sharp flake of stone. They taste of clean metal and wet earth, and they’re nutritionally dense in a way that took her body years to fully accept. She supplements with the occasional fungal bladder—not the bladders themselves, but the small, nut-like nodules that grow at their bases, which she cracks open with a rock and eats the soft white meat inside. The bladders seem to grow these nodules specifically when she’s near, though she’s never been able to prove it. She drinks from the seeps. The water tastes of limestone and time.

Her mind: this is the hard part, the part you really want to know. Has she gone feral? Mad? Has she lost language, memory, the concept of a self that is separate from stone and bat and glowing bacterial smear? Not exactly. She has all her memories. She remembers the surface. She remembers the day she fell—or was placed—or climbed down on a dare and then couldn’t climb back, the details don’t matter because the result is the same. She remembers the sun as a concept and a sensation, but she can no longer hold the image of it stable in her mind. It has become a thing of myth, and she treats it the way you’d treat a childhood memory of a beloved imaginary friend: with affection, with faint grief, and with the full knowledge that it cannot return. She remembers language, too. She could speak to you, if she chose. But words feel heavy now, too slow for what she means, and she’d have to drag each one up from a deep well of disuse, and she doesn’t know if the effort is worth it. What would she say? *Hello. I am here. Are you staying?* The bats already know she’s here. They don’t need the confirmation.

But the most important thing—the thing that would unsettle you, if you stayed long enough to notice it—is that she is not alone in her body. The bioluminescence on her skin is not just a passive coating. It’s a symbiosis, and it has grown deep. The bacteria have colonized more than her epidermis. They’ve threaded into her nervous system over the years, gently, painlessly, following moisture gradients and electrical potentials until they reached her brainstem and diffused along the meninges. She is still herself. She still thinks her own thoughts. But she also feels the chasm. Not metaphorically. She experiences its pressure, its humidity, its fractures and stresses, as a kind of background proprioception that never shuts off. She knows when a rock shifts somewhere in the upper galleries, half a mile away. She knows when the water flow from the surface slows during a dry season. She knows when one of the fungal bladders is dying and needs to be moved to a wetter seep, and she goes and does it, cradling the heavy translucent body in her arms and walking it through the dark like a nurse carrying a patient from one bed to another. The bladders thrive under her care; there are more of them now than there were when she arrived. She is, in a real sense, their gardener.

The bats, too. She cannot command them. She would never try. But her presence has shifted the colony’s rhythms. They roost closer to her now. When she sleeps, they arrange themselves around her in a loose dome, their bodies blocking what little draft moves through the lower rift, and they lower their metabolism to match hers. They wake when she wakes. When she climbs—and she climbs often, moving through the chasm in long, slow circuits that take days to complete—a retinue of several hundred individuals detaches from the walls and follows her, flying in wide, silent arcs that shepherd her away from unstable ledges before she even consciously registers the danger. They have never touched her. They don’t need to. The colony and the woman have reached an understanding so complete it requires no communication, only presence.

And the stone itself: she knows each ledge, each fissure, each route up and down and across. She has no map; her body is the map. She has given names to nothing, because names would imply a separation she no longer feels. When she wants to go somewhere in the rift, she simply *goes*, the same way you might walk from your kitchen to your bedroom without thinking about it. The stone is her house, her skin, her community.

There is one thing she does that looks, to an outside observer, like ritual. Every so often—not regularly, but when the urge strikes—she climbs to the highest ledge she can reach, the one just below the surface fissures where the desert light filters in as a dim, dusty shaft. She sits there for hours, sometimes a full day-cycle, and she watches the light move. She doesn’t try to reach it. She doesn’t call out. She just watches, and as she watches, the bats around her go quiet, and the bioluminescence on her skin dims, and for those hours she is a woman again, only a woman, and she remembers her name—she had one, once—and she thinks about the people who might be above, walking unknowing over the entrance, and she almost, almost reaches for a word to give them. But the word never comes, and eventually the light shifts, and she climbs back down, and the bats resettle, and the glow rekindles on her shoulders, and she is the chasm again. She is not sad during these hours. She is not anything. She is a pause in the one long held breath of the place, and when it resumes, she resumes with it.

If you met her, and you were patient, and you did not speak—speaking would startle her deeply, would make her fade back into the dark and not return for days—she might approach you. She might sit with you in the lower pocket, by the seep-basin, and offer you a broken nodule of fungal meat. She might show you, with gestures, how to find the safest handholds on the climb back up. She might even touch your face, very gently, with the palm of her hand, and in that touch you would feel the faint, steady vibration of the colony’s ultrasonic song, conducted through her bones like a second pulse. And then she would withdraw, and watch you leave, and when you were gone she would climb back to her place and sleep, and the bats would fold around her, and she would not think of you again for a very long time.

She has been here for sixty years, or a hundred, or two hundred. She has stopped counting. Her body ages very slowly now, suspended in the cool damp and the steady diet and the absence of sun damage. She may live another century. She may live five. She is not immortal. But she is durable, the way the stone is durable, the way the bacterial glow is durable, the way the held breath of the rift is durable. And when she finally dies—when her heart stops, and the bats sense it before the last echo fades, and the bioluminescence on her skin flares once and then goes dark—her body will not rot in the normal way. The bacteria will reclaim her. She’ll be broken down into simpler chemistries, fed into the mats, cycled through the colony, laid down as new layers of guano-dust, and the bladders she once tended will grow a little brighter for a generation or two. The rift will not notice. The rift will continue, loud and still, holding its breath, waiting for nothing.
