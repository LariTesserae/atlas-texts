In the oldest levels of the Mourn, where the city has been sealed and reopened and sealed again so many times that the walls read like the rings of a drowned tree, there lives a woman named Sedge.

She is not young. She has lived long enough that she has ceased to count the years in calendar time and instead measures her life by the movement of the stone. She knows that the west wall of her primary chamber has advanced nine inches since she first claimed it as a workshop, and that the small seep-spring in the corner—the one she drinks from—has shifted its mineral composition twice, from bitter to sweet to something she has no word for but recognizes as the taste of a particular stratum being breached far below. She has seen three full building-cycles in the district above her: the original tenement, its slow petrification and abandonment, its fifty-year tenure as a gantry preserve, and its eventual re-harvesting by the Extrusion Guild, who sounded their warning horns in the levels overhead for a month straight while Sedge went about her work, undisturbed.

She is a candlewright. This is an old and semi-respected trade in the Mourn, and it has nothing to do with wax or flame. Mourn-candles are biological lights—transgenic fungal bodies bred from the native blue-glowing organisms that pulse in the deep crevices, cross-woven with phosphorescent bacteria cultured in the mineral slurries of the factory levels. A Mourn-candle is a living thing, shaped into a column or a sphere or a delicate spiraling tower, encased in a permeable membrane that allows it to breathe but not to spread. It consumes the ambient moisture and the dissolved minerals in the air and produces a steady, cold, blue-green light. With proper care, it will outlive its owner. Some of the oldest candles in the Mourn cathedrals have been burning for four hundred years.

Sedge does not make the common candles that light the tram stations and the factory floors. Her work is custom, private, commissioned by Mourners who want something specific—a light tuned to the exact color temperature of early morning on the surface plains, where no Mourner has ever been but which they imagine with a desperate, inherited longing. A light that mimics the slow pulse of a human heartbeat, to soothe a child who has never known darkness and cannot sleep in the absolute black of a sealed room. A light that responds to sound, brightening when someone speaks and dimming in silence, so that conversation itself becomes a kind of illumination.

Her workshop occupies three chambers that were once a single large apartment in a building that was itself absorbed into a larger block during the fourth-cycle expansion. The front chamber is where she meets clients. It is sparsely furnished: a stone bench padded with layers of dried moss, a low table made from a reclaimed sable-panel, and a dozen of her finest candles arranged in niches carved into the living basalt. The light they cast is complex and layered, each candle tuned slightly differently, so that the room seems to breathe in slow, imperceptible chromatic shifts. To a client entering from the yellow glare of the factory-level corridors, Sedge's front room feels like walking into a pool of cool, still water.

The middle chamber is her culture room. It is warm—not warm by any surface standard, but warm for the Mourn, heated by the metabolic output of the hundreds of growing candles that line its walls on stone shelves. The shelves are old, cut into the basalt by some previous inhabitant, and the stone has since grown partially back, swallowing the shelf edges in smooth, organic-looking curves. The candles here are in various stages of development: some no larger than a fingernail, glowing faintly in their nutrient baths; others nearly mature, their membranes translucent, their internal structures visible as delicate, branching networks of blue light. Sedge moves among them with a quiet, unhurried attention, checking growth rates, adjusting mineral feeds, humming softly to the ones that seem to be struggling. She insists they can hear her. Whether this is true or merely a candlewright's superstition, the candles in her care bloom with unusual vigor and live decades longer than those produced by the guild factories.

The rear chamber is where she sleeps, and it is almost entirely dark. A single candle—her first, made when she was still an apprentice, small and unassuming—rests in a hollow by her bedroll. Its light is sufficient only to show the outline of her body beneath the layers of insulating fabric, and the slow, rhythmic rise and fall of her chest, and the small, six-legged shape that has crept into the hollow beside her to absorb the warmth.

Her name is Sinuous, and she is a skirl.

Sinuous is, by skirl standards, ancient. She has lived with Sedge for fourteen years, which is nearly three times the typical skirl lifespan. The other skirls in the district treat her with a deference that borders on religious, and they bring her offerings—choice insects, fragments of particularly good cradlewax—which she accepts with the weary grace of an empress receiving tribute from distant provinces. She no longer bothers with camouflage. Her skin, which in a wild skirl would constantly shift to match the surrounding textures, has settled into a permanent pattern: the dark, fine-grained gray of Mourn basalt, traced with thin white lines that exactly mirror the mineral veins in Sedge's workshop walls. When she lies still against the stone, she does not disappear; rather, she looks like a part of the stone that has detached itself for private purposes, a living fragment of the valley's geology.

She and Sedge communicate in a way that is not quite language. Sedge speaks to her in low, murmuring Mourner—the dialect of the deep levels, which has shed most of its consonants in favor of vowels and glottal stops and words that mean three things at once—and Sinuous responds with subtle shifts of posture, with the angle of her six legs, with the slow, deliberate blink of her nictitating membranes. An observer might call it a deep mutual understanding. A member of the Gantry Guild, who know more about the valley's strangeness than most, would call it something else entirely: a seep-bond, a rare phenomenon in which a Mourner and a native creature become, over years of proximity, partially attuned to each other's internal states. Sedge knows when Sinuous is hungry or cold or seeking attention. Sinuous knows when Sedge's arthritic hands ache before Sedge herself consciously registers the pain, and she will crawl up onto the woman's shoulders and press her warm, dry belly against the base of her neck, radiating a gentle heat that eases the stiffness from her joints.

They are, in the unspoken way of such things, each other's primary reason for continuing.

Sedge had a husband once. His name was Ravel, and he was a middle-level tram operator who ran the line between the Phosphor District and the South Extrusion Works. He was a quiet, methodical man who loved the order of the timetable, the precision of the switching mechanisms, the way the tram's iron wheels sang at exactly the same pitch on exactly the same curve every single run. He and Sedge met when she was still a journeyman candlewright, delivering a custom order to the tram master's office—a candle tuned to the specific orange-amber of the tram signal lights, so that the dispatchers could maintain their night vision while reading schedules. Ravel had looked at the candle, then at Sedge, and said, "It's the exact color of the emergency indicator on car forty-seven." It was, Sedge later realized, the most romantic thing anyone had ever said to her.

They lived together for twenty-two years in a small apartment three levels above the current workshop, in a building that has since been reclaimed by the stone. Ravel died in the second-cycle subsidence of '87, when a new extrusion fractured the main tram tunnel and sent three cars into a condensation chamber that had been mapped as solid basalt. Forty-one people died. Sedge received a pension from the Tram Guild and a small, smooth piece of sable-panel engraved with Ravel's name and operator number, which she keeps in the darkest corner of her sleeping chamber, where the light from her first candle does not reach.

She does not speak of him to clients. She does not speak of him to Sinuous, though the skirl knows. The skirl knows everything that Sedge feels, and when the grief rises—which it still does, less often now but no less sharply—Sinuous presses herself against Sedge's chest and pulses her chromatophores in the exact shade of amber-orange that once lit the dispatcher's office, and Sedge cries into her warm, stone-textured hide until the feeling passes.

It is mid-morning in the Phosphor District—not that morning means much in the deep levels, but the factory shifts have changed, and the distant hum of the grinders has shifted into the slightly higher pitch of the separation cycle—when the client arrives.

Sedge knows there is a client before she hears the knock, because Sinuous lifts her head from the stone bench, flicks her tongue out twice to taste the air, and makes the particular leg-gesture that means *stranger with money*. Sinuous's ability to judge a client's financial status is uncanny and has never been wrong. Sedge trusts it implicitly. She adjusts the moss padding on the bench, dims the candles slightly to create an atmosphere of contemplative exclusivity, and settles herself into her work chair—an old, salvaged tram seat mounted on a swivel base, which Ravel had installed for her thirty years ago.

"Come," she says.

The client is a young man, which is unusual. Most of Sedge's commissions come from established guildspeople or wealthy factory owners, people with decades of accumulated wealth and the specific, refined tastes that come with age. This man is barely thirty, by the look of him, with the pale, slightly damp-looking skin of someone who lives above the factory levels but below the light wells. He wears the practical, close-fitting clothes of a mid-level technician, but the fabric is too good, and the seams are reinforced with sable-thread, which is expensive. He has the look of someone who has recently come into money and is not yet entirely comfortable with it.

"Madam Candlewright," he says, and his voice betrays him further: educated, but with the faint, swallowed consonants of a deeper-level upbringing. He has risen. "My name is Kite. I've come about a commission."

Sedge gestures to the bench. He sits. Sinuous, who has remained motionless on her usual perch beside the door, blinks one eye slowly. Kite does not notice her. Most people don't.

"You've come to the right place," Sedge says. "What kind of light are you looking for?"

Kite hesitates. He reaches into his jacket and withdraws a small leather case, which he opens to reveal a fragment of something Sedge does not immediately recognize. It is stone, but not any stone she has seen in the Mourn. It is pale, almost white, with a fine, granular texture and tiny, glittering inclusions that catch the candlelight and scatter it into faint rainbows. She leans forward, interested despite herself.

"Where did you get this?" she asks.

"From the deep levels," Kite says. "Below the old cathedrals. Below the gantry preserves. There's a chamber—my team found it during a survey run. The stone there isn't basalt. It's this. And it's growing, the same way the basalt grows, but faster. Much faster. We had to leave before we could map it properly, but I took this sample."

Sedge does not touch the stone. Instead, she studies it from a careful distance, noting the way it seems to glow faintly even in the low light, a luminescence that is not biological but mineral, something inherent to the crystalline structure. She has heard rumors of white stone in the deepest levels, stone that grows in spirals and lattices instead of the blunt, lobed extrusions of basalt. The Gantry Guild speaks of it in their initiation rites, calling it "moon-marrow" and warning against its use. But rumors are rumors, and Sedge is a practical woman.

"What does this have to do with a candle?" she asks.

Kite closes the case. "I want you to make a light that matches this stone. Exactly this color. Exactly this quality. Not blue-green. Not amber. White, with the same inclusions, the same way of catching the eye." He pauses. "It's for my daughter. She's four. She's never seen natural light. None of us have. But I want her to know what white looks like. Real white. Not factory white. Stone white."

Sedge is quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above them, a tram rattles along its iron way, and the sound filters down through layers of stone and brick and concrete until it is no longer a sound at all, just a subtle tremor in the walls. Sinuous shifts on her perch, her legs making the faintest scratching sound against the basalt.

"The materials for such a candle would be... difficult," Sedge says finally. "The phosphors I would need are not the standard ones. I would have to cultivate a new strain of fungal substrate, something that can process a broader spectrum of minerals. The membrane alone would take months to grow to the right transparency. And I would need more of this stone. Not much. But some. Powdered, to introduce into the nutrient bath."

Kite nods. "I can get you the stone. Whatever you need. The survey team is going back in three weeks. I can bring back as much as you want."

"The cost—"

"Money is not a concern."

Sedge looks at him, this young man with his too-good clothes and his deep-level accent and his fragment of impossible stone, and she sees something she recognizes: the particular, focused intensity of a parent who has decided that their child will have something they themselves never had. She has seen it before, in clients who commission heartbeat-lights for anxious infants, or dawn-simulators for elderly parents who pine for a sun they've never seen. It is a kind of love that borders on desperation, and it is the most reliable currency in the Mourn.

"Three weeks," she says. "Bring me the stone, and I will begin the cultivation. The candle itself will take another four months to mature. It will not be cheap, and it will require maintenance. Your daughter will need to learn to care for it, or it will fade."

"She'll learn," Kite says. "She's a sharp one. Sharper than me."

After the client leaves, Sedge remains in her workshop for a long time, not moving. Sinuous descends from her perch and climbs into her lap, pressing her small, warm body against Sedge's stomach. The candlelight shifts and flickers, responding to some subtle change in the air currents that Sedge cannot feel but that the candles can, and for a moment the room is filled with a quality of light that is almost—almost—white.

"That was a foolish thing to promise," Sedge tells the skirl. "Moon-marrow. The Guild will not like it if they find out I'm working with their forbidden stone."

Sinuous offers no opinion. She simply curls tighter, her chromatophores cycling through their permanent basalt-gray, the thin white lines pulsing gently in time with her heartbeat. Outside the workshop, the Mourn hums its endless industrial hum. The stone grows. The water seeps. Somewhere in the deep levels, a gantry is dismantling a wall that a human built two hundred years ago, preparing the space for something new, or something very old.

In her sleeping chamber, Sedge lies down on her bedroll and stares into the darkness, where her first candle burns its faint, steady blue. She thinks about white light. She thinks about the way it might fill a room, how it might change the color of everything it touches. She thinks about a four-year-old girl seeing it for the first time, and what that might mean.

And then, because she is old and practical and has lived long enough to know that the valley does not care about human hopes, she thinks about the Gantry Guild's warnings, and the stone that grows faster than basalt, and what might happen if a candle made from such stone were to develop a will of its own.

Beside her, Sinuous pulses amber-orange in the darkness, the color of tram signals and emergency indicators and a love that has outlasted its object by three decades.

Sedge sleeps.
