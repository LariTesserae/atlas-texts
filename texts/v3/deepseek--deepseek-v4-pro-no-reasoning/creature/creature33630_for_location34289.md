You feel it before you see it—a rhythmic displacement in the water, a subtle pressure-wave that travels through the velvet heat and registers not in your ears, drowned as they are, but in the fine hairs on your arms and the back of your neck. The tower-builders feel it too. The nearest one freezes mid-saw, its dish-ears pinned flat against its skull, the obsidian flake held motionless against the bleeding reed. Its head swivels, not toward you, but toward the deeper channels where the fog lies thickest.

Then it begins to croon—a low, throaty pulse that you feel more than hear, a subsonic thrumming that travels through the water and up your legs. The sound is taken up by others, relayed from scaffold to scaffold, reed-clump to reed-clump, a wave of warning that ripples outward through the cacophony. The glass-rubbing frogs fall suddenly, ominously silent. Even the insects pause in their metallic droning, leaving a strange, expectant hole in the soundscape—a held breath before the plunge.

Something is coming through the mist.

It moves without disturbing the fog so much as parting it, and for a long moment, that is all you perceive: a shape of negative space, a corridor of clearer air pushing slowly through the haze. Then the phosphorescent sap catches it, limning its edges in pale green fire, and the shape resolves into a thing of impossible architecture.

It is, you understand with a slow, dawning vertigo, a single living creature. A mollusk of some ancient, unchecked lineage, its shell a coiled spiral that rises three times your height, a tapered helix of nacreous chambers that glimmer with their own internal light. The shell is not solid but semi-translucent, like a lantern carved from abalone, and within each whorled chamber, you can see the soft, shifting shadows of its organs—pulsing, glowing, alive. The whole structure is banded with ridges of darker material, growth-lines that speak of centuries, of silent, patient accretion in these volcanic waters.

But it is the flesh of the thing that transfixes you. It does not crawl on a muscular foot like the snails of gentler worlds. Instead, it rides the water on a rippling skirt of translucent mantle that undulates with hypnotic complexity, a living hovercraft that barely disturbs the surface. This skirt extends outward in all directions, a diaphanous platform as wide as the creature is tall, its edges fraying into thousands of fine, trailing tentacles that drift and test the water like the fringe of a jellyfish. They are white, these tentacles, a stark, luminous white against the black water, and they glow faintly from within—not with the green of the sap, but with a colder, bluer light, the color of deep-sea things that have never known the sun.

As the creature glides closer, you see that it is not alone atop its own body. Riding the broad, firm plateau of its coiled shell is an entire microcosm, a traveling ecosystem that the tower-builders treat with something between reverence and wariness. Smaller, flattened mollusks—its spawn, perhaps, or simply opportunistic hitchhikers—encrust the lower whorls, their tiny shells clicking softly as they graze on films of algae. A species of pale, blind salamander, all translucent skin and external gills like feathery red coral, nests in the crevices between chambers, their tadpole young wriggling in the thin film of moisture that always coats the great snail's back.

But it is the tower-builders' relationship with the creature that is most startling. As it passes beneath the nearest living scaffold—the one the family group was just reinforcing—they do not flee. Instead, the largest among them, an elder whose wet fur is streaked with silver and whose dish-ears are notched and scarred, clambers down from the woven reeds with a deliberate, almost ceremonial slowness. In its prehensile, multi-elbowed hands, it carries not a tool but an offering: a bundle of the youngest, most tender reed-shoots, their chartreuse stalks still furled and soft, their purple fronds not yet unfolded. They are bound with a twist of something fibrous, and they drip with the milky, glowing sap that the tower-builders seem to prize above all else.

The elder wades into the water until it is chest-deep, its huge ears swiveling constantly, tracking the snail's ponderous approach. When the fringe of trailing tentacles is almost upon it, the elder makes a sound—a complex, modulated whistle that rises and falls in a pattern you cannot parse—and gently places the bundle of shoots onto the water. It floats, buoyed by the sap's strange viscosity, a tiny raft of glowing green.

The tentacles find it. They curl around it, testing, tasting, and then with a graceful, fluid motion, the entire bundle is drawn beneath the water and under the rim of that undulating mantle. There is a soft, grinding sound—the creature's radula at work, perhaps—and the bundle is gone.

In return, something else happens. A shiver runs through the great snail's mantle, a muscular contraction that travels from its core to its fringe. And from between the coils of its shell, from a cleft you had taken for mere architecture, it extrudes something. An egg-case. A cluster of translucent, pearly spheres, each the size of a fist, bound together by a tough, gelatinous matrix. The elder takes it with hands that tremble slightly, holding it above the water like a sacred object, and begins the slow wade back to the scaffold.

The other tower-builders are already chittering with excitement, a percussive, clicking rhythm that spreads through the reeds. Young ones, their fur still a pale, unweathered gray, scramble down to meet the elder, their many-jointed arms reaching for the egg-case with eager, grasping fingers. The warning is over. The negotiation is complete. The symphony of the basin resumes, the frogs resuming their glassy circles, the insects their metallic droning, the tower-builders their percussive industry.

The great snail drifts on, unconcerned, its internal lights pulsing in a slow, steady rhythm like a heartbeat made visible. It is a living barge, a neutral power in this landscape of violent competition, trading its fertility for the tender shoots that only the tower-builders can harvest from the densest, most competitive reed-beds. A partnership, ancient and wordless, written in the exchange of glowing sap and pearly eggs.

As it passes you, the water around your thighs cools perceptibly—it carries a thermal wake of its own, cooler than the volcanic seep, a pocket of deep-water chill that speaks of slower, deeper currents far below the obsidian floor. One of its trailing tentacles brushes against your leg, feather-light and shockingly cold, and for a moment you feel something like recognition—not of you specifically, but of your presence as a new element, a new variable in this endless, churning equation.

Then it is past, and the fog closes behind it, and even its internal glow is swallowed by the mist. The weight of the sound returns, the damp wool blanket of the din. But you are changed, now. You have seen one of the true citizens of this place—not just a creature, but a landmark, a relationship, a node in a web of dik-dik deals struck in the roar and the steam and the neon green glow of the bleeding reeds.
