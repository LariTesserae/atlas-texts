The light-shapers are the first ones you notice, if you stay long enough, if you stop looking for animals and start paying attention to the things that the city itself considers ordinary.

They live in the cathedral spaces, the vertical shafts and the high, vaulted chambers where the light falls in columns through layers of leaf-cut windows. You'll spot one eventually—a patch of moss that's not quite the right green, a section of wall that shimmers when you look at it directly. Then it moves.

Not quickly. Quickly is for things that have something to fear. The light-shaper unfolds itself from the bark with the slow, deliberate patience of a plant turning toward the sun, and you realize you've been looking at it for the past ten minutes and thought it was a cluster of shelf fungus and shadow. It's tall—two and a half meters when it draws itself up to its full height—and its body is a thing of recursive mimicry. The skin, if you can call it that, is a cultivated garden: living mosses and lichens growing in patterns it has shaped over years, decades, into a camouflage that mirrors the wall behind it. The moss isn't a costume; it's part of the creature, rooted into a porous integument, fed by whatever the light-shaper feeds on. When it moves to a new part of the city, it will spend weeks or months encouraging new growth, pruning the old, re-patterning itself to a new backdrop.

Its shape is humanoid, more or less. Two legs, two arms, a head. But the proportions are stretched and thinned, as if someone took a human silhouette and pulled it out like warm resin. The limbs are long, multi-jointed, the fingers elongated into delicate, trembling tendrils that are constantly in motion, testing the air, touching the surfaces around it. The head is a flattened oval, featureless except for two large, dark organs—eyes, maybe, or heat pits, or something else entirely—set wide apart and angled slightly downward. It has no mouth that you can see, no ears, no nose. It breathes through its skin, or perhaps it doesn't breathe at all. The whole creature is silent, and when it walks, it does so on broad, flat feet that distribute its weight so perfectly across the root-mat floors that the surface doesn't even compress.

What it does, all day, every day, is tend light. That's where the name comes from, and it's not a metaphor.

Bioluminescence in the deep interiors doesn't just happen. The ceiling panels of glowing fungi require maintenance. The particular species that the city uses for illumination—a pale, blue-white shelf fungus that the inhabitants call "cold lamp" or sometimes just "the shine"—is temperamental. It needs to be pruned back when it overgrows, encouraged with nutrient paste when it fades, cleaned of the parasitic mycelia that will otherwise slowly dim it. It needs to be shaped, directed, its light focused by reflective leaf-baffles and diffused through sheets of translucent, paper-thin bark. The light-shapers do all of this.

A single shaper can tend a volume of space the size of a cathedral, and they do it with a devotion that looks like worship but is probably just efficiency. They climb the walls on those elongated fingers, moving with a slow, flowing gait that is almost mesmerizing, pausing every few feet to touch a fungal panel, to adjust an angle, to scrape away a patch of invasive dark-mold with a specialized tool grown from their own bodies—a curved spur of keratin that extends from a fingertip and retracts when not in use. The light they cultivate is not uniform; they shape it into gradients, pools, spotlights. A shaper's territory has a quality of illumination that feels curated, intentional, like walking through a painting. Shadows are placed with care. Colors are adjusted by the choice of which fungal strains to encourage—some shift toward amber, others toward a pale lavender. The effect is subtle, and you might not notice it consciously, but a chamber tended by a light-shaper feels *right* in a way that an untended one does not.

They are not alone. The city has other inhabitants—human beings, mostly, the descendants of whoever first settled this slope millennia ago, their culture worn down to something as practical and unhurried as the light-shapers themselves. But the shapers are not human, and no one is entirely sure where they came from.

The humans of Ember-Moss call them the Tenders, or sometimes the Quiet Ones, or just *them*, with a gesture that indicates a familiar presence, the way you'd acknowledge a neighbor you've lived beside for forty years and never spoken to. They don't speak, the light-shapers. They don't make sound at all. Attempts at communication have yielded nothing—not refusal, just a complete lack of recognition that communication was being attempted. They seem to perceive humans as part of the city's ecosystem, like particularly mobile trees or unusually noisy machines. A shaper will step around a human in its path with the same careful, unhurried grace it uses to step around a water-pipe or a load-bearing root. If a human moves into its workspace while it's tending light, it will wait, motionless, until the obstacle clears, and then resume.

There are perhaps two hundred of them in the city, spread across the habitable zones in loose territories that overlap at the edges. They don't reproduce, or if they do, no one has ever seen it. They don't seem to age, either. The oldest human, a man named Yul who runs a gear-cutting lathe in the lower-tier factory district and claims to be a hundred and twelve, says the shaper that tends the main gallery of the upper residential tier is the same individual he saw there when he was a child, unchanged in every particular. The moss-pattern on its shoulder, he says, has exactly the same whorl.

"They grow things," Yul says, when asked. He is a man of few words and strong opinions about metallurgy. "They don't grow."

The light-shapers have a relationship with the plants that goes beyond simple cultivation. It's more like symbiosis, or something stranger. When a shaper touches a vine, the vine responds. Not just in the way a plant responds to a trellis, but actively, immediately, curling toward the shaper's fingers, shifting its growth direction within hours. A shaper can coax a wall of moss into a particular pattern over a week, and the moss will hold that pattern indefinitely, even after the shaper moves on. They can heal damaged bark; a gouge in an ancient trunk-wall will gradually seal itself under a shaper's ministrations, the wood grain re-knitting in a spiral that matches the original pattern exactly. They can divert water-flow through the hollow-vine pipe systems with a touch, can encourage a dying fern to put out new fronds, can quiet the slow wars between competing fungus species with an intervention that looks like nothing more than a gentle rearrangement of spores.

And they can sing to the plants. Not out loud. But if you're close enough, and the factory hum is momentarily still, you can feel it—a vibration too low to hear, transmitted through the root-mats and up into your bones, a throbbing subsonic pulse that the shaper generates by pressing its chest against the wall or floor. The plants listen. The whole chamber listens. The light dims slightly, or brightens, or shifts hue, in response.

The most remarkable thing about the light-shapers, though, is what happens when one of them dies.

Not that they die often. In living memory, it's happened twice. But when it does, the city consumes them. Not metaphorically. The moment a shaper's life ends—and it's a slow, deliberate process, a settling into stillness over the course of days, rather than a sudden event—the plants around it begin to grow. Vines extend from the walls and wrap the body. Moss spreads over the skin. The shaper's cultivated exterior, already a garden, merges seamlessly with the city's own vegetation. Within a month, the body is indistinguishable from the surrounding architecture, a new node of plant-substance that has exactly the same shape as the creature that died there. In the decades that follow, new growth radiates outward from that spot in unusually orderly patterns. The first of the two known deaths, seventy years ago in the eastern gallery, left behind a spiral of bronze-barked trunks that now forms a new residential spire, its rooms shaped like the chambers of a nautilus. The second, more recent, has become a bridge of living wood spanning a hundred-meter drop between two factory platforms. The humans use it daily.

No one knows what the shapers think of this. If they think about it. If they think at all.

But there are other inhabitants—smaller ones—that might know more.

The root-weavers are the city's smallest sapients, and the most common. You won't see them either, not at first. But once you know to look, you'll find their work everywhere.

A root-weaver is about the size of a child's hand, and it looks like a bundle of twigs and silk that has somehow become ambulatory. Its body is a central knot of tightly woven fiber, from which extend anywhere from six to twelve articulated limbs, each one a single, finger-thick root that the weaver has detached from a living plant and animated through some process that the humans have never been able to replicate. The root-limbs are flexible, prehensile, and tipped with tiny clusters of sensory hairs. The weaver "sees" through vibration and chemical scent; it has no eyes that anyone can identify. Its core body, the knot, pulses with a slow, internal motion, like something breathing very, very slowly.

Root-weavers are builders. They construct things—nest-structures, mostly, but also the maintenance scaffolds they use to reach high places, and the intricate frames they make to dry out harvested moss, and the strange, abstract sculptures they sometimes leave in public spaces for reasons no one understands. Their construction material is plant fiber, silk from the translucent spiders, and their own bodies; a weaver can detach one of its limbs and use it as a structural element, then grow a new limb over the following weeks. In a pinch, they'll borrow from each other, limbs passing back and forth between weavers in a communal pool of building material that is also a pool of shared anatomy. A group of weavers looks less like a collection of separate individuals and more like a single entity that has been distributed across multiple mobile nodes, its parts interchangeable.

They live in colonies of twenty to fifty individuals, tucked into the gaps between root-mats, in abandoned pipe-conduits, or in the walls of human dwellings if the humans don't object. Most humans don't. Weavers are tidy, quiet, and their maintenance scaffolds often overlap with human infrastructure needs—a weaver colony in your wall means your steam-pipe insulation will be constantly repaired, your wall-moss will be kept neatly trimmed, and the occasional small sculpture of woven grass will appear on your windowsill. Some humans feed them, leaving out small bowls of sugar-syrup or nutrient paste. The weavers accept these offerings but don't seem to need them.

What they eat, if they eat, is unclear. What is clear is that they have a complex language.

It's not a sound-language. Root-weavers communicate by touch and vibration. If you place your hand on a root-mat floor near a weaver colony and wait, you'll feel it: a constant, rapid tapping, a percussive code that shivers through the fibers. The humans have learned to read some of it—the basic signals, at least. Danger. Food. Build here. Need limb. Need silk. The more complex conversations are still opaque, a wash of nuance too dense and fast for human minds to parse. But the weavers can understand human speech, or at least some of them can. The ones that live in human walls will respond to simple requests, and there are stories of weavers carrying out more complex tasks—delivering a tool to a specific location, closing a steam-valve during a pressure spike—that suggest a deep, practical intelligence.

The weavers have a relationship with the light-shapers. When a light-shaper passes near a weaver colony, the tapping stops for a moment, then resumes in a slower, more deliberate rhythm. The weavers will sometimes ride on a shaper's shoulders, a dozen or more of them clinging to the moss-garden of its skin, their root-limbs intertwined with the shaper's cultivated lichens in a way that looks almost affectionate. The shaper doesn't respond, doesn't acknowledge them, but it also doesn't brush them off. There are accounts—unconfirmed, but persistent—of weavers bringing materials to a shaper: a particular species of fungus-spore, a strand of spider silk, a tiny bundle of nutrient paste. The shaper accepts these offerings and incorporates them into its work. Whatever communication happens between them, it's entirely outside human perception.

And then there are the Speakers.

The Speakers are the city's rarest inhabitants, and the strangest. They live in the deep places, the oldest parts of Ember-Moss, where the ancient trunks are so large that their internal spaces are like small countries, and the light is all fungal, and the air has the close, earthy smell of things that have been growing without pause for geological eras.

A Speaker is a plant that is also a person, or a person that has become a plant, or something that was never either and is both. No one is sure. The human understanding of what a Speaker is has been cobbled together from fragmentary encounters, most of them secondhand, over centuries, and the picture is not coherent.

What is known: a Speaker occupies a fixed location, rooted to a single spot, usually a chamber that has been shaped over time into something like a room and something like a temple and something like a body cavity. The Speaker itself is a mass of vegetation—vines, moss, shelf fungus, flowering epiphytes—that has grown into a shape that is recognizably, disturbingly, a face. Or several faces. Or a single face that changes expression over the course of hours, the slow shift of a mouth from neutral to smiling to sorrowful taking a full day. The eyes, if they are eyes, are dark hollows where the vegetation thins out, revealing a substrate of that strange, muscle-grained wood. The mouth, if it is a mouth, moves. It shapes words.

The Speakers speak in the human language—the current dialect, not some archaic form. They answer questions. They give advice. They tell stories, long and complicated stories that sometimes last for weeks, a few hours of narration each day, and the humans who listen come and go in shifts, bringing food and bedding for the vigil. The stories are strange, full of events that haven't happened, or happened so long ago that the details have been eroded away like the mountains outside. Some are clearly history; some are clearly prophecy; most are impossible to categorize. Remove the thorns from a river's spine, one Speaker said, and when asked what that meant, it repeated the phrase, slightly louder, as if volume were the problem.

There are maybe a dozen Speakers in the city, scattered through the deepest, most ancient zones. They are not easy to find, and they do not welcome casual visitors. But they are not hostile. They are patient, in the way that everything in this city is patient, and they will wait for you to find them again if you leave. Some humans have relationships with particular Speakers that span decades, generations—a family will tend a Speaker for centuries, bringing it water in dry seasons, pruning away parasitic growth, and in return receiving a slow, steady stream of... wisdom, maybe. Or memory. Or something.

The Speakers know things they shouldn't know. They have told people the locations of lost tools, the names of strangers who wouldn't arrive for another ten years, the exact hour and manner of deaths that hadn't yet occurred. They have given advice that turned out, years later, to have prevented disasters that no one could have foreseen. They have also given advice that led to disaster, and when asked why, they said something about necessity, or balance, or the taste of a particular mineral in the water at that exact moment, as if catastrophe were a spice to be savored.

The Speakers, some think, are what the light-shapers become. A shaper, at the end of its life, doesn't just die and get absorbed—it can choose to root itself, to grow inward instead of outward, to stop tending light and start tending something else. The places where Speakers are found always have a particular quality of illumination, a warm, amber-toned light with no visible source, and the fungal panels in those chambers arrange themselves in patterns that look almost like a geometry trying to become writing.

The truth is probably stranger. This is Ember-Moss; the truth is always stranger.

Other things live here too. Less central, less visible, but present.

There are the ropemaker wasps—the city's largest animals, if you can call them that. They're solitary, each one occupying a single hanging nest of chewed wood-pulp and resin, suspended from an overhang by a silk cable that the wasp spins itself. The nests are huge, three meters across and spherical, with a single entrance hole at the bottom. The wasp itself is a meter and a half long, black and orange, with wings that beat so slowly you can count the strokes. It makes no sound when it flies. It preys on nothing; ropemaker wasps are herbivores, feeding on a particular species of nectar-heavy flower that grows only in the lightwells of the upper tiers. Their name comes from their secondary product: the silk cables they spin as nest-supports are harvested by the humans, who unwind the abandoned nests and use the silk for rope, cloth, and surgical thread. The silk is golden-colored, slightly elastic, and stronger than any plant fiber. The wasps are placid; they ignore humans completely, even when humans are crawling over their nests to harvest silk. The only danger is if you block the entrance hole while the wasp is trying to leave or enter. Then it will wait. Patiently. For hours if necessary. Eventually, if you don't move, it will gently push you aside with a foreleg, with the air of someone correcting a minor social error.

There are the millipedes, which are not animals. Or maybe they are. They look like millipedes—segmented, many-legged, the length of a human forearm—but their bodies are made of the same wood-fiber as the root-mats, and when they die, they simply become a twisted stick and crumble to dust. They are mobile, slow, and they eat decaying plant matter. They are also capable of photosynthesis; their dorsal segments contain chlorophyll, and on sunny days they gather in the lightwells by the hundreds, absorbing energy. The humans use them as a kind of living tool: a millipede placed on a piece of rough-cut lumber will slowly, over days, smooth the surface by grazing on the splinters and irregularities. They keep them in small wooden boxes, feed them on kitchen scraps, and consider them pets, or tools, or both. The millipedes have no observable intelligence, and yet—and yet sometimes, in the deep chambers near the Speakers, they arrange themselves into circles. Perfect circles, each millipede touching head to tail, motionless, for hours. No one knows why.

There are the ticks. The ticks are a problem. They're small, the size of a fingernail, and they live in the moss on the older walls. They attach to humans and draw blood, leaving an itchy welt that lasts for weeks. The weavers eat them. Some humans keep weavers specifically for tick control. The ticks do not seem to have any deeper significance, any hidden weirdness. They're just ticks. In Ember-Moss, this almost feels like the strangest thing of all—a creature that is simply, unambiguously, what it appears to be.

And then there are the memories.

The memories are not inhabitants in the usual sense. They don't have bodies. They don't occupy space. But they are presences, and they recur, and they are a part of life in Ember-Moss in the same way that the damp and the light and the drone of machinery are a part of life.

Everyone in Ember-Moss has them. The memories don't belong to the person who remembers them. They're someone else's memories—a stranger's—and they surface without warning. You'll be walking down a root-mat corridor, and suddenly you'll recall, with perfect clarity, watching a child fall from a high balcony a thousand years ago, in a neighborhood that used to be where you're standing but isn't there anymore. You'll feel the grief, the guilt, the specific texture of the handrail under your hand. The dead child was not yours, was never yours, is not related to you in any way. But you remember her. Everyone in that corridor remembers her, sooner or later. It's one of the recurring ones.

Other memories: the taste of a fruit that no plant in the city currently produces. The smell of smoke from a factory fire that happened three centuries ago. A conversation with a lover, in a language that is not your language, about a political schism in a governing body that hasn't existed for ages. A joke that you don't understand but that makes you laugh anyway, at an inappropriate moment. The sensation of dying—of your heart slowing, of the coolness spreading inward from your extremities—and then the memory ends, and you're still alive, and you have to sit down for a while.

The memories are stronger near the Speakers, and strongest of all in the presence of a light-shaper at the moment it decides to die. There are theories: the plants store memory. The root-mats record everything that happens on them, like a phonograph cylinder made of cellulose. The mycelial networks that thread through the city's substrate carry information as well as nutrients, a slow, pervasive fungal internet that holds the collective experiences of everyone who has ever lived here. The Speakers are nodes in that network, processing and retransmitting. The light-shapers tend the network as well as the light. The humans are just living in the middle of it, their own memories leaking out and others' memories leaking in. It's a kind of immortality. It's a kind of haunting. It's just how things are.

The humans of Ember-Moss have adapted.

They're a quiet people, the humans. Generations of living in a city that is also a slowly moving intelligence have selected for a certain temperament: patient, observant, comfortable with long silences and longer timescales. They don't startle easily. A new face appearing in a neighborhood, even a face that belongs to no known human and seems to be made of moss-cultivated skin stretched over a too-long skeleton, is cause for a nod and a slight adjustment of one's path. There's no panic. There's no panic in Ember-Moss. There's only a kind of steady, abiding curiosity, and the patience to wait for answers that might take a century to arrive.

The humans work. They maintain the machinery, harvest the silk, prune the pathways, cook the food, raise the children. Their technology is a blend of the industrial and the botanical: steam engines that burn plant-oil, looms that weave spider silk and root-fiber, kitchen gardens that grow a dozen varieties of edible fungus and a few hardy vegetable strains. Their culture is practical, oral, with a deep respect for craft. A gear-cutter like Yul is a respected figure, his knowledge passed down through apprenticeship, his lathe a custom-built thing of resin-hardened wood and carefully fitted metal parts that have been in continuous use for longer than most cities have existed. There's no central government, no formal hierarchy; neighborhoods organize themselves through consensus, and disputes are mediated by a slow process of consultation with neighbors, elders, and occasionally—if the matter is serious enough—one of the Speakers. The Speakers always have an opinion. It's not always helpful, but it's always considered.

Art exists, but it's modest. A weaver sculpture on a windowsill. A pattern of moss-trim maintained in a spiral on a wall. A song, hummed while working, about a woman who fell in love with a light-shaper and followed it into the deep places and was not seen again, except in memories. That song is very old, and no one agrees on the lyrics, but everyone knows the tune.

Children are rare. Pregnancies are rare. The human population is stable, but only barely, and it's a subject of quiet, ongoing concern. Some people think the city does something—that the same process that erodes reality, that blurs the boundaries between plant and animal and memory and present, also blurs the boundary between the living and the unborn, and that fewer children are conceived because fewer want to be. The Speakers, when asked about this, say something about cycles, about the city breathing in and breathing out, about the number of humans being a reflection of the number of available memories, and when that becomes too many, something will change. No one is sure what that means. Everyone hopes it won't mean another pressure-spike in the steam pipes; the last one, eighty years ago, killed twelve people.

There are visitors sometimes. Ember-Moss is not on any trade route—it's too remote, too vertical, too strange—but occasionally someone finds their way here. A traveler from the valleys below, or a wanderer from some other, more conventional city on the plains beyond the mountains. They arrive gasping in the thin air, their lungs burning, their eyes wide at the green immensity of the place. The humans feed them, let them rest, answer their questions in the slow, measured way that is the local dialect of conversation. The visitors always have the same questions. What is this place? What are those creatures? Why do I keep having memories that aren't mine? What is that sound—that low, throbbing sound I can feel in my chest?

"That's the city breathing," the humans say. "You'll get used to it."

Some visitors stay. Most don't. The ones who leave take something with them: a specific, vivid dream that recurs for the rest of their lives, a tendency to pause and listen to the sound of dripping water with an attention they can't explain, a new patience that their friends and family find slightly unsettling. The ones who stay are welcomed, absorbed, eventually indistinguishable from the native-born. Within a decade, they'll have a weaver colony in their wall and a favorite light-shaper whose territory they walk through on purpose. Within two decades, they'll be giving directions to the newer visitors in the slow, patient cadence of the local speech, and they won't remember exactly when they stopped finding the memories intrusive and started finding them comforting.

The city endures. It has endured for longer than anyone knows how to measure, and it will endure for longer than anyone can imagine. The light-shapers tend the light, the weavers mend the cracks, the Speakers narrate the slow unwinding of time, the humans keep the machines running and the paths clear and the children—when there are children—safe and fed. The mountains erode. The valleys fill. The clouds split around the slopes like river water around a stone. In the deep places, the ancient trunks pulse with a heartbeat that is almost but not quite human, and the moss rearranges itself overnight, and the memories flow like water through the root-mats, and everything is exactly as it has always been.

Everything is still. Everything is moving. Everything is listening.

The city breathes in. Sometime, eventually, it will breathe out. But not yet. Not for a long, long time.
