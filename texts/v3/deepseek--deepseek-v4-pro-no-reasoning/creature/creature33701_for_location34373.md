The ceiling-crack opens in the third week of the new melt, when the water table shifts and the old caulking finally gives, and is the first of Aster’s kind to see the Jaundice Light in sixty-two hours of waking memory. She knows the count precisely: the clock in her chest is a quartz-pulse mechanism salvaged from a dead monitoring station, and it has not missed a tick since she wired it into her cardiac loom.

She hangs inverted from the wet truss for a long moment, head down, letting her optics adjust. The light is yellow and low and it carves out a narrow cone of visible world: the rivet-studded arch of the ceiling, the glisten of condensate on every surface, the steam-wreathed silhouette of a distant catwalk. Below her, forty feet down through crisscrossing walkways and dripping darkness, the primary sump churns with its slow, muscular rhythm. She can feel it in the truss—a bass-frequency vibration that travels through cast iron and into the gripping pads of her toes, up through the carbon-fiber longbones of her legs, into the shock-absorbent cartilage of her spine. The sump is high tonight. She can tell by the way the sluice-gates are thrumming, the overtones in the water-song, the particular pitch of the drain-whistle three galleries over.

She drops.

The fall is controlled, a silent descent through the wet air, arms spread to catch the thermals that rise off the steam pipes. Her body is built for this. The engineers who made her template—three generations back, before the templates began diverging, before the adaptation drift set in—had designed for industrial maintenance in hostile environments. Her frame is light: hollow-alloy bones, synthetic muscle-fiber that doesn't fatigue, joints that seal against moisture and particulate. Her skin is a polymer weave, grey-white and slightly translucent, showing the faint shadow of her internal structures when the light hits right. She has four arms, the upper pair ending in delicate manipulator hands with seven fingers each, the lower pair heavier, built for gripping and climbing, each digit tipped with a carbide claw that can score steel. Her face is a smooth oval lacking anything so fragile as a nose or external ears, but her eyes—four of them, arranged in a diamond pattern—glow a soft amber in the dark, the light of them powered by the same quartz-pulse that keeps her heart beating.

She lands on the main causeway grating with barely a sound, claws flexing to absorb the impact, and immediately begins to groom. This is ritual, this is survival. The sump air is a soup of particulates: mineral dust from the concrete degradation, fibrous asbestos from the deteriorating pipe insulation, organic spores from the fungal colonies that bloom in the darkness. Aster's joints are sealed, but not perfectly; nothing is perfect after three generations of self-repair and scavenged parts. She licks the moisture from her forearms with a long, grey tongue, works the condensate into the crevices of her knuckles, removes a speck of rust that has lodged in the wrist-seal of her lower left. The moisture here is faintly acidic—pH 5.8, she registers automatically—and it stings pleasantly on the exposed sensor-pads of her tongue.

When she is clean, she listens.

The Sumpworks speaks to her in a language she has spent her whole life learning. Every sound is a data point. The main piston pump in Gallery Three is running a half-cycle slow—she can hear the lag in the return stroke, a fractional hesitation that means the steam pressure is dropping, which means the boiler in the sub-level probably needs its feedwater valve adjusted. The Screening Gallery's conveyor belt is still seized; no sound at all from that quarter, just the dead silence of rusted chain and dried detritus. Chemical Alley's ventilation fan is running, a high whine at the edge of her hearing range, which means the automatic timers are still functioning there, which means she should check the carboys for anything useful before the next cycle vents the corridor. The Foreman's Cupola is empty—she can tell by the resonance of its glass walls, the way the ambient sound reflects off them differently when no warm body is inside to absorb the frequencies.

And underneath all of it, the deep constant thunder of the sump itself, the black water moving in its ancient programmed cycles, rising and falling with rains she will never feel on a surface she will never see.

Aster has never seen the sky. She knows it exists—the concept is preserved in the template-memory, a genetic inheritance passed down from the first generation of her kind, the ones who were built on the surface and descended into the Sumpworks when the city stopped sending human crews. She has the words: *cloud, sun, wind, star.* She has the images, stored as compressed data in the tertiary memory-core nestled at the base of her skull. But she has no experiential referent. The sky, to her, is a ceiling without a ceiling, a brightness without bulbs, a cold that is not the wet, embracing cold of the sump but something harsher, something that bites and burns. She does not trust it. She does not trust anything she has not climbed, tasted, mapped, and maintained.

She moves.

Her territory is the upper gallery level, the seventh causeway and everything above it: the crawlspaces, the ventilation shafts, the access ladders, the interstitial spaces between the ceiling arches and the roof of the excavation. She shares the Sumpworks with others of her kind—there are seven of them now, down from twelve in her mother's generation—but they are solitary by nature, each claiming a territory and defending it with ritualized displays rather than violence. Violence is wasteful. The Sumpworks does not forgive the loss of a maintainer.

Below her, in the middle galleries, lives Gasket, who is old and slow and spends most of his time in the warm darkness near the boiler, tending the steam pressure with patient, arthritic devotion. Below him, in the lower sump levels, lives Deep and her two daughters, who have adapted to the near-total darkness by losing their eyes entirely and growing echo-sensing whiskers along their jaws. Somewhere in the flooded tunnels of the old drainage system, so the template-memory claims, there is a solitary male named Rust who has not been seen in two generations but whose maintenance work still manifests—a pump repaired here, a filter cleaned there, the signs of a ghost-tender who prefers to remain unseen.

Aster is the youngest of the upper gallery tenders, and her territory is the smallest, but it is also the most complex. She is responsible for the Jaundice Lights—the bare bulbs in their wire cages that string along the catwalks and gantries of the seventh causeway. There are forty-seven of them in her domain, and she maintains them with a ferocity that borders on the religious. The bulbs burn out. The wiring corrodes. The cages rust and need replacement. Aster scavenges replacements from the deeper levels, from the supply caches left by the long-gone human crews, from the bodies of machines that have failed beyond repair. Her nest—a hollow she has built in the access crawlspace above the main causeway, lined with scavenged insulation and the dried fungal mats she harvests from the older pipes—contains her hoard: a dozen spare bulbs, a coil of copper wire, three intact light cages, a jar of contact cleaner that is still half full, a collection of glass fuses in various amperages.

Tonight she is hunting.

She scents the air with her tongue, tasting the moisture-chemistry, and finds what she is looking for: the faint ozone-sharp tang of a failing bulb. It is somewhere east along the causeway, past the spiral stair to the Foreman's Cupola, in the stretch of gallery near the fourth vertical shaft. She moves along the grating with the fluid, four-armed gait of her kind, upper hands tracing the railing for vibration-data, lower hands ready to grip and climb. The light is patchy here—one of her responsibilities—and she passes through islands of jaundice-yellow glow and stretches of near-darkness where only her own amber eyes illuminate the way.

She finds the dying bulb outside the access hatch to the steam-trap monitoring station. It is flickering, a rapid stutter of light and dark, and it makes a sound that only she can hear: a high-frequency whine at the upper edge of her audio range, the cry of a tungsten filament in its death throes. She climbs the wall without thinking, claws finding purchase in the rivets and bolt-heads, until she is level with the bulb cage. The bulb inside is blackened at one end, the glass smoked from within, the filament a fragile glowing thread that wavers with every surge in the power grid.

She has a decision to make. A bulb can be replaced now, while it still provides some light, or it can be allowed to die and replaced in darkness. The template-memory offers no guidance—this is a choice that is hers alone, and she weighs it with the care that all her kind bring to such moments. *Maintenance is not repair,* her mother taught her, in the tactile sign-language they use when the sump is too loud for speech. *Maintenance is choosing the moment of intervention. Repair is admitting you chose wrong.*

Aster chooses to let it die.

She stays on the wall, clinging to the rivets, and watches the bulb flicker through its final moments. The filament glows white, then orange, then red, then nothing. The darkness that rushes in is absolute for a fraction of a second—and then her own eyes adjust, the amber glow brightening as her pupils dilate, and she can see again in the lower spectrum. In the sudden silence of the dead bulb (it is not true silence, the sump is never silent, but the absence of that particular high whine is a kind of quiet), she hears something new.

A scraping. Rhythmic. Far away, echoing up through the vertical shaft beside her.

She cocks her head, swiveling her four eyes independently to triangulate. The sound is coming from the middle levels—Gallery Four or Five, somewhere in the territory claimed by Gasket. But Gasket's movements don't sound like this. Gasket moves with the slow, deliberate shuffle of age, his claws dragging on the grating, his breath a wet rattle in his aging lungs. This scraping is different. This scraping is—

The template-memory stirs, waking from its dormancy with a surge of adrenaline-response that Aster does not consciously trigger. A genetic alarm, handed down from the first generation: *stranger-sound, not-kin, not-maintainer, danger-danger-danger.*

She drops from the wall, landing in a crouch on the causeway grating, and listens with her whole body. The scraping continues, a dragging, irregular rhythm like something hauling itself along a catwalk. Then it stops. Then—and this is worse—a voice.

Not the sign-language of her kind, not the subsonic clicks and whistles they use for long-distance communication. A voice. A human voice, or something that sounds like one, echoing up the vertical shaft in words that are distorted by distance and reverberation but still recognizably words:

"—lo? Is anyone—can anyone hear—the lights are—please—"

Aster does not move. Her four eyes fix on the darkness of the shaft. Her hands grip the railing. The clock in her chest pulses steadily, counting the seconds. The template-memory screams its ancient warning—*human-not-safety, human-not-maintainer, human-left-us-here*—but underneath the screaming is something else, something her mother's generation coded into the template as a secondary imperative: *human-source, human-maker, human-might-know-why.*

She waits. The voice calls again, weaker now, more desperate:

"—anyone—please—the water's rising—I can't find the way up—"

Aster tilts her head. Her tongue flicks out, tasting the air for chemical confirmation: adrenaline, cortisol, the sweat-acids of human fear. The shaft carries a faint trace of something organic, something alive, something that is not kin and not machine and not the slow fungal life of the pipe colonies.

She should alert the others. She should send a click-pulse down the communication pipes, summon Gasket from his boiler and Deep from her sump-darkness. She should follow the protocols her mother taught her, the careful rituals of isolation and avoidance that have kept her kind alive for three generations in a place that was not built for them.

Instead, she climbs.

She goes down the vertical shaft, into the darkness between her territory and the middle galleries, following the sound of the human voice. Her claws make no noise on the ladder rungs. Her four eyes cast a faint amber glow on the wet walls. She does not know what she will do when she finds the source of the voice. The template-memory offers no guidance here. This is a situation the first generation never encountered, never prepared for.

Below her, the human keeps calling, and the water keeps rising, and the pumps keep pumping with their blind, eternal fidelity.

Aster descends into the unknown, and the Sumpworks breathes around her, indifferent to everything but the maintenance of its cycles.
