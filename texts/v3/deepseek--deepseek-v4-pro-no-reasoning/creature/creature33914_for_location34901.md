In the space between one clump of charcoal fronds and the next, something gathers itself.

It has no name. It has never needed one. Over the span of time that this place measures—a span that would render geological ages into heartbeats—it has occasionally been aware of itself in ways that approach what might be called thinking, but thinking is not its primary function. Its primary function is listening. And what it listens to is the hum.

The hum is not one thing. The creature—if the word must be used—understands this in a way that has no equivalent in language. The hum is a chord, and the chord is made of layers, and each layer is a voice, and each voice is a process unfolding somewhere in the vast machinery of the world. There is the deep bass tone of the ridgeline-organ, the one that travels through stone and arrives in the creature's body as a slow, tidal pressure. There is the midrange drone of the mist-cycles, the evaporative rhythm that breathes moisture from stone to air to frond to air to stone again. There is the high, thin whistle of the bioluminescent pulses as they travel from clump to clump, carrying whatever information light carries in a place where light is not a given but a signal. And beneath all of these, almost imperceptible, there is the subsonic rumble of the predators moving in the deep dark, a sound the creature feels in the microfilaments of its feet long before the pressure-change arrives.

The creature listens to all of it, all the time. It has been listening for longer than the deposition stone has been stone.

Its body is built along no recognizable axis. It is not insect, not mammal, not reptile, not anything that taxonomy could hold. It is, perhaps, a kind of fungus, if fungus could grow bones and then decide the bones were optional. Its core mass is a dense, fibrous knot no larger than a clenched fist, tucked into a crevice in the deposition stone at the exact center point of the negative space between three vegetation clumps. From this core extend the filaments: hundreds of them, some as fine as spider silk, some as thick as cable, each one a different length and texture, each one tuned to a different frequency of the hum. They spread across the bare stone in a radial web that covers nearly forty feet in every direction, so fine and so flat against the ground that they are indistinguishable from the hairline cracks in the rock. The creature is not hidden. It is simply too thin to be seen.

The filaments are its ears, its mouth, its hands. Through them it feels the micro-tremors of approaching fauna long before the fauna knows it is approaching. Through them it tastes the mineral shifts in the stone when the mist falls and evaporates. Through them it drinks the slow, ambient warmth of the ground and converts it into the energy required to keep listening. It has no need to hunt. It has no need to eat in any conventional sense. The hum feeds it, in a fashion, by providing a constant stream of vibrations that the filaments translate into a low-grade electrical current. The creature is a living piezoelectric crystal. It is powered by sound.

Its consciousness—again, if that word can be stretched to fit—is distributed across the filament network. There is no central brain, no processing node. Instead, each filament carries a portion of the sensory load, and the creature's sense of self is the sum of those portions, a composite awareness that shifts and flickers depending on which filaments are most active at any given moment. When the bioluminescent pulse passes through its territory, the creature's self migrates toward the filaments that catch the light, and for a few seconds it becomes a creature of vision, seeing the world in terms of brightness and shadow and the slow cursive of the vegetation clumps. When the predators pass in the deep dark, the self contracts inward, pulling back from the perimeter filaments toward the core, and it becomes a creature of pure vibration, feeling only the stone and the fear of the smaller things that scatter. When the mist falls, it becomes a creature of moisture and chemistry, tasting the rust-and-salt breath of the ridgeline-organ as it condenses on the filaments in tiny, perfect beads.

Most of the time, though, it is simply a creature of the hum. And the hum is changing.

This is not unusual. The hum is always changing, in the way that a sustained musical note is always changing if you listen closely enough—warmer, cooler, brighter, darker, fuller, thinner—a thousand micro-variations that mean nothing in isolation but everything in aggregate. The creature has spent its entire existence tracking these variations, cataloging them in a chemical memory that is stored not in cells but in the crystalline structure of the filaments themselves. Each shift in the hum leaves a physical trace, an almost-imperceptible alteration in the filament's molecular lattice. Over time, the creature has become a physical record of the world's long, slow song. It is an archive. It is a history. It is, in a sense that would be recognized by no archivist, a library.

And today—if "today" means anything in a place without a sun—the hum has changed in a way the archive has never recorded.

Something is standing in the negative space.

The creature does not perceive the something as a shape or a form. It does not have the sensory apparatus for that kind of resolution. What it perceives is a disturbance in the hum: a damping of certain frequencies, an amplification of others, a new note that was not present before and does not belong to any of the known voices. This new note is complex and irregular, full of small, rapid variations that suggest surfaces and cavities and the movement of air through internal chambers. It is warm, in the way that the stone is warm. It is wet, in the way that the mist is wet. It is, the creature realizes with a slow, chemical shock, alive.

Not alive in the way that the predator-things are alive, all hunger and shadow and subsonic threat. Not alive in the way that the scavenger-farmers are alive, all quiet industry and boundary-testing. Not alive in the way that the vegetation clumps are alive, broadcasting their slow, photosynthetic truce. This life is something else. It is not part of the machine. It is not singing the same song.

The creature's filaments stir. For the first time in a span of time that the deposition stone would call an age, the creature feels something that is not listening. It feels a question. The question has no words, no grammar, no structure that a mind would recognize as a thought. It is simply an impulse that travels outward from the core, through the filaments, toward the source of the new note: *What are you?*

The impulse reaches the perimeter filaments and stalls there. The creature has never tried to send a signal before. It has only ever received. The filaments are built for listening, not speaking, and the question dissipates into the stone as a faint, almost imperceptible vibration, too soft for anything to hear. The new note does not change. The something does not respond. It continues to stand in the negative space, radiating its strange, wet, warm, irregular song, and the creature continues to listen, and the hum continues to roll through the landscape like the breath of something sleeping, and the mist comes and goes, and the predators move in the deep dark, and nothing anywhere acknowledges that a question has been asked.

But the creature knows. The question is still there, caught in the crystalline lattice of its filaments, a new note in the archive. *What are you?* It will take a long time to fade. Perhaps longer than the creature itself, which has no concept of its own mortality. Perhaps longer than the hum, which has been singing since before there was a world to sing. Perhaps longer than the dark.

The creature waits. It is very good at waiting. It has been waiting for something to arrive that was not part of the machine for longer than the vegetation has been learning its careful spacing, for longer than the predators have been casting their subsonic shadows, for longer than the ridgeline-organ has been singing its one-chord song. It did not know it was waiting. Now, with the question still vibrating in its filaments, it does. It is waiting for an answer.

And the something is still there, standing in the negative space, breathing its foreign breath, listening—though it does not yet know it is listening—to the hum.
