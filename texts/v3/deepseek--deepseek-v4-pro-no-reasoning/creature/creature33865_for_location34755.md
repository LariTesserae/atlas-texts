The Gardener doesn't remember her name anymore. She's not sure when she lost it—whether it seeped out through the soles of her feet into the metal grating, or evaporated with her sweat into the thick green air, or simply atrophied from disuse like a muscle she stopped needing. Names require someone to speak them, and she has been alone here for longer than she has words to measure.

She moves through the chamber with the slow, deliberate economy of someone who has learned to treat every motion as an expenditure to be budgeted. Her body has adapted to the heat in ways she occasionally finds unsettling—she no longer sweats much, her metabolism having downshifted into something closer to a reptile's, and her resting heart rate hovers somewhere in the low forties. When she presses her fingers to the inside of her wrist, the pulse that answers feels like a distant drum, something happening to someone else far away.

Her age is difficult to determine. The humidity has preserved her strangely, kept her skin soft and creaseless, but something behind her eyes has aged independently of her body—a geological patience, a stillness that belongs more to stone than to flesh. Her hair, which she keeps cropped short with a pair of rust-flecked shears from the locker, has faded to the color of the metal walls: a grey that carries undertones of copper and iron. She wears a jumpsuit whose original color has long since surrendered to the omnipresent green-black patina of the vegetation, patched at the knees and elbows with material harvested from places she's long since stopped visiting.

She is the only moving thing in the chamber, and she moves like the plants do—so slowly that if you blinked you'd miss the motion entirely, her presence registering as a gradual shifting of the light rather than a discrete action. This is not an affectation. She has learned, over whatever span of time has passed, that moving slowly conserves oxygen (though the chamber has never run short), generates less metabolic heat (though the heat is not hers to control), and most importantly, disturbs the colony less. That's what she calls the vegetation now—the colony, as if it were a single organism rather than a thousand intertwined species that have blurred into one another across generations of lightless coevolution. She has developed a kind of relationship with it, the way a beekeeper relates to a hive, or a zookeeper to a particularly intelligent octopus that has learned to recognize her face.

The Gardener's daily rounds—and they are daily, she still marks time with scratches on the inside of the locker door, 98,324 marks and counting—follow a path she has worn into the detritus over decades, a looping circuit that visits every node in the chamber's hidden anatomy. She checks the pipes first, running her fingers along their seams to feel for weaknesses, for the subtle vibration that indicates a leak is forming. Some of the pipes are original, so corroded that they crumble to powder at a firm touch; these she has learned to treat like sacred objects, never disturbing them, working around their fragility with the reverence of a priest tending an altar. Others are her own work, installed with tools from the locker—her hands steady, her patience infinite—and these bear the neat, functional marks of someone who learned engineering from necessity rather than training.

The liquid gleaming beneath the grating requires monitoring. Its level rises and falls according to cycles she has never fully mapped, though she has filled three notebooks (salvaged from a supply closet two levels up, their pages yellow and brittle) with observations. The liquid is not water, or not only water. It has a viscosity that suggests dissolved minerals, perhaps dissolved something-else, and when she accidentally touched it once—years ago, decades, she jolted her hand back with a hiss—it left a chemical burn that took months to heal, the scar still visible as a pale splash across her knuckles. Since then she has been careful. She samples it with a glass pipette, one of the precious few remaining intact, and she drips it into vials that she labels with a grease pencil and stores in the locker. She is not sure why she does this. There is no one to read the labels. No one to care about the pH levels or the trace elements or the way the liquid fluoresces faintly under the bioluminescence, a deep orange counterpoint to the blue-green glow. But she does it anyway, because stopping would mean admitting something she's not ready to admit.

The central cylinder demands most of her attention. She thinks of it as the heart, though she knows that implies a circulatory system that extends beyond this room, and she has never been able to trace its connections past a certain point—the corridors outside this chamber collapsed decades ago, blocked by fallen ceiling panels and a fibrous growth that looks like fungal coral and resists all her efforts to cut through it. She is sealed in. She made her peace with that sometime around year fifteen. The cylinder still functions, that much is clear: the milky white droplets continue to form and fall, the shapes behind the glass continue their deliberate spiraling, and the trough at its base continues to collect and drain whatever the cylinder is producing. She has tasted the white fluid once, just a drop on the tip of her tongue, and it left a sensation she has never been able to name—not a flavor so much as a memory of a flavor, something almost sweet, almost fermented, carrying undertones of something her brain interpreted as loneliness. She has not tasted it again.

Today—and it is today, the scratch on the locker door says so—something is different. The Gardener notices it during her second circuit of the room, the one she makes after the pipe inspection but before the liquid sampling. The hum has changed. It cycles up as it always does, holds for three of her slow heartbeats, but instead of fading, it holds. And holds. She stands perfectly still, one hand resting on the warm metal of a pipe, and counts her heartbeats. Ten. Twenty. The hum does not fade. The plants respond before she does—their slow-undulating fronds have gone still, their fleshy pads drawing slightly inward, the way a sea anemone withdraws when a shadow passes. The bioluminescence flickers once, twice, and then stabilizes at a brightness she has never seen it reach before.

In the central cylinder, the milky droplets have begun to fall faster. Much faster. They are no longer individual drops but a thin continuous stream, and behind the fogged glass, the deliberate spirals of temperature-shadow have accelerated into something that looks almost like agitation. The Gardener approaches the cylinder with the same deliberate slowness she always uses—fear would make her move faster, but she has forgotten how to let fear dictate her body, has substituted patience for panic over so many years that the substitution has become permanent. She places her palm against the glass. It is warmer than usual. No, not warmer—it has a pulse. A distinct, rhythmic throbbing that matches the new steady hum, as if the cylinder has suddenly become a living thing, or has always been one and has only now decided to announce itself.

She should be afraid. Some distant part of her recognizes this, the way you recognize a face in an old photograph without quite remembering the person's name. But what she feels instead is something closer to relief. Something is finally happening. After all the years of maintenance, of watching liquid levels and patching pipes and scratching marks into a locker door that has become her only calendar, her only confidant, her only proof that time is still passing—something is happening.

The cylinder's fog is clearing. The shapes behind the glass are resolving into something with edges. The Gardener does not step back. She leans closer, her breath fogging the surface, and watches the thing inside the cylinder begin to open its eyes.
