The Lamplighter moves along the north gantry, her bare feet finding the familiar grip of the rope-wound rail. She does not need to see it to know it—the wrap pattern under her toes is as readable as text. Seven turns, a knot, seven turns, a splice. She has made this crossing a thousand times, and the rope has never once shifted under her weight. It has been here longer than she has. It will be here after she has gone.

She is not human, though she has the shape of one, more or less. Her legs are long and jointed in a way that suggests a body built for climbing. Her hands are broad, fingers longer than they should be, with an extra thumb on each side. Her skin is pale as the fungal mats that trace the iron around her, with a texture more like smooth cartilage than flesh. She wears no clothing—the water is blood-warm and constant, and clothing would only slow her. At her hips and shoulders, gentle frills of translucent membrane ripple with her movements, not for propulsion, but for sensing pressure shifts, temperature gradients, the proximity of moving water.

Her face is smooth and nearly featureless—no nose, no external ears. Two large eyes, dark as engine oil, set slightly to the sides, capable of seeing in the lightless murk where the amber glow doesn't reach. Her mouth is small, lipless, and her teeth are flattened plates for scraping the fungal mats off the iron. She does not speak in words. The sounds she makes are clicks and a low humming that the engine room swallows without echo, absorbed into the thrumming.

She carries a lantern, though she does not need it. It is a fist-sized cage of copper wire, green-black with verdigris, wound around a glass bulb that emits a soft blue light—not electric, but chemical, bred from the saliva of something that lives deeper down. The light is not for her. It is for the *other* ones, the ones who live above, the ones who do not come down here. The lantern signals that the gantry is occupied, that the territory is attended. It is protocol, and the Lamplighter follows protocol, because protocol is what keeps the machinery from waking.

She was not born here, though she cannot remember anywhere else. There are others of her kind in the complex—a scattered population of maybe a dozen, each with a territory, each with a lantern. They do not meet often. They do not need to. They know one another's territories by the smell of the iron, by the arrangement of the fungal mats, by the subtle variation in the lantern light. They are solitary by nature, but not hostile. The pool is large enough, and the water is shared by all.

Her territory is this: the three pistons, the twelve access hatches, the one functioning pressure door that leads deeper into the pump complex. She does not go through the pressure door. That is not her domain. Her domain is the engine room itself, the gantries and platforms, the bolt-studded flanges, the gaps in the floor grates that open onto bottomless water. She knows every inch of it. She has mapped it in the dark, with her hands, with her feet, with the subtle pressure of her respiratory frills. She knows which bolts are loose, which plates sing when the temperature shifts, which fungal patches are ready to harvest and which must be left to recover.

The fungal mats are her primary food. She scrapes them with her teeth, taking only the outermost layer, leaving the deeper mycelial network intact. The fungus grows slowly, and she has learned patience. There is enough, if she is careful. The white crabs are also food, when they appear—she catches them in her long fingers, crushes them, eats them shell and all. They taste like brine and copper, like the water itself. They are not a staple, but a supplement, a rare protein that appears in bursts and then vanishes. The translucent ribbon-creatures that unspool from the pipe flanges are not food. They are something else. She avoids them.

She has a name, though she never uses it. It is not a name in the sense of sound, but a configuration of pressure pulses and thermal signatures that identify her to the others of her kind. When she passes near another territory, she feels the name of its holder in the water, like a faint electrical charge. Her own name is a warmth, a specific frequency of vibration that she emits unconsciously, the way the machinery emits its heat. She does not think of it as a name. She thinks of it as *herself*, the way the engine room thinks of its own noise.

Her routine is not a routine, but it is. She wakes with the dimming of the amber light—a slow fade that happens over hours, marking some circadian cycle she cannot see but has learned to feel. She unhooks herself from the resting niche she has carved into the iron, a shallow depression in the wall near the great piston, padded with shed debris and softened rust. She stretches, her joints popping with a sound like tiny gears releasing. She descends to the lower gantry to check the fungal mats. She moves through the corridors of empty machinery, past the gaps in the floor, past the access shafts that climb toward the city. She tends the lanterns—there are three in her territory, not including the one she carries, and she must refill their chemical reservoirs every cycle. This is her primary duty, the thing that gives her presence meaning. The lanterns are not merely signals. They are anchors. They keep the deep things away.

She does not know what the deep things are. She knows only that when a lantern goes dark, the water changes. The thrumming grows louder. The currents become possessive, tugging at her limbs. The small white crabs do not congregate. The translucent ribbon-creatures unspool farther from their pipes, reaching. So she tends the lanterns. She has done this for as long as she can remember. She was taught, or she learned, or it is written in her body—she does not know which.

The city overhead is a rumor to her. The faint grid of square lights through one of the access shafts is the only evidence she has of it: a pattern of illumination too regular to be accidental. She has climbed the ladder once, long ago, before the shaft became choked with silt. She reached the top and found a pressure door, its porthole fogged, but through it she glimpsed shapes—buildings, moving shadows, the glow of windows. The city was upright then, or perhaps it was the same as now, and her memory has rearranged it. She does not climb the shaft anymore. The door is sealed. The city is above, and she is below, and that is correct.

The sound is her constant companion. She does not hear it the way a human would; she feels it in her bones, in her teeth, in the jelly of her eyes. It is the background against which all other perception happens. She cannot imagine silence. She had a dream once—a vision of stillness, of water without current, of iron without vibration—and she woke disoriented, lost in her own territory. The dream was more foreign to her than the city above, and it frightened her.

She knows the machinery is dying. Not in a dramatic sense, but slowly, inevitably. The loose plate that hammers in some distant chamber has been loose for as long as she can remember, but the rhythm is changing, becoming more irregular. The steam vent that hisses from the ceiling pipe is weaker now, a low sigh rather than a shriek. The grinding gears still slip and catch, but the gaps between their engagements are longer. The erosion is settled, but the energy is not. The engine room is not finished in the sense of completion; it is finished in the sense of running down. The Lamplighter does not know this, but she feels it, the way one feels a wound healing—or a scar pulling tight.

She eats. She tends the lanterns. She sleeps in her niche. She watches the fungal mats pulse faintly, their respiration measured in hours. She catches white crabs when they appear, her long arms darting with precision. She avoids the translucent ribbon-creatures. She does not pass through the pressure door. She does not climb the access shafts. She exists, and the engine room exists, and between them there is a symbiosis too old to question.

Once, a long time ago, she found a dead thing washed against a bulkhead. It was not one of her kind, and not a crab, and not a ribbon-creature. It was larger, with a body like a segmented tube and a head that was mostly mouth. Its flesh was already dissolving, and the white crabs were feeding on it in a slow, methodical frenzy. She watched for a long time. The lantern at her hip cast its blue glow over the scene, and she understood, in a way that was not thought, that the deep things died too, and that the engine room consumed everything eventually, even the things that fed on it.

The Lamplighter adjusts her grip on the rope rail and sets out along the gantry. The water shifts, a slow muscular current pushing her gently toward the far platform. She braces, compensates, her bare feet finding the familiar warp and weft. The lantern sways at her hip. The blue light spills over the iron, illuminating the continents of rust, the canyons of pitted metal, the ridgelines of green-black copper. The sound of the generator thrums through her. The piston hangs in the dimness like a suspended whale.

She is alone, and she is not alone. The place is with her. The place is her, in some sense, or she is the place. The machinery is a body, and she is one of its organs, a small warm thing moving through the composite darkness of it, doing what she was shaped to do.

A small white crab drifts across her path, its eyeless body a delicate construction of articulated segments. She reaches out, cups it in her palm, and brings it to her mouth. She crushes it. The taste is brine and copper. The water is warm. The lantern glows.

She continues on, making her slow circuit of the territory, marking the changes, tending the light.
