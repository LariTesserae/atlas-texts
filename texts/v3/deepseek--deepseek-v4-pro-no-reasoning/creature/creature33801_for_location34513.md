The first thing you notice, once you stop flinching from the fauna and start really looking, is the hands.

Not hands like yours. These are architectures of knuckle and tendon rendered in something that is not quite bone and not quite cable, sheathed in a skin the color of old ivory left too long in the damp. The fingers are long—longer than arms, some of them, jointed in six or seven places instead of three, and they taper not to nails but to soft, spatulate pads that glisten with the same faint wetness that coats everything else. They move, always. They are never still. Even when the rest of the body is braced and motionless, the fingers keep working, a slow, ceaseless gestural language, as if they are reading the air.

There are dozens of them. Perhaps hundreds. You cannot count them because you are never sure where one individual ends and another begins. They sit in the crooks of pipes, these beings, folded into the spaces between the great copper spheres and the ribbed tracheal tubes, their bodies conforming to the metal as though they grew here, as though the structure shaped them and they shaped it back. They are large. An adult, if *adult* means anything here, might mass as much as a draft horse, but there is nothing equine about them. The core body is a slumped, pearlike form, broadest at the base where it merges with the floor—no, not merges. *Connects*. There are ports in the metal, circular openings ringed with that dark sealant, and from each port rises a thick stalk of gray tissue, and from that stalk rises the body, and you realize with a slow, deep wrongness that they are plugged into the floor. That they are not sitting. That they are growing from it.

Or perhaps they are the floor. Perhaps the distinction is not one they would make.

The bodies are covered in a fine, short down, paler than the ivory skin beneath, and this down is what the wet air clings to. Droplets bead on every filament, so that each individual wears a constant dew, a nimbus of microscopic lenses that catches the sourceless light and makes them shimmer when they move, which is rarely. They are a patient species. You stand in the crevice of a great flange and watch one for what feels like an hour, and in that time it does nothing but breathe—a slow inflation of the upper body, a subtle shift of the fingers—and yet you cannot look away. There is something about them that demands attention. Not charisma, not threat. *Significance*. The sense that this being is not an organism in the way you understand organisms. That it is, instead, a node.

A node in what? The question forms and dissolves. You have no framework.

The heads—if they are heads—are set low on the torso, cradled by the upper curve of the pear, and they are dominated by eyes. Great, dark, liquid eyes, set in pairs. Two pairs. Four eyes, two facing forward, two angled to the sides, and all of them open, all of them moving independently, scanning different arcs, and when one of those eyes swings in your direction, you feel it. Not fear, exactly. Pressure. As though the gaze has physical weight, a warm compression against your skin, and you understand that you have been seen, not as a shape or a threat, but as *information*. You are data now. You are logged.

The mouths, by contrast, are small and practical. Thin lips, the same ivory tone as the skin, sealing tightly over something that is not teeth. You see one yawn, or perform some action analogous to yawning, and inside the narrow slit is a ring of fine, flexible cilia, pale and glistening, moving in a slow peristaltic wave. They do not eat, you remember. So what is the mouth for? The cilia ripple, and the air moves slightly, and a single bead of moisture rolls down from the upper lip and is drawn inside, and you think: taste. They are tasting the air. They are reading the chemical signatures of the place, the mineral tang, the faint organic exhalations of the carpet-creatures and the multi-legged ones, the subtle gradients of heat and humidity that you cannot perceive. The wet air is their medium of speech, and the mouth is an ear.

You spend a long time—you cannot measure it; the light does not change, the warmth does not wane—wandering among them. Moving through the metal labyrinth, ducking under warm pipes, stepping over conduits, and everywhere you go, they are there. In the denser tangles, they are clustered so thickly that you must navigate between their stalks, and this is the first time you are afraid, because the stalks are warm and slightly yielding, and when you brush against one, the whole body above you shudders. A ripple passes through the down, the fingers pause, the eyes swivel. You freeze. You wait. Nothing happens. The stalk does not retract, the body does not reach for you. After a moment the fingers resume their motion, the eyes resume their scanning, and you are simply not a priority.

It is this that frightens you most, in the end. Not the strangeness, not the scale, not the perpetual almost-rain or the warm metal floor or the fauna that are not fauna. It is the indifference. You are irrelevant. A minor fluctuation in moisture and heat, a temporary block in a corridor that has no names. You could do anything here, you suspect. You could walk forever. You could lie down on the metal and sleep and wake and sleep again. You could scream. You could weep. You could die. And the slow, constant, careful motion of the fingers would never pause. The four eyes would register your cooling body as a thermal anomaly, a point on a spectrum, and then move on.

And yet, you notice something.

They are not identical.

It takes a long time to see it, because the eye wants to generalize, to lump them into a single category of *being*, but if you stay still and watch one individual for a long while, you begin to perceive the differences. The shape of the down, for one—on some it lies flat, sleek as an otter's pelt; on others it stands in soft ruffles, like the feathers of a sleeping bird. The length of the fingers varies. The spacing of the eyes. And there are scars. Faint lines in the ivory skin, old wounds that have healed to a paler white, forming patterns that might be accidental or might be names. One individual near a great copper sphere has a scar that arcs across two of its four eyes like an eyebrow, a permanent expression of skepticism or sorrow. Another, smaller, nestled in a nest of thin pipes, has hands that shake, the fingers trembling in a way that looks almost palsied, almost old.

And you realize: they age. Or something like age. Time passes for them, even here, even in this place where the temperature never shifts and the moisture never drops and the hum in the metal is as constant as a held note. They are not frozen. They are not components, not exactly. They are living things. They are simply living things whose lives are so slow, so vast, so utterly orthogonal to your own, that you cannot perceive the arc of them.

It hits you then, with the force of a physical blow: they are not plugged into the floor to draw sustenance from it. They are plugged into the floor to *give*. The warmth of the metal is not coming from below. It is coming from *them*. The steady thirty-seven-degree heat, the unchanging temperature of a living body—it is their collective metabolism, bleeding into the plates, spreading through the continent of metal, holding the entire world at equilibrium. The deep hum you feel through your feet is the sound of hearts. Thousands of them. Millions. Beating in a synchrony so perfect that it resolves into a single note.

And the moisture. The moisture is their exhalation. The wet air, the saturated atmosphere, the constant potential for rain that never falls—it is the breath of these beings, filtered through the cilia of their mouths, warmed in the great bladderlike bodies, released in a slow, endless tide. The water that beads on every surface and runs in fine, precise films down the metal grooves—it is their sweat. Their dew. The byproduct of whatever metabolic process sustains them. The place is not a machine that happens to contain life. The place is life, scaled up to continental dimensions, turned into an ecology of one single, endlessly distributed, endlessly patient organism.

And then, as you stand there, the hum changes.

It is a subtle shift, so subtle you almost miss it—a one-part-in-a-thousand change in frequency, a slight deepening of the note. The vibration through the soles of your feet drops half a tone. And every one of them, every single one of the ivory-skinned beings as far as you can see, stops moving. The fingers freeze. The eyes lock. The cilia cease their rippling. The whole immense population holds its breath.

In the silence, a new sound. A long, slow groan, metal expanding or contracting by microns, a sound that takes minutes to complete. The walls of the labyrinth shift slightly, a near-imperceptible settling, and then the hum returns to its original pitch. The fingers resume their motion. The eyes resume their scanning. The moment passes.

But you saw it. You saw every single one of them respond to that subsonic chord, that minute adjustment in the structure. They are not merely generating the heat and the moisture. They are *listening*. The structure speaks to them in vibrations, in shifts of temperature and pressure, in the long slow groans that you thought were tectonic. They are a sensory network distributed across the entire continent, a living nervous system embedded in the metal, feeling everything, responding to everything. The metal floor is their skin. The pipes are their bones. The great copper spheres are organs, though what they do—blood pumping, air filtering, signal processing—you cannot guess. And these beings, these patient, four-eyed, finger-gesturing beings, are the brain. Or the soul. Or something for which you have no word at all.

You look at the nearest one, the one with the skeptical scar. You look at its hands, still tracing their endless silent language against the wet air. You look at its four dark eyes, one of which is still trained on you, has been trained on you this entire time, you now realize. And you understand that it is not indifferent. It is not unaware. It is simply operating on a scale of attention so large that your entire lifespan, your entire species, might fit into a single pause between its thoughts.

The fingers move. The eyes blink, one at a time, a slow wave across the face. The body breathes. Somewhere deep in the metal, a note changes by a fraction of a fraction of a tone.

And the being waits, with the patience of a continent, for you to become interesting.
