The creature on the derrick leg is only the first to notice, but it will not be the last.

It is built low and long, a body plan that makes sense only here, in the perpetual wet and the vertical dark. Its legs number fourteen, each one articulated in five places and tipped with a curved, chitinous claw that can find purchase in the corrosion-roughened iron as easily as it can in the grooved limestone of the karst. The legs are not identical. Two pairs near the head are longer, more slender, raised in an almost prayerful attitude and fringed along their lower edges with those feathery antennae that now quiver in the steam. They are olfactory organs, primarily, but so sensitive to vibration that they serve as ears, as weather vanes, as instruments for reading the complex chemical grammar of the mist. What the creature scents now is not threat, not yet — it is anomaly. An interruption in the constant drizzle of data.

Its body is segmented, sheathed in a carapace the color of wet slate, beaded perpetually with condensation. The carapace is not a single shell but a series of overlapping plates, allowing the creature to compress itself to slip through fissures in the rock or expand to present a larger profile when threatened. Bioluminescence is reserved for the joints, where a faint, greenish light seeps out — not enough to see by, but enough to signal. The creature is a walking constellation of dim emerald points, a pattern that identifies it to others of its kind: sex, age, clan, mood. Right now the pattern pulses slow and steady, the rhythm matching the distant thump of the pumps. Calm. Attentive.

Its head is the strangest part. No eyes. Where eyes would be, on a creature of the sunlit world, there are instead two deep, sound-catching pits lined with fine, wiry hairs. Below them, a complex mouth: mandibles for gripping, maxillae for shredding, and a long, absorbent proboscis coiled under the chin like a fleshy spring, used for lapping the mineral film from wet rock and for the more delicate work of grooming. The head swivels now, triangulating. The anomaly is below, somewhere in the mist that pools in the sump at the base of the derrick. The creature’s name, if it had one in any language a human tongue could shape, might be something like Climb-Who-Tastes-the-Mist. Or perhaps just Kith.

Kith is not alone.

Further up the derrick, where the structure sways almost imperceptibly with each cycling of the piston, two more of Kith’s kind are feeding. They are juveniles, their carapaces softer, more translucent, their bioluminescence still a single, unmodulated band of green along their flanks. They are scraping the iron with their mandibles, harvesting the thick, gelatinous biofilm that grows on everything in this place — a composite organism of bacteria, fungi, and something that is not quite algae, something that metabolizes the iron itself. The biofilm is the base of the food chain, and it is tended. The juveniles scrape in patterns, deliberate paths that avoid the structural rivets and the places where the corrosion has pitted the metal too deeply. They are farming, or something like farming. Keeping the biofilm healthy. Keeping the derrick standing. The relationship is old, far older than the absent builders. The pumps were here before Kith’s ancestors climbed out of the sumps, before the first chisel marked the first spiral on the rock. The pumps are geology. The pumps are weather. The pumps are the world.

Kith ignores the juveniles. The anomaly is more pressing.

She — Kith is female, gravid, her abdominal segments distended with the soft, translucent eggs that she will soon deposit in a nursery crevice high in the karst — begins to descend. Her fourteen legs move in a ripple, a wave of motion that makes barely a whisper of claw on metal. She is a creature of immense, quiet precision. In the absolute dark, sound is both map and camouflage. To be loud is to be seen. To be seen is to be eaten.

The sump at the base of the derrick is a steaming, mineral-choked pool, its surface disturbed only by the occasional drip from above and the rhythmic slosh as the pump’s discharge hose vomits a fresh gout of warm water. The pool is not dead. Far from it. The water teems with life — blind, pale, writhing things that never grow larger than a thumb. But the pool is also a gathering place, a node in the territorial network of the karst, and Kith is not the only large creature who visits.

The other is already here.

It is vast. Where Kith is long and jointed and precise, this creature is a sack of wet, grey flesh, a mound of it, a landscape of it, draped across the rocks at the pool’s edge. Its skin has the texture of a mushroom’s cap, smooth and slightly tacky, and it is covered in a network of darker veins that pulse with a slow, internal peristalsis. The creature has no legs, no obvious head, no segmentation. It moves — when it moves, which is rarely — by extending broad, flat pseudopods, pouring itself from one configuration to another with a sound like wet clay being squeezed. Its bioluminescence is internal, a deep, bruised blue that seems to flare and fade with some hidden respiratory rhythm. It is a Gulper, a Sump-Lord, a Crawling-Stomach. The juveniles have no name for it. The juveniles know to be nowhere near it.

The Gulper is feeding. A thick, muscular siphon has extended from its anterior mass and plunged into the pool, and the water around it is churning with the suction. The blind, pale things in the water are being drawn in, dozens per second, processed through some internal filtration system. The Gulper is a specialist, an energy miser. It feeds on the most abundant, most easily captured prey in the biome, and it does so almost continuously, converting the endless rain of biofilm and microbial life into the immense, slow-burning calories that sustain its tonne of flesh.

This is not the anomaly. Kith has known the Gulper since it was a quarter of this size, a soft, fist-sized lump clinging to the underside of a rock. The Gulper is no threat to something of Kith’s size and armature, and Kith is no threat to it. They exist in different trophic slots, their territories overlapping without friction. The Gulper’s siphon continues its rhythmic suck. Its internal blue light pulses. It is barely aware of Kith, and Kith is barely aware of it. They are neighbours.

The anomaly is in the Gulper’s shadow.

It’s smaller than Kith, but built more heavily. A dense, compact body, more crustacean than insectoid, armoured in thick, calcareous plates. Six legs, thick and pillar-like, with blunt, spade-shaped feet adapted as much for digging as for walking. The head is broad and flat, dominated by a pair of massive mandibles that cross in front of its face like shears, but the eyes — the eyes are the anomaly. This creature has eyes. Not empty sound-catching pits, but actual, light-gathering eyes, eight of them, small and black and utterly useless here. They are a legacy, an ancestral gift from some sunlit epoch, and they have not atrophied away because the creature doesn’t need them to. It navigates by touch, by smell, by the seismic vibrations in the rock. The eyes are just there, vestigial, like the spiral marks on the karst walls. A reminder.

This is a Digger, a Stone-Grinder, a Rock-Mandible. Its kind live deep in the karst, in the narrowest, most labyrinthine fissures, chewing through the limestone itself to expand their burrows. The biofilm is their food, but so is the rock. Their guts are furnaces of acid and specialized bacteria that break down the calcium carbonate, extracting the trace minerals and leaving behind a fine, white, pastelike waste that hardens on contact with air. The Digger’s territory is below, not above. It should not be here, at a sump-pool, in the open, under a derrick.

And it is hurt.

Kith’s antennae read the injury before she is fully down the derrick leg — a sharp, acrid note of hemolymph, a fungal undertone of infection, the unmistakable high-pitched stress pheromone that even the juveniles stop their scraping to taste. The Digger’s flank is cracked. One of its armour plates is stove in, and the soft, pale tissue beneath is exposed and oozing. The wound is not random; it’s a puncture, a precise, deliberate hole, as if something has driven a spike into the Digger’s side and then withdrawn it.

Kith freezes. Her bioluminescence flickers, shifts to a new pattern — a rapid, staccato blinking that means nothing to the Gulper but would mean danger, danger, danger to any of Kith’s kind within range. The two juveniles on the derrick above go still, their own lights extinguishing entirely. They press themselves flat against the iron, becoming invisible, becoming part of the structure.

Because a wound like that is not an accident. A wound like that is a signature.

Somewhere out in the dark, beyond the sump, beyond the next derrick and the one after that, there is a sound. Not the thump-hiss of the pumps, not the chittering of Kith’s kind, not the wet slosh of the Gulper’s feeding. It’s a clicking. A fast, rhythmic, articulated clicking, like bone on stone, like a message being tapped out in a code that is just beyond comprehension. It’s moving closer.

The Digger, wounded and desperate, drags itself toward the pool. Its mandibles gape. It wants water, wants to wash the infection from its wound, wants the mineral-rich warmth that might soothe the pain. It does not know that it has brought something with it, or that it has been driven here, herded by a predator that likes its prey alive and mobile and leaking a trail of chemical fear.

Kith ascends the derrick in a silent, fluid scramble, her previous calm erased. She passes the juveniles and keeps climbing, up past the piston housing, up past the corroded crown of the derrick, up into the high, fog-wreathed lattice of epiphytes that drapes the uppermost crossbeams. From here, she can taste the entire sump basin. She can taste the Gulper, still oblivious. She can taste the Digger, its pain a hot, sharp column in the mist. And she can taste the other thing.

It tastes like nothing she has ever tasted before. Not like any of the karst’s familiar predators — the blind, winged Hookwings that drop from the high darkness, the pack-hunting Scuttlers that coordinate their attacks with clicks and whistles. This is something else. The scent is cold, dry, metallic. Sterile, almost. Wrong.

The clicking grows louder. The Digger shudders, tries to submerge itself in the shallows. The Gulper’s siphon withdraws, its vast body quivering with a sudden, belated awareness. Something is coming. Something that has evolved, or been made, to puncture thick armour. Something that hunts the hunters, that preys on the careful equilibrium of the karst.

Kith presses herself into the epiphyte mass, her fourteen legs drawn in tight, her carapace a dark, motionless shape against the dripping fronds. Her antennae splay wide, reading. Her bioluminescence is extinguished entirely. She is an absence now, a gap in the world’s awareness.

Below her, the Gulper begins to move, pouring its great grey mass away from the pool with a speed that seems impossible for something so huge and formless. The Digger keens, a high, grinding sound of pure distress. The juveniles on the derrick are so still they might be dead.

And out of the mist, into the faint green glow of the pool, comes the thing that clicks.
