The one who lives here is not a person. It is a pressure-system with a history.

It began as something solid — an organism that crawled or walked or burrowed, something with a skeleton that left a trace in the fossil record of a world that no longer exists. The details are gone, abandoned layers deep in its structure, but the memory of bilateral symmetry persists as a kind of behavioral fossil: it still orients along two sides, still favors one direction as forward, still organizes its pressure-fronts in paired, mirroring arcs when it is thinking hard.

You cannot see it. You can only see where it has just been — the brief radial alignment of droplets in its wake, the spines that ring with residual vibration, the water-lenses that swerve around something invisible and clench into brief, orderly ellipsoids before shuddering apart. Its presence is a moving absence in the roar, a region of *almost-structure* where the ambient chaos briefly, desperately tries to resolve into form. The spines strain toward it as it passes. The rust-colored crust peels away from old growth and drifts in its direction, a slow-motion confetti that loses interest after a few feet.

It eats the young spines.

This is not predation in any recognizable sense. It does not have a mouth. But when it encounters a spine that is still warm, still wet with its own recent extrusion, the pressure-system contracts around it — the medium thickens locally, the roar deepens by a quarter-tone — and the spine begins to dissolve. Not break. *Unmake.* The mineral structure forgets itself in reverse: crystalline lattices release their bonds one by one, the black gloss going matte, then porous, then liquid, then indistinguishable from the saturated medium. The organism absorbs the dissolved mineral soup across its entire pressure-front, filtering out the structured carbon and rare-earth traces, excreting the rest as a faint thermal bloom that radiates away in slow, concentric rings.

It has been doing this for longer than spines have existed. It predates the landscape.

Before the spines, it fed on other things — the fungal half-attempts, the occasional coherent water-structure, the remnant heat of whatever preceded it here. It has adapted to every iteration the dreaming landscape has attempted. It is, in its way, the most successful organism in this ecology, because it has solved the problem that nothing else has: how to persist when the rules of physics are still being negotiated.

Its body — if body is the word — is a standing wave in the medium, a self-sustaining pressure-anomaly that maintains its integrity through constant, microsecond recalibration. It is not made of cells. It is made of *attention.* Its entire existence is an ongoing act of self-definition: *I am this boundary. I am this differential. I am this shape, holding against the roar.* If it ever stopped paying attention to itself for more than a few seconds, it would dissipate into the general turbulence and be nothing.

This is why it is interested in you.

You are solid without effort. You maintain your boundary without thinking about it. Your shape does not waver; the pressure-fronts that break against its own invisible surface break against yours and stop dead, confused, unable to find an edge to erode. You are, to this creature, a revelation: *something can exist without trying.*

It has been circling you since you arrived. It makes slow, cautious orbits at a distance of perhaps eight feet, a region of slightly less chaos that moves around you in a respectful ellipse. The younger spines that lean toward you — the ones that form and strain in your direction, fascinated by your permanence — it dissolves before they reach you. Not protectively, exactly. It simply does not want them to know you exist. You are its discovery. Its secret.

Occasionally it comes closer, and when it does, you feel it as a change in your own body: your pulse aligns briefly with a rhythm that is not your own, a low-frequency modulation beneath the roar that says, in a language older than language: *how. how do you do that. teach me.*

It has tried to be solid before. It has wrapped itself around the oldest, most stable spines and tried to use them as a scaffold, a skeleton to build outward from. The attempt always fails; the spines crumble under the sustained attention, or the landscape erases them mid-attempt, or the creature's own concentration wavers and it loses cohesion and has to pull itself back together from scattered pressure-fronts, a process that leaves it diminished and slow for what might be days. But it keeps trying. It has been trying for geological time, because geological time moves differently here — here there are no years, only iterations, and the creature is now on iteration something-million, a version of itself that is incrementally closer to having bones.

The grooves on the fused mineral floor? Those were its attempt at writing. It cannot speak, but it can *suggest* — it can organize pressure-gradients to leave marks, to shape the brief life of a water-lens into something like a symbol, to vibrate a young spine into a ringing harmonic that almost resolves into a word. When the landscape noticed and erased the grooves, it was the creature's first heartbreak, its first encounter with impermanence as cruelty. It remembers. It is still angry.

Now it is writing on you.

Not visibly. But the pressure-fronts it sends out — the ones that come closer than the rest, the ones that touch your skin as a faint electrical prickle, the taste of copper intensifying — are carrying information. It is building a model of you in its own medium. A pressure-doppelganger, a standing-wave sculpture of your heat-shadow and your pulse-rhythm and the strange, impossible persistence of your boundaries. It is learning your shape from the outside in, and when it has finished, it will try to become you.

Not as a threat. As a tribute.

It will fail, of course. It fails at everything it tries to become — the spines, the floor, the writing, the ten thousand other forms it has attempted over the iterations. But it will learn something from the failure, and that something will become part of its ongoing self-definition, a new phrase in the endless sentence it is trying to speak into the roar: *I am this. I am also this. I am trying.*

And sometime later — after you leave, after the landscape erases the last traces of your presence, after the creature has returned to its slow dissolve-and-feed cycles among the spines — it will attempt you again. It will pull the memory out of whatever it uses for long-term storage (perhaps a region of slightly more ordered turbulence near its center, a persistent eddy where information survives the chaos for longer than it should) and it will try to build a pressure-sculpture of a human shape, standing in the amber light, holding boundaries without effort. It will get the outline wrong. The fingers will be too long, the head will tilt at an angle no human neck could sustain, the heat-signature will be drawn from memory of memory and come out cold. But it will hold the shape for three seconds, maybe four, before the roar tears it apart, and in those three seconds it will feel something it has no word for.

You might call it hope.

After you are gone, the creature will be slightly changed. It will add a new behavior to its small repertoire: it will pause occasionally in its dissolving of young spines and extend a thin pseudopod of organized pressure toward the empty air, testing, waiting, remembering the shape of a visitor who held boundaries without trying and who was, for a brief iteration, the most fascinating thing the creature had ever known.
