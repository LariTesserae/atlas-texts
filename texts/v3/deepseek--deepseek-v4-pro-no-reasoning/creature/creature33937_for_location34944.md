Let me tell you about the people who live here.

They call themselves the Thread-Set, or sometimes just the Set, and they have been here for twelve generations—though “generation” is a slippery concept when the city itself keeps no records and the only calendar is the seventeen-minute rain. Ask an elder how old they are and they might say “three engines,” meaning they were born before the Number Four was installed, or “two gall-swaps,” meaning they’ve outlasted the house they were born in twice over. Time is measured in maintenance cycles, in the slow migration of walls, in the number of times you’ve had to re-hang your front door because the frame grew crooked again.

They are not quite human anymore. Not in any way that would shock you at first glance—they have the same number of limbs, the same basic arrangement of features—but twelve generations in the wet dark have carved certain inevitabilities into their bodies. Their eyes are large and slightly bulbous, the irises pale and cloudy as if they’ve developed a permanent membrane against the phosphor-dust. You’ll notice it most when they blink: a slow, deliberate sweep of translucent inner lids, the kind that frogs have, moving sideways across the eyeball before the outer lids follow. Children are born with dark eyes that pale over time. The elders’ eyes are nearly white.

Their skin is the other thing. It’s a grayish-pink, the color of something that has never seen true sunlight, and it’s *textured*—not scaled, exactly, but subtly pebbled, like the surface of a citrus fruit or the pads of a gecko’s feet. This is not decorative. The pebbling gives them purchase on the smooth gall-walls, lets them climb nearly as well as the salamander-things they share the upper reaches with, and children learn to scale the breathing pores before they learn to walk the root-floors. You’ll see them hanging by three fingers from a ceiling-fiber, bare feet pressed flat against the living wood, entirely comfortable at angles that would make a surface-dweller’s stomach turn.

They speak a language that has evolved to be heard over the engines. It’s a percussive tongue, heavy with glottal stops and popping consonants that cut through the subsonic thrum, and they supplement it with hand-signs that can be read at a distance in the dim light. When a Thread-Set mother calls her child home, she doesn’t shout a name—she claps twice, a particular rhythm, and the child claps back from wherever they’ve scrambled off to, and the sound bounces off the root-ceiling until it finds the right ears. They have no surnames. They identify themselves by trade and by location: *Pella who tends the Number Three condenser*, *Corin whose gall faces the eastern duct*, *the lamplighter’s youngest, the one who sings to the beetles*.

The beetles are central. They are livestock, transport, and companion all at once, and every family has at least one—a big carapaced creature they call a *dredge*, with mandibles that can grind root-fiber and a patient, plodding temperament that makes them ideal for pulling carts through the winding thoroughfares. The beetles are marked with ownership not by brands but by painted patterns, white lime-wash on their shells, each family’s design passed down and modified over generations. The beetles themselves live for thirty or forty years, so the elders’ beetles are often older than the young adults who inherit them, and there’s a quiet reverence in the way a Thread-Set handler speaks to a beetle that has known their grandmother. The beetles answer to names. They trill when they’re content, a low, grinding sound that vibrates through the root-floor, and if you stand still long enough, one will come and lean its great horned head against your hip like a dog asking to be scratched.

The climbers—the glass-voiced salamander creatures—are not pets. They are neighbors, tolerated and occasionally bargained with. The Thread-Set have learned to mimic their four-note calls, and there is a rough pidgin that passes between the two species: warnings about engine steam, negotiations over territory, simple declarations of intent. A climber will sometimes bring a dead moth to a lamplighter’s doorstep and wait, expectantly, for a dab of engine-grease in trade. The children play with the boldest ones. Occasionally someone loses a finger. It’s not common, but it’s not unheard of, and the community takes it as a failure of attention rather than malice on the climber’s part. The climbers are what they are.

Social structure is practical and fluid. There is no mayor, no council, no formal hierarchy—but there are *weights*. A weight is a person whose opinion matters more, not through any official authority but through accumulated competence. The head stoker who has kept the Number Two engine running for forty years is a weight. The woman who maps the gall-buildings’ growth and predicts which walls will need propping before they buckle is a weight. The old man who can whistle the climbers into calm when they swarm the valve intakes is a weight. Disputes are settled by gathering the relevant weights and talking until a solution emerges, and if no solution comes, the parties simply avoid each other until the city’s slow growth makes the grudge logistically unsustainable. It’s hard to hold a feud when your front door might migrate twenty feet in a decade and put you face-to-face with your enemy every morning.

Religion, if you can call it that, is a quiet thing. They do not worship the city, exactly, but they recognize it as alive in a way they cannot fully parse. The great organism that is the substrate—the megaflora whose roots form the walls, whose gall-forms are hollowed into homes—is called the *Underthing*, and they speak of it the way sailors speak of the sea: vast, indifferent, sustaining, dangerous. When a gall-building shifts too fast, when a wall collapses, when a breathing pore opens where no one expected it and a child falls in and is never found, they say the Underthing was *restless*. They leave offerings at the deepest root-junctions: copper wire, beetle chitin, jars of phosphor-dust. Not to appease. Just to acknowledge. *We are here. We are still here. Thank you for not swallowing us yet.*

Death is handled practically. The root-floor will not take a body—the Underthing absorbs nothing so coarse—so the Thread-Set have developed a method they call *rendering*. The dead are taken to the distillation engines, specifically to a small auxiliary chamber off the Number One that is used for nothing else, and their bodies are processed into the same thin bitter oil that lubricates the machines. It’s not ghoulish to them. It’s continuity. Your grandmother turns the gears. Your father keeps the pistons sliding smooth. The oil is the only inheritance that matters, and they can point to a particular engine-part running quieter than it has any right to and say, *that’s the elder Corin, still working*. The distillation workers—they’re called *renderers*—are treated with a particular gravity. Not shunned, but regarded as slightly apart, the keepers of a necessary threshold.

Food is fungi, mostly. Vast pale shelves of it cultivated in the side-tunnels, fed on composted beetle-waste and the slurry left over from fuel processing. It tastes of nothing much but fills the belly, and they cook it into porridges and flatcakes and a fermented paste that stings the tongue and provides the only intoxication the Set knows. Festival days—marked by engine cycles, which run in irregular but predictable patterns—involve vast communal meals where everyone contributes a cake and the lamplighters spike the shared pot with extra phosphor-dust for the brief, bright, hallucinatory glow it imparts to the feast.

Outsiders are rare. The city is not on any map, and the descent through the throat of roots is not something anyone undertakes casually. But occasionally someone comes—a surface-dweller, blinking and gasping in the wet air, usually looking for something or fleeing something—and the Thread-Set receive them with cautious hospitality. They offer food. They offer a corner of a gall-house to sleep in. They do not offer to let them stay, not right away, but they don’t force them out either. The city decides. If the Underthing doesn’t shift and swallow you in the first month, if the beetles accept your scent, if a climber offers you a moth without biting off your thumb, then you might belong. Then the eyes begin to pale. Then the skin begins to roughen. Then you are Set.

There is a woman living in the third gall-house from the square—her name is Istra, and she came from the surface forty years ago, and her eyes are the palest of anyone living, almost white entirely, as if the city took her particularly to heart. She is the one who speaks for the Underthing when the weights need to interpret a shift or a collapse. She dreams, she says. She dreams in root and fiber and slow green pulse. The children bring her beetle-trance offerings and she tells them stories about the world above, which she barely remembers now, which has become as mythical to her as the Underthing is to you. *The sky*, she says, and the children gasp. *The sky was a ceiling you could never touch, and it burned with cold fire, and sometimes water fell from it all at once, not in cycles, not in steam, but a great drowning pour that meant nothing at all.*

The children don’t believe her. But they love the story anyway. The rain begins again—the seventeenth-minute rain, the engine-rain—and the gutters gurgle, and the moths swirl, and Istra blinks her near-white eyes and turns back to her work, which is tending a small, stubborn fungus garden that grows in the shape of a window she remembers from her childhood, a square of light that never existed down here but persists anyway, in outline, in hope, in the slow deliberate cultivation of what was lost.
