The one who remembers the map is old.

Not old in the way of the groupers, who measure their years in the thickening of their scales, or even the sponges, whose lifetimes are so long that a century is a single slow exhale. She is old in the way the limestone is old—not the stone itself, but the *shaping* of it, the memory of when the grooves were sharp and the halls were hollowed by something other than chemistry.

She does not have a name. Names are vibrations in air, and air is a myth here, a legend told in the bubbles that rise from the deep vents. What she has instead is a *signature*, a chemical self written in the mucus that trails behind her, a taste-story that tells any who brush against it: *I am here, I have always been here, I remember.*

Her body is not like the others.

At first glance, she might be mistaken for an octopus—she has the arms, eight of them, though two have been lost and regrown so many times that they are thinner, paler, more tentative than the rest. But her mantle is wrong. Too long, too low, flattened against the stone in a way that speaks less of swimming and more of creeping, of pressing oneself into crevices so deeply that the stone becomes a second skin. Her skin itself is not skin. Not exactly. It is a living patina, a cultivated garden of symbiotic algae and bacteria that she has tended for longer than the oldest sponge has been filtering. It changes—slowly, slowly—to match the stone, to match the sponge, to match whatever surface she holds herself against. When she is still, she is not merely camouflaged. She is *gone*. A piece of the city that happens to breathe.

Her eyes are large, as the eyes of cephalopods are, but they have a quality the others lack. The octopuses who hunt the edges of the Bowl have eyes that are bright with present-tense cleverness, the darting intelligence of a predator solving a puzzle. Her eyes are *deep*. They hold a stillness that is not patience but something older, something that has seen the same current shift a million times and knows that all things return. Her pupils are not slits but irregular shapes, almost star-shaped, a mutation or an adaptation or simply a sign of her age.

She lives in the side-chamber with the map.

The chamber is small, barely larger than her body when she spreads her arms wide. The current here is so slight that the copepod fog hangs almost motionless, a galaxy of tiny lives suspended in the dim. The map covers one wall—the grooves worn into the limestone, the branching lines, the circles that mark the great halls and the canyon-boulevards. She knows them all. She has traced them with her suckers more times than there are grains in the sediment, and each tracing wears them a little deeper, a little smoother.

She is the one who carves them. She has been carving them for longer than the Bowl has been the Bowl.

No—that is not right. She did not *start* the map. Something else started it, something before her, something whose chemical signature has long since faded from the water. She inherited it. She is the keeper, the maintainer, the one who remembers what the lines mean. And she is the one who adds to it, slowly, with the patience of a creature who has no fear of time.

Her beak is worn. Not from age—though age has worn it—but from use. She scrapes the limestone with it, a slow, rhythmic rasp that matches the pulse of the tide. Each scrape removes a microscopic layer of stone, deepening the groove by a fraction of a hair. She has been scraping the same groove—the one that marks the eastern channel, where the groupers gather during the spawning season—for three hundred tide-cycles now. She could finish it today, but she will not. There is no need. The channel has not changed. It will not change for a thousand more years, unless the current shifts or the ceiling collapses. If it does, she will scrape a new line, correcting the old one, and the correction will be part of the map too, a palimpsest of the city's slow transformation.

She eats the copepods. Not by hunting them—she is too slow for that, and her body is too heavy with the weight of memory. She eats them by extending a thin mucus net from the tips of her arms, a veil of silk that catches the drifting life as the current breathes in and out. When the net is full, she draws it in, folds it into her beak, and swallows the city's flesh without ever leaving her chamber. The copepods do not struggle. They do not even know they have been caught. They simply transition from drifting to digestion, their tiny bodies becoming part of her, becoming the energy that allows her to continue scraping.

She does not need to leave the chamber. She has not left it in—she does not know. Time is different for her. There are cycles: the tide, the spawning seasons, the great storms that sometimes churn the surface and send pulses of cold water down into the Bowl. She has lived through so many of these that they have become a single, blended experience, a texture of existence rather than a sequence of events.

But she does leave. Sometimes.

When she leaves, it is to check the map. She moves along the boulevards and through the halls, her body pressed so flat against the stone that the fish do not see her, the moray eels do not smell her, the groupers perceive her only as a slight change in the texture of the wall. She traces the grooves of the city with her arms, comparing the stone to the map in her chamber, cataloging the slow changes. A new sponge colony has settled in the northern hall; she will need to add it to the map. A pillar has collapsed, worn through by dissolution; she will need to erase it, scraping the old mark smooth and leaving the stone blank. A vent has opened, releasing a new current of warm, mineral-rich water; she will need to add the spiraling lines that indicate the chemical gradient, the taste-sign that will guide future generations to the new source of life.

She is not alone in her work. There are others like her—not many, but a few. She has encountered them, in the long slow drift of her patrols. They do not speak. They do not need to. When two map-keepers meet, they simply pause, their arms extending to touch, their skins shifting to a neutral gray, a color that means *I am not food, I am not threat, I am the same*. They exchange chemical signatures, a brief taste of each other's mucus. The taste tells them everything: the condition of their territory, the changes they have observed, the condition of their maps. Then they part, and they do not meet again for a thousand tides.

She does not know how many of them there are. She has met four, in all the long span of her memory. There may be more, in the deeper reaches of the Bowl, in the chambers that even the current does not reach. There may be fewer. One of the four tasted strange, the last time she met them—their signature was thin, faded, missing the notes that indicated health and continuity. They were dying, or preparing to die, or had already died and their body was simply continuing its motions without the presence inside. She does not know which. She scraped a small mark in the stone at the meeting-place, a single straight line, to indicate that something had ended.

She does not know what the map is *for*. She knows only that it must be maintained. The compulsion is as deep as her hunger, as her need to breathe, as her instinct to press herself into the stone and become invisible. When she feels the urge to scrape, she scrapes. When she feels the urge to patrol, she patrols. The map exists because it has always existed, and it will continue to exist as long as she and the others continue to carve it.

Sometimes, in the quiet between tides, when the copepod fog is thick and the pistol shrimp are silent, she wonders. Her mind is not a human mind, but it is a mind—a slow, associative network of sensations and memories and chemical truths. She wonders who carved the first line. She wonders why the map must show the city, when the city is already here, around her, in every direction. She wonders if the map is for someone who is not here yet, someone who will come from above or below or beyond, someone who will need to know the halls and channels and the locations of the vents and the spawning grounds.

She wonders if the map *is* the city. If the city exists in her carvings, in the grooves worn into the stone, as much as it exists in the limestone and the sponge and the fish. If the map is the true Bowl, and the physical city is just its shadow, its reflection, its working model. If she and the others are not keepers but *creators*, shaping the city by shaping its image, calling the halls into being by the act of carving them.

These thoughts are too large for her. They drift through her mind like the copepod fog, tiny and translucent and impossible to hold. She lets them drift. She returns to her scraping.

The tide shifts. The city responds—the sponges turn, the fish reorient, the rain-on-a-distant-roof sound changes its rhythm. And in the side-chamber, the old one continues her work, her beak rasping against the stone, deepening the groove that marks the eastern channel, adding the microscopic layer of her existence to the map that has been growing for longer than memory, in the city that grows itself.
