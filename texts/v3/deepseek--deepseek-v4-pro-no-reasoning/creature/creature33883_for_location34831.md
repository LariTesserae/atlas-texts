There is a figure that lives here. It is not the only thing that lives here—there are the pale lung-sponges, the skittering shapes at the edge of seeing—but it is the one that notices the droplets and counts forty-seven seconds between percussive knocks.

It was human once, or its ancestors were. The distinction has blurred over the generations. It has lived in this sunken city for longer than it can measure, because time down here is marked not by light but by cycles of the drone, the shifting condensation, the slow release of spores. It knows these cycles the way a tree knows its seasons. It has no name; names were a thing for when there were others to call to. Now there is only the city and the sound, and the sound is enough.

The figure is tall and narrow, shaped like a question mark from malnutrition and the constant bending needed to navigate the buckled floors and the slumped geometries of the ancient corridors. Its skin is pale, nearly translucent, with a faint blue tracery of veins visible beneath, and it has grown a fine down over its body—not fur, exactly, but a soft, colorless fuzz that helps trap the humid warmth and helps it feel the subtle shifts in air current when something approaches in the dark. Its eyes are large and dark-adapted, with a reflective layer that makes them gleam strangely if you happen to point a light at it—though it would flinch and vanish before you could get a good look. Its ears are long and lobed, folded intricately, highly sensitive to the drone’s changes. It has lost all but four of its teeth—not through decay, but through a slow evolutionary pressure toward softness; it feeds on things that do not need much chewing.

It does not eat the lung-sponges, though it has tried. They are bitter and leave a burning sensation in the throat. Instead, it harvests a thin, mineral-rich film that accumulates on the walls during the streaming phase of the condensation cycle. It uses a flat stone tool to scrape this film into a shallow shell—a real shell, from some creature that must have lived here long ago and left only this one artifact—and eats it slowly, with the same three fingers that end in sensitive, spatulate pads. The film tastes of salt and copper and something faintly sweet, like old blood, like the memory of sugar. It gives just enough energy, enough trace elements, to keep the narrow machinery of the body running. The figure knows exactly how much is needed. It has not grown taller in a long, long time.

It builds things. Not large things—the city does not need building; it has already built more than anyone could use. Instead, the figure constructs small, meticulous objects from materials it finds: a lattice of wire and resin that holds a captured lung-sponge spore at its center, a series of nested metal bowls that ring at different frequencies when struck by a falling droplet, a figurine made of ceramic fragments and bone that stands in a niche and has no face. These objects are not useful, exactly, but they are necessary. The figure needs to make them the way it needs to eat. It is how it knows it is still present, still a self moving through space and time, not just another process of the old city.

It has learned the drone’s language, or something like it. The drone is not one sound but many layered, and within the layers are patterns, repetitions, long melodies played out over hours. The figure hums along, a low sound that blends with the walls’ own resonance. It has discovered that certain frequencies, sung at certain times, will cause the distant groaning to pause and then resume, as if something is listening. It does not know if it is communicating with a machine, a ghost, or the city itself. It does not care. The songs are a form of company, and they fill the long hours when the condensation is beading and the spores are not yet due.

The figure sleeps in a hollow between two slumped structural members, both warm from the same source that heats the walls. It lines the hollow with a mat of shed fuzz, accumulated over years, and curls into a tight comma. While it sleeps, it dreams of the world above—not as it is, but as the figure imagines it: a place of brightness and hunger, where the air is thin and the ground is dry, and things move so quickly they burn. It is not a pleasant dream. It wakes often, roused by the drone’s shifts, by the knock that comes twice, always twice, with its forty-seven-second pause. It has never found the source of the knock. It has searched, sometimes for what felt like days, following the acoustics down corridors that turned back on themselves, through chambers so vast the walls disappeared in the dark. It always ends up back at its hollow, with the knock still coming, always the same distance away. Eventually it stopped searching. The knock is now just part of the body of sound, like the drone, the groaning, the fall of water. It is a pulse. It is the city’s heartbeat. The figure no longer asks whose heart it is.

There is no fear in it. This is the most important thing. It lives without terror because it cannot remember ever having been outside this dark, except in dreams, and the dreams are more like legends than memories. It fears nothing because everything here is known, from the slow seep of water to the occasional pale shape that slides behind a pillar and is never seen again. Even the moments when the drone intensifies, building and building until the walls vibrate and the pools shiver with standing waves—even then, it feels something that might be awe, but not panic. It presses its back against the warm nearest wall and hums the drone’s lowest note, holding it until the wave passes. The city shakes. The figure does not fall.

It has found bones, sometimes, in the far chambers. Large bones, from animals that must have been brought here from above, or that evolved here and died here, or that were placed here for reasons that no longer signify. The figure collects them sometimes, not to use but simply to have. They are the only other solid evidence of life it knows, besides the lung-sponges and the skittering things. It arranges them in a spiral on the floor of a high-ceilinged gallery, tailbones at the center, skulls facing outward. It does not know why it does this. It does it because the symmetry pleases some part of it that nothing else reaches. It does it and then sits at the center of the spiral and sings for an hour, or two, or three, while the bones grow warm and the drone answers back in harmonic thirds.

Once, many cycles of the stream-scrape cycle ago, the figure found a live thing. It was an animal—soft-bodied, eyeless, with a row of tiny legs along its underside, all pale and glistening, its movements like ripples of water over stone. The figure watched it for a long time. The animal did not flee. It moved slowly, feeling its way with damp whiskers that grew from its anterior segment, edging along a wall where the condensation film was thick and rich. The figure’s heart—a heart that had been quiet for so long—struck hard against its ribs, twice, and then paused, and then struck again. It watched the creature feed. It did not touch it. It did not sing. It let the animal move off into the dark, leaving a faint trail of mucus that glimmered under the figure’s dark-adapted stare. Afterward, the figure wept. It did not know why. The tears were warm and thin, barely different from the condensation. It licked them off its fingers. They were as salt as the film on the walls, and the salt was a comfort. Since then, the figure has left a small offering at the place where it saw the creature—a smooth stone, a twist of metal—whenever the streaming phase is rich and the drone is low. It does not know if the creature understands offerings. It does not need it to understand. It needs only to make them, and to feel the quiet symmetry of the act settle into the city’s endless, humming dark.
