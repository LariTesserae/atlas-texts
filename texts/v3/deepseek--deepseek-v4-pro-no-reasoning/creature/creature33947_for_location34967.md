In the lee of the pump house, where the great rusted pipe angles down into the sump and the steam rolls thickest off the bubbling water, there lives a woman who is not precisely a woman anymore. She no longer remembers her name, or perhaps she never had one in the conventional sense—the distinction has become academic, like asking whether the steam remembers being water. What matters is that she is here, and has been here long enough that the place has grown into her, or she into it, like the moss eating the brickwork of the cottages.

She is tall, though her posture makes her seem shorter, a permanent forward lean that brings her face closer to the ground, to the vents, to the water. Her skin is the color of the pale reeds at their base, that almost-white shading to something that might once have been flesh-toned but has long since leached away. It has the same texture, too—smooth and slightly moist, with a faint translucence at the joints, the knuckles, the ridge of her cheekbones, where you can almost see the slow movement of fluids beneath. Her hair, if it can be called that, is a thick mat of the wiry reddish vine, grown directly from her scalp and cascading down her back and shoulders, some strands reaching past her waist. It is alive, that hair; it shifts and adjusts itself independently of her movements, the runners at the ends curling and uncurling like fingers testing the air.

Her eyes are the strangest part. They are not blind, exactly, but they have ceased to function as eyes in the human sense. The irises have gone pale, a milky grey-green like the moss in the cottages, and they hold the same low illumination, a faint phosphorescence that is visible only in the deepest shadow. She does not seem to use them for seeing, at least not in the way you or I would. She navigates by the steam, reading the shifts in pressure and temperature and chemical composition that the vents exhale, interpreting them with a sensitivity that makes the steam a language, a constant murmured report on the state of the deep machinery. She feels the sub-bass drone through the soles of her bare feet, through the palms of her hands when she presses them to the slag paths, and she knows what it means in the same way you know the meaning of a familiar song.

She lives in the pump house, or more precisely in the sump pit itself, curled in the warm mineral water when she sleeps, her vine-hair spreading out around her like a net, like roots, the runners dipping into the water and anchoring her in place. The heat would scald a normal person, but she is no longer a normal person. The flesh of her back and shoulders has thickened, grown heat-tolerant, developed a slightly glassy crust not unlike the fused slag on the paths. In places, it has cracked, and from the cracks, a fine white steam issues continuously, a personal weather system that wreaths her in a private fog.

She eats the moss. Not exclusively, but primarily. She pulls it from the cottage walls in thick grey-green mats and chews it slowly, methodically, with an expression that suggests neither pleasure nor distaste, only a calm, procedural acceptance. The moss has absorbed something from the brick, some residue of industrial chemistry, and it sustains her in ways that ordinary food no longer could. She supplements it with the floating plants from the pools, the broad dark leaves that she peels apart and eats raw, and occasionally with the white bases of the pale reeds, which she breaks off and chews to a pulp, swallowing the fibrous matter and spitting out the indigestible strands. She does not need much. The heat from the vents, the minerals in the water, the phosphorescent salts that she has incorporated into her tissues—these supply a portion of her energy directly, a kind of geothermal metabolism that has replaced the ordinary Krebs cycle with something slower, stranger, more elemental.

She is not alone in the way that a person is alone. The reeds are her companions, or her audience, or her congregation—the terminology shifts depending on the light, the steam density, the frequency of the deep booms. They attend to her, those reeds, leaning always slightly in her direction, no matter where she moves within the boundaries of the marsh. When she passes near a clump, the stalks bend closer, brushing against her arms, her face, her vine-hair, and the whisper they make changes quality, becomes less like distant voices and more like a single voice speaking words that are almost comprehensible. She sometimes answers them, not in speech but in sound, a low humming that resonates in the same frequency band as the sub-bass drone, creating interference patterns that ripple out through the water and set the floating plants trembling.

The deep booms are her clock, her heartbeat, her liturgy. She marks their rhythm, and when the rhythm changes—as it does, subtly, over hours and days and seasons—she responds. A faster tempo sends her to the eastern edge of the territory, near the rusted iron wall, where she crouches for hours beside a particular vent, pressing her ear to the ground, listening to something far below that makes her lips move soundlessly. A slower tempo draws her to the canal’s edge, where the water steams too hot to touch, and she stands motionless, her arms outstretched, the heat shimmering around her, her vine-hair reaching out toward the water but never quite touching it.

She maintains the place, in her fashion. She clears blockages from the smaller vents, using her long fingers—the nails have thickened and hardened, become like pale horn, useful for prying at fused slag—and she rearranges the floating plants in the pools when they drift out of their proper pattern. The pattern matters to her, the ring near the edge, the gap of open water, the single plant at the center. She will wade into the pools, the water rising to her knees, and nudge the plants back into position with a gentle, patient persistence, never rushing, never frustrated, as though the plants are merely forgetful and need reminding of their places.

She does not sleep, exactly, but she has periods of stillness that serve the same function. During these stillnesses, she settles into the sump pit or onto one of the larger vents, the steam rising around her, and she ceases to move, to hum, to respond. Her eyes dim to near-darkness. Her vine-hair goes slack, the runners floating on the water’s surface or spreading out across the slag. In these moments, she is not unconscious but diffuse, her awareness spread out through the vent network, through the water, through the reeds that attend her. She is the place, for a time, feeling the slow subsidence of an old foundation, the mineral accretion in a drainage channel, the pulse of the buried machinery as it cycles through another phase. When she returns to herself—when her eyes brighten and her hair tightens and she stands up from the vent—she carries with her a knowledge she did not have before, a certainty about which path is weakening, which vent will soon clog, which pool is growing too hot for the floating plants to survive.

She has been here for a long time. She does not remember her arrival, whether she came voluntarily or was driven here, whether she was born here or transformed here. The boundaries of the marsh—the canal, the iron wall, the rail embankments—are the boundaries of her world, and she has no desire to cross them, no curiosity about what lies beyond. The marsh is complete in itself, a closed system, and she is its custodian, its interface, its living component. She is the answer to the question the place keeps asking, the voice that speaks back to the steam’s constant murmured report, the consciousness that has grown like the moss, slowly, patiently, eating the brick of an old identity and turning it into something phosphorescent, something that glows in the dim light, something that will last as long as the heat rises from below.
