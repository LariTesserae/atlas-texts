A man—if you can call him that—is squatting on his haunches at the edge of one of the old stone walls, watching a bladder inflate.

He doesn't look up when you see him. It's possible he hasn't noticed you at all, or it's possible he noticed you long before you noticed him and simply chose not to react. In this place, where everything is constantly becoming and unbecoming, a stillness like his is more startling than any motion. He is utterly motionless, balanced on the balls of his feet with a practiced, anatomical precision, his forearms resting on his knees, his hands hanging loose. The posture is not a rest. It is a waiting shape. A held note.

He is old, but old in a way that has nothing to do with years. His skin has the quality of something that has been dried and rehydrated too many times—dense with creases, but not fragile. The lines on his face do not radiate outward from expression points the way a normal face's do. They run in long, parallel striations, as if his skin were a sediment that had settled in layers, each one recording a different era of stillness. His eyes are pale, almost colorless in the amber light, the iris faded to a watery ring around pupils that are contracted to pinpricks. He does not squint. He does not seem to need to.

He is naked, or nearly so. A strip of what must once have been cloth is wrapped around his waist, but it has been patched and over-patched so many times with strips of the pale, fibrous tissue harvested from the mat itself that it's impossible to tell where the original material ends and the botanic repairs begin. The fabric has fused into a kind of living composite, and if you watch closely, you can see the faintest pulse moving through the plant fibers, a sluggish circulation that is not his own. He is wearing a graft. He has, in some small way, let the place begin to wear him.

His feet are bare. The soles are thick, callused into something that resembles hoof or horn, stained a deep greenish-brown from decades of contact with the living floor. His toenails are yellow and dense, curled slightly under, like the claws of a thing that digs but no longer needs to. He has no hair on his head—not baldness, but an absence, as though the follicles themselves gave up long ago and sealed over. What hair remains on his body is limited to his forearms and a sparse scattering across his chest, and it is white, brittle, and oddly still, unaffected by the constant vibrational movement of the air. It does not stir. It is as if the sound simply passes around him, or through him without resistance.

Around his neck, on a cord woven from the same pale root-fiber that laces the stone pillar's bowl, hangs a single object. It is a tooth. Not a human tooth—too large, too curved, the enamel grooved with a pattern that suggests something reptilian, something that does not exist anywhere in the churning faunal chaos of the mat. It is fossilized, heavy, the color of old ivory gone to stone. It is very, very old. Older than the plant. Older than the chasm. He touches it sometimes, absently, his thumb moving across its ridged surface as if reading a text he has long since memorized, a braille of deep time.

He lives here. That much is clear from the way he occupies space—not as an intruder, not as a survivor huddled in a hostile environment, but as a feature of the landscape, something that belongs the way the stone walls belong, the way the pillar belongs. The mat has grown around him, not over him. There is a small depression in the living floor a few feet to his left, a bowl-shaped concavity in the tissue that bears the impression of a human body. He sleeps there. The plant has accommodated his shape, or perhaps he has lain there so long that the plant has simply stopped trying to reclaim that particular space. The edges of the depression are smooth, sealed, a callus on the flesh of the world.

His shelter, if it can be called that, is one of the ancient stone enclosures. He has roofed it—not with the stones the original builders might have used, but with a lattice of dried faunal bones and the stiff, fibrous stems of the plants that stiffen and die. The construction is not architectural. It is nest-like, a weaving, a basket turned upside down. It sheds the occasional drip of sap from the canopy above. It would not keep out wind or cold, but there is no wind here and the cold is unknown. It keeps out nothing. It is a gesture, not a barrier. A statement of *here*.

He eats. You can see the evidence of it scattered around his resting place—the husks of the ephemeral creatures, their soft bodies consumed, their brittle limbs discarded. He does not cook them. There is no fire here, no fuel that would burn in the humid, oxygen-thick air. His teeth, when he opens his mouth slightly to breathe, are worn down to flat planes, the enamel scoured into faint parallel grooves from a lifetime of grinding chitin and cartilage. Some are missing. The gaps have not been filled by anything but time. He swallows the creatures whole when they are small enough; the larger ones he tears apart with his hands, and you can see the evidence of that, too, in the way his fingernails are rimmed with a permanent dark residue, the keratin themselves stained and split from the work.

He drinks rarely. When a bladder inflates near him, he watches it with the same still patience he watches everything, and if it swells to the right size, the right tension, he will rise—slowly, joints creaking like the groan of the heated metal in the sound above—and approach it. He does not pierce it. He presses his mouth directly to the translucent wall and waits. Eventually, the bladder will release on its own, a slow seepage rather than a burst, and he will catch the fluid in his mouth. It is a communion, not a consumption. The plant gives; he receives. He has learned to ask politely.

He has a name. It is not a sound anymore—to speak here would be futile, the roar of growth swallowing any voice less than a god's—but it exists in the way his lips sometimes move when he is alone, shaping a silent syllable over and over. It is a name that belonged to the people who built the stone walls. He is the last of them. The knowledge of this sits in him not as grief, not as loneliness, but as a simple fact, like the heat or the sound or the color of the air. He is the last, and when he stops, the name will stop with him, and the wall-builders will be fully absorbed into the tissue of the world, their stones dissolved, their pillar toppled, their memory exhaled into the atmosphere as another component of the warm, electric, eternal breath.

He does not know you are there. Or he does not care. He watches the bladder inflate, his pale eyes reflecting its swollen curve, and he waits. He has been waiting for something for a very long time—not rescue, not death, not the end of the sound. Something else. Something the tooth around his neck reminds him of. Something the world, in all its furious creation, has not yet managed to produce again. And until it does, he will remain exactly here, squatting at the edge of the stone, a held note, a paused breath, the final syllable of a language the plant has never learned to speak.
