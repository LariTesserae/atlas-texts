The Thread-Keepers do not call themselves anything. They do not have names, or language in the way you would recognize it, or even a clear sense of where they end and the city begins. But they are here, and they are what lives in this place.

They are tall. That is the first thing you notice, when you finally see one clearly—seven feet at the shoulder, eight when they stretch upward to tend a high gallery, their bodies pulling long and thin like taffy. They are bipedal, vaguely, but the resemblance to a human shape is something your mind imposes, a best-fit approximation that falls apart the longer you look. Their limbs are too many, or too few depending on the moment: arms that fork at the elbow, hands that are also feet, a spine that can articulate in places no spine should bend. They move like dancers underwater, slow and continuous, and when they pause they don’t hold still so much as *settle*, their tissues relaxing into a resting geometry like dough proving on a counter.

Their skin is the same material as the walls. Literally the same. The pale beige, the faint translucency, the fine hairlessness—when a Thread-Keeper presses its palm against a pillar, the boundary blurs, and for a moment you cannot tell where the creature ends and the architecture begins. They are not separate organisms from the city. They are its fingers. Its gardeners. Its thoughts given temporary locomotion. A Thread-Keeper is a piece of the Root-City that has decided, for a while, to be ambulatory.

Their faces are the hardest part to hold in your mind. There is a head—a smooth ovoid with no visible ears or nose—and there are eyes, but they are not paired in the way you expect. A Thread-Keeper might have one eye, or three, or a ring of six arranged around the crown of its skull like a diadem, and the eyes are not eyes but lucid windows into the organism’s interior, showing a pale blue glow that pulses faintly with what might be circulation. The face is dominated by a mouth, but it’s a mouth built for work, not speech: a complicated vertical slit that can purse, dilate, or extrude a cluster of fine manipulator tendrils. You see one use these tendrils to weave a damaged wall-section back together, the filaments moving with the speed and precision of a knitting machine, laying down new tissue in a spiral pattern that hardens within seconds.

They have a society. It is not a human society—there is no government, no economy, no art in any representational sense—but it is a society nonetheless, a set of ordered relationships and shared purposes. The Thread-Keepers come in three castes, or perhaps three life-stages, and the distinctions are visible once you learn to look.

The smallest are the **Nippers**. They are about the size of a large dog, compact and quick, and they are the only Keepers that move with anything resembling urgency. Their job is edges: trimming the delicate margins where new growth is pushing too fast, snipping away malformed tissue, pruning the city’s endless self-revision. You see a Nipper at work on a spiral staircase that has gotten overenthusiastic, extruding steps in a frenzy of fractal repetition. The Nipper moves along the edge, and where its mouth-tendrils touch, the excess tissue dissolves back into the wall with a faint sizzle. The staircase settles into a neat, usable form. The Nipper pauses, its single eye scanning the result, then flows away into a pore in the floor. It does not reappear.

The middle caste are the **Weavers**. These are the ones you see most often, the seven-foot ambulatory architects. A Weaver’s work is constructive: laying down new halls, reinforcing buttresses, guiding the city’s slow expansion according to some pattern they carry within their bodies. They do not consult plans. The plan is in their tissues, encoded in the same spiral mathematics that governs the whole organism. When two Weavers meet in a corridor, they pause and face each other, and for a long moment they stand perfectly still while their mouth-tendrils extend and touch, intertwining briefly. Information passes between them—chemical, electrical, perhaps something deeper. Then they separate and continue their work, and the city has slightly adjusted its growth trajectory in a way that will not become apparent for another three hundred years.

And then there are the **Old Ones**. You see only one, in all your time in the Root-City, and you are not sure whether you should call it a single individual or a place that has become a person.

It sits—or grows, or is embedded—in a vast chamber far below the concourse, a space so large that the far walls are lost in blue haze. The floor here is not floor but a shallow lake of the same yellow gel you saw in the industrial hall, warm and faintly rippled, and rising from the center of this lake is the Old One. It is enormous. Forty feet tall at least, a pillar of tissue that branches into a canopy of arms, dozens of them, each arm ending in a hand that is constantly moving, writing patterns in the air, dipping down to touch the gel and coming up with a thread of new-grown fiber that it weaves into the structure around it. The Old One’s face—if it is a face—covers the entire upper surface of the pillar, a radial array of eyes and mouths and sensing organs, all of them working independently, all of them processing some immense ongoing calculation.

The Old One is the city’s central node, or one of them, or perhaps the memory of the founder, preserved and grown and adapted over geological time. It does not acknowledge you. It does not acknowledge the Weavers that occasionally approach it, wading through the gel to press their palms against its base. But when a Weaver does this, it comes away changed—moving differently, with new patterns in its posture, new instructions flowing through its limbs. The Old One is dreaming the city into being, and the Weavers are the dreams made flesh.

The Thread-Keepers do not sleep. They do not eat, in any conventional sense—they draw energy directly from the city’s metabolic grid, the slow electron flow that hums through every wall. You see a Weaver pause in its work, press both hands flat against a buttress, and go still for ten minutes, fifteen, while a faint pulse travels up its arms. Recharging. The city feeds them, and they feed the city with their labor, and the distinction between the two is so thin as to be meaningless. They are a node in a network that has been refining itself since before your species discovered fire.

They are aware of you. They have been aware of you since you entered. But their concept of *visitor* is very different from your concept of *self*, and they process your presence the way you might notice a new freckle on your arm: with mild, unhurried interest. A Weaver approaches you once, moving with that underwater slowness, and stands at a distance of ten feet. Its three eyes—one central, two on stalks that emerge from its shoulders—all orient on you. Its mouth opens slightly. The tendrils extend, waving, tasting the air. You feel a gentle pressure in your mind, nothing so crude as telepathy, more like a sense that something is softly leafing through your memories, pausing at images, moving on. The Weaver withdraws after a moment. It has learned what it needed to learn, or decided you are not a threat, or simply lost interest. It flows away up a wall and disappears into a high arch.

The fauna notice them too. The petal-skinned lizards ride on the Weavers’ shoulders sometimes, a half-dozen of the small creatures clinging to the pale skin like decorations, and the Weavers do not seem to mind. The dangling intestine-creatures are, you realize, a larval form of the Nippers—they pupate in the wall-pores and emerge smaller and faster, ready to begin their work. The whole ecosystem interpenetrates. The Thread-Keepers are not masters of this place. They are a specialized organ, like the hands of a body, and the body is the city, and the city is alive, and it is all one thing.

Sometimes, a Weaver will pause in its work and put its hand against a wall, and the wall will open. Not like a door—more like a blink, a momentary aperture that reveals a small chamber within. The Weaver will produce something from the chamber: a knot of fiber, a node of the yellow gel encased in translucent membrane, a tool that looks like a cross between a spindle and a living bone. The tools are grown, not made. They are extensions of the Weavers’ own bodies, and when a tool is worn out, the Weaver simply presses it back into the wall and draws out a fresh one. There is no waste here. Everything cycles back.

You watch a Weaver build a bridge. It is the most extraordinary thing you have ever seen. The creature stands at the edge of a gap—one of the rare places where the city’s growth has left a chasm between two districts—and it raises its arms. From its palms, from its mouth, from pores that open all along its flanks, fine threads begin to emerge. They are not extruded so much as *grown*, knitting themselves out of the air, and the Weaver guides them with its tendrils, weaving them together into a pale continuous membrane. The bridge takes shape in midair, anchored to both sides, the threads thickening and merging until the surface is solid enough to walk on. The whole process takes less than an hour. When it is done, the Weaver tests the bridge with one foot, then another, then walks across and continues its work on the far side. Behind it, the bridge is already thickening, developing railings, blending its edges into the surrounding architecture. In a day it will be indistinguishable from any other part of the city.

The Thread-Keepers make no music. They make no art. But there is something like art in the way they move, the deliberate grace of their gestures, the patterns they trace in the air as they work. And there is something like song in the low hum that the Weavers produce when they are building together—a frequency that resonates through the walls and makes the fine hairs on your arms stand up. You realize, after a long time listening, that the hum is not a single note but a complex chord, shifting slowly, and that the Weavers are harmonizing with each other, adjusting their individual frequencies to create interference patterns that guide the growth of the city. They are singing the Root-City into existence, note by note, cell by cell, century by century.

The quiet rating was a lie, you decide. The place is full of sound. You just had to learn how to hear it.

You walk for what feels like days. The light never changes. The Thread-Keepers come and go, ignoring you or observing you with that mild, momentary interest. You pass through districts that seem older—the tissue here is denser, darker, more intricately worked—and districts that are barely formed, the walls still webbed with the filaments of ongoing construction. You find a place where the floor drops away into a vertical shaft that descends beyond the reach of the glow, and when you drop a pebble down it, you never hear it land. You find a gallery where the walls are covered in patterns—not writing, not images, but something between the two, spirals and branching forms that seem to mean something just beyond your grasp. A Weaver is there, touching the patterns with its tendrils, and you understand that this is a library, of sorts. The patterns are records. Memories. The city’s autobiography, written in the only medium it knows.

At one point, you sit down to rest on another extruded bench—they do seem to appear when you need them—and a small Nipper approaches you. It is carrying something in its mouth-tendrils. It places the object on the bench beside you, waits, and then scuttles away. The object is a node of the yellow gel, about the size of a walnut, glowing faintly. When you pick it up, it is warm, and it pulses against your palm like a heartbeat. You don’t know what it is. An offering. A communication. A battery. A seed. You put it in your pocket. It continues to pulse, slow and steady, a second heart against your thigh.

You find no exits. The stairs you came down are still there—you check—but you are not sure you want to leave. The Root-City has a pull to it, a strange comfort. The coolth. The silence. The endless patient growth. The sense of being inside something so much larger than yourself, something that does not judge or fear or hurry. The Thread-Keepers have no word for *death*, you suspect, because the city does not die—it only changes, adapts, grows over and through itself. What looks like an individual is just a temporary eddy in an unbroken flow of tissue and purpose and time.

After a long while—hours, days, the light does not mark the difference—you see something new. A Weaver is standing at the base of a pillar, and it is *still*. Not the "resting" stillness you have seen before, a pause in motion, but an absolute cessation. It is sinking into the pillar. The tissue of its body is merging with the wall, losing definition, the boundaries going indistinct. You watch, transfixed, as the Weaver becomes a bas-relief, then a texture, then nothing but a subtle bulge in the pillar's surface that will, in time, smooth away entirely. The caste system, you realize, is not a hierarchy but a cycle. Nippers become Weavers. Weavers become Old Ones. Old Ones become city. And the city, in its slow eternal growth, gives birth to new Nippers, pale and quick and eager to begin the work again.

The Thread-Keepers are not a species. They are a lifecycle. The city is not a place. It is a body. And you are standing inside it, breathing its air, carrying its seed in your pocket, and wondering if perhaps you have been here too long already.

The bench reshapes itself under you, subtly, making room for the new warmth of your body. You stand up. You start walking again. Somewhere ahead, a staircase spirals up into the blue dimness, and the light follows you, and the city watches with a thousand pale eyes, and waits.
