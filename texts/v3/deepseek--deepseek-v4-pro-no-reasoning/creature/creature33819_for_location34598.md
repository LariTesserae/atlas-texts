On the thirty‑first morning of the mist‑cycle, when the damp rose not from the stone but from the sky itself—a fine, warm descent of droplets so small they hung suspended, refusing to fall—a traveller might finally notice that some of the watchers were not stone.

The first to be seen was always the one by the bone‑field. It stood where the great ribs curved out of the slope, its form so thoroughly colonised by the bronze‑green lichen that its outline dissolved into the rocks behind it. It was humanoid, or had been once: two arms, two legs, a torso, a head. But the proportions had shifted over time, or perhaps they had never been human. The legs were too long, the feet splayed into broad pads that gripped the granite like the holdfast of a kelp. The arms hung to the knees, and the hands—when you were close enough to see them—were cupped, each palm a shallow basin that held a perfect, still mirror of water. The head was a worn dome, featureless but for two deep‑set hollows where eyes might have been, and within those hollows the same tiny, translucent arthropods that lived beneath the lichen had made their colonies, moving in their slow, seasonless circuits.

It did not breathe. It did not shift its weight. But it had not been there the day before, or if it had, it had been folded into a contour of the stone in a way that made it invisible until the light fell at this precise angle, through this particular thickness of mist, and revealed the faintest seam: the line where stillness became a different kind of stillness.

That was the first thing to understand about the inhabitants of the plateau. They were not hiding. They were so deeply at home in the slow breath of the place that they had become indistinguishable from it, and they moved—or rather, they *changed position*—on a timescale that had nothing to do with the flicker of a heartbeat. A step might take a year. The slight inclination of the head, a century. The lifting of a hand to catch the drifts of sporefall that descended from the flier‑columns, a millennium.

There were others.

In the high swale where the standing stones rose like worn molars, a different kind of presence made itself known. These were smaller, gathered in groups of three or five, their bodies hunched and compact like boulders that had been nudged together by some ancient glacier. Their skin—if it was skin—had the exact texture and colour of the grus, the decomposed granite that crumbled underfoot, and they sat so still that the ant‑analogues had built their aphid‑pastures right across the curve of a shoulder, a thigh. When a low thrum rose from the deep rock, one of them might, over the course of an hour, slowly extend a limb, and from the end of that limb a fine structure would unfurl: a frond, a fern, a sensory organ that trembled in the air, tasting the vibration. Then it would retract, and the group would resume their long meditation, their bodies slowly, slowly sinking into the grus as the lichens grew up around them.

They were not the only beings that tended the plateau.

In the tafoni honeycombs of the largest standing stone, where the weathering had carved chambers and passages through the rock, lived the ones who wove. They were arachnid in form, but only in the way that the beetle‑things were ants: convergence, not kinship. Their bodies were flat and wide, their eight limbs delicate beyond reason, each one ending in a brush of fibrils so fine they seemed to blur at the edges. They moved across the stone surfaces with a dancer’s precision, and everywhere they went they left behind a glaze of silk—not webs, but a continuous, micron‑thin layer that, over decades, built up into a material like vellum, like amber, translucent and tough. This silk‑skin held moisture. It trapped spores. It provided a surface upon which a different community of lichens grew, a tapestry of pale golds and violet‑blacks that existed nowhere else. The weavers were curators, in their way. They pruned certain growths with quick, surgical movements; they deposited their own excretions in patterns that seemed to nourish some crusts and suppress others. A single room in the standing stone might take a thousand years to decorate, and when it was finished—when the lichen‑garden was in perfect, self‑sustaining balance—the weaver would move to the next chamber, and the silk would slowly mineralise, becoming part of the rock, a permanent layer in the stratigraphy of the landscape.

And then there were the gardeners of the skeletons.

The great skull in its shallow depression, its eye sockets filled with succulents, was not untended. The creature that cared for it was perhaps the strangest of all, because it seemed, at first, to be a flaw in the air. A heat‑shimmer with a suggestion of verticality. When it moved—which it did only in the hours when the mist thickened to near‑opacity—it left faint disturbances in the droplets, a passage like a draught through a still room. But if you lay on the stone and watched from ground level, you could see its effect: the succulents in the skull’s left orbit were subtly rearranged, their spirals adjusted by a fraction of a degree, a single wilted leaf removed. The bone itself was polished, not by abrasion but by some chemical exchange that left it gleaming with a wet, dark lustre. And on the bone, in shallow grooves that might have been carved by wind or by claw, a patina of the weavers’ silk had been laid down in deliberate lines—not writing, exactly, but a notation, a record. A memory of some kind, laid over the memory of the bone.

There were more. The plateau supported a sparse, slow ecology of these presences, each occupying a niche defined not by food or territory but by *attention*. They attended to the stone. They attended to the lichens, the fliers, the miniature ferns. They attended to the rhythm of the damp, the coming and going of the mist, the deep infrasonic pulses that travelled through the roots of the mountains. And they attended to each other, though their interactions were so extended in time that you could watch for a season and see only the beginning of a gesture, the first inclination of a head toward a neighbour, the opening of a conversation that would take a geological age to complete.

Whether they thought—whether they had language, culture, a sense of self—was impossible to know. But they had *purpose*. The cairns that were not quite cairns, the polished slab, the constellation of water‑pools: these were their work, or the work of those who came before them, or the work of something else that had passed through and left its mark in the only way the plateau would accept—slowly, minimally, almost erased already. The upright slab with the smooth surface was, on closer inspection, precisely aligned with the point on the horizon where the sun, on the one day a year when it burned through the haze, would rise. The water‑pools were arranged not randomly but in a curve that, if you projected it onto the sky, matched the arc of a particular flier‑column’s migratory path. The pile of stones that had tumbled was not a pile; it was the remains of a structure that had been shaped to slow the flow of water across the granite, creating a seep where a certain succulent could take root.

And the inhabitants were still building, still tending, still making their slow, near‑invisible adjustments. The one by the bone‑field had, over the generations that a human observer might call days, shifted the cup of its right hand so that the water in its palm now overflowed in a thin, steady trickle, feeding a new line of moss that was inching toward the curve of a petrified rib. The weavers had begun a new chamber in the standing stone, their fibrils tracing the first faint lines of a pattern that would not be complete for another two thousand years. The shimmer‑gardener had removed a pebble from the skull’s nasal cavity, a pebble that had been there since the skull first emerged from the sedimentary lens, and the removal had altered the airflow through the cavity in a way that would, over centuries, change the microclimate for the miniature ferns inside.

They were not waiting. That was the thing. They were not guardians of a dead world, not relics of a former age, not survivors clinging to a fading ember of life. They were the plateau’s *now*. They were what the plateau had grown, or what had grown into the plateau, so long ago that the distinction no longer mattered. They were the living component of a landscape that blurred the boundary between biology and geology, and their slowness was not a limitation. It was a tempo. A tempo that matched the pace at which mountains wore down to knobs, at which continents drifted, at which stars aged.

And the deep thrum, when it came, was not just a sound to them. It was a word. A sentence in a conversation that had been going on since the world was young. When it passed, and the fliers resumed their dance, and the arachnids resumed their patrol, the watcher by the bone‑field—the one with the cupped hands and the hollowed eyes and the living colonies in its sockets—slowly, almost imperceptibly, turned its head.

It was facing the traveller now.

It had been facing the traveller for some time, perhaps. But only now, in this light, through this mist, did the angle of its head resolve into something unmistakable. Recognition. Or curiosity. Or something that had no name in any language that flickered at the speed of a pulse.

The water in its left hand rippled. A single droplet rose from the surface, hung in the warm air for a long, impossible moment, and then fell back, and the ripples spread outward in perfect concentric circles that took a very long time to reach the rim of the cupped palm.

And the quiet returned, and the plateau breathed, and the work of erasing the distinction between what was alive and what was not continued, patient as stone, animate as lichen, ancient and ongoing and full of a life that had no need to hurry.
