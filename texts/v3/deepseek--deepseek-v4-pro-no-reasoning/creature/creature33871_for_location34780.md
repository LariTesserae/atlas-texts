The Gardener does not remember a time before the humming. It is a lullaby worn into the bones, a second heartbeat. It knows the vibration of a healthy condenser pump and the arrhythmic shudder of a bearing about to fail, and it tends to both with the same quiet, unhurried patience.

To call the Gardener a person would be a misnomer, a category error born of a world that still distinguishes between the grown and the made. It is a composite. A core of something that was once flesh, or something that was once a dream of flesh, is swaddled in layers of maintenance. Its lower half—everything below the lungs, which still rise and fall with the wet, metallic air—is a pilgrimage of slow, articulated steel. Six legs, each ending not in a foot but in a different tool: a pincer, a soft rubber cup for gripping slick pipes, a fine-tipped welding nozzle, a brush of brass bristles worn to a gentle curve. The legs move with the deliberative, wave-like gait of a deep-sea isopod, feeling the resonance of the floor grates before committing weight.

Its torso is a basket. That's the only word. A chassis of woven cable trays and repurposed bracket arms has been lashed around its chest, creating a concave nest lined with something dark and fibrous—frayed asbestos cloth perhaps, or a felt of compressed moss. From this nest protrudes an assortment of handles: the cracked wood of a valve wheel, the hexagonal shaft of a socket wrench, a copper trowel with a verdigris patina. It is a votive offering to the god of the right tool for the right job.

But the head. The head is why the Gardener is a who and not an it. The head is a diver's helmet, a bulbous copper sphere with a rectangular viewport of thick, bubbled glass. The copper is blackened with age, streaked with the constant condensation into weeping vertical lines, like charcoal wept from a funerary mask. Behind the glass, in the dim, grey-green luminescence of the plateau, something moves. It's not a face, not exactly. It's a slow, constant rearrangement of what looks like pale fungal filaments, a mycelial web that sometimes coalesces into the suggestion of a brow, the hollow of an eye socket, before dissolving back into its cryptic, seeking pattern. A single, fat, pigmentless moth, tattered as old lace, lives on the inside of the glass, occasionally opening and closing its wings in a slow, silent beat. The Gardener does not seem to mind.

It is a curator of equilibrium. Its days—though day is just a dimming and brightening of the never-ending gloaming—are spent in gentle, incessant maintenance. It does not build, and it does not destroy. It adjusts. It slides down a stairwell that is not a stairwell but a tightly coiled nest of condensing pipes, its rubber cup feet making soft, kissing sounds against the weeping metal. It stops before a bank of pressure gauges, each dial face hosting a congress of translucent slugs, their bodies like beads of living glass. It watches the needles tremble. With a slow, deliberate motion, a leg tipped with a pincer reaches out and turns a small, red valve wheel the barest fraction of a degree. A spasm of pressure-relief valves chatters hysterically a hundred feet away, then falls silent. The humming shifts by a micron. The Gardener moves on.

Its most sacred task is the tending of the moss. The moss is everything. It is the skin of this world, the velvet on the iron. The Gardener uses its brass-bristled foot like a priest's aspergillum, gently sweeping the fronds of a huge, prehistoric fern that has erupted from a steam manifold, checking for any rust scale beneath its roots. It will spend an hour, two hours, carefully scraping away a single orange bloom of corrosion with its welding nozzle foot turned to its coolest, most precise flame, before gently coaxing a patch of liverwort from a neighboring pipe to cover the newly-bare metal. It is a skin graft, a benediction. It whispers, or makes a sound that a world with bird song would call a whisper—a susurrus of air through brass, a metallic sibilance, a soft *"shh, shh, shh,"* to the plant life as it works.

Does it remember? The fungal threads behind the glass sometimes coalesce into a more defined shape when it rests. It stops on a wide processional catwalk, surrounded by the weeping, industrial forest, and its copper head tilts up toward the lattice of gantries and the ceiling of blackened copper hoods far above. The moth in its helmet beats its wings once, twice. In that posture, for just a moment, the suggestion of a face is clear, and it is not a peaceful one. It is a face caught in a long, slow-motion gasp of terrible understanding. Then the threads dissolve, and it is a basket of tools to tend a dying world that will never die, a humble gardener of the perpetual, purposeless, beautiful storm. It lowers its head and, with its trowel foot, gently detaches a slug from a dial face that has recorded the same pressure for ten thousand years, moving it to a fresher, wetter patch of moss. There is work to be done. There is always work to be done. And the humming goes on.
