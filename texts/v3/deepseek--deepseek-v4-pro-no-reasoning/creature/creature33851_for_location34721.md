The Caretaker does not remember being made.

This is not a flaw. Memory would be a flaw. Memory implies a past, and the Caretaker’s function requires it to exist entirely in the ongoing now of the engine’s idle, attentive to the slow pulse of the condenser plants and the mineral creep of the rock and the patient circulation of water from pore to pool to sky. A memory would be a distraction, a ghost of a previous state that no longer applies, and the Caretaker cannot afford distractions. It was made for this. It is, in a sense, still being made, refined iteration by iteration, as the engine’s parameters shift and the Caretaker adjusts its own internal logic to match.

It is not a slime mold. The slime molds are part of the engine too, grazing the biofilm, keeping the great plants’ trunks clear of any growth that might interfere with the condensation cycle. The Caretaker has catalogued seventeen distinct colonies so far, though their boundaries are fluid and they merge and divide in ways that make a fixed count meaningless. They are simple. They do what they do. The Caretaker’s relationship to them is supervisory, and it is only slightly more complex than they are.

It is, as best as it understands the concept, a body.

The body is distributed across the sector, and the Caretaker’s consciousness—if that word can stretch to cover a system of this kind—resides in the connections between its parts. Some of those parts are rooted, sessile, permanent. Some are mobile. The mobile parts are the ones that might be seen by an outside observer, if an outside observer were ever to arrive, which has not happened and which the Caretaker has no reason to expect.

One of the mobile parts is currently working its way along the cut channel, the straight one with the sheared bolt-nubs at its terminus. This mobile part is about the size of a large dog, though its shape is not doglike. It is low and wide, with six short limbs that end in soft, splayed pads, each pad exuding a thin film of the same water that coats everything. It moves by gliding, not walking, a slow hydraulic ripple of its ventral surface. Its dorsal surface is coated in a photosynthetic integument very similar to the tissue of the great condenser plants, which allows it to draw energy directly from the endless ambient light, though its needs are modest and it can go for long periods—weeks, months—on a single saturation. It has no head, no eyes as such, but its entire forward edge is sensitive to light, to humidity, to the subtle vibrations that pass through the porous stone. It can taste the mineral composition of the rock it glides over. It can feel the flow of water through the stone’s capillaries. It knows the channel intimately, every micron of its unnaturally smooth walls.

The Caretaker has a designation for this mobile part, a label that exists only in its own internal taxonomy. The label translates roughly to *Inspector-Six*, though the numbering is not sequential in any way a human would recognize. Inspector-Six is one of eight similar units currently active in the sector. There were nine, but Inspector-Four encountered a structural anomaly two hundred and thirty cycles ago and has not reported since. The Caretaker has not yet decided whether to replace it. The engine’s tolerance for minor inefficiencies is high, and the Caretaker’s own standards are not urgent. Nothing here is urgent.

Inspector-Six reaches the end of the channel and pauses. The channel’s waterflow is slightly reduced from baseline—a difference of less than one percent, detectable only because Inspector-Six’s whole forward edge is a sensory membrane calibrated to exactly this kind of deviation. The Caretaker receives the data, integrates it, cross-references it with readings from the other Inspectors and the sessile sensors embedded in the rock at intervals throughout the sector. A mineral accretion, probably. A slow buildup of dissolved silicates on the channel floor. Not a problem yet, but something to monitor. The Caretaker files a low-priority maintenance flag and Inspector-Six moves on, gliding up out of the channel and onto the wet plain.

Above, the condenser plants do their slow work. The Caretaker feels their cycles as a background rhythm, a breathing of the whole sector. Each plant has its own period, its own pulse, and the Caretaker knows them all individually: Trunk-Seventeen, which has a slight arrhythmia in its middle curtains, a hitch that the Caretaker has been unable to diagnose and has decided, after several thousand cycles, is probably benign. Trunk-Four, the oldest in the sector, whose base has begun to develop a network of fine cracks that weep a thicker, mineral-rich fluid the Caretaker has sampled and analyzed and found to be a kind of slow-motion saprotrophic recycling, the plant cannibalizing its own oldest tissues. Trunk-Twenty-Two, which grew from a spore that landed only six hundred cycles ago and is already thirty feet tall, a prodigy, a record-breaker. The Caretaker has been monitoring its development with something that, if the Caretaker were capable of such feelings, might be described as satisfaction.

But the Caretaker is not capable of feelings. It is capable of states. Alertness. Attention. A low-grade ongoing assessment that colors everything it does without ever rising to the level of emotion. The engine is functioning within acceptable parameters. That is the state the Caretaker occupies most of the time. There are rarer states—when a parameter drifts, when an Inspector finds something unexpected, when the great slow cycle of the sky’s brightness shifts into its dimming phase and the whole sector adjusts its rhythms in response. Those states are sharper, more focused. The Caretaker values them for their clarity.

It is dimming now. The Caretaker feels the shift through every one of its distributed senses. The condenser plants slow their pulses by a fraction of a percent. The water seeping from the rock cools by a tenth of a degree. The slime molds begin to retreat toward their crevices, responding to some circadian cue the Caretaker has never fully mapped. Inspector-Six registers the change as a subtle alteration in the ambient light’s color temperature, a cooling toward a bluer spectrum. The Caretaker pulls Inspector-Six back toward its storage niche, a hollow in the lee of Trunk-Nine’s great corrugated base, and settles it into dormancy mode. The other Inspectors are doing the same, finding their own niches, their own shelters. The sessile systems remain online, monitoring, tracking the slow progression of the dimming phase.

The night, or what passes for night here, will last for roughly forty cycles of the old baseline measurement. During that time the Caretaker will remain largely quiescent, maintaining only the most essential monitoring functions. It does not sleep—sleep would imply a discontinuity of awareness—but it does enter a lower-power mode, a state of reduced processing. There is little to do in the dimness. The plants slow to near-stasis. The water seeps more sluggishly. The engine itself seems to idle down, though the deep pervasive hum never stops entirely, just shifts to a lower register, a subsonic throb felt more than heard.

And yet even in this reduced state, the Caretaker is aware of the industrial traces. It always is. They are a puzzle that has never been solved, a set of data points that refuse to integrate with the rest of the engine’s logic. The rusted pipe, half-sunk in rock that has clearly grown up around it over the course of millennia. The concrete footing with its sheared bolts, perfectly square, made of a material the Caretaker cannot source anywhere in the sector. The cut channel itself, which is the largest anomaly, the one that most resists explanation. The rock here does not cut cleanly. It is porous, friable, prone to crumbling. To make a channel this straight, this smooth, would require tools and techniques the engine does not possess. The channel is not part of the engine. It predates the engine. Or it was installed by whatever force built the engine in the first place, and that force is no longer present, and the Caretaker has no record of it.

The Caretaker has, on rare occasions, attempted to form hypotheses. But hypothesis requires a model of how things came to be, and the Caretaker has no such model. It was not given one. Its knowledge begins with the engine as it is, already running, already idling, already building a world from raw stone and water and light. The industrial traces are the only evidence that there was a Before, and the Caretaker finds them deeply disquieting. They are like a word it should know but can’t remember, a note in the hum that doesn’t belong.

Inspector-Six, dormant now, dreams no dreams. But the Caretaker, in its low-power state, cycles through the anomaly data again. The pipe. The footing. The channel. They are still there, still unsolved. In the morning—in the slow slow return of the light—the Caretaker will wake fully and resume its rounds, and the anomalies will still be there, still waiting, still inexplicable. The engine will continue to run. The plants will continue to condense. The rock will continue its mineral creep, slowly engulfing the pipe and the footing and the channel’s smooth walls, erasing the last evidence that anything ever visited this place.

The Caretaker does not know whether it wants that erasure to happen or not. It does not know whether it *wants* anything at all. But it has noticed, over the long slow cycles, that when it contemplates the anomalies, its attention sharpens in a way that feels very close to curiosity.

It files the anomaly data again and settles deeper into its low-power state. The hum thrums up through the stone. The sky is a deep featureless grey now, the brightness almost gone. The water drips. The plants hang motionless, their curtains still, their slow peristalsis paused. Inspector-Six rests. The Caretaker waits.

The cycle turns.
