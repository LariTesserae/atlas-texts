The old woman who gave you the water has no name that translates, but if you pressed her scarred palm to your ear, you might hear it as a series of percussive rhythms—three clicks, a rising trill, and a sound like water dropping into a deep pool. Call her Click-Trill-Drop. She is the one who remembers.

She is not the oldest. The oldest is a man so folded into the wall-garden that his body has become a substrate. His name is a long silence. He does not move anymore, but he breathes, and the fungus that fruits from his shoulders is the sweetest in the gorge. The children harvest him gently, singing. But he cannot speak, not in any way the others understand. So Click-Trill-Drop is the memory-keeper by default, because she has lived long enough to have forgotten things and then remembered them again, which is the true mark of age.

She was born here, as all of them were born here. The birth is a wet, green business. A mother will squat in the lowest hollow, where the root-mat is softest and the water pools warm from decomposition heat, and she will push a new body into the world while the monkeys shriek and the bats churn overhead. The newborn is immediately placed on a bed of damp moss. The placenta is buried in the root-mat, a deliberate offering, and within a season the spot will sprout a new fern, which will be that child's fern, their particular node in the network. Click-Trill-Drop's fern is an ancient thing now, a cascade of fronds that covers half the eastern wall. She visits it daily, stroking the spores into her palm and blowing them into the mist.

Her body tells her story. The scarification began when she was weaned—her mother cut the first lines into her shoulders with a chert blade, rubbing fungal paste into the wounds so they would heal raised and pale. The pattern maps her lineage. Each line is a mother, a grandmother, going back seven generations. The pattern on her left side is incomplete because her mother died before it could be finished, killed by a fall from the open wall during a storm. Click-Trill-Drop remembers this. She remembers everything she has been told, and she tells it constantly, because telling is how the people exist beyond their bodies.

She is telling now, to no one in particular, as she moves through the canopy. Her hands are always moving. They strip fibers from an orchid root, twisting them into cord. They pluck a moth from its cluster and pop it into her mouth. They press a fungal shelf to test its ripeness. Her voice carries over the chitter and shriek, a constant stream of clicks and trills that only a few can follow. The children ignore her. The adults catch fragments. Only one other person understands the full weave of her speech, and that is the man who is not quite a man, who waits near the ceiling.

But she is not speaking to him. She is speaking to the city itself. She believes that the roots can hear, that the water carries vibration, that the limestone remembers pressure. She is narrating the history into the stone so that when she dies, the stone will hold the story until another mouth learns to ask.

What is she saying?

She is saying that there was a time before the gardens, when the people lived in the air and died in the air. She is saying that the first seed was carried in the gut of a bird that fell from the sky and rotted here, and from its body grew the first vine. She is saying that her great-great-grandmother was the one who learned to graft, who watched the fig trees strangling their hosts and thought: *what if they strangled each other, and I helped*. She is saying that the water never stops, but once, long ago, it did—a dry season that lasted three cycles of the moon, and the people had to lick the moisture from each other's skin to survive. She is saying that the monkeys came later, after the gardens were established, drawn by the fruit, and the people learned to eat the monkeys, and then learned to become the monkeys, and now they are so entangled that no one knows who followed whom.

She is saying all of this, and no one is listening, and it does not matter.

——

The man who is not quite a man hangs near the ceiling where the light never reaches. His name is a texture, a particular vibration of the throat that mimics the sound of a bat's wing in the dark. Call him Wing-Buzz. He is the one who tends the night.

Wing-Buzz was born different. His mother knew it immediately. He did not cry. He opened his eyes—already clouded, already milky—and he turned his head toward the darkness at the back of the nave, and he smiled. The people understand difference as a gift. A child who cannot see is a child who will learn to see differently. They raised him in the dark places, the crevices, the spaces between the epiphyte layers where the light-feeders cannot grow. He learned to navigate by sound and touch and the subtle temperature gradients that tell him where the stone is and where the void is.

He has never seen the open wall. He does not know what the sky looks like. But he knows the bats. The bats know him. They roost in his hair, which he keeps long and tangled for this purpose. They crawl over his shoulders and lick the salt from his skin. He has learned their language—the constant chitter of echolocation, the social calls, the warning pulses. He can chirp a command, and a cloud of bats will lift from the walls and swirl around him, a cloak of living leather. He is the night-shepherd.

His role is to tend the things that bloom in darkness. There are fungi that fruit only when the light is gone, pale and phosphorescent, and he harvests them. There are blind crickets that live in the deepest crevices, and he catches them. There are lizards that sleep on the undersides of the bromeliad leaves, and he knows how to pluck them without waking them, so that their dreams will still be sweet when he bites into their skulls. He brings his harvests down to the light-dwellers, and they give him water and fiber and worked stone. He does not need to trade—he could take what he wants, because he moves so silently that no one would know—but he trades anyway, because the trade is a language too, and he loves all languages.

He listens to Click-Trill-Drop's constant narration, and he is the only one who listens to all of it. When she stops speaking, he will know it, even from his high perch, and he will descend to find her body, and he will carry it to the fungal gardens deep in the rock, and he will lay her among the mycelium, and she will continue.

——

But the city is not just Click-Trill-Drop and Wing-Buzz. It is eighty-three other bodies, give or take the infants who are not yet named and the elders who are not yet dead. They are a turbulent, chaotic, deeply organized people. Their organization is not hierarchical—no chief, no council—but relational. Everyone has a role, and the roles shift with the seasons and the fruiting cycles and the deaths and the births.

There is a woman whose role is to tend the water. Call her Cup-Hands. She is young, barely scarred, but her palms are cupped by nature, a genetic gift that makes her the ideal vessel. She can carry water from the catchment hollows to the higher nests without spilling a drop. She knows every bromeliad pool, every drip-point, every rivulet. She knows which water is sweet and which is bitter with tannins. She knows the taste of every seep. When a child is thirsty, they go to Cup-Hands. When a new mother cannot produce enough milk, Cup-Hands brings her water from the calcium-rich drip near the east wall, which makes the milk come in thick and white. She is the city's circulatory system.

There is a man whose role is to tend the grafts. Call Him Who Binds. His hands are the most scarred of all, from decades of stripping bark and braiding fiber and pressing cambium to cambium. He is shaping the next generation of pillars—five saplings that he has been training since they were sprouts, guiding their roots along channels he carved in the limestone floor. He speaks to the saplings in a low, constant murmur that no one else can hear over the chaos. He tells them what to become. He is making a room within the room, a chamber that will be complete after his death, a place where the next memory-keeper can sit and speak and be heard clearly for the first time in a thousand years.

There is a child whose role is to tend the moths. She is seven, or what passes for seven in a place without seasons. She found the moth-cluster when she was four, a trembling mass of wings on a shelf fungus, and she has claimed it as her own. No one contests this. She knows the life cycle of the moths in her bones. She knows which are ready to mate, which are ready to lay eggs, which are ready to be eaten. She harvests them with a gentleness that belies her age, taking only the old ones, the ones whose wings are tattered, whose bodies are swollen with approaching death. She pops them into her mouth and savors their dust. She is the one who discovered that moth-dust, mixed with water, makes a paste that can seal wounds. She taught this to Cup-Hands. The knowledge spread.

There are hunters. There are gatherers. There are those who knap the chert, those who braid the fiber, those who tend the newborns, those who dispose of the dead. The dead are not buried. They are given to the gardens, laid in the warmest hollows where the decomposition runs hot, where their bodies will be absorbed into the root-mat within a season. The people understand that they are food for the city. They do not mourn this. They celebrate it. A death is a feast for the roots, and the roots will return the gift in new growth, new fruit, new moths, new monkeys.

The monkeys. The monkeys are not pets. They are not prey, not exactly. They are...competitors. Partners. Reflections. The people's relationship with the monkeys is one of mutual irritation and mutual dependence. The monkeys eat the same fruit, drink from the same bromeliads, fight over the same roosting spots. They steal food from the people's hands and screech mockery and throw scat. But they also provide warning of predators—the sudden silence of a monkey troupe is the most reliable alarm the people have. They also process fruit pulp in their guts and deposit it across the gardens, spreading seeds. They also are warm bodies in the cold nights, huddling together with the people in the larger hollows, a pile of fur and scarred skin and chattering teeth.

The people eat the monkeys when they must and mourn the monkeys when they die and curse the monkeys when they steal and laugh at the monkeys when they fall. There is a story that Click-Trill-Drop tells—how the first people and the first monkeys were siblings, born from the same hollow, nursed on the same milk, until one climbed up and one climbed down and they became two peoples who forgot they were one.

——

The man who is about to die is named, in the language of his people, a sharp inhalation followed by a long, slow exhalation. Call him Out-Breath. He is thirty-two, an elder by the standards of a people who rarely see forty. A fungal infection has colonized his lungs, a risk of the constant damp, and his breathing has become a wet rattle that the others can hear even over the din. He has been dying for a month. He is ready.

He lies in the warmest hollow, the one near the back wall where the decomposition bacteria run so hot that the root-mat steams. His family is around him—his sister, his two living children, the woman who was his mate before she died three years ago of a cracked skull from a bad fall. Their patterns are on his skin, and his patterns are on theirs. They touch him constantly, not to comfort him—the concept of comfort is different here; it is about presence, not ease—but to remind his body that it is part of a larger body, that his dying will feed the network, that his flesh will become fern and fungus and moth and monkey.

Click-Trill-Drop squats beside him. She is reciting his lineage, the full seven generations on his mother's side, the four on his father's that they can remember. She is telling him what he will become. She is describing the exact spot where they will lay him, the particular fern that will sprout, the children who will harvest it. She is promising him that he will be remembered.

Wing-Buzz descends from his high perch for the first time in days. He cannot see Out-Breath, but he can hear the rattle, and he knows. He brings a gift—a phosphorescent fungus, still glowing faintly, that grows only in the deepest crevice. He presses it into Out-Breath's hand. The dying man's fingers close around it, and he smiles. He has always loved the glowing things.

Cup-Hands brings water, the calcium-rich drip from the east wall. She holds it to Out-Breath's lips, and he drinks. Most of it spills down his chin, but what enters his mouth eases the rattle for a moment.

Him Who Binds is not there. He is with his saplings, murmuring, shaping the future. But he has sent a gift—a length of fiber cord, freshly braided, still green. It is draped over Out-Breath's chest. It will be used to bind his body for the laying-out. The cord will rot with him, and the fibers will be taken up by the roots, and a generation from now, a child will harvest a vine that carries the memory of this death.

Out-Breath dies at dusk, just as the frogs begin their chorus. His rattle stops mid-inhalation. The silence that follows is not silence—the city does not do silence—but a subtle shift in the texture of the noise, a space where his breathing used to be. His sister notices first. She lets out a cry, a series of percussive clicks that mean *gone, gone, he is gone*. The cry is taken up by others. The news spreads through the nave in the same way that a predator alarm spreads, a ripple of sound that carries information.

The monkeys fall silent for a moment. Just a moment. Then they resume their chattering.

By morning, Out-Breath's body will be laid in the hollow that was prepared for him. His sister will place the first stone, a piece of chert that she knapped herself. His children will cover him with moss. Wing-Buzz will seed his body with fungal spores. Cup-Hands will pour water over the mound. Click-Trill-Drop will speak his name into the stone, into the water, into the roots, for the last time.

And the city will continue. The grafted pillars will thicken. The bromeliad pools will brim. The bats will roost and the moths will cluster and the hummingbirds will stab at the flowers. The water will drip, drip, drip, never ceasing. A new child will be born, and a new fern will sprout, and the turbulence will accommodate the change as it has always done.

——

But there is something else. Something that no one sees, or no one says they see, because it does not fit the story they tell themselves.

Deep in the rock, behind the thickest layer of epiphytes, in a crevice so narrow that only a child could reach it, there is a carving. It is not the work of any living hand. The stone is different there—older, smoother, deliberately shaped. The carving shows a shape that is not quite human and not quite monkey and not quite anything that lives in the gorge. It has too many limbs, and the limbs end in tools. It is holding something that might be a seed or might be a sun or might be a word. The carving is weathered by millennia of seep, but the lines are still visible, and if you trace them with your finger, you can feel the intent.

The children know about the carving. They visit it on dares, squeezing through the crevice, touching the stone face, screaming with delighted terror. But the adults do not speak of it. Click-Trill-Drop does not include it in her narration. Wing-Buzz has never touched it, though he knows exactly where it is, because the bats avoid that crevice. The carving makes a sound, or not a sound—a pressure, a vibration that the bats interpret as wrongness.

What does it mean? The people do not ask. They are not a people who ask questions about origins. They know where they came from. They came from the gardens, and the gardens came from the first seed, and the first seed came from the belly of a bird that fell from the sky. This is the story, and the story is complete.

But the carving is older than the story. The carving was here before the first vine, before the first seep, before the gorge itself. The carving was made by someone or something that shaped the stone before the stone was shaped by water. And if you, standing here now, could reach that crevice, could press your hand to that ancient surface, you might feel a pulse that is not the water's pulse, a rhythm that is not the city's rhythm, a presence that is not the gardens and not the people and not anything that has a name in the language of clicks and trills.

You might understand that the city is not the first city here. That the garden is not the first garden. That the people are not the first people, and that what they call the beginning is only a middle, and that what they call the world is a palimpsest—a surface that has been written on and scraped clean and written on again so many times that the original text is lost, but still legible, if you know how to feel for the grooves.

But you cannot reach the crevice. You are a stranger, too large, too clumsy. And the people, even if they noticed you, would not lead you there. They would offer you water. They would call you a monkey in their language. They would forget you as soon as you left, because you are not part of the turbulence, and what is not part of the turbulence does not matter.

The water in your leaf-cup is still cool. It still tastes of flowers. You drink it, and the taste stays on your tongue long after you leave the hanging garden, long after the sound fades, long after you forget the face of the old woman who gave it to you. But she will remember you. She is already telling your story into the stone. A new movement in the canopy. A new voice in the chaos. A stranger who drank the water and vanished.

Click-Trill-Drop will remember until she dies. And then Wing-Buzz will carry her to the fungal gardens, and she will become the story, and the story will continue, dripped and chattered and carved into the limestone by the water that never stops.

And deep in the crevice, the ancient shape with too many limbs will hold its seed or its sun or its word, and wait. It has been waiting since before the word *waiting* was invented. It can wait a little longer.
