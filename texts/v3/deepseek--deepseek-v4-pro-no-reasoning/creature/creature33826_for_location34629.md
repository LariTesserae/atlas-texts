Not an animal. Not a plant. Something between—or before—that division ever mattered.

You notice it first as a wrongness in the vegetation. One of the copper-fronded ferns is *too* symmetrical, its pinnae repeating in a spiral that breaks the organic rule of slight imperfection. You stare at it for three breath-cycles before you understand that it's staring back. Not with eyes. With orientation. It has turned, incrementally, in the time you've been watching, and now its broadest face points toward you like a parabola toward a signal.

It has no face. That's the second thing you notice. What you took for a fern is a composite organism—or mechanism, or both—its "fronds" mineralized at the edges with the same obsidian-sharp stone as the terrace. They overlap in scales, in vanes, in something halfway between feathers and heat-sink fins. Along the central stalk, nodules of amber tissue pulse in time with the breath, but slightly offset: not quite in rhythm, as if it's listening to a slightly different tempo.

A keeper.

That word surfaces from nowhere, supplied by a brain desperate for category. But the thing—the being—doesn't *keep* in the way you mean. It doesn't guard or hoard. It tends. It adjusts. As you watch, one of its fronds extends with the slow, patient speed of a vine in time-lapse, reaches toward a patch of moss on the channel wall, and *touches* it. The moss doesn't react visibly, but something passes between them—a micro-shift in color, a brief flush of deeper amber through the keeper's nodule—and when the frond withdraws, the moss has been reshaped, its growth vector redirected by a fraction of a degree.

There are more of them. Now that you know what to look for, you see keepers at every level of the visible city. They occupy the vertical spaces, anchored to the terrace edges, the channel walls, the undersides of the hanging structures. Some are large, their mineral vanes spreading two meters across, dominating their niches like sea fans on a reef. Others are small, barely bigger than the moss they tend, tucked into crevices or wrapped around the vein-cut water channels like living collars. None of them move quickly. None of them need to. The city breathes on a seven-second cycle; the keepers operate on one far slower, hours or days to complete a single gesture.

You count them. You can't. They blur into the vegetation because they *are* the vegetation, or have become it, or built it, or all these things at once. The mosses are not separate from them. The ferns with their copper fronds—some are just ferns, but others are keepers in a dormant phase, their animal mineral intelligence turned down to a pilot light, waiting for a change in humidity, a fluctuation in the breath, a patch of stone that needs their attention. The line between ecosystem and inhabitant doesn't exist here. The keepers are the gardeners who have become the garden.

One of them moves.

Not the slow, vegetal motion of the fronds. This is something else. A larger specimen, anchored to the terrace edge three meters from where you stand, detaches. It doesn't *walk*—there are no limbs in the sense you understand—but it contracts and releases in sequence, its vanes shifting along the obsidian stone with a sound like slate being shuffled, a dry, careful rasp. It advances toward you.

You don't move. This is not courage. It's the paralysis of a prey animal that has not yet been classified as prey, and you are waiting, your own breath fallen into sync with the city's seven-second inhale, for the classification to resolve. The keeper stops just out of arm's reach. Up close, you can see the layers of its construction: the stone-like base material that is almost certainly grown, precipitated from the mineral-rich water by a metabolism you can't begin to fathom; the organic substrate that lines the vanes, warm and vascular, faintly gleaming; the nodules that pulse with amber light, each one a tiny, slow-burning thought.

It speaks. Not in sound. The hum of the city is already so dense with noise that sound is the wrong medium. It speaks in pressure. The air directly in front of the keeper tightens, pushing against your face in a sequence of micro-pulses that your skin reads before your brain can translate: a pattern, a rhythm, a *sentence* in a language of differentials. You don't understand the words. But you understand the grammar. It's the grammar of the lung itself, the inhale-hold-exhale cycle, but compressed, accelerated into a series of puffs that brush your cheeks like a moth's wings. It's asking you something. Or telling you. Or greeting you by reading your own pressure back at you, a mirroring of the carbon dioxide and water vapor you've been trailing through the atmosphere since you arrived.

The keeper has no face, but it has attention. It has curiosity. It has, you realize with a sudden lurch that has nothing to do with the breath-cycle, *patience*. It has been here since the city was cracked into its hollowed dome, since the first channels were cut into the basalt, since the first spores of moss arrived on the first engineered wind—and it has been tending, adjusting, listening to the pressure-language of the lung, and it will be here long after you have gone back to the surface with your single, fragile, two-lobed respiration.

It knows you're temporary. It finds you interesting, perhaps, or perhaps it simply registers you as a new variable in the humidity of the terrace. Either way, it holds its position, its vanes slightly angled to catch your breath, its nodules pulsing their slow, amber, off-rhythm counterpoint to the city's seven-second heart.

And then it does something that breaks the category all over again.

It *parts*. The mineral scales along its central stalk separate, and from within—from a cavity that is warm and wet and filled with the same amber light that seeps from the air—it produces an object. Not a thing. A keepling. A smaller version of itself, curled tight as a fiddlehead, its vanes still soft and translucent, its nodules dark. It places the keepling on the stone between you. The gesture is unmistakable. A gift. An offering. A question in a grammar older than language.

The keepling unfurls slightly, drawn by the warmth of your presence or the carbon dioxide of your exhale, and one soft, mineral-tipped frond reaches toward your shoe.

Above you, the city exhales. The water sheets down the vein-channel nearest you, and the larger keeper catches some of it on its vanes, the droplets beading like amber pearls before rolling down the scales to irrigate the moss at its base. It stays there, watching you in its pressure-language, waiting for your response—for your breath to change, for your humidity to shift, for you to do whatever it is that the city's newest, strangest variable will do.
