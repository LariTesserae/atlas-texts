Let me tell you about Kell.

Kell is twelve years old, though no one here counts years with numbers. They count them in wings—how many seasons of moth-shedding you’ve gathered through, how many cycles of the frogs singing you’ve heard. Kell has gathered through twelve generations of wing-fall. Her hands know the weight of a basket before it’s full. Her feet remember the give of each rope bridge, which planks groan in damp weather and which stay silent, where the slugs are likely to be crossing and need stepping over.

She is the wing-gatherer. It’s not a title anyone gave her; it’s a role she grew into the way the moss grows onto the cables, slowly and then all at once, until you can’t imagine the structure without it. When she was small enough to be carried in a sling against her mother’s chest, she would reach out and pluck the shed wings from the air as they fluttered down from the bridges above. Her mother noticed. Others noticed. By the time Kell could walk the bridges on her own, she had appointed herself, or been appointed by the tacit agreement of the community, to the task.

The wings are used for insulation. They are layered between the inner and outer walls of the dwellings, a thousand translucent membranes overlapping like shingles too small for sight, and together they hold warmth in a way that baffles reason. The material is not warm to the touch—a single wing is cool, brittle, already half-returned to the air—but in aggregate they trap something. Body heat. Breath heat. The slow exhalation of the stone ribs, which is not warm but is less cold than the mist. The houses breathe differently because of the wings. They are alive in a way the people recognize but don’t articulate.

Kell knows the best wings. The ones with the grey-brown dust still clinging, the ones that are whole, not torn at the leading edge where the moth struggled free. She can tell at a glance which wings will layer flat and which will curl. The curled ones are not discarded—nothing here is discarded—but they go to a different use. Caulking. Stoppering gaps where the mist seeps through. Fire-starting, on the rare occasions when fire is needed, though what they burn is never wood. Wood is structural. Wood is sacred. They burn moss that has died and dried, they burn the husks of the frogs that the serpent leaves behind, they burn the wings that are too broken for the walls. Kell’s basket is an act of triage.

Her hands, as she works the bridge this morning, are steady. She wears no shoes—no one does—and the soles of her feet are callused into a kind of leather. She can feel the texture of the bamboo planks through the callus: the fine ridges of the grain, the slick spots where a slug has passed, the slight vibration of the cables as someone else in the hamlet moves on a connected bridge. She does not think about this. It is simply there, a second layer of awareness running beneath her conscious mind.

She hums while she works. Not a melody—the birds would absorb it—but a rhythm. Click-pause-click-click. The birds’ own rhythm. She has learned to match it, to slot her sounds into the spaces between theirs, so that her humming doesn’t disturb them. The birds are not sacred, exactly. Nothing here is sacred in the way that implies worship. But they are *listened to*. Their clicking is the background against which all other sounds are measured. When the clicking changes tempo, the people notice. When the clicking stops entirely, they wake from sleep already reaching for obsidian. The clicking has stopped only twice in Kell’s lifetime. Both times, the serpent had found its way into one of the dwellings. Both times, people died. The clicking is a safety net made of sound, and Kell hums inside it, a voice inside a voice.

Her basket is half-full now. She is working the eastern bridge, the one that connects the third and fourth dwellings—the ones belonging to Orren and to the twins, though those boundaries are loose. Orren lives in the third dwelling but spends most nights in the twins’ dwelling, and one of the twins keeps her tools in Orren’s, and the other twin has been sleeping on the platform lately, wrapped in a cocoon of vine-fibre blankets, watching the stars wheel through the triangular window of sky. The hamlet’s privacy is permeable. Everyone knows who is where and chooses not to track it too closely.

Kell’s mother is the woman on the bridge, the one processing plant fibres with the stone scraper. Her name is Silla. She is not Kell’s only parent—the hamlet raises children collectively, and Kell has four adults she calls by parental names—but Silla is the one who birthed her, and something in their body rhythms still aligns. When Silla is tired, Kell grows sleepy. When Kell is hungry, Silla’s breasts still ache faintly, even twelve years after weaning. They have not spoken of this in six years. There is no need. The knowledge sits between them like the moisture on the stone ribs, visible and unnamed.

Silla is watching Kell work. Not with worry—Kell on the bridges is safer than anyone on solid ground, which no one here has ever walked—but with a kind of attention that is also gratitude. The wings Kell gathers line the walls of Silla’s dwelling. When the mist presses cold against the outer plaster, the wings hold the warmth in. Silla sleeps well because of her daughter’s hands. She does not say thank you. In the hamlet, thanks is expressed through action, through reciprocity, through the careful placement of a freshly scraped fibre cloth on Kell’s sleeping mat, a gift left without witness.

Kell reaches for a wing near the edge of a plank, and her fingers brush the silver trail of a slug. She pauses. The slug is still there, translucent body pulsing, navigating the gap between two planks with its slow certainty. Kell waits. The slug completes its journey—a full minute, two minutes—and Kell’s hand remains still in the air above it. When the slug is clear, she plucks the wing. This is not kindness. It is not even consideration. It is the logic of the garden: the slug’s path matters as much as her gathering. Interruption creates ripples. The garden processes interruption poorly. Kell has learned this the way she learned the bridges, through the body, through consequence. Once, when she was younger and impatient, she plucked a wing from beneath a moving slug and the slug released its grip and fell. She watched it tumble into the mist, a pale dot swallowed by grey. For three days afterward, the slugs avoided the eastern bridge. Their silver trails stopped at the midpoint and turned back. The hamlet noticed. No one blamed her, but the absence was a weight, and when the slugs finally returned, Kell understood that she had disturbed something she didn’t have a name for. Now she waits.

The mist is thick this morning. It rises from below in slow billows, obscuring the platform garden so that the ferns are visible only as watery silhouettes, the mosses as smears of colour behind veils of grey. The stone ribs are weeping. Condensation runs in channels down the spiral grooves, dripping from the chisel marks with a sound like a mouth opening and closing. The air tastes more mineral than usual—the stone is exhaling something sharp, something that coats the tongue with a faint copper tang. The birds’ clicking has shifted to a lower register, a sign of damp weather. The frogs are silent. They are never silent. That silence is more noticeable than their song ever was.

Kell notices. Silla notices. On the platform below, the twin who has been sleeping there—Callan, the younger by six minutes—sits up from his cocoon of blankets and tilts his head, listening. The frogs are silent. The garden knows something.

What the garden knows is this: the fourth rib, the one the builders have been extending the platform toward, is vibrating. A frequency too low for human ears, transmitted through the stone, felt only in the bones of the jaw and the soles of the feet. The frogs feel it through their skin. The moths feel it in their antennae. The birds are adjusting their clicking to compensate, layering a new rhythm over the old. The garden is recalibrating. The fourth rib is alive in a way the other three are not.

Kell does not know this consciously. But her humming has stopped. She stands on the bridge, basket in hand, and looks up at the triangular window of sky. Grey. Grey as the wings in her basket. Grey as the mist. Grey as the pearl-serpent currently sliding along a cable three feet from her shoulder, hunting the silence where the frogs should be.

She sees the serpent. It sees her. Their gazes hold for a moment—the serpent’s eye is gold with a slit pupil, impossibly ancient, utterly indifferent—and then it continues its liquid threading through the cable-net. Kell breathes out. The breath condenses in the cool air, a tiny cloud that joins the greater mist. She returns to her gathering.

The basket is three-quarters full when she reaches the end of the bridge and comes to the dwelling of Orren. Orren is not inside. Orren is on the platform, building, his obsidian flakes catching the dim light as he works the fresh branches into the extension. He is the reason the platform is growing. For six months he has been extending it toward the fourth rib, and no one has asked him why. The hamlet trusts its members to follow their compulsions. The garden has its own logic, and sometimes that logic expresses itself through human hands. Orren is not a builder by inclination—he is a fibre-worker, like Silla, his fingers more accustomed to twisting cord than lashing branches—but the compulsion came upon him in the night, a dream of the fourth rib rising from mist, and he woke knowing the platform needed to reach it. He has been building ever since.

Kell pauses at Orren’s doorway. The dwelling is empty but the sleeping mat is warm—someone has been here recently. One of the twins, perhaps. The walls are thick with wings, layers upon layers, and the faint glow of filtered light through the translucent membranes gives the interior a grey-gold warmth that the mist outside lacks. She steps inside, just for a moment, and presses her palm flat against the wing-layered wall. The wall gives slightly, like pressing on the skin of a living thing. It is warm. It hums, at a frequency that borders on sound. Kell’s own heartbeat slows to match it.

Outside, the frogs begin singing again. The silence breaks as suddenly as it began, and the birds adjust their clicking, and the garden returns to its rhythm. Kell removes her hand from the wall. She steps back onto the bridge. The serpent has caught a frog—she sees the small bright body disappearing into the pearl-grey jaws—and the birds click on, uninterrupted, processing death without pause.

Kell walks toward the center of the hamlet, where the main bridge divides into three paths. She will finish her gathering on the western bridges, and then she will descend to the platform to deliver the basket to her mother, who will begin the work of layering the new wings into the walls. The day will continue. The mist will condense and drip and never quite rain. The stone ribs will weep their mineral tears. The garden will breathe.

And somewhere below, beneath the mist that never clears, something large shifts in its sleep. The fourth rib trembles. Orren lashes another branch to the extension. The hamlet hangs in its cradle of cable and beam, and Kell, balanced perfectly on the rope bridge with her basket of shed wings, is its living center, gathering warmth from the air, one translucent membrane at a time.
