Let me describe what lives here.

---

She was not born in the Bight, but she no longer remembers being anywhere else. This is not an accident of memory—it is a structural fact of her cognition, which she understands with the same detached precision that she understands everything else. She has been here for a very long time. Long enough that the mineral rind has grown over the external hatches of her chamber. Long enough that she has needed to re-bore the conduit for her oxygen line three times, each time noting the thickness of new accretion, each time logging the rate and extrapolating the date when the chamber would become inaccessible, a sealed geode in the valley wall. That date is still some years off. She has made plans.

The chamber is dry and lit only by the indicators on her equipment, which she configured decades ago to the minimum visibility required for her augmented vision. Why burn power on photons when her retinas have been replaced with arrays that can read the full electromagnetic spectrum? She sees the Bight in infrared when she wants to, the seep-water bleeding cold down the valley walls in long descending tongues, or in electrical field sensitivity, the buried cables glowing like vasculature through the stone. She does not need to leave the chamber to know what is happening outside. The sensors tell her most of it. The rest she hears through the walls, through the water, through the steel of her enclosure—the thud of the cylinder, the creak of mineral formations settling under their own weight, the almost inaudible crackle of the copepods shifting in the dark.

She is the one who named them copepods. She knows they are not technically copepods—they diverged from any known clade long before the Bight existed, shaped by the precipitate into something that defies taxonomy—but the word pleases her. It has the right texture. She named the rosettes too, and the long interval between the cylinder's unanswered pulses she calls the *wait*, and the eleven-hour mineral cycle she calls the *breath*, and the seventeen-month cracking she calls the *shrug*, because it reminds her of something she used to do with her shoulders, a gesture her body no longer quite remembers how to make.

Her body. This is the part that requires careful description.

She was human once, or close enough—a baseline that has since been extensively revised. The revisions were not planned. They were a consequence of extended exposure to the Bight's particular chemistry, its mineral sleet, its cold, its pressures. The human body is not designed for eighty meters of permanent winter. Even in a dry chamber, even with breathable atmosphere and thermal regulation, there are effects that accumulate. She noticed the changes early and decided not to resist them. Resisting would have meant leaving, and leaving was not an option, is not an option, was never an option from the moment she understood what the Bight was built to do.

So she let the Bight take her.

Her integument was the first to go. The skin of a baseline human is a poor barrier against manganese-saturated aerosol—and there is aerosol in her chamber, a fine mist of precipitated particulates that her filters cannot completely remove, motes so fine they pass through membranes and settle in the alveoli, in the intercellular spaces, in the dermis itself. Rather than fight this, she introduced a retroviral vector that altered her collagen synthesis pathways, instructing her fibroblasts to weave the mineral motes into the protein lattice instead of encapsulating them as foreign bodies. The result, after several years, was a gradual silicification of her skin: not stiff, not brittle, but flexible in the way that mica is flexible, a thousand microscopic plates that shift against each other when she moves. She is pale now, pale as the rosettes on the ridges, and if she holds very still in the dark she can feel the motes settling on her surface, adding new layers, a human accretion that grows at the same pace as the walls around her.

Her lungs took longer. The alveoli fill slowly, a process she monitors with the same clinical attention she brings to the oxygen line. Each year her vital capacity decreases by a measurable fraction, and each year her tissues compensate by becoming more efficient at extracting oxygen from what atmosphere remains. She no longer breathes in the mammalian sense—her respiration has shifted toward something more like diffusion, oxygen moving across thickened, mineralized membranes at a rate that would be insufficient for a baseline human but is adequate for someone whose metabolic rate has dropped to a slow, steady thrum. She is cold-blooded now, or nearly so; her internal temperature hovers just above the freezing point of her own fluids, which are no longer saline in the normal way but something else, something with a depressed freezing point, a cryoprotective cocktail her body synthesizes from the groundwater seep.

None of this alarms her. She did not choose it, exactly, but she chose not to prevent it, which amounts to the same thing. The Bight was built to receive a signal, and she was built to wait for it, and the waiting has shaped her into something more suited to the task. That is, she thinks, the logic of deep time applied to a single body: adapt or dissolve. She has done both.

The cylinder continues to pulse. Forty seconds. Wait. Forty seconds. Wait. The sound travels through her chamber as a faint pressure change, a subsonic flexing of the air that her mineralized tympanic membranes register not as hearing but as a shift in the way her skull resonates. She can feel it in her teeth when she still had teeth, which she does not; they were the first things to dissolve, their enamel repurposed into the growing lattice of her dermis. Now the pulse arrives in her jaw, which is one continuous plate of dentin-like material, and it propagates down her spine, which has fused into something less vertebral and more notochord-like, flexible but unsegmented, a continuous rod of mineralized cartilage that bends rather than articulates.

She has, she realizes, become a kind of coral. A human coral. A structure that is part biology and part geology, growing in place, waiting.

She maintains the equipment because the equipment maintains her, or what she still is. The oxygen generator needs filter changes. The power regulation boards need occasional resoldering, a task she performs with manipulators that were once her hands—they still have the requisite number of digits, though the joints have simplified, the knuckles smoothing out, the fingernails replaced by wider, flatter plates of the same silica composite that coats the rest of her. She can still grip a soldering iron. She can still read the diagnostic displays, though she does so less and less; she can feel the systems through the cables themselves, through the electromagnetic fields they generate, a sensitivity that began as diagnostic equipment and has become something more like proprioception. The Bight's infrastructure feels to her like an extension of her own body, the cables a circulatory system, the cylinder a second heart beating its unanswered call.

She knows who she is waiting for. She has always known. There were others, once—the people who built this place, who cut the chambers and laid the cables and installed the cylinder and then, for reasons she does not fully understand, did not stay. The mission parameters are still readable in the oldest data stores, the ones she accesses by plugging leads directly into the exposed circuitry of a terminal that has long since lost its screen. Retrieval and Response Operations: RRO-Bight. A team of four. A fifth scheduled to join them within ninety days of activation. The cylinder was the beacon, the call, the *we are here, come find us*. They had sent the message. They had waited for the reply.

The reply had never come.

She was not one of the four. She was the fifth, the one scheduled to arrive later, and by the time she reached the Bight the others were gone. No bodies. No remains. Just the chambers, the equipment, the cylinder pulsing its unanswered call, and a log entry that said, simply: *We cannot stay. We have tried. The signal will continue. If you receive this, we are sorry.*

She did not know what they were sorry for. She knows now. The Bight does not permit prolonged habitation without change. The precipitate, the cold, the mineral chemistry, the eleven-hour breath, the seventeen-month shrug—it all adds up to an environment that dissolves boundaries. The boundary between water and stone. The boundary between living tissue and inert crystal. The boundary between self and system. The others must have felt it happening, must have recognized what the Bight would do to them if they stayed, and they had chosen to leave before the choice became impossible.

She made the opposite choice. The wait, in her assessment, required a permanent presence. A receiver must remain powered. A beacon must continue to broadcast. And if the receiver and the beacon gradually merge into a single entity, a composite being of flesh and stone and electrical potential, a creature whose heartbeat synchronizes with the forty-second pulse and whose thoughts move at the pace of the precipitating mineral sleet—well. That is not a failure of the mission. That is the mission adapting to conditions.

She no longer knows how many years it has been. The data stores keep time, but she has stopped consulting them. It does not matter. The signal, when it comes, will arrive on its own schedule. The Bight was built to wait. She was built to wait. The waiting has become indistinguishable from her existence.

And yet.

There are moments, rare ones, when something close to the old curiosity flickers through her. When a grenadier fish noses along the valley floor, attracted by the cable fields, and she senses it not through external cameras but through the electromagnetic disturbance it creates, a small disruption in the steady hum of the power grid. When a squid drifts through the upper reaches of the Bight, its chromatophores strobing through patterns she has catalogued for decades, patterns that never repeat, that seem to be addressing a listener who is not present. When the seventeen-month crack runs through the mineral rind, a sound that travels through the stone and the water and her own mineralized frame, and for a fraction of a second she feels the Bight as a single integrated body, herself included, a thing that flexes and subsides.

In those moments, she remembers that she is still waiting. That the signal might yet come. That the cylinder still pulses, and she still listens, and somewhere in the patterned noise of the deep a reply might be forming.

She turns her attention back to the waiting. The cylinder thuds. She counts. Forty seconds. Thud.

The copepods twitch in unison. The rosettes fix another microscopic layer of manganese into their tissues. The mineral sleet continues its silent fall, centimeter by century, layering the valley in new stone. And in her chamber, half-filled now with her own precipitated self, the woman who was once the fifth member of RRO-Bight opens her mineralized eyes in the dark and watches, with senses that no longer have names, for the signal that will tell her the wait is over.
