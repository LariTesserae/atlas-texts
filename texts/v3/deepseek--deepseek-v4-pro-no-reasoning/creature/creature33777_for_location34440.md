She is called the Keeper, though she has no one to name her and no voice to speak the name aloud. She has been here as long as the city has been here, which is to say not very long and also forever, the way a fire that has only just been lit already contains the shape of its own dying.

She moves through the basin with the pace of the water-threads, slow and wandering, pulled by gradients no one else can feel. Her body is not quite a body in the way most things are bodies. She is tall and narrow, attenuated, shoulders that slope downward into arms too long for her torso, fingers that trail almost to her ankles when she stands still. Her skin is the same grey-green as the walls, with the same matte quality, the same way of swallowing light, and if she presses herself flat against a building and goes still, you would not see her at all. You would walk past, your eyes sliding over the surface, registering nothing but architecture. She does this sometimes, when the mood takes her, when the city's slow pulse suggests a retreat. She stands against a spiraling tower and lets the mist bead on her skin and waits, breathing in time with the building's own respiration, until she forgets she is separate from it.

She is not separate from it. This is the first thing to understand.

Her hair, if it can be called hair, is a cascade of fine filaments, colorless and translucent, like the threads the small segmented creatures extend to drink. It falls past her waist and moves independently of any breeze, because there is no breeze, lifting and drifting in slow coils as if suspended in water. Sometimes a filament detaches and floats away, joining the mist, and she does not notice. She notices very little, in the way that a person with eyes notices seeing; it is simply the condition of her existence, too constant to require attention. When a filament leaves her, it becomes part of the city's weather. It might settle on a windowsill and slowly dissolve into the grey-green substance of the wall. It might be taken up by a wandering water-thread and carried uphill to somewhere it has never been.

She walks the open stretches between the building nodes, her bare feet leaving no prints in the purple groundcover, though the vegetation responds to her passage. The low, breathing mat pulses faster when she is near, its expansion-contraction rhythm quickening as if in anticipation, then slowing again once she has passed. She does not look down at it. She looks at the water mostly, tracking its slow movements with her pale eyes—eyes that are the color of the standing pools, silver-black, without visible pupils. The water responds to her gaze. It alters its course fractionally, a thread bending toward her, climbing the air in a thin arc to touch her outstretched fingers before falling back into its channel. She tastes it that way, through her skin. The water carries information: the mineral content of a new extrusion forming on the northern edge of the basin, the slight shift in pH where a spire has been adjusting its angle, the chemical whisper of one of the small dark creatures having passed this way hours ago. She reads it all without conscious effort, the way you might read the mood of a room when you enter it.

She does not eat. She does not sleep in the way sleep is usually understood. There are times when she folds herself into one of the smaller, enclosed spaces the city has extruded—a room shaped like a seed pod, smooth-walled and warm, tucked beneath an overhang where the broad purple leaves form a curtain. She curls there, knees drawn up, long arms wrapped around her shins, and she goes still. Her breathing slows to a cycle so long that if you were watching you might think she had stopped entirely: one inhalation every three or four minutes, the air moving through her in a sigh so faint it doesn't disturb the mist. During these periods, which might last hours or might last days, her awareness diffuses outward. She is not unconscious; she is distributed. She feels the slow turning of the helical tower a quarter mile away, the torque in its base as it tracks its absent sky-mark. She feels the water threading along a new path on the eastern slope, where a declivity has shifted overnight. She feels the small segmented creature pause at the edge of a pool, extending its proboscis, and she feels the water enter its body, and in that moment she is the creature, briefly, tasting the metallic tang of the water through senses she does not have when she is herself. Then the creature moves on, and she lets it go, and she is only the city again, waiting in the dark of her pod.

She is not lonely. This might be surprising, but loneliness requires a concept of others, and she has never met another. She has a concept of the city, which is not other but self. She has a concept of the water, which is not other but a circulating system within the self. She has a concept of the creatures, the plants, the slow-turning spires, and they are all part of her in the way that your fingers are part of you, distinct but not separate, capable of independent motion but never truly autonomous. If she were to encounter another being like herself—another person, another mind housed in a body—she would not know what she was seeing. She might mistake it for a new extrusion, a building shaped oddly, and she would press her palm to its chest to feel the resonance of its material, and the confusion when it felt warm and soft and alive in a way that was not her aliveness might be the first truly new thing she had ever felt.

She has a purpose, though she does not think of it that way. She maintains. Not consciously, not with tools or plans, but by her presence, by her walking, by the way her trailing fingers touch the walls as she passes and leave behind a faint shimmer that sinks into the grey-green substance and heals the microscopic fractures that time and the slow grinding of the city's motion have opened. She is the city's immune system, its circulation, its quiet autonomic nervous system. When a water-thread becomes sluggish, clogged with some particulate she cannot name, she kneels beside it and lowers her face to the surface and breathes out, and her breath is a solvent that dissolves the obstruction into its component molecules, which the water then carries away to be remade into something useful. When one of the small creatures becomes injured—she has found them occasionally, a cracked segment in their dark carapace, leaking a fluid that smells like ozone and wet earth—she cups it in her long hands and holds it against her chest and hums. The hum is the same tone the city makes, the low sustained note that sounds every so often across the basin, and the creature's carapace knits together under the vibration, and it scurries away when she opens her hands.

She does not know the word "hum," or "note," or "vibration." She does not know any words. When she needs to communicate—and she rarely does—she uses the water. She dips her fingers into a thread and thinks a thought, and the thought travels along the thread to wherever it needs to go, which is usually nowhere, because there is no one to receive it. Occasionally she sends a thought out anyway, a wordless impulse of quiet attention, a feeling of warmth-and-moisture-and-low-light that is her equivalent of contentment, and it travels the whole network of threads and pools and drifting mist until it dissipates at the edges of the basin, where the water loses its purpose and sinks into the dry ground of the plains. She does not know if anything receives it. She suspects, in a way that is not quite suspicion but more a kind of ambient awareness, that the city itself receives it, and that the city is grateful, or whatever the city's equivalent of gratitude might be.

She has memories, but they are not arranged in a sequence. They are simultaneous, a field of impressions that she can dip into without moving: the taste of the first water that ever climbed uphill, sharp with novelty; the sensation of the ground splitting open as the first extrusion broke through, the grey-green material hot and pliable, cooling into the shape of an archway that still stands in the oldest node; the first time she opened her eyes and saw the amber light diffusing through the wool-grey clouds, and understood without being told that this was where she belonged, that she would never leave, that leaving was not a concept that applied to her. She also has memories that are not hers. They belong to the city, or to whatever the city was before it was the city. She remembers a river, a real river, water that flowed obediently downhill, carving channels in the clay. She remembers the river learning to climb, though she cannot remember how or why, only the feeling of it: a wrenching, a reorientation, a fundamental law being gently set aside. She remembers the moment the city began, which was not a beginning but a transformation, the river becoming the threads, the clay becoming the extrusion-material, the empty basin becoming occupied by something that had always been latent in its contours.

This last memory is not visual. It is tactile, proprioceptive, the feeling of a body that was once linear and flowing becoming distributed and intricate. She feels it sometimes when she walks the open ground between nodes, an echo in her own long limbs, a ghost of river-current in her veins.

She has a name, though she has never spoken it and never will. The city knows it. The city murmurs it in the low sustained tone that sounds every so often across the basin, a frequency that vibrates in the walls and the water and the slow-turning spires. If you were to stand in the basin and listen, really listen, with the kind of attention that takes hours to build but that the place encourages in you, you might almost make it out. It sounds like the mist condensing on resonant surfaces, like the plash of water dripping upward, like the hum of a machine running at idle, waiting. It sounds like home.
