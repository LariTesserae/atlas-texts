I’ve been trying to find the edge of the basin field for—I don’t know how long. The light doesn’t change, so time here is just a count of footsteps and the slow awareness of thirst. I’ve been drinking from the rivulets that weep down the rock faces, water that tastes faintly of metal and something organic, something almost sweet. It hasn’t killed me yet. I don’t think this place is hostile, exactly. I think it simply doesn’t register me as a category.

I’ve started noticing the things that live in the margins.

There’s an animal—if that’s the word—that moves along the dry ridges between the basins. I saw the first one on what I’m calling the third day, though the light never dimmed. It was about the size of a large dog, but low to the ground, its body a flattened oval of overlapping plates like the shell of a chiton, if chitons came ashore and learned to run. The plates aren’t rigid; they ripple as it moves, a wave of articulation that carries it over the sharp crystal edges of the rock without injury. Its legs—eight of them, jointed in three places, ending in pads that grip the stone—are arranged not in pairs but in a ring around its body, so there is no front or back to it, no head. It can move in any direction instantly, a skittering reorientation that makes it hard to track.

But it has a head. I think. There’s a sensory cluster on the upper surface, a tight rosette of stalks that each bear a different organ: one a glossy black bead that I assume is an eye, another a feathery structure that vibrates constantly, tasting the air, a third that pulses with a faint internal light in a rhythm that matches—I checked this, I stood still and counted—the contraction rate of the deep masses in the nearest basin. The whole cluster rotates independently of the body’s movement, so the animal can be running at full tilt in one direction while its senses are trained on something else entirely. It’s unsettling to watch. It’s like watching a creature that is never fully in the same moment as its body.

I saw it hunting. Or gathering. Whatever the verb is.

It approached a basin rim slowly, the plates of its body flattening further until it was almost invisible against the stone, and it extended a long proboscis from beneath the sensory cluster—a tube of muscle as thin as my finger, translucent, the inside lined with cilia that moved in a spiral. The proboscis dipped into the vegetation mat, not piercing it but insinuating between the fronds, and I watched as the cilia began to collect something I couldn’t see. After a moment, the animal withdrew the proboscis and reversed its direction without turning around—just a shift of its gait, the legs reorienting—and carried its invisible harvest back along the ridge.

I followed it. I had nothing else to do.

It led me to a place where the rock rose in a low hummock, one of the few elevations I’ve seen that wasn’t a basin rim. And there, in a crevice between two crystalline outcroppings, I found a den. The animal had hollowed out a space in the softened rock—softened how, I don’t know, maybe by some secretion—and lined it with dried vegetation mats that had been woven into a kind of felt. Inside the den were others of its kind, smaller, their plates not yet hardened, their sensory clusters only nubs. The adult approached them, and the proboscis extended again, and this time I saw what it carried: a pale fluid, faintly phosphorescent, which the young ones absorbed directly through the surfaces of their bodies. They didn’t feed. They simply pressed against the adult, and the fluid transferred by contact, and when the adult pulled away the young ones glowed slightly brighter for a few minutes, then settled.

I don’t think that fluid was food. I think it was light, or something like light—metabolized photons, captured by the vegetation and concentrated into a chemical slurry that the adult had siphoned from the mat. The young ones needed it to grow their plates, to develop their own sensory clusters. They were feeding on light once removed, on solar energy that had been processed by plants and then refined by whatever glandular alchemy the adult carried in its body.

I spent a long time watching the den. The adults came and went in irregular cycles, sometimes bringing the fluid, sometimes bringing larger prey: flyers they’d caught on the wing—I didn’t see how, I only saw the results—their iridescent wings still twitching, their tubular bodies already dissolving from the enzymatic saliva the adults coated them with. The young ones fed on the flyers with a kind of passive absorption, pressing their soft underbellies against the carcasses until the flesh was reduced to a dry husk that the adults then removed, carrying it away to the basin rims and dropping it into the water, where the deep masses would take it.

That was the moment I started to understand the system.

The flyers eat the vegetation. The slow deep masses eat the fallen flyers and the vegetation the flyers and the adults don’t eat. The fluid they bring their young—that’s not from the basin mats, I realized. It’s from somewhere else. I followed another adult a day later—or what felt like a day—and watched it go to a different kind of basin, one I hadn’t recognized before. This one was smaller, shallower, and the water in it was not clear but milky, thick with a suspension of something that caught the light and held it, so the whole basin glowed faintly from within. The vegetation here was different: not the dense green mats but a pale, almost white growth, filamentous, like a fungus or a bacterial mat, and it pulsed with the same rhythm as the adult’s light-organ. The adult dipped its proboscis into the milky water and drank, and the light-organ on its sensory cluster brightened perceptibly as it did.

The milky water was the product. I think. I think the whole system—the flyers, the mats, the deep slow digesters—all of it was refining something down to this: a photochemical slurry that the adults collected and carried to their young, and that the young metabolized into their own bodies. And the flyers were a byproduct. Or maybe the flyers were part of the refining process. Maybe the light they refracted in their iridescent wings wasn’t just display; maybe it was a step in the cascade, a frequency-shifting that the system required, and the deep masses absorbed the shifted light and used it to drive their slow digestion, and the milky water was the end state, the final concentration.

The whole High Plateau was a light-factory. The plasma-sky provided the raw input, a constant flood of photons, and the ecosystem processed them through trophic layers that were also industrial steps, each organism a living machine that performed one part of the cascade. The vegetation absorbed. The flyers transformed. The deep masses concentrated. And the plate-animals collected the concentrate and fed it to their young, who would become the next generation of collectors.

But what were the collectors collecting for? What happened to the adults when they died? Where did the photochemical energy end up?

I still don’t know. I haven’t found a carcass yet, not of one of the plate-animals. Maybe they don’t die. Maybe they go somewhere else, when they’ve collected enough—maybe there’s a larger system beyond this plateau, a wider machine that the collected light feeds into, and the adults migrate there when they’re full, their bodies engorged with the fluid, their sensory clusters blazing.

Or maybe the system has no end. Maybe the light is just cycled, endlessly, from sky to plant to flyer to deep mass to milky water to collector to young collector, and the energy is never spent, only transformed, and the whole plateau is a closed loop, an eternal production line that makes nothing except its own continuation.
