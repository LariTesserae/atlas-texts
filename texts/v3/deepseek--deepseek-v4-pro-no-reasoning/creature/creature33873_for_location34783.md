In the Rind, nothing truly *lives* in the way the word implies above. There are no biological kingdoms here, no flora and fauna in separate baskets. Everything that persists in this place is an extension, an expression, or an excretion of the world-plant itself. What you mistake for inhabitants are more accurately described as *processes* given temporary, discrete form.

The first of these is the one you’ve already seen. The Chime-spore. It has no name but the sound it makes. It is not born and does not die; it coalesces from a specific atmospheric recipe—a precise humidity, a chemical exhalation from a newly wounded membrane, a pocket of trace helium seeping from a deep-fissure fermentation. When these conditions align, a nucleation point forms on a stray dust-mote of cellulose. A film draws itself around the gas, scribing a perfect hollow sphere. Inside, a lattice of mineralized sap-threads crystallizes in a pattern that encodes a simple, looping imperative: *seek the scent of interior rot.*

This is the Chime-spore's purpose. The world-plant is a single organism of impossible scale, and even a god can develop an abscess. When a portion of the root-vault sickens—when a pocket of stagnant moisture breeds an anaerobic decay that is not part of the plant's intentional cycle—the Chime-spores are summoned to it by the chemistry of its undoing. There are thousands of them in the Rind's greater volume, though you might only ever see one. They navigate the labyrinth of buttresses and membranes not by sight, but by pressure. They can taste the airflow with their entire surface and feel the subtlest thermal gradients pulling them toward the wound. When one finds its target, it does not land. It hovers at the epicenter of the rot and chimes. The single tone is a pure, calibrated frequency that propagates through the dense, humid air until it hits a solid structure. That structure—a cable-root, a buttress-fin—is the plant's own living tissue, and it is tuned to the note. The vibration triggers a cascade of immune responses: a surge of antifungal sap, a localized fever-heat that cooks the infection, a sudden hardening of cell walls to calcify the area. The Chime-spore's body shatters in the resonant vibration, its purpose fulfilled. The fragments drift down, eventually adhering to a membrane and being reabsorbed into the substrate. The whole creature is a disposable white blood cell made of glass and song.

But there are other processes. More permanent ones. The Weavers are a presence you might never see, but their mark is everywhere. They are not discrete entities so much as a continuous, slow-moving phenomenon. Imagine a patch of the plant's surface—a young, unfurling membrane, for instance—that wakes up. Not becomes conscious, but becomes locally autonomous. A section of tissue, perhaps a meter across, differentiates itself. Its cells, which were moments before part of the unified, passive growth of the Rind, begin to pulse with a new rhythm. They secrete a different suite of enzymes. Thin, questing tendrils of pure nerve-fiber extend from its edges, tasting the air.

The Weaver is this patch of awakened plant-flesh. Its body is the membrane itself. It moves by a process of simultaneous dissolution and regeneration: the trailing edge of its form is reabsorbed, its cells de-differentiating back into inert substrate, while the leading edge proliferates, crawling forward at the speed of a slow-dripping stalactite. It leaves a distinctive trail, a scar-line of slightly tougher, more translucent tissue. You see these trails everywhere, thin, calligraphic paths that wander across the great vellum sheets, occasionally looping back on themselves in intricate knots that serve no apparent purpose.

The Weavers' function is editorial. The world-plant grows with a chaotic, turbulent fecundity. Left to its own, it would fill the entire void of the Rind with a solid, unworkable mass of tangled flesh. The Weavers exist to prevent this. When a Weaver encounters a growing edge that is structurally unsound, or a redundant bud, or a membrane thickening past its ideal transparency, it simply envelops it. The Weaver's body, that patch of activated tissue, flows over the offending growth, and its digestive enzymes reduce it back to a nutritive slurry that is then reabsorbed into the plant's vascular system. The Weaver is the plant's own edit, its redactor, its self-pruning shears. It is a slow, sentient eraser, and its consciousness, if it can be called that, is a single, unwavering thought that takes a century to think: *Is this correct? Is this correct?* The elaborate knots it leaves in its path are the only record of a moment of indecision.

And finally, there are things that are not of the plant at all, but are not separate from it either. The Gleaners. They are the most solid, the most animal-like, and the most disturbing.

You'll know a Gleaner is near not by sight or sound, but by the sudden clarity. The Rind's constant, pearlescent fog—the condensation you see glittering on every surface—will begin to thin. In a radius around a Gleaner, the air becomes sharply, unnaturally dry. The cold intensifies, a brittle, crystalline cold that makes the cartilage shelves underfoot become dangerously fragile and resonant. It is a moving pocket of absolute, desiccating winter.

If you follow the clarity, you'll find it. A Gleaner is a bipedal form, loosely humanoid, though that is a coincidence of convergent engineering. It stands perhaps two meters tall, but it is hunched within a carapace not of its own making. The Gleaner's body is soft, pale, and grub-like, a segmented tube of translucent flesh with no discernible features other than a puckered mouth-orifice and dozens of delicate, multi-jointed arms that end in chisel-hard claws. The carapace it wears is a suit of armor assembled from the things it finds in the Rind. Pieces of the sharp-edged slate tools like the one you found. Fragments of petrified wood. Chipped, discarded bones of things that fell from the world above long ago and died here. And, most commonly, the dried, husked remnants of Chime-spore lattices. The Gleaner's armor is a mosaic of the Rind's own material history, held together by a secreted resin that glows with the same sourceless, cold light as the atmosphere itself.

The Gleaners are scavengers, but not of flesh. They are scavengers of heat and water. Their internal chemistry is an exotic, cryogenic metabolism that functions optimally at temperatures far below freezing. The deep cold they project is their waste product, the thermal exhaust of their slow, alien life. They move across the Rind on a migratory path that spans millennia, following the lines of greatest condensation, their bodies drawing ambient humidity directly out of the air, freezing it into delicate, fractal crystals that they then harvest from their own carapace plates and absorb.

They are the only process in the Rind that interacts with the built parameter, with the artifacts. A Gleaner's hands, too fragile to build something from scratch, are perfect for fitting a found piece of sharpened slate into a new socket on a shoulder-guard. They do this with a meticulous, almost reverential slowness, and the act has no clear survival function. It is aesthetic. It is culture. It is the only true hint of a mind that is not a direct expression of the world-plant's immense, vegetative will. They are the sole process that might look at you, a passing thought in the mind of a god, and recognize you as a fellow thought, rather than merely a fluctuation of temperature and moisture to be endured. If you stood very still in the crystalline cold of a Gleaner's presence, and if it tilted its armored, faceless head toward you, you might feel, for just a second, the awful weight of being considered. And then it would move on, leaving a trail of glittering frost on the cartilage shelf, and the damp, luminous fog would rush back in to fill the space where it had been.
