It is an old man who tends the highest cistern, and his name is Miron, though almost no one calls him that anymore. The children of the Roost call him the Moss-Skimmer, or sometimes the Cistern Father; the Bridge Council, on the rare occasions his name appears in their ledgers, refers to him as the Keeper of the Tenth Filter. He is not the only person who tends the water—there are pipe-keepers and cistern-wrights and the singing guild of rope-splicers who lower the basket-lifts—but he is the only one who has worked the same cistern for as long as anyone can remember, and that alone has made him a kind of landmark, the way the leaning walls of the Quiet Quarter are landmarks, or the cable-trams with their ancient, individual names.

The cistern itself is not the largest in the Roost, but it is the highest by some margin, perched on the uppermost shoulder of the Central Pillar where the infrastructure begins to fray into a latticework of wind-bent aerials old electrical insulators and the remains of a semaphore tower that has not sent a message in living memory. The cistern is a great iron tank, riveted together from plates that may once have been part of a ship, or a boiler, or the hull of some machine no one now alive could identify. It sits on a stone cradle that has been shimmed and patched over the centuries until it is more shim than stone, and its surface blooms with the pale green patina of copper runoff and the darker, liverish stains of rust that has somehow become waterproof, the same way the old iron bridges have. Around it runs a narrow walkway of gratings and plates, and around that, a hair-fine network of gutters, troughs, weirs, and settling pans that looks less like engineering than like the plumbing of some enormous metallic leaf, exquisitely veined, forever conducting water from one state to another.

Miron has tended this cistern for sixty-one years. He was born on the East Pillar, in the Quiet Quarter, in a house where the ferns grew so thick over the windows that the light inside was always green, even at noon. His mother wove moss-filters for the cistern intakes; his father was a steam-fitter who died young, cooked by a burst pipe. Miron remembers little of them, but he remembers the day he first climbed to the high cistern as an apprentice. He was nine years old, and the cistern-keeper then was a woman named Arel, ancient even by the standards of the pipe-keepers, with a face like the cracked bed of a dried stream and hands that moved with the slow, unstoppable deliberation of water freezing. She had looked at him for a long time, then handed him a brass spoon with a long handle and told him to skim the scum from the settling pan.

“Don’t hurry,” she said. “The water doesn’t hurry. If you hurry, you’ll only stir it up and have to start again.”

He has not hurried since.

He is old now, though not so old as the cistern, and his body has adapted to the demands of his work. He is narrow-shouldered and thin, with a slight stoop that comes not from age but from the habitual posture of leaning over filter-beds. His hands are large for his frame, the knuckles swollen with damp and the cold of high altitude nights, but his fingers are precise, patient, capable of untying a knot no one else would bother with. He wears the same clothes every day, more or less: a heavy canvas apron that has been patched so many times it is no longer possible to tell which patches are original; a waxed wool coat with deep pockets full of small tools—brass snips, copper wire, a mussel-shell for scraping, a magnifying lens from the clockmaker’s shop, a tin of fish-paste for the geckos. His boots are made by the cobbler on the West Pillar, and they are the only things he owns that he calls young, being a mere fifteen years old, barely broken in.

His face, when the children of the Roost see it—which is not often, because he rarely descends below the fourth level of the Central market—is long and creased in the way of old waxed canvas, with deep lines down the cheeks and a high, unfinished-looking forehead. His eyes are the color of the steam-clouded sky just after dawn, a pale grey-blue, and they are preternaturally clear for a man of his age. He can see the beginning of a rain-shower in the way a single fern-frond droops two hundred meters away, and he can tell the difference between condensation from a steam-drain and condensation from an exhaust-bell by the taste on the back of his hand. He rarely speaks. When he does, his voice is rough at first, like a tap turned after a season of disuse, and it has the peculiar property of making people stop and turn, even in the loudest market crowd, because it is so rare that it seems to cut through the ambient noise like a knife through hot butter.

His days are the same, and he likes it that way. He wakes before the sun clears the plateau rim, in the small lean-to of corrugated iron and glass that he built against the lee of the cistern’s stone cradle. The lean-to is bed, and kitchen, and workshop, and all three are the same room, which is not large but is somehow full of light because he has arranged polished tin reflectors around the single window to pull in every scrap of dawn. His bed is a hammock strung with braided moss-fiber, and his stove is a small iron pot over a steam-coil that he can adjust with exquisite precision, and his workbench is the top of a barrel that once held pickled mackerel, and the whole place smells faintly of mackerel and moss and hot brass. He makes tea from rainwater and a dried herb the pipe-keepers call shy-eye, which grows only in the cracks of the north-facing walls and which he picks by feel in the early morning, when the leaves are still furled and soft. The tea is bitter and green and he drinks it standing, looking out over the layered roofs, while the swifts stir under the eaves of the Foundry Warrens a kilometer away.

Then he goes to the water.

The work of the cistern-keeper is not hard, but it is endless, and it requires a kind of attention that most people are not willing to give. The water comes to the cistern by way of the great condensation runs: the vast sheets of corrugated iron that cap the upper terraces, sloped just enough that the nightly condensation and the frequent rain slide into gutters, and from the gutters into rough filtration pools, and from the pools into the cistern itself. But the water never arrives clean. It carries with it dust, which at this altitude is fine and sharp and reddish, and the ash of the geothermals, and the occasional feather of a swift or the dropping of a pigeon, the pollen of the rooftop gardens, the soot of the foundry, the verdigris from the copper flashings, the rust from every iron railing, the crumbled mortar of three hundred years of walls. Miron’s job is to catch it at the top of the cistern, where the inlet pipe discharges into the first settling pan, and to guide it through the series of weirs and troughs and filter-beds—layers of sand and charcoal and moss—until it is clear enough to drink.

The lamplight of his days is not measured by hours but by the quality of the light on the water. In the thin dawn, the settling pans are the color of polished pewter, and the reflections of the aerials and the semaphore tower lie on their surfaces like the ribs of a skeleton. By midmorning, when the sun has cleared the eastern pillar, the water begins to glow, and Miron can see the faint traffic of the bioderms: the tiny swimmer-beings that live in every cistern, water-clear, exquisitely fragile, which the children call “glass needles” and which Miron has studied for decades without ever being able to decide whether they are animals or plants or something else entirely. They pulse and drift in the slow current, and he watches them from the corner of his eye, because looking at them directly seems to make them submerge. By noon, the glare off the water is intense enough that he wears his hat—a broad-brimmed hat of oiled canvas, green with age—and works in the reflected light that flashes all around him, making his slow movements sparkle. By mid-afternoon, when the shadows swing from west to east, the cistern is a great black mirror, polished and still, and the clouds that tear past above are reflected so clearly that he feels, sometimes, that he is standing on the bottom of the sky, looking up at the tower of the earth.

And then the rain comes, and everything changes. The rain on the high cistern is not like rain on the lower terraces. Up here, it becomes a torrent in seconds, a sheer blinding curtain, and the sound on the iron is so loud Miron cannot hear his own footsteps, and the water leaps and dances in the settling pans, and the overflow gutters sing with the sudden rush. He knows the cistern’s capacity to the liter, to the drop; he knows exactly how much rain the filter-beds can take before they begin to channel, and how quickly the overflow must be steered to the lower reservoirs. He knows this the way a sailor knows the balance of a mast, the way a bird knows a thermal. His body responds before his mind does, and his hands move on the valve-wheels and the baffle-boards, opening, closing, redirecting. It is not hurrying, though it looks like hurrying to an outside eye; it is movement in a tempo older and deeper than haste. It is the same tempo as the swifts, which come out in the rain to hunt the insects that are driven out of the moss-beds, and which wheel around Miron’s head in a thunder of tiny bodies, close enough that he feels the whisper of their wings on his cheeks.

When the rain passes—and it always passes, here, quickly and cleanly—the whole world seems to exhale. The last drops fall from the aerials and the semaphore cables, slow and heavy, each one a tiny prism. The cistern steams faintly in the sudden sun, and the moss-beds in the filter troughs turn a deeper green, vivid as a parrot’s wing. The swifts vanish as suddenly as they came, tucking themselves back under the eaves. The mule on the bridge shakes its head, and its bells chime. Miron lets the filters settle, then walks the rim of the cistern, checking the rivets with the palm of his hand, listening for the deep subsonic creak of iron contracting as it begins to dry. Some of the rivets are older than the city walls, and two of them are shaped by smiths whose names he has forgotten, and one of them has a star-cut head that no one else alive can identify.

He is not alone on the high works. Below him, on the terraces, the Roost is waking again. The children come out to play in the puddles with their little paper boats, and the laundry-women open their shutters to let the steam out, and the gear-cutters lift the canvas awnings of their shops, and the sound of ten thousand small industries rises and falls like the breath of the mountain. There are other cisterns and other keepers; there is a whole civic apparatus that sees to the water. But Miron is the only one who cleans the highest filters, and the filters are the only thing that stands between the Roost and the slow accumulation of grit and rust and time itself. The water is not just a resource; it is, as the citizens say, a citizen. And Miron, in his slow and patient way, is in love with it.

He has had no family of his own, though there was once a woman on the South Pillar, a weaver of moss-fiber cloth, who he thinks of from time to time, and who he sometimes believes thinks of him. But the cistern came first, and the years passed, and now his only companions are the geckos that live in the gaps behind the rivets. He has named them all, though he cannot always tell them apart. There is Sliver, who is missing the tip of her tail; there is Big Blue, who is not blue but pale as milk-glass and larger than most; there is Cup and Saucer and Little Bell, and there are dozens more, and they come out in the late afternoon to sit on the warm iron and watch him work, their translucent bodies glowing rosy in the slanted sun. He leaves them saucers of sugar-water, and they drink in tiny, delicate sips, and he believes—though he would never say this to the Bridge Council—that they understand something about the water that he does not.

In the evenings, after the sun has dropped below the plateau rim and the temperature has fallen enough to make the steam rise thick and white from every vent, Miron sits on the high walkway with his back against the cistern and eats his supper. It is usually bread, dense and sour and steamed rather than baked, from the communal bakery on the third terrace; some hard cheese from the flocks that are pastured on the plateau below; a handful of dried figs from the tree that grows out of the collapsed cistern on the East Pillar. He drinks the water of his own cistern, which tastes faintly of moss and mineral, and he listens to the sound of the Roost settling into its night mode. The foundry’s red glow pulses on the underside of the clouds. The cable-trams make their last runs, their bells chiming across the abyss. The basket-lift rope-splicers stop their work, and their chanting fades until there is only the wind in the stays, and the distant cry of a hawk, and the soft, continuous, oceanic murmur of steam.

He is old, but he is not tired. Once, years ago, he fell asleep on the high walkway and woke to find his clothes wringing wet with the midnight condensation, and a gecko sitting on his knee, and he had felt, for a moment, that he had become part of the cistern’s slow dream—a dream of water and iron, light and rust, the stop and begin of centuries. He has never hurried since. He will not hurry now. He will finish his meal, and he will walk the rim one last time in the dark, and then he will sleep, listening to the water breathe.

They call him the Moss-Skimmer, and it is enough.
