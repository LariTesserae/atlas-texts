Here, in the High District, live the ones who maintain the city's breath.

They are called the **Vent-tenders**, and they are not quite like the people of the lower terraces. You can tell them by their hands—long, pale, the knuckles gnarled from decades of working with heated flux-lines and cold salt-fittings. Their skin has taken on a faint translucence over generations, as though the mineral character of the place has begun to seep into the flesh itself. In certain lights, when they stand before a boiler's chromatic glow, you can see the shadows of their bones through the skin of their forearms, grey-white like salt-veins in the Mass.

They live in the upper reaches of the industrial cathedrals—not in the machinery halls themselves, but in the interstitial spaces, the maintenance galleries and bell-towers and narrow thermal-lofts that nest between the great heat-exchangers. Their quarters are small and warm and smell of hot mineral and the faint aldehydic tang of flux-gum. The walls are bare salt-brick, smoothed by centuries of oiled palms. The windows, where they exist, are not glass but thin sheets of translucent salt-crystal, sliced from the Mass and polished until they admit a diffuse, cloudy light. Through these windows the Vent-tenders look down upon their city and see, not a landscape, but a system—a living thermodynamic body whose pulse they are sworn to keep steady.

They tend the city's breath as a duty and a devotion. Each Vent-tender is bound to a specific engine-house, a specific cluster of exhaust-shafts and pressure-valves and condensers. They know their machines not by manual but by memory, by the minute changes in vibration that travel through the floor-grids, by the way a particular valve-gasket warms slightly before it fails, by the tiny shifts in the pitch of the discharge-hum when the flux-gum runs too rich or too lean. They carry no tools but a soft iron key and a small flask of refined gum-oil, and they move through the great machinery with a quiet, almost religious familiarity, touching a housing here, listening at a pipe-joint there, adjusting a needle-valve by a quarter-turn that no one else would notice.

Every Vent-tender keeps a **cyst-column** of their own. Not in a garden—there are no gardens—but in a small stone basin outside their quarters, on the gallery ledge, or in the angle of a buttress where the discharge-air flows warm and constant. The columns are cultivated, shaped over years by the careful application of mineral-rich flux-ash and the selective chipping of nascent growths. They grow slowly, resentfully, but they grow, and a Vent-tender's status is measured, in part, by the height and symmetry of their column. Some have been tended by the same lineage for six or seven generations, standing now taller than their keepers, their dendritic furring dense and regular as cropped wool. The Vent-tenders speak to them, not in words but in small clicks of the tongue and the occasional tap of the iron key against the column's base, a conversational rhythm that echoes the piston-tempo of the engines below.

They do not eat, not in the way the lower-district sort do. Their diet is mostly gum-cake—a dense, waxy confection made from refined flux-gum and mineral flour, pressed into discs and aged in the exhaust-heat until it becomes something like very hard, very salty cheese. They gnaw at it slowly over the course of a shift, and it leaves a faint pale dust at the corners of their mouths. They drink no water. What liquid they take is a thin, bitter infusion of salt-lichen scraped from the lower cyst-column furring, steeped in hot condensate from the cooling coils. It tastes, outsiders say, like licking a hot stone. The Vent-tenders find it comforting.

Their speech is spare, economical, and timed to the city's rhythm. They pause at intervals that correspond to the engine-cycles, and they speak in a dialect full of mechanical metaphor: *pressure* for urgency, *venting* for anger, *cold-start* for a difficult birth, *coking* for a slow grudge. They have no words for the sky; they call it *the high exhaust*. They call the sun *the great boiler*. They call the distant, wind-sculpted salt-fields *the settling floor*. They do not see the world as landscape but as process; they do not see the city as architecture but as a living machine, and themselves as its lungs.

There are perhaps two thousand of them in the High District, scattered across the upper terraces and the cathedral-factories in loose kin-groups of ten or twenty. They do not gather often, but when they do—on the rare days when the engine-hum falls briefly out of sync, or when a great boiler must be cold-shut for maintenance—they come together in the wide exchange-halls, the ones with the spiral-carved gear-housings, and they speak for hours in their slow mechanical idiom, debating the city's health. Their voices are low and flat and carry little emotion, but their hands are never still—they trace diagrams in the air, tap rhythms on the salt-brick pillars, draw pipe-networks in the fine mineral dust that settles on every surface. It is said among them that the city dreams in these gatherings, and that the Vent-tenders are simply the medium through which the dream speaks.

Because they believe the city dreams. They believe the engines are not merely machines but organs of a larger body, and that the Salt-Mass is not dead mineral but a slow, deep form of life—too vast and too old for human categories, but life nonetheless. They point to the flux-gum that weeps from the seams, the way it flows more freely when the temperature is right; they point to the cyst-columns, which grow faster near the vents, which lean toward the exhaust-flows like plants toward light. They point to the city's own breath, the infrasonic pulse that rises and falls across days, and they say: *that is not machinery. That is a body, thinking.*

And so they tend. They keep the boilers lit, the valves clear, the gum flowing. They raise their children in the thermal-lofts, teaching them the feel of a good bearing by touch, the sound of a clean burn, the smell of a seal about to fail. Their children are born with the same long pale hands, and by the age of five they can tap out the piston-tempo on the floor-grids with perfect accuracy. At seven they are given their first small cyst-column, a finger-sized nub of salt-crystal set in a stone cup, and they learn patience by watching it grow. At twelve they walk the maintenance galleries alone for the first time, and the city's breath becomes their own.

They do not fear death. They speak of it as *de-pressurization*, a gradual and natural release. The bodies of their dead are not buried—there is no soil—but are given to the Salt-Mass, laid in the open seams where the flux-gum weeps, covered with a thin shroud of mineral ash. The gum flows over them slowly, over months and years, and incorporates their bones into its amber matrix. The Vent-tenders say the dead become part of the city's blood, and that their thoughts join the slow dream of the engines. Perhaps this is true. When you stand in the High District at dawn, in that brief still moment before the boilers fully catch, you can sometimes hear something in the subsonic pulse—a pattern that is not quite mechanical, a rhythm that seems, for a moment, to have a voice.

And high above, in the roosts of the needle-flights, the many-winged things stir and settle, and their paper-friction sound drifts down through the thermal layers, and the Vent-tenders look up from their work and listen, and nod, and return to the valves and pipes and the slow, patient breathing of the world.
