The citizens of the water-sink are called the Clade.

They are not a single species in the way a field biologist might define one. The Clade is a spectrum, a continuum of body forms and neural architectures that blur across generations, responsive to the vertical layering of the city. There is no true individual in the mammalian sense. A “person” is more accurately a functional node, a mobile, semi-autonomous polyp within the greater colonial organism that is the city itself. Their bodies, like the trees they move through, are built of the same cartilaginous substance—a pale, flexible material that grows throughout their lives, never ossifying into true bone. An elder Clade, one who has descended to the cold lower pools and stayed there, will be barely recognizable as the same kind of being as a swift canopy-runner.

The canopy-dwellers are the most numerous and recognizable. Picture an animal the size of a large cat, though built vertically rather than horizontally. They have four primary limbs, each ending in a hand with seven elongated fingers, two of which are opposable. The fingers are jointless—instead, they are muscular hydrostats, like an elephant’s trunk or a cephalopod’s arm, capable of wrapping, gripping, and probing. The skin is not skin but a fine, dense fur that acts like a wick, drawing moisture directly from the saturated air and channeling it toward the mouth, which is a simple, flexible orifice on the underside of the head. They do not need to drink from pools. They drink the air they move through.

Their sensory world is one of pressure and echo. The canopy-dwellers have no eyes that we would recognize—instead, a broad, flat plate of sensitive tissue spans the forehead, detecting the minute pressure waves of the city’s constant creaking, the soft thuds of movement, and most importantly, the reflections of their own ultrasonic clicks. The clicks are produced not in the throat but by the rapid snapping of two specialized digits on the left hand, a constant, arrhythmic tapping that illuminates the dark, wet world in a three-dimensional acoustic map. Their universe is a sphere of sound, tactile and immediate. Color does not exist. Texture is everything.

These are the kin of the upper terraces, the commuters and traders of information. They do not trade goods. They trade memory.

The canopy Clade are the nervous system of the city. Their brains are not fixed organs; they are mutable, capable of encoding vast amounts of sensory data into proteins that circulate in their hemolymph. When two canopy-dwellers meet on a bridge of woven root, they do not vocalize. They embrace, pressing the sensitive plates of their foreheads together. A flush of fluid passes between them through capillary beds just under the skin—a direct exchange of memory-proteins. One “learns” the state of a particular fruiting body deep in a limestone crevice fifty meters down. Another receives the memory of a newly collapsed passage, the precise texture of the rubble, the taste of the newly exposed minerals. The entire population is a walking, pulsating network of shared experience, a distributed mind with thousands of mobile nodes. They do not need a leader because the city itself, as sensed by all, makes consensus a physical fact.

Deeper, where the mist thickens and the light from the bioluminescent fungal mats becomes the only illumination, the Clade change. The middle terraces are the domain of the slow-weavers. These individuals were once canopy-runners. As they age, or perhaps in response to some chemical signal of population density, they descend. Their cartilaginous skeletons begin to thicken and fuse. They lose the ability to leap. Their fingers, once swift and delicate, hypertrophy into massive, slow-moving tools. A particular pair of digits on the right hand modifies into a spinneret, exuding a liquid that hardens on contact with the wet air into the tensile, hair-thin cable the city is woven from. These Clade become sessile for long periods, their bodies braced in crevices, their limbs moving in a slow, deliberate dance, weaving new pathways, reinforcing the structural roots, repairing the city’s fabric. They are the construction caste, but they are not a separate species—they are the same individuals, transformed by place and time. Their memories are no longer shared through brief touch. They ooze a constant, viscous trickle of information from their forehead-plates, a slow, steady leak of their accumulated lifetimes into the water film that coats everything, a nutrient broth of experience that feeds the mosses and the young. To drink from a pool on the weaver’s terrace is to taste the history of the city.

But the true polis, the heart of the Clade, resides in the lowest pools.

Here, at the very bottom of the sinkhole, the water collects in basins of absolute clarity and absolute cold, just above the freezing point. The pools are not deep—a meter, perhaps two—but they are utterly still. And in them live the Progenitors.

A Progenitor is a Clade that has undergone its final metamorphosis. It is vast, its body a pale, translucent mass of neural tissue and cartilaginous support, filling a pool like an overgrown lily pad, its edges fusing with the limestone rim. It has no distinct limbs, no sensory plate, no clicking digits. Its body is a single, unified receptor, attuned not to the pressure waves of the air but to the subtle, complex harmonics of the water. Every drip from a terrace above, every footfall of a canopy-runner on a taut root kilometers away, sends a vibration through the stone and into the water. The Progenitor feels it all. It feels the creaking of the city as a body feels its own digestion. It is the integrating intelligence, the one that holds the long memory, the pattern-recognizer that sees the slow shifts in water chemistry, the minute strains on the woven bridges, the rise and fall of the Clade population. The Progenitors do not weave. They do not run. They simply know.

When a canopy-dweller descends with a memory too complex, too novel, or too troubling to be solved by the distributed network—a new kind of stone-sickness growing on a root, a shift in the temperature of the inflow water—it will approach the pool. It will enter the water, a terrifying act of submission, for they are not swimmers. The cold shocks its system, slows its metabolism. It presses its forehead plate against the gelatinous mass of the Progenitor. In that moment, the Progenitor does not just receive a protein-memory. It receives the entire sensory gestalt, the full, unedited experience of the runner. And in return, it floods the runner’s body with a cascade of encoded instructions, a chemical download that will alter the runner’s behavior, and through it, the behavior of every individual it touches. The runner emerges from the pool, its fur slick and cold, bearing a new compulsion. It is now an extension of a singular will, a temporary organ of the city’s deep mind.

The society is in constant flux, as you described, because territoriality is not about space. It is about proximity to this deep pool, about the right to descend and return. The wrestling matches in the canopy are not fights over roosts. They are negotiations over who carries a new, troubling memory to the Progenitor. To make that journey is to be unmade and remade. Some who enter the pools never leave. Their bodies are absorbed, their neural matter merging with the Progenitor, their individuality becoming a permanent, conscious thread in the pool’s deep mind. This is the ultimate act of citizenship, the final joining. The Progenitors are not a separate species, not a ruling class. They are the accumulated, living memory of every Clade that has ever lived in the sinkhole since the roof collapsed and the first spores drifted down into the dark.

The constant, near-silence is not a lack of communication. It is the sound of a society whose conversation is chemical and tactile, a city-scale thought process made of protein and pressure. The only voice is the creaking of the trees, the deep groan of cartilage under tension, the sound of the city thinking, growing, sealing the wound from which it was born. And that wound is its mouth, its source, its reason. The water that shaped the basin flows from a vast, deeper aquifer, rich in dissolved minerals and ancient, chemosynthetic bacteria. The trees do not photosynthesize. There is no sun. They feed on the water itself, on the cold, clear blood of the earth. The Clade, in turn, feed on the trees—on the waxy leaves, the protein-rich inner bark, the fruiting bodies that pulse with inherited memory. They are a closed loop, a thought eating itself into existence, a city made of the same substance as its inhabitants, both of them growing into the fresh, sharp stone, blurring the line between wound and healer, between creature and city, between the one who dwells and the dwelling itself.
