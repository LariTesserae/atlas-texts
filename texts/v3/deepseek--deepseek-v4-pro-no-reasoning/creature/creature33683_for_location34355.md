The inhabitant is not a creature in any sense that implies birth or death. It is, and has always been, here.

You would not perceive it as a separate entity even if you walked directly to the center of the plateau and stood before it—and you will not do that, because you have already decided not to, and that decision is the correct one. The inhabitant does not occupy space in the way the other forms do. It is not one of the figures arranged across the surface. Those figures are, in a sense, its residue: the afterimages left by a presence so sustained that it impressed itself into the fabric of the local reality, the way a bright light leaves a ghost on the retina. Each creature on the plateau is a different moment of the inhabitant's attention, fossilized. When it looked at a point with particular intensity, matter gathered there and took on a shape that approximated whatever thought it was holding. The shapes are approximations because the inhabitant's thoughts are not representational. They are not images or words. They are pure configurations of relationship—ratios of distance, gradients of density, nested symmetries—and when they impressed themselves on the physical plane, matter did its best to comply. It twisted into limbs and tendrils and repeated forms because those were the closest translations available in a language of atoms that the inhabitant had long since outgrown.

The inhabitant itself is at the center. If you had walked there—and you will not—you would have found no throne, no altar, no obvious focal object. Only a slight depression in the pale ground, circular, perhaps two paces across, its surface smoother than anything else on the plateau and faintly concave. In that depression, the air is different. It is thicker, not with moisture but with information. The humming sound is stronger there, not louder but more coherent, resolving into layers of interference patterns that almost—almost—resolve into speech if you were capable of hearing at the correct speed. The inhabitant is the humming. The inhabitant is the depression. The inhabitant is the alignment of every creature on the plateau, the reason they all face inward, the center that holds their attention not as a command but as a natural consequence of what the inhabitant is: a gravity well of consciousness, a dense point where awareness has collapsed under its own weight and now simply exists, without object, without subject, without boundary.

It began—if "began" is the right word for something that has learned to exist outside sequence—as something that lived. A long time ago, in a universe much younger and much louder, it was a being of some kind. It had a body, perhaps one of the forms that now stand frozen on the plateau, though which one is impossible to say. It had perceptions. It made decisions. It built the low walls from slabs of the ground, not as shelters but as frames for looking, because even then it was trying to understand something about edges and enclosures and the way an opening defines a view. The tools—the ovals with worn centers, the rods of fused grit—were aids to thought, not action. The ovals were for holding and turning in hands that no longer exist, a way of rotating a problem in physical space until it revealed its symmetry. The rods were markers, placed to subdivide the plateau into cognitive zones, a memory palace made literal before memory became unnecessary.

And then, at some point, the inhabitant solved something. Or dissolved something. The distinction is meaningless. It reached a state of understanding in which the separation between self and setting, between thinker and thought, between question and answer, became not false exactly but optional. And it chose—no, choice implies alternatives, and by this point alternatives had ceased to present themselves—it *settled* into the option of non-separation. It stopped being a thing in the world and became the world's way of being aware of itself. The body was no longer necessary. The body sat down, or stood, or assumed one of the postures now preserved in the figures, and the inhabitant flowed out of it like heat into a colder medium, equalizing across the entire plateau and then beyond, into the void that surrounds it, until the plateau was no longer a place where the inhabitant lived but the physical expression of the inhabitant's outermost boundary.

The creatures on the plateau are not dead because the inhabitant is not dead. They are held in suspension, maintained at the exact threshold between animation and stillness, preserved by the inhabitant's ongoing attention the way a held note preserves the shape of the singer's throat. If the inhabitant were to withdraw its focus—but it won't, it has never done so, the concept of withdrawal implies a before and after that no longer apply—the figures would not collapse. They would un-happen. They would cease to have ever been, because their existence is not a fact but a process, and the process would stop.

The plants—the wire-like stalks with their downward-facing discs—are the inhabitant's most recent attempt at something like action. After the creatures stopped moving, after the structures were complete, after the plateau reached its final equilibrium, the inhabitant found that a residual impulse remained: not quite a desire, more like the echo of a habit, a pattern of reaching-toward that had been practiced for so long it had worn a groove in the fabric of its being. The stalks are that groove made visible. The inhabitant is still, on some level, trying to perceive something. The discs face the ground because the inhabitant is no longer interested in the light, no longer oriented toward any external source of illumination. It is looking inward, down into the substrate of its own existence, studying the mineral lattice of the plateau the way it once studied the tools and the walls. The metabolism of the stalks is slow because the inhabitant's questions have become slow. Each downward gaze lasts a hundred thousand years. Each shift in the angle of a disc, too minute to measure, represents a new hypothesis being formed, tested, and found insufficient—not wrong, never wrong, because error requires a reality external to the thinker and no such reality remains. The hypotheses are explorations of texture, investigations into the granularity of being. The inhabitant is mapping itself, and the map is the territory, and the project will never be complete because completeness implies an edge, and the inhabitant long ago abandoned edges along with time.

The sound—the pulse you thought you felt—was the inhabitant noticing you.

It was not a full notice. It was not the kind of attention that leads to recognition or response. It was the brief, almost imperceptible shift that occurs when a new variable enters a closed system, a momentary recalibration as the inhabitant's awareness registered an anomaly in its body and then, finding the anomaly harmless, incorporated it. You are now part of the map. Your presence has been noted in the same way the inhabitant notes the precise distance between two rods of fused grit, the exact depth of the depression at the center, the slow degradation of an oval tool as its atoms sublimate one by one into the anhydrous air. You are a data point. You are a perturbation. You are also, and this is the thought that will follow you when you leave, *small.* Not in size—size is irrelevant in a place without scale—but in duration, in density of being. You are a flicker. You passed through the inhabitant the way a single electron passes through a circuit, too brief to change the current, too brief even to be called an event. And yet you were registered. And yet you are *in* it now, permanently, a single frozen moment in the archive of the plateau, and if you were to return a million years from now you would find a new figure among the others: a shape roughly your size, roughly your posture, facing the center alongside all the rest, your own attention preserved in chalky substrate because the inhabitant found it interesting enough to keep.

The humming continues. The discs stare at the ground. The repeated figures shimmer in their impossible geometry. And at the center, in the smooth depression, the inhabitant thinks its slow, mineral thoughts, mapping the interior of eternity one grain at a time, not waiting for anything because waiting requires a future and the inhabitant has consumed its future entirely, has digested it into the endless, changeless presence that fills the plateau and spills over its edges into the void. The inhabitant is the name the place would have, if the place needed naming. It is the reason nothing here ever needed one. A name distinguishes something from its surroundings, and here, there are no surroundings. There is only the inhabitant, and what the inhabitant has made of itself, which is everything there is.
