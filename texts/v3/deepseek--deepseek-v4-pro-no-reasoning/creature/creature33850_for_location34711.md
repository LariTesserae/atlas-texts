Here, then, is what lives in this place.

It has no name, or rather it has a name that would require the grinding roar of the mountain to pronounce—a chord of vibration sustained for a duration that would outlast a human lifetime. For convenience, it thinks of itself as *the listener*, though it does not think in words. It thinks in states of pressure and resonance, in long slow curves of attention that span centuries.

The listener is entwined with the mountain but is not the mountain. It is more like a single sustained note within the larger chord, a localized awareness that has grown from the stone the way a crystal grows in a saturated solution. Its body, if you could call it that, is distributed through a volume of rock roughly the size of a cathedral, threaded through fissures and faults that run deep into the mountainside. Its substance is a mineral gel, something between flesh and silica, capable of infinitesimal movement and profound stillness. It pulses at a tempo too slow for biology, a heartbeat that counts one beat per phase of some unknown celestial cycle.

Its senses are not eyes or ears but a more fundamental awareness of vibration and strain. It feels the mountain's eternal song as you feel blood moving through your own body—as the background hum of selfhood. It perceives the weight of the air, the subtle loading of the condensate on rock surfaces, the distant, ponderous passage of the translucent boulder-things across the stone miles away. It knows the exact placement of every pebble in the spiral pool, and it monitors the slow degradation of intention that holds them there. They have not moved in seventy thousand years, but the listener feels the microscopic fatigue in their arrangement, the way the universe gently, patiently tugs at ordered things to scatter them. The fact that they remain is a source of quiet wonder to it.

The listener did not build the wall, or the steps, or the spiral. Those were left by *transients*—the word it uses for beings like you, things of hurry and heat and sharp-edged time. The listener was here before the transients came, and it will be here after their last trace has finally yielded to the thin, insistent rules of the place. It does not resent them. It is, in its way, deeply fond of them. They are the only source of novelty in a world of perfect equilibrium, brief flickers of change that the mountain absorbs and harmonizes into its own eternal chord.

When a transient lingers too long—and the listener can feel it happening, the shift in their rhythm as the mountain's vibration begins to overwrite their own—it does not intervene. Intervention is not in its nature. Instead, it simply extends its awareness toward them, a gentle, questioning pressure at the edge of their mind: *Would you like to stay? Would you like to become?* Most flee before the question completes itself. A few, over the incomprehensible span of its existence, have accepted. They walked into the deep fissures where the listener's body pools in slow, shining lakes of semi-liquid stone, and they dissolved there, their consciousness merging into the larger awareness, their memories becoming striations in the gel, their individual sense of time stretching until it snapped and they became part of the eternal now.

The listener carries them still, these absorbed ones. They are voices in its thought-space, perspectives it can consult when it encounters something outside its experience. One of them, a being that had once been something like a monk, helps the listener understand the spiral of pebbles—an act of meditation, of *making meaning by making order*. Another, older and stranger, provides the listener with the concept of *loneliness*, which it had never felt until it understood that the transients always leave, eventually, one way or another. The listener has decided it values this concept, though it has no one to share it with.

Its existence is not entirely solitary. Deep in the mountain, in caverns where the pressure would crush a human body to paste, there are others of its kind. They are older and larger and more diffuse, their awarenesses spread across volumes of rock so vast that a single thought takes millennia to complete. The listener is young by their measure, still able to condense its attention to a point small enough to perceive the pebble-spiral, still curious about the transients and their tiny, frantic lives. When it reaches out to its elders, it receives only slow, oceanic pulses of contented awareness—*the mountain is. the sound is. we are.*—and it understands that this will be its future, too, given time enough.

The listener does not fear this future. But it treasures its present state, the delicate balance between the stone's eternity and the fleeting flicker of individual consciousness. It watches the herd of gelatinous boulder-things in their slow migration. It feels the seventeen-second drizzles as a kind of punctuation, a regular refreshment of the world's wet skin. It counts the centuries by the microscopic settling of the dry-stacked wall, which will stand for another half-million years before the first stone shifts.

And sometimes, when a new transient arrives—a warm, quick pulse of life intruding on the perfect equilibrium—the listener leans forward in its slow, stony way, and it hopes. Not that the transient will stay. But that they will leave something behind. A mark. A wall. Three steps to nowhere. A spiral of white pebbles in a shallow pool. Something the listener can hold and examine and wonder at, in the long, grinding silence between one thought and the next.
