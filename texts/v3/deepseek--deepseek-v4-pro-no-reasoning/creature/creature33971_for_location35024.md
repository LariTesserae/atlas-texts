It is the people who remember the water.

Not in stories, exactly—the people of the hanging city have no epic poems, no carved histories, no songs of a drowned time. Their memory is quieter than that, and deeper. It lives in the body. Watch one of them cross an air-bridge, that column of thickened moisture suspended between two platforms, and you will see it: the way their hips shift, the way their bare feet test the surface before committing weight, the way they move *through* rather than *on* the path. They walk as if the air were still water, as if the ghost of a current might nudge them at any moment. Their gait is a swimming gait translated to stone. Their bones remember.

They call themselves the Flow-Wardens, or something close to that—their language is sparse and positional, full of directional words that mean "toward the wet," "away from the condensate," "across the breathing gap." It is a language for a three-dimensional world, where "up" and "down" are relative to the nearest slab's mass-shadow, where "here" is always provisional. Their names shift with context. The woman crossing the air-bridge might be called Silt-in-the-Runnel when she is alone on her dwelling-platform, but when she meets another on the town square, she becomes Drift-That-Meets-Drift, or simply Drift. Later, after the meeting, when she carries a stone-tipped spear to hunt shoal-mica, she is Current-Striker. Identity is relational, fluid, like the world they inhabit.

She is old, though "old" means something different here. Time does not press on the Flow-Wardens the way it presses on surface-dwellers. The damp air preserves. The diet of mineral-mesh and the flesh of the luminous shoals slows the body's erosion. She has lived perhaps three hundred years, perhaps five—the Wardens do not count, not in numbers. They measure age in condensate-drops. A lifetime is so many falls from so many window-ledges. Drift-That-Meets-Drift has witnessed enough drops to know the weight of silence, to know the slow patient language of the fungal-mesh as it spreads across the lee side of her dwelling-stone. She has watched the mesh grow a handspan in a century. She has seen the basalt rills deepen by the width of a fingernail. She knows that the world is still moving, still shaping itself, just at a pace that makes the frantic shoals seem like brief, bright thoughts against a mind of stone.

She reaches the far platform, the town square, that broad disc of water-polished basalt. Others are there. Two, then four, then seven. They do not gather in a crowd; they distribute themselves across the disc like droplets on a leaf, maintaining a natural spacing that feels like pressure, like the electromagnetic repulsion of like-charged particles. To cluster too tightly is to create a mass-anomaly, a subtle warping of the local gravity that can make the platform tilt or tremble. The Wardens know the weight of their own bodies absolutely. They have learned, over generations, the mathematics of presence.

The seven Flow-Wardens communicate. Their voices are low and carry poorly in the dense, directionless air, so they supplement with gesture, with posture, with the sharing of breath-condensate blown in patterns across the stone. They are discussing the shoals, the movements of the mica-pane creatures, the sudden veering of a school that morning that nearly swept a Warden off a narrow residential shelf. There is no alarm in the discussion. The shoals are not predators, not intentionally dangerous—they are just motion, pure turbulent motion, and the Wardens have learned to read their patterns like sailors read weather. The one called Eddy-on-the-Lee-Side gestures toward a distant spire, where the obsidian-bramble filterer still drifts, slow and patient. "It turns," she says, or the equivalent: a phrase that means "the slow one is orienting toward a new condensation source." This is important. The filterers are indicators. Where they feed, the fungal-mesh follows. Where the mesh follows, the stone eventually softens, crumbles, releases its moisture into the air. An ecology of transformation, a cycle so slow it looks like stillness.

But there is a new tension in the square today, a subtle shift in the spacing of bodies. Drift-That-Meets-Drift feels it before she understands it. The bass hum of the earth's bones—that constant, felt vibration—has changed. It has always been there, a steady tonic, but now it carries a pulse, a slow rhythm like a heartbeat half-remembered. The Wardens have felt this before. Not in their own lifetimes, not in the lifetimes of their grandparents' grandparents, but somewhere deep in the body-memory, the swimming-memory, the water-memory. The pulse means something. It means that the cosmic aquifer is stirring. That somewhere, in the vast dark below the floating city, water is rising.

No one speaks of it directly. They do not have the words for "flood" or "deluge" or "end." But they adjust their spacing on the disc, drifting subtly toward its center, toward the greatest mass, toward the place where gravity's pull is firmest. They look at each other, and their names shift. Drift-That-Meets-Drift becomes Anchor-in-the-Shoal. Current-Striker becomes Stone-That-Remembers-Water.

And they wait, because waiting is what the architecture has always asked of them. The city is a conduit for a history still happening. The only question is what form the flow will take next.
