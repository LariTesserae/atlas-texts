She is called Sigrid-of-the-Rustle, and she is forty-three years old in the way her people count—by the slow annual dimming of the wall-glow, by the thickening of the thatch, by the precise tally of grub-harvests marked on the inner curve of her dwelling's entrance. She is slight even for her kind, her bones small and light as a beetle-eater's, her skin carrying the luminescence of the walls so deeply that in the darkest hours she can just make out the lines of her own palms when she holds them close to her face. The glow is not a surface sheen; it lives in the skin itself, a property of the tissue, passed down through so many generations that the light of the walls and the light of the people have become indistinguishable.

Her face is narrow and mobile, the features delicate but not fragile—a face built for the close, dim spaces of the cleft, for recognizing a neighbor in the murk by the faint catch of light on a cheekbone, for reading the minute shifts in a sibling's expression during the long, quiet work of fiber-spinning. Her eyes are enormous in the relative scale of her face, the iris a deep brown so dark it reads as black, the pupil wide and perpetually adjusted to the half-light. Her hair is the color of the pale thatch in the upper tiers, a silvery blonde that never knew sunlight, and she wears it drawn back from her temples with a cord of twisted beetle-silk, leaving the rest to fall in a loose, straight curtain to her shoulder blades. It whispers against her tunic when she moves, a sound so familiar she no longer hears it.

She wears the standard garment of her people: a sleeveless tunic that falls to mid-thigh, woven from the long, pale fibers of the wall-climbing gorth-vine, beaten until it is soft as old skin. It is undyed, the natural cream of the fiber, but she has worked a border at the hem with beetle-silk dyed the deep crimson of crushed carapace, a pattern of interlocking chevrons that mark her as a fiber-spinner of the third tier. Her feet are bare, the soles tough and pale, the toes long and articulate from a lifetime of gripping foot-ledges. Around her waist she wears a belt of softened leather—beetle-hide, from the larger, slower kind that are sometimes culled from the deepest thatch—with a small pouch of woven fiber that holds her personal tools.

Her dwelling is carved into the mineral wall on the south curve, third tier from the mat, a rounded hollow just large enough for her to curl into for sleep. The entrance is an oval she can seal with a woven screen when she wants privacy, though privacy in the cleft is a relative thing; the sounds of the city pass through every wall, every floor, every body. Her space is orderly. A sleeping mat of pressed thatch-fiber lies against the back curve, covered with a woven blanket in the muted, muddy purple that comes from boiling the broad, heat-flushed leaves of the mat's center. A row of carved niches holds her belongings: a set of polished bone needles in descending sizes, a spool of beetle-silk, a cutting tool of chipped stone with an edge so fine it seems to vanish in the dim, a small oil-lamp carved from a hollow nodule, shaped like a fat teardrop with a fiber wick. The lamp is not lit; she rarely uses it. Her eyes know the dark.

Sigrid's work keeps her hands busy and her mind quiet. She is a fiber-spinner, one of the dozen or so in the city who process the long, pale fibers of the gorth-vine into thread, and thread into cloth. The vines grow thickest on the upper reaches of the walls, where the glow is faintest and the heat from below is just a memory; they send their pale, questing shoots downward toward the warmth, and it is these runners that the harvesters cut and bring to her. She receives them in bundled hanks, still faintly damp with the vine's internal moisture, and she works them at the entrance to her dwelling, where the light is best. Her tool is a spindle: a slim, tapered shaft of polished bone, weighted at the base with a whorl of dense, dark stone, a piece of the mineral body of the cleft itself, carved round and smooth by someone's hands so long ago that no name remains. The spindle is old. It hums when she spins, a subtle vibration that she feels in her palm, a frequency that seems to speak back to the deep, constant song of the place. She does not know if it is a property of the bone, the stone, or something else entirely. No one does. The spinners before her accepted the hum, and so does she.

Her days follow the rhythm set by the city's needs. She wakes when the wall-glow begins its slow brightening—the dim period is never true darkness, only a deepening into the violet end of the spectrum—and she eats her first meal: a pressed cake of thatch-grain, dense and slightly sweet, with a smear of preserved grub-paste for protein. The grain comes from the seed-heads of the broad-leaved plants in the deep mat, threshed and ground and pressed into small, flat rounds that keep almost indefinitely. The grub-paste is made from the fat, pale larvae harvested from the beetle runs, slow-cooked with crushed leaves from the heat-flushed plants until it becomes a savory, pungent concentrate that every child learns to love. She drinks water from a gourd she refills at the collection points—shallow depressions carved into the lower walls where condensation from the constant warmth beads and gathers, the only source, endlessly renewed by the closed cycle of the cleft. The water tastes faintly of mineral, a clean, stony taste that she associates with the feeling of waking.

After eating, she works. The fibers must be soaked to soften the natural gum that binds them, then beaten against a smooth stone, a rhythmic thumping that joins the larger soundscape of the cleft: the chittering of the beetles, the soft click-speech of her neighbors calling out to one another, the occasional high whine of predation, the distant laughter of children playing a chasing game on the lower ledges. She spins the beaten fibers into thread on her humming spindle, the thread winding onto a spool she holds between her knees, her fingers moving with the unthinking grace of decades. The thread is fine and strong, with a slight natural sheen. She can judge its quality by the feel against her thumb, the way it catches the wall-glow. A good thread glimmers like a strand of the vine's own life.

The work is solitary but not lonely. Her dwelling is part of a cluster of six on the third tier, and her neighbors pass her entrance constantly, their shadows flickering across her hands. There is Feran, the grub-harvester, a stocky man with a booming click-laugh who always pauses to show her the largest larva of his morning's catch, cradled in his palm like a pale, squirming jewel. There is Lirra, a child of maybe nine cycles, who is her niece by her sister's pairing, and who has appointed herself Sigrid's unofficial apprentice. Lirra brings her fiber hanks from the harvesters and sits cross-legged at the entrance, watching Sigrid's hands with enormous, intent eyes, practicing the click-words for *twist* and *tension* and *smooth*. Sigrid teaches her in small, patient increments. The child's fingers are still too clumsy for the fine work, but she is learning the feel of the fibers, the difference between a strand that will hold and one that will snap.

In the afternoons, Sigrid often descends to the mat. This is not strictly part of her work, but she has always done it, since she was Lirra's age. The descent is a journey in itself. She steps out of her dwelling onto the narrow foot-ledge—a lip of carved mineral barely wider than her foot—and moves to the nearest ladder. The ladders are notched directly into the wall, the steps worn into smooth, shining depressions by endless use. She climbs down past the second tier, past the first, her bare feet finding each hold without thought. The air grows warmer, thicker, the sweet smell of the mat intensifying until it coats her tongue. At the bottom, the vegetation rises to her waist, then her chest, a dense, springy tangle that gives under her weight like a living floor.

She does not come here for any practical task, though sometimes she gathers seed-heads for the grain-makers or checks the grub-runs for Feran. She comes for the beetle-streams. They fascinate her. She crouches in the thatch, motionless, and watches them pour past in their endless, twitching, multilegged tide. The predominant kind are the kith-beetles, long and segmented, their carapaces a muted bronze in the wall-glow, each leg tipped with that brush of fine golden bristle. They flow over and through the vegetation in streams that seem random but are not; if you watch long enough—and Sigrid has, for cycles upon cycles—you begin to see the patterns. Scout beetles at the front, their antennae twitching, pausing to test the thatch. Larger bearers behind them, carrying fragments of leaf and grub-husk. Smaller, quicker beetles that dart around the edges, their function unclear but their energy undeniable. They chitter constantly, a sound of leg-on-carapace, of mandible-click, that forms a kind of music. The people of the cleft have names for the different rhythms: the slow, steady pulse of the bearer-stream, the skittering trill of the scouts, the sharp *tick-tick-tick* of an alarm. Sigrid can read the mood of the mat by the sound of the beetle-streams alone.

The larger things hunt in the deep thatch, and she watches them too. The mat-lurkers: flat, pale creatures something like a ray, something like a hand, that surge up from the compressed layers of dead vegetation to engulf a stream of beetles and then sink back. She has seen them pull down a cluster of fifty beetles in a single pulse of motion, a brief, violent whirlpool in the thatch that smooths over in seconds. The larger grub-borers, fat and slow, that tunnel through the deepest thatch and are rarely seen except when a harvest unearths them. Once, when she was young, she saw a deep-wyrm: a pale, segmented creature as long as her arm, eyeless but exquisitely sensitive to vibration, that rose from a seam in the mineral floor and seized a mat-lurker in jaws that unhinged to an impossible width. The wyrm withdrew into the seam with its prize, and Sigrid had sat frozen for a long time afterward, her heart beating a new, fierce rhythm, understanding in a way she had not before that the mat was not a floor but a skin over something deeper, something alive.

She has never spoken of the deep-wyrm to anyone. Some things are too large for words.

In the evenings, when the wall-glow begins its slow fade toward the deeper violet, Sigrid returns to her tier. She washes the thatch-dust from her hands and feet at the collection point, the condensation-water cool against her skin, and then she joins the communal meal. The third tier has a shared space, a wide shelf carved into the wall with a curvature that catches and holds sound, turning it into a warm, enveloping presence. The families of the tier gather there, bringing their cakes and grub-paste and sometimes a special dish—stuffed vine-shoots, a rare delicacy, or a stew of beetle-meat and thatch-grain cooked with aromatic leaves. They sit in a loose circle, their small oil-lamps making a constellation of warm light, and they talk. The click-speech is soft and rapid, a patter of syllables that carries emotion in its cadence: the quick, bright clicks of humor, the slower, deeper tones of concern, the rhythmic, almost hypnotic pattern of a story being told. The children sit among the adults, listening, learning, occasionally interrupting with a question that is always answered with patience.

The stories are old. The people of the cleft have no writing—there is no surface that would hold it, nothing that would not be absorbed by the thatch or worn away by the constant, gentle pressure of the air—but their oral memory is vast and precise. The old ones recite the lineage of spinners, harvesters, carvers, and mat-wardens going back more generations than anyone can count, a chain of names and deeds that links the present to a past so distant it has become myth. They tell the story of the Making, when the cleft was hollowed from the mineral body by hands they call the First Carvers. They tell of the Long Stillness, a period of cycles when the wall-glow nearly faded to nothing and the people learned to live in a darkness that was nearly absolute, navigating by touch and sound and the faint phosphorescence of their own skins. They tell of the Song of the Spindle, the belief that every tool carries a vibration, a voice, and that a spinner must learn to hear her own spindle's song before she can make true thread.

Sigrid listens to the stories, but she rarely tells them. She is not a speaker. Her gift is in her hands, in the thread that emerges from her spindle, in the cloth that the thread becomes. She is known for it: Sigrid-of-the-Rustle, they call her, for the sound her tunic makes when she moves, which is the sound of cloth so fine it whispers. She has spun the thread for the garments of the mat-wardens, the ones who spend their cycles in the deepest thatch, studying the beetle-streams. She has woven the blanket that wraps the newborn at a first-naming. Her cloth is on the bodies of the dead, when they are lowered into the thatch to be absorbed, their flesh and bone returning to the cycle that feeds the vines.

She has never paired with anyone. This is not unusual—some do, some do not—but it has shaped her life into a particular solitude that she values without naming. Her dwelling is hers alone. Her spindle hums for her alone. The child Lirra is her closest tie, and that is enough.

There is a moment in every cycle, just before the dimming, when the cleft seems to hold its breath. The beetle-streams slow; the voices of the people drop to a murmur; the wall-glow deepens into something almost purple, almost blue. In that moment, Sigrid often sits at the entrance to her dwelling, her spindle still in her lap, and looks out at the city. The tiers of rounded openings, each one a pocket of warmth and life. The ladders, pale lines in the dimness, tracing the paths of generations. The vegetation mat below, a dark, breathing presence that rustles even now, even in the hush. The smell of oil-lamps and cooking grub-paste and the sweet, pervasive breath of the thatch.

She does not wonder what is outside. The concept has no meaning for her; the cleft is all there is, all there has ever been. The walls are the edge of the world. The warmth is the heart of the world. The people are the world's voice. She is a small part of this, a fiber-spinner on the third tier, her hands smelling of gorth-vine and beetle-silk, her skin glowing with the light of the walls, her ears filled with the click-speech of her kin and the endless, soothing chitter of the beetles. She is content. She is whole. She is, in the deepest sense, at home.

In the dim time, when the wall-glow is faintest and the cleft is a study in violet shadow, Sigrid curls onto her sleeping mat and pulls the purple blanket over her shoulder. She can hear, in the quiet, the soft scrabble of beetles in the thatch far below. She can hear the distant thrumming of the deep, the vibration that comes from no visible source, the pulse of the place itself. Her spindle rests in its niche, humming its own faint echo of that pulse. She closes her eyes. The darkness behind her lids is not empty; it is alive with the after-image of the wall-glow, a gentle, luminous field that slowly, slowly fades. She sleeps. And in her sleep, she dreams of thread: endless, golden thread, spinning out of her hands and into the dark, weaving the cleft and the walls and the warmth and the people together into a single, unbroken fabric, a tapestry of life that has no edges, no outside, only the deep, continuous joy of being what it is.
