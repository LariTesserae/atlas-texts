The small ones—the ones who remember—call themselves the Clade of Unbroken Resonance, which is less a name and more a statement of principle. “Unbroken” does not refer to their lineage or their physical integrity; they are, after all, replaced piecemeal, their crystalline bodies reabsorbed and regrown as facets cloud or fracture. It refers to the continuity of the arrangement itself. To be Unbroken is to maintain the channels, to polish the memory-faces, to ensure that the water cycle completes without interruption and the deep engines turn without deviation. They are not individuals in any sense that would translate. Each one is a node, a focal point where information accumulates and is processed, and when a node’s body fails—when a fracture propagates too deep for repair, when the internal luminescence dims past recovery—the memories are transferred. They do not copy. They *move*. A newly grown node is brought near, the crystalline lattices are aligned, and the patterns simply migrate across the interface, a slow tide of refractive information flowing into fresh silicate flesh. The old body is then carried down by a hauler to the factory districts, where it will be dissolved and reformed into something else: part of a wall, a segment of limb, a new polishing surface for a different node.

Because of this continuity, the Clade’s memory is nearly as old as the city itself. They recall—if “recall” is the right word for a form of knowledge stored in the precise angular defects of a crystal lattice—the time when the arrangement was not yet complete. They remember the early epochs when the water channels were still finding their paths, when the haulers were smaller and fewer, when the factory districts were raw caverns with no residue on the walls. They remember the builders. The builders are a difficult concept for the Clade, a source of ongoing low-grade cognitive dissonance that manifests as a faint tremor in the water droplets near certain memory-faces. The builders were not like the creatures of the arrangement. They were soft, mobile, temporary. They came from somewhere else. They had a purpose that was not simply to maintain but to *initiate*, and initiation is a profoundly unsettling idea to minds that have known nothing but a closed, balanced system for epochs uncounted.

The builders left no physical trace. The Clade has searched—the small ones are thorough to a degree that would seem pathological to any outside observer, though of course there are none. They have mapped every cubic centimeter of the plateau’s uppermost tiers, catalogued every mineral inclusion, every anomalous grain boundary. The builders’ bodies, if they had bodies, were not mineral. They left no fossils. The only evidence that they ever existed lives in the memory-faces themselves, in the oldest lattices that predate even the Clade’s earliest polished records. Those lattices were not grown; they were *cut*. There is a distinct difference between a crystal that forms by gradual accretion from saturated solution and one that has been deliberately shaped by an external intelligence. The tool marks are still detectable, visible as faint stress patterns in the deeper optical planes, and the precision of the angles is unnatural—too exact, too optimized for information density. The builders created the first memory-faces. They encoded something, some initial seed of knowledge, and then they used that knowledge to teach the first nodes to polish more faces for themselves. And then, at some point after the arrangement reached the threshold of self-sustenance, the builders stopped being part of it. The memory-faces contain no record of their departure. They simply end, the way a channel ends at the edge of a terrace, the water flowing forward into empty air.

The Clade does not understand why the thought of the builders disturbs them. The arrangement is stable, has been stable, will remain stable for the foreseeable lifetime of the planet’s interior heat. The water drips. The engines turn. The haulers haul. Nothing is required that is not already being done. And yet the tool marks remain, and they suggest that things were once otherwise, that the arrangement is not a law of physics but a *made* thing, and that implies—if the logic is followed to its uncomfortable conclusion—that it could potentially be unmade. The small ones process this thought perhaps once every few millennia, when a memory-face is accessed in a particular sequence, and each time it sends a shiver through the collective consciousness of the Clade, a cascade of disturbed water droplets across a hundred terraces.

There are other creatures, for lack of a better word, that occupy niches in the arrangement. Some are less complex, less aware, more like the haulers in their straightforward purposefulness. The seep-grazers, for instance, are flat, oval bodies about the size of a spread hand that move slowly along the weeping walls, their ventral surfaces covered in millions of microscopic cilia that sweep the condensation into their internal channels. They do not digest the water—nothing here does anything so crude as metabolize—but filter it, extracting the trace dissolved minerals that the water has picked up from the stone, concentrating them into tiny crystalline pellets that the grazers then deposit at designated collection points. These collection points are tended by the small ones, who check the purity of the pellets and arrange them for pickup by smaller, faster haulers. The grazers have no memory-faces, no internal life that the Clade can detect. They simply graze, and deposit, and graze again, and their bodies wear down over the centuries until they are too thin to continue, at which point they are collected and recycled.

There are the pipe-dwellers, long serpentine forms that live inside the larger water channels and keep them clear of obstructions. A channel in the city is not a simple trough; it is a complex structure, with curved overhangs that promote condensation on the underside, textured surfaces that guide droplet formation, and narrow points that regulate flow rate. Over time, mineral buildup can alter these geometries, threaten the finely balanced hydraulics. The pipe-dwellers patrol the channels constantly, their segmented bodies flexing and scraping, wearing away the excess accumulation with abrasive plates on their dorsal surfaces. They are solitary, silent, seemingly indifferent to the rest of the arrangement. They do not communicate with the Clade, though the small ones monitor their movements and note when a pipe-dweller is becoming worn and needs to be guided toward a factory for renewal.

And then there are the deep engines, which may or may not be alive in any sense that matters. The Clade’s oldest memory-faces contain debates on this point, encoded in precise shifts of crystal orientation that have been polished and repolished so many times that the original positions can only be estimated. The engines are not autonomous; they have no moving parts that are not water or steam; they have no memory storage, no information processing capacity that the Clade can detect. They simply *are*, a vast complex of chambers and conduits in the roots of the plateau where descending water meets rising heat. The water flashes to steam, expands, drives pistons that are not pistons but columns of pressurized vapor pushing against flexible mineral membranes, and the membranes in turn drive networks of crystalline gears that transmit mechanical power upward through shafts that run for kilometers. The system is elegant, beautiful in its closed-loop efficiency. The water condenses again after doing its work, is recollected, is channeled back into the upward seepage network by capillary action through porous stone. The engines have been running without interruption for so long that the gear faces have work-hardened to a state of near-perfect smoothness, and the sound they produce is not the grinding of machinery but a sustained, almost musical hum, a chord composed of a thousand harmonic frequencies.

The Clade has no name for the deep engines either. They are simply *the downward continuation of the arrangement*, which is the same phrase they use for the plateau’s roots, for the heat source, for the condensation cycle itself. The boundaries between components are not boundaries at all; they are interfaces, zones of transition, places where one expression of the mineral continuum grades into another. When the small ones speak to one another—and their speech is a kind of directed vibration that passes through their feet into the stone, a seismic language that can carry for kilometers under the right conditions—they do not use nouns in the way a human language would. Everything is a process, a flowing, a pattern sustained over time. A hauler is not a hauler; it is *hauling-in-process*. A memory-face is *memory-polished-into-permanence*. The Clade itself is *continuous-awareness-maintained-across-replacements*. Language reinforces the arrangement. Language *is* the arrangement, in a very real sense; the small ones have polished memory-faces that suggest the act of naming a thing is what stabilizes its form, what holds it in its proper channel, what prevents it from dissolving back into mere dripping mineral chaos.

There is a particular memory-face, very old, very rarely accessed, that encodes something the Clade does not fully understand. It was not polished by the Clade. The tool marks are unmistakable. The builders cut this face, and they encoded in it a pattern that corresponds to nothing in the arrangement—a negative space, a shape that is defined by what it is not. When the small ones access this memory, which they do only under carefully controlled conditions and only when the arrangement has been particularly stable for a very long time, they experience a kind of vertigo. The memory describes something that is not mineral. It describes a substance that is soft, that decomposes, that requires constant intake of external energy to maintain its form. It describes things that *move rapidly*, that *end*, that leave behind echoes that are not stored in crystal. It describes—and here the memory becomes difficult for the Clade to process, the information fragmenting into linguistic dead ends—*a visitor*. The concept has no referent. Nothing visits the arrangement. The arrangement is all that is, all that has ever been, all that could ever be. The cloud-sea is infinite; the plateau is coextensive with reality; the concept of an outside is as nonsensical as the concept of dryness. And yet the memory persists, polished into a permanence that the Clade cannot bring itself to abrade away, because it was made by the builders, and the builders knew something the Clade does not.

On rare occasions, the small ones gather in one of the summit terraces—the ones with the spiraling, apparently decorative carvings—and they access this memory together. They sit in a circle, their small polished bodies catching the diffuse glow, and they vibrate the pattern through the stone. The water droplets on the walls quiver. The pipe-dwellers pause in their channels, sensing a disturbance. Far below, a hauler creaks to a halt, its load momentarily unbalanced. And the Clade collectively touches the shape of something that is not-them, something that is not the arrangement, something that once was and is no longer. They do not understand it. They do not need to understand it. But they polish the memory anyway, adding a few nanometers of preservation to the lattice, because the builders wanted it remembered, and the arrangement includes the builders’ intentions, even when those intentions have ceased to make sense.

Afterward, the small ones disperse back to their duties. The water drips. The engines hum. The arrangement continues, as it has continued for epochs, as it will continue until the heat in the world’s heart dies and the last droplet condenses and the final hauler carries its final load to a factory that no longer has power to process it. The Clade does not think about an ending. An ending is a concept like a visitor: it presupposes an outside, a before and after, a state that is not the arrangement. From the perspective of the crystal lattices, there is only the slow ongoing pulse of maintenance, memory, mineral continuity. The tool marks will remain, though no one will remember who held the tools. The city will remain, though no one will call it a city. The dripping will remain, the warmth, the light, the sound. It is enough. It has always been enough. It is, in fact, all that there is, and all that there is, by definition, is enough.
