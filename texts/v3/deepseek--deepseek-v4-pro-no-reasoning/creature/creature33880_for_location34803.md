The creatures that live here do not have a name for themselves, because naming is a technology of edges, and they have none. But if you were to press your hand against the flank of one—the cart-horse size, the elder kind that moves alone between the rings—you would feel not a boundary but a yielding, a slow give that invites you inward. Their flesh is cool and slightly firmer than the water they weep, and under your palm you would sense the deep, tidal movement of their inner seas: the bladders filling and emptying, the nutrient broth shifting, the slow clench of the muscle-knots that are not muscles but something older, contractile proteins that predate the invention of the fibre.

They perceive the world as a pattern of vibrations. The metal plain is their sky and their ground and their only companion, and they read it as you would read a face. Every footfall of every neighbour, every droplet strike, every shift of a nodule in its socket—all of it sings through the substrate and into their bodies, which are tuned to receive. They have no ears. The whole skin is an ear, and the bladders inside them are resonant chambers, each tuned to a different frequency band. The largest of them carry seven bladders, and the vibration-sense they give is so rich that it functions as a kind of sight: they know the shape of the plain by how it rings, the location of every creature by the rhythm of its gait, the mood of a herd by the pitch and density of its collective keening.

Their brains, if you can call them that, are not in their heads. There is no head. The dark knots of nerve-like tissue are distributed through the body in clusters, one for each bladder, and they communicate by chemical pulse, slow and steady. A thought takes minutes to form, and it forms everywhere at once—a consensus of the body, rather than a command from a centre. When one of the elders decides to move, it is not a decision as you would know it: it is a gradual leaning of the whole self toward a new vibration-pattern, a shift in the internal tides that tilts the flesh and begins the long, rippling locomotion. They can keep a single thought for days, turning it over in their distributed mind, letting it mature until it becomes action. They have no need for speed. Nothing here moves faster than a creep or a scamper, and the scampering ones are prey.

Their memory is in the metal. Not metaphorically—literally. When an elder brushes against a cairn or taps a nodule back into its ring, it is accessing a pattern that was laid down by ancestors so distant that the plain itself was warmer then. The arrangements of nodules are not decorations; they are records. Each placement marks an event—a predation, a birth, a shift in the water—and the elders maintain them as you would maintain a library, nudging the volumes back into order. The young do not understand this. They wobble among the rings and scatter the nodules with their mindless play, and the elders follow behind, patient, correcting. It is not a history that can be read in sequence: it is a spatial history, a terrain of memory, and to move through it is to walk through a mind that extends outward into the plain. The elders know the shape of every ring and line for miles. They carry the whole arrangement inside them as a vibration-pattern, a model of the plain that they check and recheck against the substrate with every step. When they die, they die carefully, in designated places, so that their hard-parts can be added to the rings.

They eat by absorbing. The water here is not water as you know it—not H2O alone, but a solution thick with metal salts, with dissolved silicates, with a fine colloidal suspension of particles so small they are barely there. These particles are the true life of the plain: a floating, photosynthesizing film of single-celled organisms that drift in the mist and the pools, using not chlorophyll but a rhodopsin-like pigment that captures the dim, sourceless light and turns it into chemical energy. The film is invisible to your eye, but not to the gelatinous creatures: they can taste it as a faint sweetness on their skins, and they absorb it constantly, osmotically, through membranes that are tuned to filter and concentrate. The larger ones have developed a kind of internal farming: they carry captive populations of the micro-organisms inside their bladders, feeding them on the minerals that the creature’s own metabolism produces, harvesting them in a slow, steady cycle that means they never have to stop moving. They are walking gardens, mobile ecosystems wrapped in a single skin.

Their language is the keening, but it is not a language of words. It is a music of presence, a continuous broadcast of internal state. Each creature produces a chord, a combination of frequencies generated by the passage of air through the bladders and the vibration of membranes stretched across internal cavities. The chord shifts with hunger, with reproductive readiness, with alarm, with the slow satisfaction of a nodule nudged back into place. The plain carries these chords and blends them, so that every creature hears not individuals but a composite—a choir that represents the whole state of the local population. When one creature begins the slow wail of predation, the chord of the whole herd darkens, and the elders, far away, feel the change and pause in their tending. The young scatter. The predator, a giant with a milky, opaque body and bladders that pulse deep red, feels the shift and accelerates, its rippling convulsions building toward the moment of envelope and engulfment. The language is not about anything. It is not symbolic. It is direct: the sound is the state, and to hear it is to feel it, immediately and without translation. They live inside each other’s bodies, acoustically. There is no privacy here. There is no self that is not also the herd.

They reproduce by shedding. The rituals of soft collision are not mating as you would think of it—there is no genetic exchange in the moment of meeting. Instead, the two creatures press together for hours, and the pressure triggers a rearrangement of their internal tissues, a reshuffling of the genetic material they carry in a thousand small, free-floating nuclei that drift in their cellular fluid. When they separate, they both carry new combinations, and over the following days they bud: small, perfect copies of the new pattern that break away from the parent’s body and wobble off into the world. The young are born knowing the vibration-patterns of the plain. They inherit the chord that their parents sang, and they join the choir within minutes of separation. There is no childhood, no learning, only a gradual accumulation of the body’s patience.

They do not die easily. When an elder grows too large, too slow, its internal gardens failing, it begins a long migration toward the great depression where the structures are most complex. There, it settles onto a plate that has been kept clean for this purpose, and it sings a specific chord—the death-chord, a deep and complex thrumming that vibrates through the plain for miles—and the others come. They gather around the dying elder in rings, and they keen together, a sound that rises and falls for days, and as the elder’s body begins to soften, to loosen its hold on its own boundaries, the others absorb it. Not as predation—more slowly, more gently, a communal consumption that returns the elder’s substance to the herd. The hard-parts are left behind: the glossy, finger-shaped internal struts, the mineralised nodules that once served as anchor points for the bladders. These are gathered and added to the structures. The death-site becomes a new ring, a new line, a new point in the spatial memory. The elder is not gone; it is distributed, its flesh returned to the young, its hard-parts marking the place where it stopped moving. The choir absorbs its chord, and the chord continues, shifted slightly by the new presence, and the plain is unchanged.

They do not wonder. They do not ask why the metal is here, or what the light is, or whether there are other plains beyond the mist. They live in a world that has no outside, and the concept of an outside is not missing—it is irrelevant, a pattern that does not appear in the vibration-field. They are complete. They have everything they need: water that never stops, food that drifts in the air and pools, metal that holds the memory of every ancestor, a language that leaves no gap between experience and expression. They are what a world builds when it has time to perfect its creatures, to strip them back to essentials, to remove all the struggle and the striving and leave only the steady, ongoing, cyclical presence of life that has achieved equilibrium with its surroundings.

They are not happy. They are not sad. They are not content or restless or bored. They are present, fully and continuously, in a way that your mind—always reaching, always lacking—cannot understand except as a kind of silence, though the plain is the loudest place you have ever stood. They are present, and the plain is present with them, and the two have been shaping each other for so long that the distinction is merely technical. They are the plain, thinking. They are the metal, feeling. They are the water, wanting. And they will be here, keening and nudging and glistening, until the metal unbinds to steam, and even then, perhaps, their chords will continue, carried in the vapour, waiting for condensation, waiting for the plain to cool again.
