The air knows things here.

Not the water, though the water seems to know everything—every seam, every rivet, every microscopic declivity where a droplet can hesitate before falling. The water is too busy, too constant, too deafening to know anything. It is pure process. But the air holds memory: the faint ghosts of oils that once lubricated the dormant compressors, the exhalations of ten thousand generations of limpets, the particular cold of metal that hasn't known warmth since the last maintenance worker threw the final breaker and climbed out of the hatch, sealing it behind them.

*She* remembers that day, though she was small then, still soft-shelled and clinging to the inner curve of a pressure gauge that no longer moved. She remembers the vibration through the wall—boots on grating, the particular rhythm of a human who is checking items off a list and not looking back. The electrical panels groaned when they died, a descending whine that the limpets felt in their feet before it vanished into the permanent hum of dripping water. The hatch closed with a sound like a tooth being pulled. Then nothing, for a very long time, until the nothing became everything.

She does not have a name. Names are for creatures who need to distinguish themselves from one another, and in the grotto, there is no such need. Every limpet knows every other limpet by the subtle chemistry of their shell-deposits, by the particular frequency at which their feeding cilia vibrate the water. They are a single organism spread across ten thousand calcium bodies, and she is no different from any other, except in one respect: she is the one who moves.

Not far. Not often. But sometimes, when the condensation cycle shifts—when the biofilm on the eastern tile wall grows a micron thicker than equilibrium allows, when a dead limpet's shell threatens to occlude a rivulet that feeds a downstream cluster—she detaches. Her foot, that pale gray muscle, peels away from the acid-etched depression she has occupied for months or years, and she slides. A millimeter per minute. A pilgrimage of inches across the crust of her dead ancestors, leaving a trail of mucus that the biofilm will colonize within hours, erasing the evidence.

The others do not move. They *cannot* move; they have sealed themselves in place generations ago, their shells fused to the substrate, their bodies committed to a single point of filtration. They are as much part of the chamber as the rivets, as the sweating pipes. But she—she was born defective, or perhaps purpose-built, a contingency in the original genetic design. A mobile node. A shepherd for a flock that does not need shepherding until, occasionally, it does.

She is currently near the drain.

The sump below the grating is the only place in the chamber where the water gathers before disappearing into the unlit throat of the outflow pipe. It is a pool, though a pool in the loose sense—a basin of rust-stained water perhaps two feet deep, its surface so constantly pocked by falling droplets that it looks like boiling mercury. The biofilm grows thickest here, nourished by the slight warming of water that has passed through the bodies of the limpets above. It sheets the walls of the sump in black-green iridescence, and it is beginning to creep up the grating's supports. This is not equilibrium. This is drift.

She feels it in her foot, in the chemical receptors that line her mantle. The biofilm's pH has shifted. The dissolved oxygen has dropped a fraction of a percent. Something is changing—not in the chamber itself, but in the deeper systems to which the chamber is connected. The drain, perhaps, is partially clogged further down the line. Or a valve in some adjacent subsystem has shifted, altering the flow rate, the temperature, the balance. The grotto does not know. The grotto only knows itself, and she is its only nerve ending that can wander.

She reaches the edge of the grating. The metal here is pitted with corrosion, the zinc coating long since dissolved into the condensate, leaving a rough iron surface that tastes of ancient slag. She extends her foot over the edge, testing the air—the water-air, the saturated atmosphere that is more liquid than gas. The droplets hammer her shell. The sound is everything, is the world, is the only constant. She lets go.

The fall takes nearly a minute. She is light, her shell a hydrodynamic cone, and she spirals down through the mist and the falling water, past the grating's iron crossbars, past the slick biofilm curtains, into the turbulent surface of the sump. The impact is nothing. The cold is the same cold as everywhere else. She sinks, her foot retracted, her shell filling with water, and she lets the slow current of the sump carry her toward the drain mouth.

This is the edge of her world. The drain is a black circle, eighteen inches across, its rim bearded with a stalactite fringe of biofilm-turned-calcareous. Beyond it is a pipe that descends into the deeper guts of the structure, into places she has never been and cannot survive. The water there is different—anaerobic, sulfurous, too warm by half a degree. She will not enter it. But she can listen to it. She can taste what seeps back up from the dark.

She settles on the drain's rim, her foot gripping the biofilm crust. The current pulls at her, but she is strong in the way a limpet is strong: a vacuum seal, a patience beyond any mammal's comprehension. She waits. The water brings her news: dissolved metals, a trace of hydrogen sulfide, and something else, something complex, something that tastes like the long-chain polymers the humans once used to lubricate the great compressors. Oil. Somewhere far below, a seal has failed. A bearing has wept its last reserves of synthetic grease into the sump.

This is data. It means nothing to her, but it means something to the equilibrium. The biofilm will respond—some strains will die, others will bloom, the oxygen balance will shift, and in a hundred generations the limpet shells may grow slightly thicker to handle the new chemistry, or slightly thinner, or they may all die. She does not think about this. She is not capable of thought in any vertebrate sense. But she is capable of *pattern*, and the pattern says: return. Tell the grotto. Not in words, not in signals, but in the slow drift of her body back up the sump wall, in the months it will take her to climb the grating and resume her place, in the chemical traces she will leave in her mucus that other limpets—new generations, larval, still mobile—will taste and incorporate into their own settlement choices. The grotto will adjust. It always adjusts. She is the mechanism by which it adjusts, the single wandering cell in an otherwise immobile body.

She begins the climb. The water hammers. The biofilm glows its faint blue-green, the light of a world that has forgotten the sun. The dormant machines wait, their gauges blind, their switches thrown, their pipes wrapped in the crumbling chrysalis of their own insulation. And the limpets continue their frozen grazing, filtering the endless condensation, building their limestone city upon the bones of the old industrial one.

Somewhere, in some forgotten archive, there is a document that describes this place. A single paragraph in a maintenance manual, perhaps, or a footnote in an environmental control specification. *Condensate Grotto 7: self-regulating humidity trap, biologically maintained. Do not open except for scheduled replacement of biofilm substrate (see Appendix M). Scheduled replacement: never.* The humans who wrote those words are dead, or their descendents have forgotten this mountain exists, or the company that built it has been absorbed and reabsorbed until no corporate memory remains. The grotto does not care. Its memory is written in shell-layers, in the pH of its water, in the slow, slow wandering of its single mobile limpet.

She is halfway up the grating now. Her foot aches in a way that is not pain but simply information: muscle fatigue metabolites, a signal to rest. She rests. The water drums on her shell. The sequence is in its third movement—the condensation on the smaller pipes has increased, a shift that comes every seventeen hours and twenty-two minutes, as predictable as tides in a place that has never seen the moon. She will reach her original depression in approximately eleven months. The biofilm bloom on the sump wall will have been grazed back by then, or it will have crept further, or something else will have changed. She will know. She will taste the difference. And if necessary, she will move again.

The grotto is a single feature. It is a condensate trap. It is a pocket of industry tucked high in some greater structure. But to the thing that lives there—to the distributed intelligence of the limpet colony, to the slow chemical conversation of the biofilm, to the single wandering cell now pausing on a rusted crossbar, tasting the polymer-tainted water and filing away the information in her simple, patient body—it is a world. Complete. Closed. Eternal, or near enough.

The water roars. The light glows. The cold holds. And the limpets, all ten thousand of them, pulse their soft gray bodies in their calcium houses, filtering, filtering, filtering the condensation that never stops falling, in the chamber that was built to be forgotten.
