The keeper of the pools has no name, because there has never been a second voice to speak it.

It moves across the geyserite plain with the deliberate, unthinking patience of a process rather than a creature. Seen from a distance—if anything here could be said to see from a distance through the perpetual white-gold haze—it might register as a slow articulation of the landscape itself, a section of the sintered pavement deciding to rearrange its topography. It is tall, though height here is a slippery concept, measured against the fossilized factory halls and the soft, dissolving silhouettes of the thermal vents. It stands perhaps twice the height of a person, but this is a meaningless comparison; no person has ever stood here to provide the scale.

Its body is built from the same substance as the plain. It is geyserite accreted around a structural core, but not grown blindly. The layering is deliberate, almost architectural, as if the creature has—over spans of time that would feel geological even to the geothermal turbines turning in the deep—curated its own shape. The torso is a column of creamy, opalescent silica, wider at the base where it dissolves into the pavement like a stalagmite that learned to walk. The surface is not smooth but intricately textured: a record of every thermal cycle it has endured, every season of mist deposition, every incremental adjustment of its form. Ripples and ridges run across its chest in patterns that seem almost calligraphic, a Braille of mineral accretion.

Its head—or the structure that occupies the position of a head—is a tapering, faceted node, like an eroded spar of hydrothermal quartz. There are no eyes. There are no features at all in the conventional sense, but across the broad front of this head, just below the apex, runs a horizontal seam: a lipless mouth that opens and closes with the same wet, adhesive sound as a stone lifting from mud. When it parts, you can see that the interior is lined with thousands of minute, crystalline teeth, each one a perfect, hexagonal prism of opal. They are not used for chewing. They are a sieve, a filter, a comb.

Its limbs are asymmetrical, a testament to growth and damage and repair over uncountable cycles. One arm is long and jointed in three places, terminating in a three-fingered hand where each digit is a slender, brittle spire of the same sort that snaps underfoot across the plain. The fingers are prehensile, articulate, moving with a precision that suggests an internal tendon system of flexible, heat-resistant silica fibers. The other arm is shorter, thicker, and ends in a broad, spatulate blade—a tool, not a hand, shaped for scraping and planing the geyserite surface. Its legs are pillars, columnar and slow, the feet flat and wide to distribute its weight across the sinter crust without cracking through. When it moves, which is rarely and with immense deliberation, the flat feet make a sound like a wet hand smoothed across polished stone.

The keeper’s function is written into the landscape. It tends the pools.

There are thousands of them, the rimmed basins that stair-step across the gentlest slopes. Some are no wider than a cupped hand; others could swallow the keeper itself. They are not natural, not entirely. They are the industrial descendants of something—settling tanks, cooling ponds, precipitation basins for a mineral extraction process so ancient that the original ore has been exhausted and forgotten. The machinery that once fed them has become the dormant geysers and the quartz-turbines in the deep. The pools remain, still doing what they were designed to do, but the purpose has drifted. They precipitate silica now. They grow the lattice-spores.

The keeper curates this growth. It moves from pool to pool in a circuit that takes what might be days or might be years; the concept frays in a place where the only timekeeping is the rhythm of water drops and the slow throb of the subterranean turbines. At each pool, it lowers itself with a grace that belies its mineral bulk, crouching over the steaming water. The long-fingered hand extends, and the three brittle digits submerge. The comb-teeth in its mouth-part vibrate at a frequency just on the edge of the audible; the water shivers. It is tasting the chemical composition, reading the thermal gradient, sensing the density of the silica-lattice spores that bloom in delicate, frost-like patterns just beneath the surface.

Sometimes, it scrapes. The spatulate arm descends and planes a layer of excess mineral growth from the pool's rim, smoothing the sinter back to a precise curvature that encourages the water to sheet evenly, to cool at a specific rate, to concentrate the dissolved silica in exactly the right way. It is gardening, in a sense. Cultivation. The keeper is a farmer of the inorganic, a shepherd of glass.

The glass-mites do not fear it. They recognize it, if recognition is a meaningful term for a creature whose entire sensorium is a chemical gradient and a vibration in the superheated water film. They skitter around the keeper's flat feet as it moves, a glittering tide that parts and reforms. They know that where the keeper scrapes, fresh lattice-spores will bloom. The keeper, in turn, harvests them. The opal comb-teeth are for this: it lowers its mouth to the water's surface, not drinking but filtering, drawing the microscopic, semi-crystalline filaments across its sieve and absorbing them. They become part of it. Its body is a continuous accretion, a slow, living sedimentation that incorporates the spores into its structure. The ridged patterns on its torso are not purely decorative; they are a log of diet, a fossil record of the pool chemistry over the long, slow unfurling of its existence.

The sinter-crabs are more circumspect. A predator does not last long in an ecology this minimalist unless it is cautious. The keeper has seen individual crabs—recognized them by the unique mosaic patterns of their articulated shells—across spans of time that would represent generations in a carbon-based system. But here, growth and molting and death operate on a different tempo. The keeper will occasionally, with a movement so deliberate it seems ritual, place the tip of one brittle finger against a sinter-crab's carapace. The crab freezes. A tremor passes between them, some communication conducted through the medium of the geyserite, a frequency that vibrates in the silica lattices they both partially share. Then the keeper moves on, and the crab resumes its silent, motionless hunting, and nothing has changed except that the keeper now knows this crab is still here, still part of the circuit, still functioning.

It does not think in words. Thought, for the keeper, is a slow, crystalline process: the gradual alignment of internal structures, the settling of silicate particles into new configurations, a computation performed by accretion and erosion. Its memory is its shape. Every scrape mark on a pool rim is a stored datum; every layer of its body is a record. It has been here since the factory halls were still active, since the steam-stacks exhaled industrial smoke instead of geothermal vapor, since the first thin films of silica began to encrust the pipes and walls. It was not always the keeper. It was something else, something smaller and simpler, perhaps a maintenance construct, perhaps a waste-remediation organism, perhaps a piece of the original machinery that learned, slowly and inexorably, to grow. The line between engineered and evolved is irrelevant here. The keeper is the conclusion of both processes, the final convergence of industry and biology and geology into a single, self-sustaining form.

There are moments—rare, marked by a particular configuration of the thermal plume, a particular stillness in the mist—when the keeper pauses in its circuit and turns its faceted head toward the white-gold sky. It has no eyes, but it senses the broader thermal landscape, the vast breathing of the Boréal Vent, the slow pulse of the planet's heat. It feels the turbines turning in the deep, their quartz shafts grinding with a friction that generates a faint, steady hum. It feels the water weeping from the lips of the sintered pools, the collective susurration of the plain. It feels itself as part of this, an organ in a body that encompasses the entire raised shelf of the world.

And sometimes, in these moments, its lipless mouth parts, and a sound emerges. It is not a voice. It is the same sound the comb-teeth make when filtering, but amplified, shaped by the internal chambers of its torso into something resonant and low. A harmonic of the deep turbine hum. A note that matches the frequency of the dripping water. It is, perhaps, the closest thing to a name this place will ever speak, a name for the steaming pools and the glass-mites and the opal-shelled crabs and the fossilized factories and the blank, bright, eternal mist.

It is a sound that means: *here*. *Now*. *Still*.
