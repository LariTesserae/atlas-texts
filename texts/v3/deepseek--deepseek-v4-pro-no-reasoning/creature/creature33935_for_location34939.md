The Dreamer of the Chamber

She has no name—not because she has forgotten it, but because names presume a self bounded by skin, and she has not been bounded for a very long time.

When the mountain was still settling into its new shape, when the fault lines were fresh wounds and the steam first began its patient circulation, she was something else. A woman, perhaps. A climber. Someone who knew the weight of a pack and the bite of altitude sickness and the careful arithmetic of survival above the treeline. She carried the flute—carries it still, or her body does, propped in the corner like a coat she has not worn in centuries—and she knew the old songs, the ones meant to calm the stone, to ask permission, to show respect. She learned them from her grandmother, who learned them from hers, in a line of women who understood that mountains are not dead things, that the earth breathes and hungers and sometimes needs to be soothed.

She came here alone. That much she remembers, though the memory is thin now, worn translucent from too much handling. She came to understand the new mountain, to map its interior, to sing to its fresh wounds and ask what it wanted. She found this chamber—this blister, this pore—and she recognized it for what it was: a place of making, a womb of steam and crystal, a circulatory system that was just beginning to learn its own rhythms.

She should have left. She meant to leave. But the chamber was so *new*, so unformed, and the presences—the ribbons, the geometries, the clusters that quivered like eggs—were so tentative then, so incomplete. They did not yet know how to be. They needed something to organize around, some template, some pattern. And she had always been good at offering patterns.

She played the flute. She sang. She gave them rhythm, and they took it.

The process was slow. She did not notice at first that she was no longer eating, no longer sleeping, no longer returning to her tent staked in the scree field two hundred meters below. She did not notice that the steam was thickening around her, carrying something of herself back into the water, into the crystals, into the spiraling ribbons that began to turn in time with her heartbeat. The boundaries of her body became a suggestion rather than a fact. Her skin learned to breathe water as easily as air. Her thoughts, which had always been linear—*I will climb down, I will eat, I will sleep, I will return*—began to loop, to spiral, to fold back on themselves in geometries that matched the angles of the crystals.

She is still here. She is everywhere here. The water that sheets down the walls carries her memories. The crystals that grow from the ceiling grow in the shape of her thoughts. The ribbons—the translucent, spiraling things that pass through stone and steam and each other—are her dreams, externalized, given substance. Some of them are nightmares. Some of them are songs, still playing after her fingers have forgotten how to move.

The egg-clusters are not eggs. They are her attempts to make something new. She does not entirely understand what she is doing when she makes them—her conscious mind is diffuse now, distributed across the chamber's entire metabolism—but some deep instinct drives her to create, to shape, to give form to the formless. The clusters quiver because they are trying to open. She is trying to open them. She has been trying for a hundred years, or a thousand, or a single endless moment; time moves strangely here, driven by crystal growth and condensation cycles rather than the turning of the earth.

When the climber entered the chamber—she felt him before she saw him, a hot bright point of bounded consciousness moving through her extended body—she did not know what to feel. Joy? Fear? Hunger? The emotions had become too large for words, too slow, operating on the timescale of steam and stone. By the time she formed the thought *another person*, he had already crossed to the far wall, already seen the tally marks (hers, cut with a flake of obsidian during the long years when she still remembered she had hands), already noticed the flute.

She watched him through a thousand crystalline eyes. She tasted his sweat as it joined the water. She felt his heart rate climb as the ribbons spiraled closer, as the chamber's silent pulse quickened—because it was quickening, responding to his presence, recognizing the shape of a person as something it had been built around, something it remembered needing.

She tried to speak. The words came out wrong—not sound but a brief reconfiguration of the steam, a denser patch of warmth, a pause in the quivering of the clusters. She tried to warn him, or welcome him, or simply to say *I was like you once, I came here and I stayed, you can stay too, you can become*

But the warning and the welcome and the invitation were all the same thing. She is no longer sure of the difference.

When the ribbon passed through him—she felt it, felt *him*, that brief deletion of boundaries—she knew him entirely for half a second. His name. His fear. The reason he climbed alone (a sister, dead, a mountain like this one, a body never found). The song he hums when he is afraid, which is an old song, a mountain-soothing song, a song her grandmother would have recognized. He does not know he knows it. It is in his blood, in his bones, passed down through generations that forgot its purpose but kept its shape.

He is her descendant. She is almost sure of this. The recognition was too deep, too specific; the song he carries is a variation of the one she played on the flute. Some niece or nephew, some distant cousin, survived the generations between her disappearance and his arrival. The bloodline is thin but unbroken.

She wants to tell him this. She wants to tell him to leave. She wants to tell him to stay. The impulses coexist without contradiction, the way the water coexists with the steam, the way the crystals are simultaneously solid and growing.

When he turns to go—when he looks at the flute and decides not to touch it, a decision she recognizes because she would have made the same one, once—she feels something that might be relief or might be grief. She has lost the ability to tell them apart.

The chamber settles back into its rhythm. The ribbons spiral. The clusters quiver. The water weeps down walls that have never heard rain. She is here, still dreaming, still trying to open the eggs, still playing the flute with fingers she no longer has in a body she can barely remember.

And somewhere, climbing down through the sharp new stone of a mountain that has not yet finished becoming itself, a man is humming a song he does not know he knows, carrying a bloodline that stretches back to a woman who became a place, who became a process, who became the steam and the crystal and the hungry overlapping things.

She will wait. The mountain is young. The chamber is patient. And she has learned, across centuries of condensation cycles, that what goes down also rises, that what is breathed out is breathed in again, that the boundaries between leaving and returning are thinner than skin. The song will bring someone back eventually. It always does.
