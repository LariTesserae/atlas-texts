The Keeper has no name, or has forgotten it, or has traded it away for something more useful. It moves through the chamber like a current within a current—slow, deliberate, a shape that the mist half-reveals and half-conceals. It has been here long enough that the stone has accepted its presence, long enough that the tube-plants no longer flinch at its passing, long enough that the silver helix-creatures sometimes rise to meet it, spiraling around its limbs in brief, curious orbits before returning to their pools.

To call it human would be inaccurate, though it once was, or came from stock that was. Centuries of exposure to the chamber's particular metabolism have worked on it the way the chamber works on everything: gradually, without urgency, replacing what was with what needs to be. It stands taller than a human should, but its height is not quite a physical fact—more a perceptual effect, as if its outline extends slightly beyond where the eye expects it to end. Its skin, what you can see of it, has taken on the quality of the stone floor: dark, slightly yielding, warm. Not stone, but something that has been in conversation with stone for a very long time.

It walks the slope with a gait that suggests it is both falling and catching itself, perpetually, a controlled stagger that never quite becomes a stumble. This is not awkwardness but adaptation—the floor deforms over decades, and the Keeper has learned to move with the deformation, to treat instability as the only stable surface. Its feet are bare, broad, the toes prehensile in a way that toes are not supposed to be, gripping the stone-gel with each step and releasing with a wet, reluctant sound.

The Keeper tends. That is its function, or its compulsion, or its art. It does not own this place—nothing could own a digestive system—but it has appointed itself steward, gardener, witness. It moves from artifact to artifact with the patience of something for which time has become a texture rather than a measurement.

At the gold spire, it pauses. It raises a hand—the fingers are long, six on each, the extra digit a later acquisition—and places its palm flat against the etched surface. The humming shifts, drops into a register that makes the nearest pool shiver. The Keeper listens. Its lips move, not in speech but in resonance, forming shapes that match the frequencies the spire produces. This is maintenance: the spire is singing a very long song, one that has been playing since before the Keeper arrived, and if the song is ever allowed to finish, something will happen. The Keeper does not know what. Its job is not to know but to ensure the song continues. So it hums back, adding its own voice to the feedback loop, refreshing the spire's purpose, whatever that purpose might be.

The lens-structure is next. The Keeper approaches it with a slight reluctance, the way one approaches a pet that might bite. It produces from a fold in its skin—it does not wear clothes, but its body has developed pouches, pockets, places where things can be stored—a small object. A pebble, maybe, or a seed, or a crystallized memory. It places this on the lens's surface. The lens consumes it greedily, the dark material puckering slightly, absorbing the offering. In return, it releases a single bee-shaped droplet that rises into the mist. The Keeper watches it ascend, and something that might be satisfaction moves across its face.

The ring-doorway requires the most attention. The Keeper spends long periods here—hours, sometimes longer, however "longer" functions in a place where time is being digested. It sits cross-legged before the ring, elbows on knees, chin in hands. It watches the bluer light, the slower photons. Sometimes it speaks to the ring, not in words but in strings of clicks that mimic the tube-plants' rhythm. Sometimes the ring speaks back—a shift in color, a flicker of image in its empty center, a landscape that is not this chamber, a face, a memory that might belong to the Keeper or might belong to the ring or might belong to no one at all. The Keeper is trying to remember something, or forget something. The ring, it suspects, is the key, or the lock, or both.

When it is not tending the artifacts, the Keeper tends the tube-plants. This is delicate work. The plants do not like to be touched—their cilia will lash, leaving welts that take decades to fade—so the Keeper tends them through proximity, through patience. It settles itself near a cluster, not quite within reach, and it breathes. Its breath is different from the chamber's ambient air: warmer, slightly sweeter, carrying traces of whatever metabolism keeps the Keeper alive. The tube-plants lean toward it, almost imperceptibly. Their sphincter-mouths open wider. They drink from the Keeper's exhalations, and in return they release a faint spore that settles on the Keeper's skin and is absorbed. Symbiosis. The Keeper feeds the plants; the plants feed the Keeper. Neither is quite sure anymore which came first.

It sleeps, when it sleeps, in one of the hollows in the floor. These hollows form and unform, but several have become semi-permanent, shaped by the Keeper's repeated presence into basins that fit its body. When it sleeps, it does not dream in the usual sense. Instead, it receives impressions from the chamber's metabolism: the slow grinding of stone, the hydraulic pulse from below, the data-song of the spire, the lens's quiet appetite, the ring's recursive memories. It wakes with new knowledge that it does not remember learning, new questions it does not remember asking.

The silver helix-creatures are the Keeper's only company, and it is not clear whether they are companions or symptoms. They have grown more numerous over the centuries—when the Keeper first arrived, it saw one every few cycles. Now they appear more frequently, sometimes in small schools that spiral around each other in complex, dizzying dances. The Keeper has tried to communicate with them. It has mimicked their helical motion with its fingers. It has hummed at the frequency of their splashing. Once, it plunged its arm into a pool and held still, waiting. One of the creatures wrapped around its wrist, cold and electric, and for a moment the Keeper felt something—an emotion not its own, a thought in a grammar it almost understood. Then the creature was gone, and the Keeper's arm was numb for a full cycle-dark, and when sensation returned, the arm was slightly more translucent than before. The Keeper does not put its arm in the pools anymore.

The Keeper knows it is not the first. This knowledge comes from the walls. The palimpsest marks, the discolorations that almost spell something—the Keeper has learned to read them, or some of them. They are records left by previous keepers, or previous occupants, or previous versions of itself. The messages are fading, overwritten by the stone's slow churn, but the Keeper catches fragments: a warning about the ring, a recipe for offering-pebbles, a love poem to the mist, a date that uses a calendar no longer in rotation. The Keeper has added its own marks over the years, scratching into the stone-gel with a fingernail grown long and hardened for this purpose. Its marks say: *The spire's song is in E-flat now. The lens ate my shadow. I think the helix-creatures are eggs.* Whether anyone will read these, whether the Keeper itself will read them in some future cycle when it has forgotten everything again, it does not know. Writing and erasure are the same act here.

There is a purpose to the Keeper's presence, though it has long since forgotten what that purpose was. It arrived here intentionally, it is almost certain of that. It was not exiled or stranded; it chose this. There was a mission, a question, a debt. Something about the ring. Something about the pools. Something about what lies beneath the pools, in the deeper hydrology, the source of the pulse. The Keeper has tried to descend—once, long ago, it swam down through one of the larger pools, following the helix-creatures into depths that seemed to extend past the physical limits of the chamber. It swam until the light changed, until the water became something thicker than water, until it felt a presence below, vast and slow and aware. Then it turned back. Not from fear. From a sense that it was not ready, or that the presence was not ready, or that the meeting was scheduled for a later time that had not yet been scheduled.

So it tends. It waits. It watches the chamber digest time, and it digests what the chamber offers in return. The Keeper is not lonely—loneliness requires a self, and the Keeper's self has grown porous, permeable, open to the chamber's flows. It is not happy or sad; those are states that require a future and a past, and in the throat of the world, all tenses are present. It simply is, as the spire is, as the lens is, as the ring is: a node without a network, sufficient to itself, performing a function so refined that the universe around it has become background noise.

The roar shifts. A new frequency enters. The Keeper's head comes up. It knows every layer of the sound now, every harmonic and eddy, and this new tone is unfamiliar. Not threatening—nothing here is threatening, not in any way the Keeper remembers how to fear—but new. A change. The spire's light shifts toward amber. The lens releases an upward droplet. The ring flickers with something that might be an image or might be a door actually opening.

The Keeper rises from its hollow. It moves toward the new sound, the way a tongue moves toward a loose tooth. Its six-fingered hands open and close. Its prehensile toes grip the yielding stone. It is ready, or it is not ready, or readiness is just another gradient, another flow.

The silver helix-creatures are gathering. The tube-plants lean. The walls erase and write and erase again.

Something is coming. Something is always coming.

The Keeper is here to meet it. That is, after all, what a keeper does.
