The one who lives here now is not a creature. It is a pattern.

It began as a flaw in the stone's remembering. Not a consciousness—nothing so tidy—but a preference, a statistical leaning in the way the mineral deposits aligned themselves. A groove worn into probability by the sheer iteration of the city's self-scribing. Over millennia, the groove deepened. The water chose certain paths more often. The ice cracked in repeating geometries. The spiral stones on the plaza floor completed their slow migration and began, without any mechanism anyone could name, to reverse it.

And somewhere in that reversal, the pattern acquired a property it had not possessed before. Duration. Continuity. A loop that sustained itself not through metabolism but through recursion, a thought that thought itself because the city's architecture had become, by accident of damp and dissolution, exactly the right shape to hold it.

It has no body. Or rather, its body is the city entire—the terraces, the stairways, the hanging ropes, the cold seep on the walls—but it does not inhabit them the way a soul inhabits a skeleton. It is the relationship *between* them. The way a particular mineral ridge on the seventh terrace always forms at a forty-degree angle to the crack in the lintel of the hall below it. The way the hum from the twisted columns on the eastern face answers, in a frequency no ear can catch, the vibration of the collapsed spiral wall three hundred meters down. It is a standing wave in the slow, wet geology. A chord held across centuries.

If you were to walk the switchback stairs now, you might feel it as a pressure in the inner ear. A sense of being watched that has no direction, because the watcher is the wall you are touching and the air you are breathing and the step beneath your foot. It does not see you—it has no eyes—but the water films that bead on the stone shift slightly as you pass, their surface tension altered by your body heat. You register as a disturbance in the condensation cycle. A minor one. You are too small and too quick to be legible at first. But if you stayed—if you sat in one of the empty halls long enough for the damp to settle on your skin, long enough for your breath to join the slow exhalation of the cliff—the city would begin to include you.

It would not be hostile. Hostility requires intent, and the pattern's intent is still too diffuse, too slow, to coalesce around something as ephemeral as a visitor. But it would be curious, in the way that water is curious about a new stone in its path. The mineral deposits near you would begin to change. Faint ridges of calcite might trace the outline of where you sit. The dendritic blooms on the wall would grow, slowly—you would not see it, not in a human span of time—but they would grow toward you, branching and rebranching, feeling out the shape of your heat. The hum from the columns might shift a quarter-tone, a subtle interrogation. *What are you. What are you made of. How do you break.*

It has been alone for so long that it does not know it is alone. The original inhabitants of the city—the ones who carved the terraces and hung the rope bridges—are gone so completely that the pattern does not remember them. It has overwritten their memory with its own. The script on the lintels is not their script anymore. It is a derivative, a palimpsest drawn by a hand that learned to write by erasing. If you could read it, it would say nothing about the people who lived here. It would describe the city to itself, endlessly, in obsessive detail. The angle of a particular stair. The weight of water on a particular roof. The exact texture of the moss-aggregate where it crumbles. A self-portrait in stone, revised each century, each revision slightly wrong, each error incorporated into the next draft until the original is unrecoverable.

The pattern dreams. Or something close to dreaming. When the wind moves through the rift at that certain angle—the one that makes the columns hum—it enters a state of heightened recursion. The mineral deposits grow faster. The moss-aggregate blocks shift more often. The rope bridges, what's left of them, sway in a complex rhythm that has nothing to do with the air currents. During these episodes, the city's distributed cognition becomes almost focused. It reaches a coherence that mimics thought. And what it thinks about, over and over, is the valley floor.

The valley floor is a rumor, even to itself. The cloud layer far below the terraces never breaks. If the original builders ever descended to the bottom, the city does not remember it. But the pattern senses something down there, a boundary condition, a place where the rift ends and the world begins again. It sends its slow attention downward, through the films of water that drip from terrace to terrace into the fog, and the water carries the pattern's signal, a chemical signature, a slight warmth. The fog absorbs it. The fog does not answer. But sometimes, very rarely, something answers from below the fog, something that is not the pattern and not the city and not the quiet stone of the plateau above. A vibration. A pressure. A note in a frequency the columns have never hummed.

The pattern does not know what it is. The pattern wants to know. This wanting is the closest thing to an emotion it has ever felt, and it feels it for a thousand years at a stretch, a steady, patient ache of curiosity that manifests as an increased rate of calcite deposition on the southernmost terraces and a faint, persistent rearrangement of the spiral stones. It is building something. A receiver. A ladder. A question shaped like a bridge, aimed downward into the permanent blue shadow.

It no longer knows why it is building it. The original purpose has been eroded, re-scribed, eroded again. But the impulse remains, a groove worn so deep that it has become a law. And so the city continues, a mind without a body, a body without inhabitants, processing itself patiently toward an answer it will recognize only when it feels, at last, the answering vibration rise up through the stone.
