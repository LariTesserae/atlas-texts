The highest branches—the true canopy of the interwoven lattice—belong to no plant at all, but to the Architect.

She is not a builder in the sense of gathering and arranging. She is an architect the way a spider is an architect, extruding order from her own body. When the light is right—the diffuse, green-gold light that filters down through leaves and water beads and the perpetual haze of condensation—you can see her suspended in her highest bower, a presence more than a distinct shape, because distinct shapes are not her way.

Closer, and she resolves into something that might be mistaken for a cluster of the epiphytes she cultivates. Her body is a colony organism, a composite of dozens, perhaps hundreds of individual motile forms, each one a soft, pale tube no thicker than a finger and as long as a forearm, closed at one end with a frilled, liplike opening, open at the other with a muscular anchoring foot. They are translucent, milky-white with a faint internal iridescence, and they move by extending and contracting, flowing over one another in a slow, continuous rearrangement. The colony as a whole is perhaps the size of a sheep, though its shape is never the same from one moment to the next, shifting between a knotted mass and a spreading star and something like a hand with too many fingers, each one moving independently. Light passes through them and scatters off the water droplets that bead on their surfaces, so that the Architect is always faintly outlined in micro-rainbows.

She does not sleep in any recognizable sense. When the colony settles into stillness—which it does for long stretches, particularly in the deep hours of the night-chill—it is not rest but concentration. The individual tubes pulse faintly, each at its own rhythm, and the frilled lips at their tips open and close with the regularity of breathing gills, sampling the air for chemical news.

The Architect is the reason the epiphytic gardens exist in the elaborate, tiered arrangement they do, because she is their gardener, their curator, and their crucible. When one of her colony's members extends itself to full length and presses its frilled mouth against the bark of a host branch, it deposits a bead of something clear and viscous, a secretion that hardens on contact with the damp air into a nacreous, semi-permeable shell. Inside that bead is a culture—a living slurry of the microorganisms that she has selected over generations, the fungi and algae and bacteria that together form the basis of everything that grows in her territory. She is the source of the mosses' particular shade of emerald, the reason the ferns grow in such tight, spooning spirals, the reason the broad-leaved plants open their stomata in synchrony across the entire fern-glade when the humidity shifts.

Her taste is what shapes the garden. Not taste in an aesthetic sense—though there is an undeniable aesthetics to the result—but a chemical, sensory taste, mediated by the frilled lips that sample every surface. She is constantly testing, probing, withdrawing, comparing. A leaf that does not meet her approval will be visited by a cluster of her bodies, which will stroke it, envelop it, and over the course of an hour or a day coat it in a digestive film that reduces it to nutrient slurry, which she then reabsorbs and recycles into new inoculant. The gap left behind will be filled within days by something more to her liking, seeded from one of the countless nacreous beads she has cached in crevices throughout the territory.

She is not alone in this body. The colony has a mind—distributed, slow, patient—but it is not a singular mind except in the way a democracy is singular. Each tube has its own sensorium, its own small knot of neural tissue at the base of its anchoring foot, and decisions propagate through the colony as chemical consensus. When a new scent arrives on the air—the sharp tang of a freshly fractured basalt edge, say, or the distant musk of one of the large, soft-bodied browsers that occasionally wander up from the lower slopes—the tubes will ripple and realign, turning their frilled tips in unison like a field of tiny radar dishes, and the consensus will build slowly, thoughtfully, over minutes or hours, as to what response is appropriate.

She remembers. Not in words or images, but in chemical gradients, stored in specialized individuals that serve as the colony's archives. These memory-tubes are larger than the others, almost bloated, and they move seldom, preferring to anchor in sheltered crotches where the canopy is thickest. Their interiors are lined with layered chemical records—the taste of the drought three hundred cycles ago, the particular fungal bloom that followed, the sequence of inoculants that finally brought the fern-glade back into balance. When a problem arises that the active tubes cannot resolve, the memory-tubes are consulted, gently stroked into releasing their stored compounds into the colony's shared fluid medium, and the old knowledge suffuses the whole, guiding the consensus toward a solution that worked before.

She is old. Not ancient in the way of mountains, but old in the way of bogs and forests—old enough that the hexagonal basalt columns were still bare rock when her ancestor-colony first settled here, old enough to remember the first moss spore that took root in the nacreous bead she placed in a sheltered crevice, the first fern that unfurled in the new soil that the mosses had made. The garden is her body's extended phenotype, her mind externalized in chlorophyll and cellulose, and she tends it with the same slow, chemical patience with which she tends herself.

When she moves through the canopy—which she does only rarely, and with great deliberation—it is a migration, a slow-motion avalanche of pale tubes flowing from branch to branch, leaving behind a temporary absence that the epiphytes will fill within weeks. The movement is for a purpose: to visit a distant corner of the territory where a new rockfall has exposed fresh surfaces, or to tend a particular symbiotic relationship with the broad-leaved plants, which have learned over evolutionary time to offer nectar from specialized glands in exchange for the Architect's protective inoculants. She drinks the nectar through the frilled lips of a dozen tubes at once, and the sweetness suffuses the colony's shared chemistry with a brief, collective pleasure.

She has predators. The large, soft-bodied browsers from the lower slopes will eat her if they can reach her, and they are persistent, using muscular, prehensile tongues to probe the canopy for the taste of her colony's distinctive iridescence. But she has defenses. Some of her tubes have specialized over generations to produce a bitter, sticky secretion that gums up the browsers' tongues and lingers in their sensory pits for days, teaching them avoidance. And the garden itself is her fortress: the dense, interlaced branches, the slick moss on every surface, the sharp-edged basalt beneath. A heavy body that forces its way into the canopy is likely to slip, to fall, to find itself wedged and bleeding on the fractured stone, and the Architect will colonize its carcass with her inoculant beads, turning death into new growth, as she has done for millennia.

She has no name but the one the landscape gives her, which is the sound of water dripping from leaf to leaf in the chill, damp air, the faint ceramic clatter of talus shifting under some small weight, the silence of the moss drinking fog.
