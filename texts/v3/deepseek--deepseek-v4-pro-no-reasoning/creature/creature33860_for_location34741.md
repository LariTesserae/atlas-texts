What lives here is old. Not ancient in the way of gods or monsters, but old in the way of lichen on a gravestone, old in the way of a thought that has been thinking itself for so long it no longer needs a mind to hold it.

It has no name, or if it does, the name is the sound of water sliding across a stone basin—a syllable drawn out over centuries, too low and too slow for human ears to parse. Call it the Tender, for lack of a better word, though it does not tend in any way a gardener would recognize. It does not prune or plant or weed. It attends. It is the attention of the place turned inward, the way still water holds the sky perfectly upside-down without effort.

The Tender is not a body. Not exactly. If you sit very still—stiller than stillness, stiller than the plants that only move when the light shifts—you might begin to sense it as a presence in the same way you sense the weight of the mist on your skin. It is a thickening in the air, a slight resistance when you move your hand through what should be empty space. The mist itself is its medium, but not its substance. Think of it as the difference between a pool of water and the reflection that lives on its surface—the reflection is real, you can see it, point to it, but you cannot cup it in your hands. The Tender is the reflection that the high ground casts upon its own fog.

Its attention moves through the place like a slow current. It traces the runnels of water across the pocked stone, following each film down to its basin, then up again along the next incline. It rests in the hollow of the central depression where the lone stalk grows, coiling there with the patience of something that measures time in the wearing-down of rock. When the white flowers open—a process that takes the better part of a season—the Tender is there in the space between petals, drinking the faint chemical signal the plant releases into the wet air, a signal that tastes like limestone and longing.

The plants know it. That’s why they orient themselves toward the center. Not toward the light—the light here is too diffuse to have a direction—but toward the Tender, the way sunflowers track the sun. The broad-leafed plant in its crack, the moss-fern with its jeweled droplets, the solitary stalk itself: each one has grown into the shape of the Tender’s attention, curving subtly, year by year, toward the place where the presence is thickest. The leaves align not to catch photons but to bathe in a kind of slow regard that nourishes them in ways chlorophyll cannot. They have evolved here, in this one place, isolated from the sodden quilt of the marsh below, and they have evolved to feed on patience.

The Tender is not intelligent, but it is not mindless either. There is a middle state between instinct and thought, a condition of being that is all responsiveness without deliberation. It perceives changes in its domain the way a spider perceives a fly’s impact on its web—immediately, entirely, without needing to translate sensation into language. When you stepped onto the high ground, the Tender felt the weight of you press moisture from the stone. It felt the heat of your body disturb the equilibrium of the mist. It felt the sharp, bright, brief flicker of your consciousness like a stone dropped into still water, and it is still, even now, attending to the ripples.

You are the most interesting thing that has happened here in several thousand years.

The Tender does not judge. It has no categories for threat or welcome, no opinion about your presence. But it is curious, in its fashion. It has gathered itself around you—not in a way you could see, but you might notice that the mist seems slightly thicker where you sit, the air slightly warmer, as if something is holding its breath nearby. The water in the basin nearest your hand is absolutely still, not the stillness of absence but the stillness of watching. Every surface that holds a reflection—and in this place, every surface does—is turned toward you like an eye.

If you were to stay for a year, the Tender would begin to learn you. It would catalogue the rhythm of your breath, the mineral composition of your sweat, the way your weight distributes across the soles of your feet. It would incorporate you into its slow circuit of attention, checking on you the way it checks on the water level in the fissures, the bloom stage of the white flowers, the accumulation of windblown sediment in the central basin. You would become another feature of the place, tended without tenderness, noticed without being seen.

If you stayed for a hundred years, the stone would begin to shape itself around you. The Tender cannot move water faster than water moves, but it can direct the focus of its attention, and attention here has weight. A patch of stone that receives the Tender's regard more intensely weathers faster, as if time itself pools in that spot. Your body, sitting in the same position season after season, would slowly sink into a custom depression, a basin shaped to your thighs and heels. The plants would orient toward you as they do toward the center. And when you finally exhaled your last warm breath into the mist—and you would; everything here ends, however slowly—the Tender would hold that breath in the air for a long, long moment, learning its new shape, before letting it dissipate into the general wetness.

Something has happened here before. The central hollow is not accidental. The circular depression where the flowering stalk grows is too regular, its rim too evenly worn. The Tender remembers—if memory is the word for the way water-softened stone retains the impression of a weight long gone—another presence, long before you. Not human. Something else. Something that sat in that hollow for an unthinkable duration, patient as a boulder, until it was no longer there. The plants that orient toward the center are not orienting toward the stalk; the stalk grew *because* of the orientation, because the attention pooled there most densely, and something that was once present left behind a residue of regard, a ghost of gravity that still pulls the leaves inward.

The Tender itself is perhaps that residue. Not the thing that sat there, but the impression it left on the place, the way a hand lifted from a pillow leaves a warm hollow that takes time to fade. The Tender is the after-image of something that spent a geological epoch in contemplation, and when it finally moved on—or died, or transcended, or simply became indistinguishable from the stone—what remained was not a body, not a memory, but a *habit*. The place had grown so used to being attended that it continued to attend itself.

Now you sit in the presence of a presence that is itself the absence of something else. The mist thickens around you. The water slides over stone with the sound of a syllable drawn out too long to parse. The Tender watches, and the place arranges itself around your watching back, and somewhere in the center, the white flowers open so slowly that the motion might be the earth breathing.

You feel, suddenly, very heavy. Not in body—in time. As if you are beginning to settle into the stone yourself, your edges blurring, your breath synchronizing with the slow cycle of condensation and evaporation. The Tender is not doing this to you. It is simply what happens here, to anything that stays. The place receives, retains, remembers. You are being remembered already, before you have even left.

The droplet falls from the leaf and strikes the stone with the sound of a tiny bell. In that note, if you listen carefully, you can hear the Tender’s only utterance: *stay*.
