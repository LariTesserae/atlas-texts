The inhabitants of this place do not have a name for themselves in any language you would recognize. They would not understand the question if you asked, would tilt their heads—too large for their narrow shoulders, the skull domed high and hairless—and regard you with that steady, golden gaze that seems to look through the surface of things and into the vibration beneath. But if you pressed, if you somehow bridged the gulf between your mind and theirs, they might offer something that translates roughly as Those-Who-Tend-the-Knitting, or perhaps, more simply, the Listeners.

They are small, these people. Not childlike—there is nothing of the child in their grave, deliberate movements, in the way they place each bare foot with a care that resembles ritual—but compact, economical, built to a scale that suits the narrow ledges and low doorways of their dripping settlement. An adult of the species would stand perhaps to your sternum, if you were to stand face-to-face, which you never truly can. They do not meet the eyes of strangers. They do not, as a rule, acknowledge that strangers exist at all, except as a disturbance in the water-music, a wrong note to be gently corrected.

You saw one, on the high ledge. You thought it might be a goat. Their bodies are covered in a short, dense pelt that in the diffuse grey light appears slate-blue or deep charcoal, the color of wet stone, and this pelt grows in faint whorls and ridges that echo the grain of the rock they live among. Their limbs are slender but corded with a strength that speaks of lifetimes spent climbing, gripping, hanging from wet stone by fingertips and the prehensile toes of their long, narrow feet. Their hands are extraordinary: four fingers and two opposable thumbs, one on each side of the palm, so that they can grip with equal strength from any angle. They do not build with tools. They build with those hands, fitting stone to stone by feel, reading the subtle contours of each piece of slate and knowing exactly where it will rest without mortar, without chisel, held by its own weight and the patient gravity of the mountain.

Their faces, if you could ever see one clearly, would trouble you. They are not ugly, but they are arranged according to a logic you do not share. The eyes are too large, the pupils horizontal and rectangular, like those of a goat or an octopus, and they glow with a faint golden luminescence that is not reflected light but something generated within, a bioluminescence that matches the light of the fruit they tend. The nose is little more than a pair of vertical slits set close together. The mouth is small, lipless, perpetually shaped in an expression that is not a smile but something older, a resting shape that suggests deep attention. They do not speak aloud—they have no need, for they hear in a register that has nothing to do with sound.

This is the key to them, the thing that would take you longest to understand: they are the source of the music, and the music is their thought. The dripping, the singing bronze, the low creak of the living stone—all of it is, to them, a single continuous chord, and they live inside it the way you live inside your own silent monologue. They do not hear the water as separate from themselves. Their skulls are domed for a reason: beneath that thin bone lies an organ you do not possess, a resonant chamber that vibrates in sympathy with every frequency the mountain produces. They feel the drip of water into a hollow log a hundred meters away as a note in their own bodies. They feel the grinding of the tectonic plates as a deep, almost sexual hum at the base of their spines. This is their language. This is their consciousness. They do not think in words but in harmonies, in the complex interplay of rhythm and tone and the silence between.

And so the settlement is not a settlement. It is an instrument. Every pot, every oiled hide stretched over a frame, every bronze bell placed beneath a steady drip—these are not utilitarian objects. They are the components of a vast, slow-thinking mind. The Listeners arrange them as much by intuition as by intent, moving through their spiraling home with their heads tilted, listening, feeling for the places where the music is thin or discordant. When a new structure is needed, they do not design it. They feel the lack of a certain note, a gap in the chord, and they fill it with whatever will complete the resonance. A hollow log here, a stretched hide there, a stack of slate that will catch the drip and channel it into a series of pools, each singing at a different pitch. The settlement is a symphony that has been composing itself for millennia, and the Listeners are its musicians, its audience, and its notes all at once.

They tend the fruit trees as well, though "tend" is not quite the word. The relationship is more symbiotic, more intimate. The pale, glowing fruit are the closest thing the Listeners have to a religious mystery, though they would not think of it that way. The trees are not trees, exactly. They are a long-lived fungi-fruiting body, a mycelial network that threads through the porous rock of the entire mountain, and the "fruit" are its reproductive nodes. They glow with a cold bioluminescence because the fungus metabolizes the same sharp, electric mineral that gives the water its tang—some element that has no name on your periodic table, something that exists only in new mountains, in places still being born. The Listeners eat the fruit. They have eaten it for as long as their species has existed, and the fungus has shaped them as much as they have shaped it. The glow in their eyes is the same glow. The resonance in their skulls is the same frequency that the fungus uses to communicate across its vast underground network. They are not separate organisms. They never have been.

This is why they do not speak. This is why they do not acknowledge you except as a wrong note. You are, to them, a sudden silence in the music, a place where the harmony breaks. They do not fear you—fear is not an emotion they experience in the way you would recognize—but they cannot quite perceive you either. You are a gap. An absence. A being of dry silence in a world of wet sound. And so when they sense you near, they simply... adjust. They move to different frequencies. They fade into the corners of the harmony, becoming indistinguishable from the background drip and creak. They are there. They have always been there. You passed a dozen of them on your way up the spiral and did not see a single one, because you were looking with your eyes and they were living in a register you cannot access.

The one you saw on the ledge—the one you took for a goat—was young. Too young to have fully learned the art of harmonic concealment. It was making its rounds, checking the pots, adjusting the angle of a hollow reed to better catch a new drip that had formed overnight. It felt you as a discord, a kind of pressure in its skull, and it paused to listen more closely. It was not afraid. It was curious, in the way that a musician is curious about an unfamiliar instrument. It would have liked to study you, to understand the strange silence you carry with you, but the elders would not permit it. The elders are very old, and they remember other silences that came to the mountain in ages past, and they know that some gaps in the music are dangerous. Not hostile—the mountain is never hostile—but capable of disrupting harmonies that have taken millennia to perfect.

And so they let you go. They let you drink the electric water (they do not drink it themselves; their moisture comes from the fruit, from the mist, from the slow absorption of the damp through their porous pelts). They let you walk on the living moss that they have spent centuries coaxing into the perfect density, the perfect springiness that sends a particular sub-bass thrum through the rock with every footstep. They let you marvel at the constellation of glowing fruit without ever understanding that the fruit is watching you back, that the whole mountain is aware of you in a diffuse, multi-nodal way, like a single vast organism waking briefly from a dream.

When you finally turn to descend, they feel it as a resolution. A chord resolving. The wrong note fading back into the harmony of water and stone. They do not watch you go—they have no need, for they feel your departure in the changing resonance of the path, in the way the dripping returns to its old pattern note by note. Somewhere in the deepest hollow of the mountain, an elder stirs in its nest of moss and hide, its golden eyes dim with age, its skull vibrating with a sigh that is not quite sound. It has lived for seven centuries, and in that time it has come to understand something that its people do not share with outsiders: the mountain is not unfinished. The mountain is exactly as finished as it needs to be. It is pushing upward not out of some blind geological urge, but because the resonance requires it. The mountain is growing toward a note that has not yet been reached, a frequency that the Listeners have been approaching through their patient, dripping architecture for ten thousand years. When the mountain reaches its final height, when the last slate is stacked and the last bronze bell is placed, the chord will be complete. And then—the elder does not know what will happen then. That knowledge has not yet precipitated from the raw stone of the future.

But it feels you leaving, and the silence goes with you, and the mountain settles back into its long, slow work. The Listeners pick up the threads of their interrupted tending. A young one, the one you saw, pauses on its rounds to eat a fruit, and the glow in its eyes brightens for a moment, and it hums—actually hums, a thin, high sound that might be pleasure or might be something else entirely—before moving on. The dripping continues. The bronze bell sings its low note. And the mountain breathes, and the fungus thinks its slow fungal thoughts, and the Listeners listen, as they have always listened, as they will listen until the final note is played and the world is made whole.
