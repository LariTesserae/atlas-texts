The one who lives here came from the water.

Not exactly—she coalesced from it, condensed out of the saturated air and the moving skin of the floor and the patient chemistry of the vessels, though "chemistry" is a poor word for what happened. It was more like a slow recognition: the water in the hollow, after cycling through the pots and the plants and the gelatinous cultures for long enough, began to remember a shape it had once held. And once it remembered, it could not forget. So it gathered.

She is not human, though if you caught a glimpse of her in periphery you might mistake her for one—a small woman, slight, the proportions almost right but not quite. Her limbs are too long in the forearm and too short in the upper arm, giving her a tucked, compressed look when she stands still, which she does for long periods. Her fingers are the wrong number. Not wrong as in missing or extra, but wrong as in subdivided—each finger branches at the last joint into two finer digits, so that her hands read as a blur of tendrils unless she spreads them flat against a surface. She does this often: presses her palms to the wet stone and holds them there, feeling the temperature, the vibration, the faint seismic hum of the ridge breathing.

Her skin is what happens when you live in total darkness for longer than a species exists. It has no pigment, but it is not pale—pale implies an absence. Her skin is *clear*, translucent in the way the plant stems are translucent, and beneath it the vascular threads are visible, not red or blue but a faint amber, the color of the fluid that moves through her. In bright light she would be almost invisible, a woman-shaped distortion, a lens. Here, in her own darkness, she is visible only because she is wetter than the air around her, and the water on her skin catches the not-quite-phosphorescence from the floor and holds it, so that she glimmers faintly when she moves. She glimmers like a fish in deep water, like something that lives at the boundary where light gives up.

Her face is simplified. Her features are what a face needs to sense the world and nothing more: dark patches where eyes would be, but they are not eyes, they are concentrations of photosensitive cells that register only the presence or absence of the faint glow from below. A slit for breathing, which she does slowly, one inhalation every three or four minutes, and the air she exhales is indistinguishable from the air she inhaled—same temperature, same saturation, no metabolic signature a human nose could detect. A mouth, but she does not eat with it. She eats through her skin. The water that sheets the floor is thick with dissolved sugars, amino acids, the metabolic byproducts of the cultures in the vessels, and she absorbs them directly by lying flat in the shallows, spreading her subdivided fingers and her long toes—toes similarly branched, similarly sensitive—and letting osmosis do what digestion would in something more complex. She feeds while she sleeps, if you can call it sleep. It is more like a trance, a state of reduced differentiation from the water, during which the boundary between her body and the sheet-flow blurs and her edges soften and she becomes, for hours at a time, a slightly thicker part of the liquid cycling through the hollow.

She tends the vessels. This is her purpose, or her instinct, or her compulsion—the line is not clear, even to her, if she thinks in such categories. She lifts each pot, each flask, each stoppered basin, and holds it to her chest, feeling its weight, its warmth, the tiny vibrations of whatever lives inside. Some of the vessels contain cultures like the one that pulses in the open pot—microbial consortia, domesticated over spans of time that have no measure, bred to produce specific compounds, specific flavors, specific subtle adjustments to the water chemistry. Others contain things more structured. In one, a slow-growing fungus that fills the interior with a lattice of hyphae so fine it feels like silk when she dips a finger in. In another, a fermentation of plant matter, the same pale translucent plants that grow from the open mouths, harvested stem by stem with her branching fingers and packed into the pot with a care that borders on ritual. She seals the pots with wax she collects from a particular vessel that produces it—a culture of organisms that excrete long-chain lipids, and she scrapes the wax from the inner walls and warms it in her hands until it softens, then presses it into the mouths of the pots to make them airtight. The wax glints in the dark; she can feel the quality of the seal by the way the glint changes.

She makes the marks on the walls. She is making one now, though "now" is a fluid concept here. She holds a shard of crystal—a splinter of the raw rock, sharper than anything humans make—and she incises a new groove into the stone beside a cluster of spirals. The groove is her record, her memory, because she does not store memory in her body the way an animal does. Her brain, if you can call it that, is distributed through her tissues, a neural net woven into the water that saturates her, and it does not hold onto the past. Each cycle of the water through her body—each slow respiration, each osmotic exchange with the floor—flushes the traces of previous states. She forgets continuously, gently, without loss, the way the water forgets every rock it has ever flowed over. The marks on the wall are her external memory, a record of the cycles she has witnessed: the slow shift in the culture's pulsing rhythm, the incremental change in the sweetness of the water, the appearance of a new pale shoot from a pot that had been sealed for a span she cannot recall. The spirals mean growth. The chevrons mean stability. The parallel lines are a count, but of what, even she no longer knows—she just knows that when a certain vessel warms in a certain way, she adds a line, and the pattern of lines across the wall is a reassurance, a proof that patterns persist even when the memory of them washes away.

She has a name, or what serves as a name. It is not a sound—she has never made a sound, has no vocal apparatus beyond the breathing slit, and the pressed quiet of the hollow has never been broken. Her name is a gesture. She presses her palm flat to her chest, fingers spread so the branching tips radiate outward, and the pattern of her fingers against her translucent skin creates a particular configuration of the amber veins beneath—a shape that, if you could see it, would read as a signature, unique and repeating. When she encounters a vessel that requires her attention, she performs the gesture against the vessel's surface, leaving a faint thermal print that fades in seconds. It is a greeting. An acknowledgment. A way of saying *I am the one who lives here, and you are part of the living here, and we are continuous*.

She is not solitary, though she is the only one of her kind in the hollow. The hollow itself is her companion, her extended body, her gut and her lungs and her skin. The water is her blood. The vessels are her organs—each one performing a function she depends on, each one tended by her hands, each one reciprocating by sustaining the chemical balance that keeps her boundary distinct from the water's. The cultures are her microbiome, her digestion, her immune system. The marks on the wall are her memory. The plants are her hair, her nails, the slow-growing parts of her that reach upward while the rest of her flows downward. And the fissure high above, the narrow rent that opens to the starless sky, is her one connection to a larger world she does not think about—a world that sometimes sends a wisp of mist across the opening, a dilution of the black, a reminder that there is an outside. She does not climb to the fissure. She has no curiosity about it. The hollow is entire, is complete, is exactly as large as a universe needs to be to contain one creature and the ecology that sustains her.

When the mist passes over the fissure, she pauses. She lifts her simplified face to the darkness above, and the photosensitive patches register the change—the darkness becoming fractionally less dark—and she holds still until it passes. Then she returns to her work. Checks the temperature of the water with her palm. Lifts a vessel and feels its warmth. Scrapes a curl of wax. Adds a line to the wall. Breathes in. Breathes out. Becomes indistinguishable from the hum that is not quite a sound, the pulse that is not quite a heartbeat, the long slow equilibrium of a place time has forgotten how to wear.

Her name, in her own language of gesture and thermal print, translates roughly to *the one the water remembered*.
