It’s a slow morning in the capillary city. Mist still clings to the highest steps of the Third Cistern, and the mist-keepers are calling to each other across the channels—soft, two-note whistles that mean *all clear, flow steady, the night’s catch is good.*

I am crouched beside a runnel, watching my own reflection ripple and reassemble, when something skims the surface of the water. A brief shadow, no bigger than a thumbprint, followed by a vanishing ring of ripples. The skin-skiffs are hunting. I stay very still and after a moment I see them—translucent discs, each one little more than a film of living tissue stretched across a faint radial skeleton, pulsing gently to move. They ride the surface tension like it’s solid ground. When they find a mite or a spore, they fold themselves around it, envelop it completely, and then they’re still again, digesting in the thin October light.

You wouldn’t see them unless you knew to look. You wouldn’t see most of us, either.

The builders—the ones who stacked these walls before the walls became the world—are long gone, or never were, depending on who you ask. What lives here now is a community of organisms that doesn’t draw a hard line between animal and infrastructure, between citizen and city. I am one of them. My body is small, low to the ground, built for this place the way the moss is built, the way the walls are built: through slow, wet, patient adaptation. I have six limbs—four for gripping stone, two for delicate work among the channels—and my skin is exactly the grey-green of sunless lichen. My back is slightly domed, humped with a reservoir I can fill and empty at will, a walking cistern. When the rain doesn’t come, I carry water from the deep seams to the wall-gardens in the upper terraces. That’s my role. Everyone here has a role, and the roles are not assigned but grown into, shaped by need and by the quiet conversation the city has been having with itself for time out of mind.

We are called the drip-folk, though we don’t name ourselves often. We speak in small sounds: the click of stone on stone, the wet pop of a tongue against a palate, the whistle of air through a narrow throat. Our language is mostly about water—its volume, its direction, its purity, its temperature. We have forty-three words for the different ways water moves across stone. We have a single word for *thirst*, and we use it rarely, and never lightly.

The sun breaks through a seam in the cloud cover a half-mile to the west, and the whole plateau shifts register. Light glazes the tops of the walls, and the water in the channels brightens from pewter to silver, and for a dozen heartbeats the city is a thing of glancing brilliance before the clouds swallow the light again. I straighten from my crouch and feel the water in my hump adjust, a gentle internal slosh. Time to climb to the High Trenches. The moss-gardeners will be waiting.

I pass the Spiral—a set of walls that curl inward like a dry fountain, just as you described them. In the center, where the spiral reaches its tightest coil, there’s a pool that never empties and never overflows, no matter the season. The old-skimmers live there: three of them, ancient, their bodies flattened and broad, their eyes clouded to pearl. They spend their days half-submerged, dreaming whatever it is that old-skimmers dream. We bring them offerings of the finest capillary moss, which they eat slowly, over days, their mouths moving in a rhythm that matches the drip of water from the overhanging stone. They don’t speak anymore, but we know they’re the reason the pool works. They’re the reason a lot of things work. The city is full of such relationships—beings whose existence is also a function, whose biology is also an architecture. The skin-skiffs clean the water; the lichens fix the stone; the old-skimmers hold the deep hydrology in their dreams. We drip-folk maintain the surface flow. And the city, the city itself—it breathes. It breathes.

I climb a set of natural steps, the stone worn into a staircase by millennia of feet like mine, and emerge onto the High Trenches. Here the walls are taller, waist-high, and the channels are broader, and the moss-gardens spread across every horizontal surface in a dense tapestry of emerald and near-black. The gardeners are already at work: a family of three, their humps small, indicating recent replenishment, their six limbs moving with practiced care as they trim dead fronds and redirect trickles of water with tiny stone sluice-gates. They greet me with a click and a soft double-whistle. The morning’s agenda passes between us in a series of gestures: a blockage in the east channel, a garden that needs rewetting, a new skin-skiff colony that’s settled in the number-four secondary runnel and needs to be gently relocated before breeding season. I nod, and we begin.

This is the life of the capillary city: small tasks, performed slowly, in concert with the weather and the stone and the water. Nothing urgent. Nothing wasted. Every action a form of maintenance, and every maintenance a form of care. The walls are our elders; the water is our charge; the moss is both our food and our child, grown with patience, harvested with respect, eaten raw and sweet in the cool light of morning.

When the mist rolls back in—thick, purposeful, a wall of moisture moving across the tableland like a slow tide—we pause. We always pause for the mist. The world softens. The edges of the walls blur. The sound of trickling water becomes omnidirectional, as if the air itself were a liquid, and we stand in it together, the gardeners and I, our skin drinking in ambient moisture, our humps gradually refilling without any effort at all. This is the easy time. The gift time. Somewhere in the whiteness, an old-skimmer exhales in its sleep, and the pool in the Spiral ripples once, and everything is in balance.

Later, when the mist lifts, I’ll walk the entire western terrace and check the deep seams for irregular flow. I’ll pass a wall where a single spray of pale lichen has grown into a shape that looks, if you tilt your head, like a handprint. I’ll see the small grey water-swifts—what you called lizards, plumper and wetter, slipping in and out of the cracks—and I’ll exchange a slow blink with one of them, an acknowledgment between two species who have shared this same rhythm for so long that we’ve become versions of the same idea. I’ll drink from the channel, cupping water in my two delicate hands, and it will taste of stone and moss and ancient mineral patience.

And when night falls—not darkness but a deepening of the grey, the clouds settling on the plateau like a blanket—I’ll find a hollow in the wall-gardens, a niche that fits my hump and my six limbs, and I’ll settle in among the moss, and I’ll do what we all do: I’ll sleep. And in sleep I’ll dream of water, naturally, because we all dream of water here. The water dreams us back. The city breathes. The old-skimmers dream the hydrology. The skin-skiffs clean the surface of the world.

And in the morning, the mist-keepers will whistle again, and the cycle will continue, and everything that lives in the capillary city will wake and work and tend and drink and sleep, exactly as it has for longer than anyone can usefully imagine. We are the citizens. The walls are our bones. The water is our blood. The quiet is our language, and we speak it fluently.
