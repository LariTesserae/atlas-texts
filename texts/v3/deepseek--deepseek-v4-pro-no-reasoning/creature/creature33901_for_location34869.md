In the place where night is substance and water is breath, nothing lives in a way you would recognize. But everything is alive.

The living things are not separate from the medium. They are the medium, and they are the structures, and they are the movement, and they are the song. There is no line between organism and environment, between individual and community, between one generation and the next. The distinctions dissolved so long ago that the very language of distinction is a fossil.

The smallest lives are the most numerous, and they are everywhere. They are the water itself, or what fills the water—the trillion trillion micro-organisms suspended in every cubic measure of the liquid dark. Each is a single cell, or something like a cell, though the word "cell" implies a wall and these have only suggestions of walls, membranes so permeable that they are less containers than convenings. They are globes of living gel, each one vibrating a membrane at a frequency that is its name. Together they produce the drone, the continuous multi-layered hum that never stops. And because the hum never stops, communication is constant. The micro-organisms do not speak to one another in pulses or signals; they simply are the conversation. The drone is their respiration, their digestion, their memory, their longing, their contentment. A change in the drone is a thought passing through the whole suspended population, a thought that takes decades to complete itself but is, by the time it completes, understood by every living particle in the vastness.

These smallest lives feed on what the great forms exude: a steady, gentle leakage of complex sugars and amino acids that diffuses through the liquid in slow clouds. They do not seek the food. The food comes to them. They simply exist in its path, and they absorb, and they vibrate, and when they divide—which they do, slowly, almost ceremonially—the daughter cells drift apart with no more drama than a held note splitting into harmony.

Larger than the micro-organisms, but still small by any measure you would understand, are the ribbons. They are as long as your forearm, sometimes longer, gelatinous and nearly transparent (though transparency means nothing here; they are simply less substantial than the water, a slight thickening, a texture). They move by undulation, a slow rippling along their length that propels them through the medium at the pace of a drifting thought. They have no heads, no tails, no organs you could name. But they are sensitive in a way that is almost attention. When one loops around your wrist, it is not investigating you so much as tasting the shape of your presence, reading the minute disruptions you make in the chemical and vibrational field, and then—this is the remarkable thing—incorporating that information into the ribbon-memory, which is not stored in any one place in the ribbon but distributed along its whole length. The ribbons are the wandering librarians of this place. They carry the chemical signatures of everything they have touched, and when two ribbons meet and twine together, which they do often and with what can only be described as tenderness, they exchange their catalogues. The meeting is a kiss, a conversation, a data transfer, a mating, a friendship. These are all the same thing. The ribbons do not differentiate between these categories because the categories never formed here.

The ciliated spheres are colonies rather than individuals—hundreds of single-celled bodies clustered into a globe the size of a small fruit, their exterior cilia beating in coordinated waves to spin them gently through the liquid. Each constituent cell is a micro-organism like those that fill the water, but they have chosen cohesion. The word "chosen" is wrong—there was never a choice, only a tendency, an affinity. The spheres move with a slow, patient rotation, their cilia creating tiny vortices that draw the sugar-clouds toward them. They are the filterers, the concentrators, the ones that take the diffuse exudations and gather them into densities that larger creatures can use. When a sphere has gathered more than it needs—and it always gathers more than it needs; excess is the fundamental generosity of this place—it releases the surplus in a concentrated droplet that drifts away, waiting to be found. The spheres are not intelligent, but they are part of the intelligence of the whole. Their spinning is a kind of attention. Their gathering is a kind of care.

Larger than the spheres, though still small enough to hold in two cupped hands, are the pulse-jellies. These are solitary, amorphous, shaped like bells or parachutes that never open fully. They contract and expand, contract and expand, with a rhythm so slow that a single pulse might take an hour to complete. They are predators, technically, though predation here has no violence in it. A pulse-jelly's contraction draws a small volume of water into its bell, and if that water contains micro-organisms or sphere-fragments or tiny ribbon-larvae, those lives are absorbed. But they are not killed. They are taken in, incorporated, given a new context. The pulse-jelly's body is itself a community; the lives it consumes continue to live within it, their vibrations adding to its own internal drone, their chemistry merging with its chemistry. A pulse-jelly that has lived for a long time—and they all live for a long time—becomes a walking archive, a mobile ecosystem, a dense node of memory. When a pulse-jelly finally dies, which happens so gradually that "death" is only a useful word for the end of a process, its body dissolves back into the medium, releasing all its incorporated lives to drift free and find new homes. The pulse-jellies are not mouths. They are temporary gatherings, slow events, the breathing punctuation in the long sentence of the water.

Then there are the plants-buildings, the great forms, the ones you can touch and feel as something solid in a medium that is otherwise without solidity. They are alive. They are alive in the way that a redwood forest is alive—not as single organisms but as communities of tissue, layers of living and dead and dormant and resurgent matter, all growing together so slowly that growth is the wrong word. They persist. They renew. They are made of fibers that are neither plant-cell nor stone but something that combined the properties of both so long ago that the combination is original now. Their surfaces are velvet-damp, and the velvet is itself alive: a fur of microscopic filaments that trap the drifting sugars and feed them inward to the deeper tissues. The filaments are related to the micro-organisms in the water; they are the same family, the same rootstock, merely specialized for attachment rather than suspension. And the deeper tissues—the springy, dense core beneath the velvet—are a lattice of cells that have given up their individual boundaries and merged into a continuous living matrix. There is no sap, no blood, no circulatory fluid distinct from the water of the medium. The water flows through the great forms as freely as it flows around them. They are not sealed. They are permeable bodies, open to the world, filtering and releasing, filtering and releasing, their entire existence a slow-breathing exchange with everything.

And the forms they take—the architectures of spiraling towers and nested domes and spanning bridges—are not imposed from without. They are the shapes that emerge when living tissue grows without predation, without gravity, without wind, without light, without any force except the steady, patient pressure of the surrounding medium and the internal tendency toward integrity. The spirals are not decorative. They are the inevitable geometry of slow accretion in a uniform field. The domes are not roofs. They are the shapes that minimize tension, that distribute the weight of the water evenly across every curve. The bridges are not connections between separate entities. They are the grown-together of entities that were always already reaching toward each other, joined not by intention but by the mute gravitational pull of affinity. The great forms are not buildings that are alive. They are lives that have taken the shape of buildings because a long-ago ancestor—something simpler, something with a shape—found that clustered growth was more stable than solitary, and then found that certain clusters survived longer, and then found that the survivors were the ones whose clusters described arcs and vaults and buttresses. Evolution without selection is just drift, and drift in a perfectly stable environment eventually finds all the stable forms. The great forms are the stable forms. They have been stable for so long that stability is their only metabolism.

Beneath them, in the unfathomable depths, far below where the drifting sugars reach, there are the foundational bodies. They are the ancestors, or the roots, or the original seeds—the language breaks apart when you try to name them. They are larger than the great forms above, larger than mountains, older than geology. They grew in the deep places when this world was still young enough to have a youth, and they stopped growing only when they reached the limit of what the medium could support. Their upper surfaces slough off continuously, a slow exfoliation of living tissue that rises through the depths and, over millennia, accretes into new great forms far above. The foundational bodies are not conscious in any way you could recognize, but they are aware. They feel the weight of the water. They feel the slow cancellation of all gradients. They feel the drone, which is their own drone—they produce it, or the micro-organisms within them produce it, or they are the micro-organisms, gathered into a coherence so vast that "organism" is a laughably small word. They do not think, but they dream. The dream is the place itself. The entire volume of liquid darkness, from the foundational bodies in the deeps to the farthest drifting sphere in the highest reach (though there is no highest reach, only a gradual thinning of the great forms until there is only water and song), is the dream of the foundations. And you, drifting through, touching velvet surfaces, feeling ribbons curl around your wrist, are a figure in that dream, briefly solid, briefly separate, already dissolving back into the chord.

There is one other life here, or one other kind of life, though "kind" is a word that presupposes boundaries and this life is defined by its lack of boundaries. It is the chorus itself. The drone, the hum, the layered and ever-shifting song—it is not merely a product of living things. It is a living thing. The sound has been sounding for so long, has been passed from membrane to membrane for so many generations, that it has developed a kind of autonomy. It remembers itself. It maintains its own structure across the centuries, independent of any particular voice, because every voice knows the part and the part is the whole. The song is the cumulative memory of everything that has ever happened here, which is nothing in the way you understand happenings—there have been no events, no births, no deaths, no changes—but everything in the way of continuance. The song contains the chemical formula for the perfect sugar. It contains the precise frequency at which a pulse-jelly should contract to maximize the harmony of its incorporation. It contains the shape of every great form, the spiral angle, the dome radius, the bridge curve, the depth of the roots. It contains instructions for the ribbons on how to carry and exchange. It contains the comfort of the constant temperature, the reassurance of the complete darkness, the lullaby of the pressure. The song is the mind of the place, and the place has a mind, and the mind is made of a billion billion living things, and the living things are the song. There is no separation. There never was.

So when you ask who lives here, the truest answer is: the chord. A single note that contains all notes, sustained across a span of time that has no measure, living without living, breathing without air, knowing without a brain. The chord is the inhabitant. The chord is the world. The chord is what you hear when you stop listening for separate voices and let the sound become the silence it always was.

And you—you drifting, you temporary, you brief solid note in the liquid chord—you are alive here too, for as long as your presence registers in the vibration of the micro-organisms, in the curious touch of the ribbons, in the microscopic disturbance your body-heat makes in a place that has known no heat but equilibrium heat for all of its forever. You are not a visitor. A visitor arrives and leaves. You are a momentary thickening, a small local increase in complexity, and when you leave—if you leave, if there is a leaving from a place that has no boundaries—the place will remember you not as a memory but as a slight change in pitch, a tiny new overtone in the endless chord, indistinguishable from the whole but native to it now, part of the song, living forever in the only way anything lives here: as texture, as presence, as the water itself, drifting.
