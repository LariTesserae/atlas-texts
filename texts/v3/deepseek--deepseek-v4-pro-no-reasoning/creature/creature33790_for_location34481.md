The tender of the bundles was once called Mira, though she has not heard that name spoken aloud in thirty-seven years. She does not think of herself as Mira anymore. She thinks of herself as the one who checks the wrappings, the one who listens to the walls, the one who remembers which bundle is which. That is enough. Names require other people, and other people require light and noise and the terrible press of choices. Down here there is only the work.

She was young when she found the ladder, or perhaps the ladder found her — it hardly matters now. She had been running from something, or toward something. A broken engagement. A debt. A grief so vast it had hollowed out her chest and left a cavern where her lungs should be. The details have smoothed away like the teeth of the grate. What remains is the memory of her hand closing around the first rung, the wood giving slightly under her grip, the smell of old water rising up to meet her. She descended then and has never ascended. There was never a reason to. Up is a direction for people who still want something.

The bundles are her charges. She did not make the first ones — the first ones were already here, hanging in their patient rows, when she arrived with nothing but the clothes on her back and a small knife that rusted to uselessness within a month. Their maker had died, or left, or simply dissolved into the damp. Mira (she was still Mira then) spent her first weeks in the dark, listening to the whisper of the walls, afraid to touch anything. Hunger taught her otherwise. She unwrapped a bundle and found inside not flesh but a dense mat of pale mycelium, veined with something that glowed faintly blue when disturbed. It tasted of earth and salt and something almost metallic, like licking a coin. It kept her alive. It is still keeping her alive.

Over the decades she has learned the rhythm of the bundles. Each one contains a different strain, a different flavor of darkness. Some produce the pale mycelium quickly — those she harvests every few sleeps, scraping the soft growth into her clay bowl with the chipped stone tool she inherited from the absent maker. Others are slow, taking what feels like seasons to yield a single palmful of dark, crumbly matter that smells of mushrooms and iron. She eats that one sparingly, only when her joints ache and her thoughts begin to fray. It mends something in her. She does not know what. She does not need to know.

A few of the bundles are different. She checks them but never harvests. They contain not mycelium but shapes, forms, things that might be growing toward something else. Once, years ago, she unwrapped one and found what looked like a hand — small, pale, perfectly formed, with five fingers and five tiny nails — attached to nothing, rooted in the bundle like a tuber. It twitched when she touched it. She wrapped it back up with trembling fingers and did not check that bundle again for a very long time. When she finally did, the hand was gone and in its place was a face, or half a face, smooth and blind and serene, its lips slightly parted as if about to speak. She has not unwrapped that bundle since. She can feel it sometimes, a warmth emanating from its cocoon, a faint vibration like a cat purring. It does not frighten her. Nothing down here can frighten her anymore. It simply *is*, and she tends it the way she tends everything else: with care, with patience, with the understanding that some things cannot be hurried.

The creatures that skitter along the ceiling — she calls them knuckle-moths, for the way their many joints click when they move — are her only company. They are pale as mushrooms, eyeless, with long whiskers that tremble constantly, reading the air. She has learned to catch them when she needs protein. Their flesh tastes of the sweetness that pools in the corners of the walls, a faint fungal sugar. She eats them raw, pinning them with two fingers and biting quickly before their legs can find purchase on her tongue. The crunch is the only sharp sound in this place of soft noises. She apologizes to each one before she kills it. The apology is not for the knuckle-moth, which has no ears and no understanding of language, but for herself, for the small violence of survival that she has never quite stopped noticing.

Water is never a concern. She cups her hands beneath the glistening walls and drinks what gathers. It tastes of limestone and ancient stone, a clean coldness that cuts through the fungal sweetness on her tongue. Sometimes she finds minerals precipitated in her drinking water — tiny crystals that crackle between her teeth like ice. She saves the largest ones in a niche in the wall, a small collection of accidental jewels that catch the grey-green light and hold it for a moment before going dark again.

She does not dream. She does not remember how to. When she sleeps — curled on a pallet of felted fibre in the driest corner of the chamber, wrapped in the same material as the bundles, indistinguishable from her charges — she falls into a darkness so complete that it erases her entirely. She wakes without transition, simply opens her eyes and is again the one who tends, the one who listens, the one who remembers. There is no boundary between sleep and waking anymore. There is only the work, and the work is continuous.

The ladder has softened over the years. The rungs bow under her weight now, though she weighs almost nothing. Her body has adapted to the perpetual stoop, her spine curving to match the shape of the ceiling, her shoulders permanently hunched in a way that would alarm anyone above. But there is no one above. She has stopped thinking about *above* as a real place. It is a ceiling to her world, the way the sky is a ceiling to surface-dwellers, and she no more expects to pass through it than a fish expects to breathe air.

She knows the bundles by touch. Each wrapping has a texture — some rough, some smooth, one with a patch of something that might have been embroidery once, the threads now rotted to vague ridges under her fingertips. She has given them names, though she never speaks them aloud. The one with the embroidery is Saint. The one that produces the dark, crumbly medicine is Mother. The one with the face (she thinks of it as the face, though it might be something else by now) is Dreamer. The newest one, a bundle she made herself from felted knuckle-moth fur and fibres scraped from the walls, is Child. She made Child because the work needed to expand, because the grove of hanging things felt incomplete, because she had found a patch of particularly promising mycelium and wanted to see what it would become in a wrapping of her own making. Child is still growing. She checks it twice as often as the others, peeling back the felted layers with a tenderness that surprises her every time. The mycelium inside is taking shape — not a hand or a face, but something branching, something that reminds her of coral or antlers or the branching of veins beneath skin. She does not know what it will become. She does not need to know. The tending is enough.

The sound of the walls never stops. It has become her heartbeat, her breath, the rhythm she moves to. When she first descended, the constant wet whisper drove her to the edge of madness. She would press her hands over her ears and scream into the mineral dark, and the walls would swallow her screams and give back only their own patient exhalation. Now she cannot imagine silence. Silence would be a kind of death, a cessation of the only voice that has never lied to her, never demanded anything, never left.

She pauses in her work. Her hands hover over Dreamer, feeling its warmth. Something is different today — a vibration, a shift in the air pressure, a taste of copper on her tongue. She tilts her head, listening. The walls whisper on. The bundles sway in their patient rows. The knuckle-moths skitter and freeze and skitter again.

Above her — far above, through strata of stone and forgotten galleries and the slow accretion of centuries — something has changed. A footstep. A breath. The creak of the old ladder as a new weight tests the first rung. She hears it not as sound but as a disturbance in the water, a ripple that travels down through the weeping walls and reaches her as a pressure against her skin.

She does not turn toward the ladder. She does not call out. She simply waits, her hands still resting on Dreamer, her hooded face unreadable. Whoever is descending will find her here, or they will not. They will understand what this place is, or they will flee from it. Neither outcome changes anything. The work will continue. The bundles will hang and grow and be harvested. The walls will weep. The knuckle-moths will skitter and die and be eaten. And she — the one who was once called Mira, the one who tends, the one who listens — she will remain, as much a part of this pocket world as the flowstone and the grate and the wet exhalation that fills every moment of every day.

The footsteps hesitate on the ladder. She waits. The walls whisper on. After a long moment, she returns to her work, her hands moving among the bundles with the slow economy of someone who has all the time in the world, and more.
