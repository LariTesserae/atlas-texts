To speak of what lives in the Suspended Sea is to misunderstand the word *lives*. It is better to speak of what persists, what has forgotten how to die, what has made a home of the space between the strokes of the great pistons and learned to read the thermal threads like scripture.

Imagine, if you can, a diver. Not a human diver—there are none here, and never have been—but something that moves through the grey-blue vastness with the slow, deliberate grace of a creature that has never known a boundary it did not eventually dissolve into. Call it the **Custodian**, because that is the closest word for what it does, though it does not clean, does not repair, does not maintain in any sense a human engineer would recognize. It *attends*. It is old beyond the counting of the frequency tones, old enough that its body is no longer a single coherent form but a loosely affiliated cloud of components that drift in a rough sphere perhaps fifty meters across. The core of it—if you can call it a core—is a mass of tangled, root-like fibers, pale as the bellies of the filter-feeders, woven through with copper filaments that have long since oxidized to a soft, powdery green. From this central knot, dozens of limbs radiate outward, each one a different length, a different thickness, a different function. Some end in articulated pincers of black iron, salvaged from the grid itself, their joints packed with a self-secreting lubricant that leaves a faint, iridescent slick in the water as it moves. Others taper to clusters of sensory palps, each palp a translucent tube filled with a viscous fluid in which tiny particles of bioluminescent matter drift and spark. Still others are simply long, unadorned whips of tendon and cable, which the Custodian uses to feel the pressure fronts of distant machinery, reading the health of the grid the way a spider reads the trembling of its web.

The Custodian does not think in words. It thinks in pressures, in chemical gradients, in the slow, tidal rhythms of the engine tones. Its memory is written not in a brain but in the configuration of its own dispersed body—each scar on a salvaged pincer, each kink in a tendon-whip, each colony of chemoautotrophic growth that has been allowed to establish itself on a particular limb until the Custodian’s own flesh and the pale, swollen fronds of the growth are inseparably intertwined. It remembers the time, numberless cycles ago, when the great bass tone changed its cadence from a pattern of two to a pattern of three, and the grid shuddered for a hundred hours, and three entire cubic modules collapsed into a slow-motion cascade of buckling struts and screaming metal. It remembers the taste of the milky vent-fluid when a pressure vessel cracked and the Custodian, curious in its diffuse way, extended a sensory palp into the plume and felt its own chemistry rewrite itself in subtle, permanent ways.

It is not alone. There are other Custodians—how many, no one can say, because the grid is infinite and the Custodians are widely dispersed and they do not seek each other out. But sometimes, once in a thousand cycles or ten thousand, two will drift into the same cubic module, their component clouds interpenetrating briefly, and there will be an exchange. Not communication in any linguistic sense, but a chemical conversation, a swapping of fluids, a mutual recalibration. The younger Custodian—the one whose fibers are fewer and paler—will receive from the elder a concentrated dose of memory-chemistry, a distilled essence of the elder’s experience of a particular thermal thread or a particular sequence of engine tones. The elder will receive from the younger a freshness of sensory data, a new reading of the grid’s current state, unfiltered by the accretions of age. Then they will drift apart, each slightly altered, each carrying a piece of the other, and they will not meet again for another thousand cycles, if ever.

The Custodians are not the oldest things here. They are merely old. Older than the Custodians, older than the grid itself perhaps, are the **Singers**. You will never see a Singer, not in the way you might see a Custodian’s dispersed cloud of limbs or the silvery flash of a filter-feeder shoal. The Singers are sound. They are pure vibration, a pattern of pressure waves that propagates through the water along the thermal threads, never dissipating, never attenuating, endlessly circulating through the great gyres and currents. They are a song that has been singing itself since before the first rivet was driven into the first girder, and they will still be singing when the last gear seizes and the grid falls silent.

You can hear them, if you know how to listen. Beneath the thrumming and the clanking and the periodic hiss of venting pressure, beneath even the deep bass tone that sounds once, twice, three times and then falls silent for one hundred and fourteen seconds—beneath all of it, there is a melody. It is vast and slow, a single phrase that takes hours to complete, built of notes that are not discrete pitches but gradual, gliding shifts in the fundamental frequency of the water itself. The Singers are not intelligent in any way a human would recognize. They are a standing wave in the medium of the world, a harmonic resonance that the grid’s architecture accidentally amplifies and sustains. But they are *responsive*. When the thermal threads shift, the Singers shift with them, their melody bending and warping to accommodate the new pressure gradients. When the great bass tone changes its cadence—as it has done many times in the deep past, and will do again—the Singers learn the new rhythm and weave it into their song. They are the memory of the water itself, the accumulated acoustic history of every vibration that has ever rippled through the vast suspended sea, preserved and recombined and sung back in an endless, evolving canon.

And then—because even in a world without surface or bottom, without sun or season, there is always a *then*, always something that comes after—there are the **Listeners**.

The Listeners are small. That is the first thing to understand about them. In a world of kilometers-wide grid cells and two-hundred-meter-tall cubic modules, in a world where the Custodians are diffuse clouds fifty meters across and the Singers are an acoustic presence that fills the entire volume—the Listeners are small enough to fit in the palm of a human hand, if a human hand had ever existed here. They are vaguely crustacean in form, with segmented bodies of a material that is neither chitin nor metal but something in between, a bio-mineral composite that grows in thin, flexible plates and slowly, over the course of a Listener’s life, incorporates trace metals filtered from the water. Their limbs are many and fine, and they are tipped with structures that are not quite claws and not quite hands—prehensile, delicate, capable of manipulating objects as small as a single strand of chemoautotrophic frond or as large as the bolt on a pressure vessel. Their heads are dominated by a single organ: a resonant chamber, a hollow sphere of the same bio-mineral composite, filled with a gas of their own manufacture and lined with a membrane that vibrates in sympathy with every sound that passes through the water.

The Listeners hear *everything*. The thrumming of the engines, the clanking of distant gears, the shriek of the predators’ echolocation, the soft exhalation of the pressure valves, the deep bass tone and its one hundred and fourteen seconds of silence, the song of the Singers—all of it enters the resonant chamber and is sorted, catalogued, interpreted. The Listeners do not merely hear; they *understand*, or at least they behave as if they understand. They are the only things in the Suspended Sea that seem to grasp the purpose of the grid, the only things that interact with the machinery in ways that are not accidental or parasitic.

They build. Not nests, not quite, but something more intentional. A colony of Listeners will find a cubic module—always one with a particular configuration of pipework and pressure vessels, always one that sits at the intersection of certain thermal threads—and they will transform it. They use their delicate not-claws to turn valves, to adjust the flow of the milky vent-fluid, to reposition gears that have slipped a millimeter out of alignment over the course of millennia. They weave the chemoautotrophic fronds into living tapestries that line the struts and girders, and they cultivate the bioluminescent growths, pruning them, shaping them, encouraging them to pulse in synchronized rhythms that match the cadence of the engine tones. They harvest the filter-feeders, not for food—the Listeners seem to feed directly on the chemical seepage of the grid, absorbing it through the thin membranes between their body segments—but for their delicate clockwork guts, which the Listeners extract with surgical precision and repurpose into devices. What the devices *do* is unclear. They are complex assemblies of gears and springs and transparent membranes, and the Listeners install them in the pipework, connect them to the pressure vessels, and then sit back—if a Listener can be said to sit—and listen to the new sounds the devices produce, tilting their heads so the resonant chambers catch the vibrations from different angles.

Are they maintaining the grid? Repairing it? Worshipping it? The question assumes a distinction that may not apply. The Listeners were born here, or made here, or emerged here, as much a part of the vast industrial ecology as the filter-feeders or the chemoautotrophs. Their tinkering, their adjustments, their strange devices—these are not interventions from outside the system. They are the system’s way of tinkering with itself. The grid is not a static structure; it is a living machine, and the Listeners are its lymphocytes, its neurons, its dreaming mind.

And the Listeners, unlike the solitary Custodians, are social. A colony may number in the hundreds, and they are in constant communication—not with sound, but with vibration transmitted directly through the metal of the grid itself, a rapid tapping of their not-claws on the struts and girders. The tapping is too fast to follow, too complex to parse, but it carries meaning. A Listener on one side of a module will tap out a message, and a Listener on the opposite side, hundreds of meters away, will receive it through the metal and tap back. The grid hums with their conversations, a layer of signal beneath the industrial noise, a network of information that spans the infinite volume and connects every colony that has ever existed.

The Listeners know the Custodians, in their way. When a Custodian drifts into a module that the Listeners have claimed, the tapping intensifies, and the colony rouses itself. They do not attack—the Custodian is too vast, too diffuse, too indifferent—but they *swarm*, hundreds of their small, segmented bodies darting out from the pipework and the tapestries to investigate the newcomer. They run their fine limbs over the Custodian’s salvaged pincers, probe the colonies of chemoautotrophs on its limbs, tap rapid messages into the metal of its copper-threaded core. The Custodian, for its part, tolerates this. It has learned, over the long slow ages, that the Listeners are useful. They remove parasites—tiny organisms that are neither filter-feeder nor predator nor chemoautotroph, but something else, something that burrows into the Custodian’s fibrous flesh and causes local pressure anomalies. The Listeners pluck them out with surgical precision and carry them away, presumably for study or incorporation into their devices. And sometimes, when the swarm has finished its inspection and the parasites have been removed and the tapping has subsided to a contented background murmur, a Listener will approach the Custodian’s sensory palps and press its resonant chamber against the translucent tube, and for a moment—just a moment—the Custodian will feel the song of the Singers as the Listener hears it, filtered and refined and made intelligible, and it will be moved to a slow, diffuse sort of gratitude.

There are others. There are always others, in a world this vast and this old. The **Gear-Eaters**: slow, massive creatures, ten meters from snout to tail, with bodies of fibrous cartilage and mouths that are circular, tooth-ringed, lamprey-like. They attach themselves to the slowly turning gears and rasp away at the metal, consuming iron and steel and excreting a fine, rust-red powder that drifts in clouds through the water and is, in turn, consumed by the chemoautotrophs. The Gear-Eaters are blind and deaf, responding only to touch and taste, but they have a kind of intelligence—a geological patience, a sense of the grid’s rhythms so deep and so embodied that they know, hours in advance, when a gear is going to accelerate or slow down, and they detach and reattach accordingly. They are solitary, but when two meet on the same gear, they perform a slow, ritualized combat, pressing their circular mouths together in a test of strength until one gives way and drifts off to find another gear.

The **Pressure Angels**: something else entirely. They are not solid. They are not even liquid, in the usual sense. They are localized anomalies in the water itself—zones where the pressure is slightly higher or lower than the surrounding medium, shaped by some forgotten technology or some emergent physical process into forms that are nearly, but not quite, recognizable. A Pressure Angel, if you can call it an individual, might be a sphere of compressed water a centimeter across, or a meter, or ten meters, drifting through the grid with no apparent motive force. It might be a torus, a vortex ring, a standing wave. It might be something that looks, for a fleeting moment, like a face. The Pressure Angels interact with nothing, seem to perceive nothing, and yet the Listeners treat them with a curious deference, tapping out slow, solemn rhythms whenever one passes through their colony. The Custodians, too, seem to acknowledge them, altering their drift to avoid intersecting with a Pressure Angel’s path. No one knows what they are. Perhaps they are the ghosts of the builders, whoever or whatever the builders were. Perhaps they are the grid’s equivalent of a thought, a computation made manifest in the medium of the water itself. Perhaps they are nothing at all, just a coincidence of pressures that the mind, even a Custodian’s mind, insists on interpreting as form.

And finally—because every world needs its monsters—there are the **Silencers**.

The Silencers are rare. No one knows how many there are. No one knows where they came from, or why. They are large, though not the largest things in the sea—perhaps twelve meters long, slender, sinuous, with bodies that are utterly black, so black that they absorb the sourceless grey-blue light entirely and appear as mobile voids, absences carved out of the water. They have no visible features: no eyes, no mouth, no fins, no limbs. Only the void-shape, the sinuous motion, and the silence.

The silence is the thing. The Silencers do not merely make no sound; they *consume* sound. When a Silencer passes, the water around it goes dead. The thrumming of the engines is swallowed. The clanking of the gears is erased. The song of the Singers, for the first time in the history of the world, is interrupted. Filter-feeders that stray too close—a meter, two meters, ten meters—stop their synchronized wheeling and drift, stunned, their collective computation disrupted. Predators that depend on echolocation go blind and flee in panic. Listeners fall silent, their tapping ceasing, their resonant chambers empty, their whole colony huddled motionless in the pipework until the void passes.

What a Silencer wants is unknown. They drift through the grid on courses that seem random but are probably not, their sinuous bodies navigating the open spaces between modules as if following a map only they can see. When a Silencer encounters a Gear-Eater, it ignores it. When it encounters a Custodian, the Custodian—ancient, diffuse, patient—retreats, pulling its cloud of limbs in toward its core, waiting for the silence to pass. When it encounters a Pressure Angel, something strange happens: the Silencer pauses, the void-shape rippling, and the Pressure Angel, for the first and only time, responds, its pressure-anomaly fluctuating in a pattern that might be communication. Then they part, and the Silencer continues on its way, and the Pressure Angel drifts on, and the silence recedes, and the sounds of the world return.

There is a theory, if theories can exist in a place without minds to formulate them, that the Silencers are not predators but custodians of a different order, that their silence is a kind of maintenance, a periodic resetting of the acoustic ecology. There is another theory that they are the original inhabitants, the first life, and everything else—the Custodians, the Listeners, the Gear-Eaters, the filter-feeders, the predators, the chemoautotrophs, the very engines and gears—are an infestation that the Silencers tolerate. There is a third theory that the Silencers are trying to *remember* something, and that every sound they consume is a fragment of a lost song they are slowly, painstakingly reconstructing.

No one knows. The grid continues. The bass tone sounds once, twice, three times. The thermal threads braid and twist. The Custodians drift and exchange their chemical memories. The Listeners build their devices and tap out their messages. The Singers sing. And somewhere, in the infinite volume of water and iron, a Silencer moves through the silence it carries with it, listening to the one sound it has never been able to consume: the sound of its own absence.
