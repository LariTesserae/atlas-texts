The people are called the Tenders, if they would think to name themselves, which they rarely do. They are human in shape but not quite in substance — as if the basalt’s unfinished cooling extended to them, as if they too are still deciding what to be. Their skin is gray, not the gray of ash or death but the gray of the stone itself, dark and faintly glossy in certain lights, with a surface that seems to absorb rather than reflect. When they stand still against the carved walls, they vanish, their bodies reading as shadow, as negative space, as just another fold in the dark glass.

Their faces are long and narrow, with high cheekbones that catch the light like the sharp edges of the basalt ridges. Their eyes are large and set wide, the irises a pale mineral green that matches the vegetation outside, the pupils vertical like cracks in cooling rock. They blink slowly, deliberately, once every thirty seconds or so, and in the low light of the deeper rooms their eyes seem to glow faintly, a trick of the tapetum lucidum that lines the backs of their retinas — an adaptation for the dim corridors and the windowless chambers where they spend much of their time. Their ears are small and set flat against their skulls, the cartilage thin and flexible, able to fold down to block the rare sharp sound but more often simply resting, unused. They hear through their bones more than through the air. The basalt carries vibration well, and a Tender resting their palm against a wall can sense the rhythm of a companion’s heartbeat three rooms away, the scuttle of tiny feet in a nearby niche, the slow creak of the stone expanding in the morning warmth.

Their mouths are small, their lips thin and pale, and they speak rarely not because they have nothing to say but because their speech is not primarily auditory. They communicate through posture, through the angle of their long fingers, through the way they position their bodies in relation to the light. When they do vocalize, the sounds are low and brief — not words so much as tonal markers, a single note that means *here*, another that means *enough*, a soft descending hum that means *the light is changing*. Their language, if it can be called that, has no grammar of time. There is no past tense, no future. There is only what is present: the warmth, the stone, the green, the bodies moving through the rooms. They have no names. They recognize each other by the pattern of their movement, the particular way one Tender’s shoulders curve when they bend to tend the plants, the subtle difference in how another’s fingers spread against a wall to listen. A Tender knows their companions the way a gardener knows individual plants — not by label but by the accumulated memory of thousands of small interactions, the shape of their presence in the stone.

They are not born in any recognizable sense. New Tenders emerge from the deepest rooms, the ones where the pale leafless plants grow in the dark. No one speaks of this process, and no one observes it directly — to be present at an emergence is considered deeply inappropriate, a kind of trespass against the one who is becoming. The community simply knows when a new presence is forming. There is a shift in the rhythm of the stone, a subtle change in the way the small fauna behave, a particular stillness that settles over the carved corridors. The Tenders who tend the deep chambers leave offerings at the threshold — crushed leaves, small piles of mineral dust, a woven mat of plant fiber — and then they withdraw. Days pass. Sometimes weeks. And then one morning a new Tender appears in the common rooms, fully grown, their gray skin still soft at the joints, their eyes still adjusting to the light. They know nothing, and they know everything. They carry no memories of a previous self, but they understand the stone, the plants, the quiet, as if they have always been here. The other Tenders recognize them immediately — not by face, which will take months to settle into its final shape, but by the particular quality of their stillness, the way they fit into the negative space the community has been holding open for them.

New Tenders spend their first months in the outermost rooms, where the light is brightest and the green spills in through the carved openings. They learn the plants first — the feel of the dry leaves crumbling under their fingers, the faint mineral taste of the sap, the way the growth shifts over weeks in response to the sun. They learn the small fauna, the leaf-like creatures that rest on their palms without fear, the tiny soft-footed things that run up their arms and nestle in the hollow of their collarbones. They learn to carve, not by instruction but by imitation, watching the older Tenders work and then taking up a stone tool and finding that their hands already know the motion, the angle, the weight. The basalt yields to them in a way it yields to nothing else — not easily, but willingly, as if the stone recognizes its own. The carvings they make are extensions of the natural cracks, following the geometry of the cooling rock, amplifying what is already there rather than imposing a new order.

Their bodies are cool to the touch, several degrees below the ambient temperature, and they draw warmth from the stone rather than generating it internally. This is why they spend their sleeping hours pressed against the basalt walls, curled into niches carved exactly to their dimensions, their skin drinking heat from the rock that has been storing sunlight all day. Their metabolism is slow, their heartbeat a deep, infrequent thud that a human ear would strain to detect — perhaps twelve beats per minute at rest, quickening only when they carve, when they knead the crushed leaves into the feeding troughs, when they press their palms to a chamber wall and feel a distant vibration that means something is wrong.

They do not eat, exactly. They consume the mineral sap of the plants, a thick gray paste they extract by crushing the leaves and stems in stone mortars and then letting the pulp ferment in carved basins for several days. The paste is consumed sparingly, a small daub on the tongue once every few days, and it serves not so much as fuel but as maintenance — a way of replenishing the trace elements their bodies lose to the stone over time. The fermentation process is the closest thing the Tenders have to a ritual. The basins are kept in a specific chamber, cooler than the rest of the settlement, and the Tender responsible for them visits every day to turn the pulp with a carved stone paddle, to check the consistency, to add a pinch of mineral dust scraped from a particular vein in the deepest corridor. The smell of the fermenting sap is the only strong odor in the settlement — a rich, earthy, almost metallic scent that drifts through the corridors when the chamber door is opened and then fades quickly in the dry air.

The Tenders do not reproduce, do not die. The oldest among them — and they are all of an age, really, formed in the same slow emergence over the past few centuries — show no signs of senescence. Their skin does not wrinkle; their movements do not slow. Occasionally, a Tender will simply stop. One moment they are present, a familiar shape moving through the rooms, and the next moment they are gone, and the stone where they last stood is slightly warmer than before. No body remains, no trace. The other Tenders sense the absence immediately, like a room gone suddenly dark. They pause in their tasks, press their palms to the walls, and wait. After a time — hours, sometimes a full day — a new hum begins in the deep chambers, and the community knows that a new Tender will soon emerge. There is no grief. There is no name to mourn, no memory to preserve beyond the habits of movement and the particular way the absent one carved their niche. The new Tender will not replace the old, but the rhythms of the community will adjust, the pattern of presence shifting to fill the new absence.

Their relationship with the small fauna is one of quiet reciprocity. The creatures live in the walls, in the niches carved just for them, in the dense green that mats the plateau outside. The Tenders do not domesticate them in any meaningful sense — the creatures come and go as they please, following rhythms of their own — but certain rooms are known to be gathering places, and the Tenders visit them daily to leave offerings of crushed leaves and stone dust. The creatures, in turn, keep the vegetation from overtaking the carved rooms entirely. Their small bodies move through the green, pruning, disturbing, shifting the growth in subtle ways that the Tenders could never achieve with their own hands. Some of the smaller creatures — the thumb-sized ones with the leaf-like bodies — are gently harvested for their sap, a clearer, thinner fluid that the Tenders use to polish the walls of the deep chambers. The harvest does not kill them. A Tender will hold one in their palm, stroke its dorsal surface with a fingertip until it secretes a bead of clear fluid, and then release it back into the greenery. The creatures seem to understand the exchange, presenting themselves for harvest at certain times of year, climbing onto the Tenders’ hands without being asked.

The settlement itself is not named. The Tenders have no word for it, no way of distinguishing it from the plateau, the green, the basalt. It simply is. The rooms are arranged in a rough spiral around the central core of the basalt plug, following a natural fracture that winds upward through the stone. The outer chambers, where the light reaches and the green spills in, are the communal spaces: the carving workshops, the fermentation room, the chambers where the small fauna are fed and harvested. The inner chambers, accessible only through narrow passages that twist and double back on themselves, are the sleeping niches, each carved to fit a single Tender. These rooms are no larger than the body they accommodate, smooth-walled and warm, with a single small opening to admit air and the faint rustle of the green outside. And beyond the sleeping niches, deeper still, are the rooms that no one speaks of — the smooth, dark chambers where the pale plants grow, and where new Tenders emerge from the stone in a process that is not birth and not death but something else entirely.

The Tenders have no art, no decoration, no ornament. The carving itself is their only expression, and it is functional, precise, never excessive. A doorway is carved exactly large enough to admit a single Tender; a niche is shaped exactly to the contours of the small creature that will rest there. There are no markings on the walls, no images, no symbols. The only record of their presence is the cumulative effect of thousands of small decisions — the angle of a corridor, the depth of a sleeping niche, the particular way a feeding trough is positioned to catch the light at a certain hour. A visitor, if one ever came, would see no evidence of culture, no history, no identity. They would see only the stone and the green and the quiet, and the gray-skinned beings moving through it all like shadows, like memories of motion frozen in basalt.

But the deep rooms. The deep rooms would give a visitor pause. No visitor has ever seen them, but if one did, they would find chambers no larger than a skull, polished to a high glassy sheen, and in each one a single plant — pale, white, translucent in the darkness, its stems like threads of quartz, its leaves absent, its roots gripping nothing but air. The plants grow without soil, without water, without light. They draw their sustenance from the warmth of the stone and from the breath of the Tenders who visit them, kneeling in the darkness, exhaling slowly over the fragile stems. The plants do not feed on carbon dioxide. They feed on something else — some exhalation of the Tenders’ slow metabolism, some compound that only their lungs produce. The deep rooms smell of this exchange, a scent like ozone and crushed minerals, like the air after a lightning strike on bare rock.

The Tenders do not know why they tend these plants. They do not ask. The knowledge is built into their bodies, the way the carving is built into their hands. When a new Tender emerges from the deep rooms, they already know the path to the pale chambers, the particular rhythm of exhalation, the way to polish the stone walls until they gleam. The oldest rooms have plants that have been tended for centuries, their stems now thicker than a Tender’s thumb, their pale surfaces etched with faint concentric lines that might be growth rings if these plants grew in any recognizable way. The newest rooms have seedlings no larger than a fingernail, pale and trembling, their first tendrils just beginning to reach for the warmth.

Outside, the green continues to the horizon. The small fauna move through the leaves. The sun tracks its slow path across the plateau. And in the deep chambers, in the warm darkness, the pale plants breathe in what the Tenders breathe out, and the stone holds everything — the heat, the rhythm, the long slow pulse of a world that has not yet decided what it wants to be.
