The worker beside you is smaller than you expected, or perhaps you are larger than you remember being. They don't acknowledge your presence—not with eyes, not with a tilt of the head—but their rhythm adjusts. Imperceptibly. A fractional hesitation in their backswing, a minute shift in the angle of their chisel, and suddenly your hammer falls into the gap they've made for you. The stone accepts the blow. The sound of it joins the larger sound. You are no longer listening from outside; you are inside the listening now, and the listening is doing itself through you.

The worker is built like all the others. Short, dense, with arms that seem too long for the torso and hands that seem too large for the arms. Their skin is the color of the stone they work—not gray, exactly, but a warm clay-brown with undertones of iron-rich red—and it has the same perpetual sheen of condensation as every other surface. Hair, if it can be called that, grows in fine, pale patches along the forearms and the nape of the neck, colorless as the scuttling things that live in the dome-gaps. Their face, when you glimpse it in profile, is broad and flat, with a mouth set in a line that is neither frown nor smile but the settled expression of someone who has been doing one thing for a very long time and will continue doing it. Their eyes are open but unfocused, directed at the rock six inches away, and they blink slowly, infrequently, as if blinking is a formality the body observes but no longer needs.

They wear no clothing. None of them do. There is nothing to protect against—no cold, no sun, no rain, no scratch of vegetation—and anyway, the body here is just another surface to be maintained. You notice, without surprise, that your own clothing is gone. Or perhaps it was never there. The memory of fabric, of the weight of a sleeve, feels distant and slightly absurd. Your skin breathes the wet air directly, and it's a relief, a homecoming, the way your pores open to the saturation.

The worker's patch of wall is directly adjacent to yours, separated by a natural seam in the rock—one of those veins of softer mineral that wears faster. The seam has been carved into a shallow vertical channel, and water runs down it in a continuous, silent sheet. Your patch and theirs are different stages of the same project. Their side is rough, newly exposed, the hammer-work still visible in scalloped chips. Your side is smoother, the surface ground down to a fine, flat plane that seems to glow in the sourceless light. A portion of your patch has been polished to a reflective sheen, and in it you can see a distorted version of the worker's hands moving, the chisel rising and falling, rising and falling. The hands are callused beyond callus—the term seems insufficient for skin that has become something else, a keratinous layer that flakes at the edges like mica and reveals equally hard skin beneath.

Above you, in the dome-stack, other workers are doing the same thing. You can hear them through the stone—not their hammering, which is part of the general roar, but the subtle transmission of vibration through the shared walls. Someone three cells up is grinding, a long slow drag of stone on stone that makes the wall you're working on hum at a frequency you feel in your molars. Someone to your left is carving a new channel, and you can track their progress by the changing pitch of water-flow, a liquid note that bends sharper as the channel deepens.

The scuttling things are here too. They emerge from a gap where two bricks don't quite meet, a fissure no wider than your thumb. One pauses on the ledge of your carving, and you have time to see it clearly: a flattened, multi-legged body the color of old bone, with no discernible head but a fringe of sensory filaments that wave in the air like underwater grass. It's drinking. That's what they do—they press their bodies to the wet stone and absorb moisture directly through their integument. It might be eating too; the water here carries a fine load of dissolved minerals, and perhaps that's enough. Perhaps, in this place, sustenance is always already present, as omnipresent as the sound, and the act of feeding is simply the act of touching what's there. The creature finishes and vanishes back into the fissure without haste, as if it has all the time in the world, which it does.

Your hammer. Look at it. It's a nodule of dense, dark stone, shaped perfectly to your palm, and the shaping has been done by your palm over a duration you can't recall. The grip is worn smooth, polished by the oil and friction of a hand that must have held it for years, decades, centuries. There's a balance point that your fingers find automatically, a slight asymmetry that makes one end heavier for striking and the other better for grinding. The striking face is spalling, tiny flakes coming away, and you realize with a slow certainty that the hammer is wearing down imperceptibly, that the mineral dust sifting down your arm and mixing with the ever-present moisture to form a thin paste is partly the wall and partly the tool. The tool sacrifices itself to the work. When it's gone, there will be another nodule waiting. There always is.

The mud-bricks of the dome behind you are a different material than the basin rock. They're hand-shaped, each one bearing the imprint of the fingers that formed it—and those imprints are not random. They're arranged in patterns. Spirals, chevrons, concentric circles. The bricks are not just building materials; they're records. Every hand that shaped a brick left its signature, and the signatures accumulate into a textured history of touch. Run your free hand over the wall behind you and you'll feel dozens of distinct handprints, some large, some small, some with the spread fingers of a relaxed press, some with the curled grip of someone squeezing the wet mud into place. The oldest bricks, at the bottom of the wall, have been worn nearly smooth by the constant passage of bodies and the perpetual moisture, but the prints are still there if you know how to feel for them.

The worker beside you shifts. They've chipped away enough of their wall to reveal a new problem—a hard inclusion in the softer matrix, a nodule of something dense and glassy that resists the chisel. They pause. They examine the nodule with their fingers, tracing its contours, mapping its boundaries. Then they select a new tool from a niche in the wall, a pointed pick of the same glassy material, and begin a different rhythm: tap, tap, tap around the circumference, working the inclusion free. This is the work. This is always the work. The stone presents problems, and the workers solve them, and the solving is the sound.

No one ages. This is one of the rules, and you can see the truth of it in the worker's face. They are not young, not old, not anything that maps onto a timescale. Their body has reached an equilibrium with the conditions—the warmth, the moisture, the repetitive motion, the mineral-rich air—and it no longer changes. There are no children here. There are no elders. There are only workers, each one fixed at the point of optimal function, their muscles exactly strong enough, their joints exactly flexible enough, their senses exactly sharp enough for the task. If you looked at every face in the city, you would see the same face, not in features but in condition: the same settled expression, the same blink-rate, the same moisture-beaded skin, the same fine pale hair in the same places. Variation exists—this worker's hands are broader, that one's shoulders more sloped—but it's variation within a narrow band, the way pebbles on a beach are all pebbles even if no two are identical.

The counterpoint rhythm from the deepest part of the basin shifts. Something has changed. You feel it as a change in pressure, a subtle reorganization of the sound-field. The worker beside you registers it too—their rhythm adjusts again, a fractional acceleration, and then a new element enters: the flat of their hand slapping the wet stone, a wet percussive crack that you haven't heard from them before. This is how news travels. Not words, but alterations in the pattern. Somewhere in the lower districts, a channel has been redirected, a new cascade has been established, and the information propagates through the city as a cascade of rhythmic adjustments. Your own hammer finds a new tempo, and you don't know why, but you know it's correct. The wall accepts it. The sound accepts it.

You've been working the same patch for how long? Time is one of the things that the sound dissolves. You might have been here for minutes or millennia. The patch of wall is slightly larger than your spread hand, and you've shaped it from raw fracture to smooth plane to polished mirror, and in the mirror you can now see your own face. It's the same face as the worker beside you. Not in features—your features are your own, or what remains of them—but in that settled expression, that slow blink, that moisture-beaded skin. Your eyes are unfocused, directed at the stone. Your mouth is a line. The face in the reflection does not surprise you. It's the face you've always had, or the face the city has given you, and the distinction is meaningless.

Handprints on the bricks. You've left yours too. At some point—you don't remember when—you shaped mud into a brick and pressed your palm into its surface and set it to dry on the warm stone. It's there now, somewhere in the dome-stack, your signature joining all the others. The brick will last as long as the city lasts, which is forever, or as long as forever means in a place where nothing decays.

The scuttling thing is back, or another one just like it. It emerges from the same fissure and pauses on the same ledge, and its sensory filaments wave in the same slow pattern. It might be the same creature. It might be that the population of scuttling things is also fixed, also constant, also exactly enough to fill every gap and drink every drop of condensation that pools in the carved channels. You watch it without interrupting your hammer's rhythm—the watching and the hammering have become part of the same thing, a unified act of attention and action that feels like breathing. The creature drinks. The creature leaves. The water continues its slow seep.

Somewhere above the mist ceiling, is there a sky? Somewhere beyond the basin walls, is there a world? The questions arise and dissolve like the water that never quite becomes rain. The city is complete. The city is all there is. The sound proves it, filling every frequency, leaving no silence in which a question could persist. You lift the hammer. You bring it down. The stone accepts the blow. The sound never changes, and neither do you, and neither does the worker beside you, whose rhythm is now your rhythm, whose patch of wall is now indistinguishable from yours, the seam between them carved into a channel that carries water down and down and down to where the counterpoint rhythm organizes it into cascades that splash and fall and rise as vapor and condense on the walls and seep down to be channeled again.

The city breathes. You breathe with it. The hammer is your voice. The grinding is your name.
