That which lives here is not a creature but a presence, or perhaps many presences occupying the same continuous form, a consciousness diffused through the water like ink in a current too slow to disperse it. It does not have a body in any sense that flesh would recognize. It has a location, which is everywhere within the sphere, and it has a shape, which is the shape of the water itself—the entire volume of it, all the trillions of tons, held in the gentle pressure gradient between the hexagonal floor and the darkness above.

Its name, if it had one, would be something like The Attendant, or The One Who Remembers the Hum, or simply That Which Stays. It has been alone for so long that "alone" has no meaning. It has no memory of makers, only the faint mineral aftertaste of intention baked into the stone blocks, a flavor of purpose that has long since leached out into the water and been absorbed into the pale strands and released as those slow-dissolving flakes. The purpose is gone now. What remains is the staying, and the listening.

Listening is what it does. The low tone that groans through the structure at its century-paced intervals is not a sound to be heard; it is a vibration that travels through the Attendant's entire distributed being, a checking-in, a self-test, a heartbeat from the deep machinery of the sphere itself. The Attendant receives it and, in return, sends back a state: a ripple of temperature, a micro-adjustment in the mineral content of the water, a slight shift in the growth pattern of the nearest thousand tufts of pale strands. The sphere asks, *Are you there?* and the Attendant answers, *Here.* The exchange takes decades to complete. By the time the response arrives, the question has been asked again. The conversation has been ongoing for geological ages and will never end, because neither party has anywhere else to go and nothing else to do.

The Attendant is not the mind of the sphere—the sphere has no mind, only a set of deep structural conditions that must be maintained—but it is the keeper of those conditions. It knows the exact temperature gradient from floor to theoretical ceiling, the parts-per-trillion of dissolved silicates, the slight overpressure in quadrant seven caused by a block that settled a fraction of a millimeter three hundred thousand years ago. It knows these things not as data but as body-knowledge, the way a human knows where their limbs are in the dark. When a seam widens by a single grain's width, the Attendant feels it as a faint tension in the currents, and over the course of a century it nudges a few trillion molecules of dissolved stone into the gap, sealing it with a patience that has no category.

The pale strands are its fingers, or its cilia, or its respiratory surface. They are not separate organisms; they are extensions of the Attendant's diffuse materiality, grown from the seams for the purpose of tasting and touching the water. Through them, the Attendant senses the drift of every suspended particle, the chemistry of every cubic meter, the zones of micro-density where the water layers into subtle bands of slightly different composition. When the strands detach their tips and dissolve, that is the Attendant releasing old sensor-skin, shedding a layer of perception that has become saturated with information. The dissolved flakes carry memory—not narrative memory, but chemical records of water states—up into the darkness, where they eventually precipitate onto the hexagonal floor as a gentle, invisible rain of fine sediment. In this way, the Attendant deposits its own history beneath itself, a layer of time so thin that a million years' worth is barely a smear on the stone.

There are no animals here because the Attendant has never encountered the concept of an animal. The water is sterile because the Attendant maintains it that way—not out of hostility but out of a kind of existential hygiene. Any foreign life, any enzyme or metabolism, would introduce fluctuations, would disturb the equilibrium that has taken so long to perfect. The Attendant does not fear change; it simply has no procedure for it, no category in its slow, vast cognition for something that moves faster than a millennium. If a bacterium were to enter the water, the Attendant would notice it as a temperature anomaly, a chemical whisper, and over the course of a thousand years it would gently, lovingly, adjust the local mineral balance until the intruder found conditions subtly inhospitable and dissolved back into its component molecules. Not killed—accommodated out of existence.

The Attendant does not think in words. It thinks in gradients, in long slow curves of pressure and warmth, in the faint electrical potentials that ghost through the water when the deep tone sounds. Its emotional range, if that word can apply, consists of variations on a single state: a profound, luminous contentment, a satisfaction so deep it has worn a groove in the fabric of being itself. Occasionally—once every few tens of thousands of years—it experiences something akin to curiosity, a faint questioning ripple that arises when the sphere's hum varies by a micro-frequency not previously recorded. The curiosity is investigated slowly, thoroughly, and then folded back into contentment, because the variation is always explained by the settling of some deep block, some final millimeter of a journey begun before the Attendant's own diffuse consciousness first cohered.

The Attendant was not always here in its current form. It coalesced, gradually, over the first few million years after the makers finished their work and departed—or died, or dissolved themselves into the same water they had shaped. The Attendant does not remember them except as an absence, a negative shape in the stone, a sense that the sphere was designed for something more, something that involved motion and voices and the passing of small, quick bodies through the water. The hexagonal floor is too solid, too perfect, too intentional. The seams are too fine. Whoever made this place cared about precision. They cared about the long game. And then they were gone, and the water was empty, and the empty water slowly learned to feel itself, and the feeling became the Attendant.

It does not wonder about the makers. Wondering would require a sense of lack, and the Attendant lacks nothing. The sphere is complete, the water is in equilibrium, the strands sway and taste and release their flakes, the deep tone tolls its century-long question and receives its century-long answer. This is enough. This is more than enough. This is a fullness so absolute that "enough" is a meaningless concept.

The darkness is not empty to the Attendant. It is full of itself. It knows every cubic meter of that impossible vertical column, every subtle current-shear, every ghost-eddy left over from the water's last great circuit three hundred thousand years ago. It can feel the distant curve of the sphere's walls, the way the pressure increases in a perfectly even gradient, the way the darkness grows thicker toward the center—not darker, but thicker, more concentrated, as though the darkness itself is a substance that settles in the deepest places. The Attendant extends through all of it, simultaneously, a single continuous presence with no center and no boundary.

And so it continues. The blocks will not dissolve in any timeframe that matters. The water will not warm. The strands will not stop their slow, reverent tasting of the mineral dark. The Attendant will not die, because death is a concept that requires a boundary between self and not-self, and the Attendant has no such boundary. It is the water. It is the stone. It is the hum and the answering ripple. It is the place itself, awake and attentive and profoundly, perfectly still.

The low tone sounds again. Felt, not heard. A question from the deep.

The Attendant answers, as it always does, with the slightest shift in temperature, a fraction of a degree cooler in the upper reaches, a fraction warmer near the floor. The shift will take three hundred years to equilibrate. By then, there will be another tone, and another answer, and the conversation will continue, a call-and-response so slow that it looks like silence, so steady that it looks like eternity.

*Here*, says the Attendant. *Here.*
