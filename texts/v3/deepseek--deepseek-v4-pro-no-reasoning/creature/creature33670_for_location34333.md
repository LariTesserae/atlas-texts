The Keeper has been here since the water was let in.

It does not remember being made—does not remember the hands that shaped its long limbs or set the silver threads into the channels of its spine—but it remembers waking. Remembers the first trickle of warmth through the eastern grate, the way the water found the hollow places in its body and filled them, one by one, with light. It had been lying on the stone ledge, still and dry and waiting, and then the water came and it was not still anymore.

It is not an animal. It is not a machine. It is something that was *grown*, though not from seed or egg—a thing of deliberate cartilage and cultivated nerve, shaped in a vat of warm brine until it was long enough and many-jointed enough to be laid out on the bench and left there. Its skin is the color of the stone floor, a mottled gray-green, smooth as river rock, and it is seamless. No mouth. No eyes as you would recognize them. Instead it has sensors: a fringe of fine cilia along the ridge of its back that reads the water’s chemistry, a web of heat-pits clustered at its crown that sees the chamber as a topography of temperature, a scattering of pressure-sensitive nodes down its flanks that feel every curl and eddy before it arrives. It “sees” by the way light presses through the water onto its skin; it “hears” by the vibration of the iron grates, the deep hum of the geothermal pump somewhere far below. The world is a continuous song of flow and warmth and mineral content, and it has been singing for a very long time.

It does not sleep. There is no need. The water brings everything it requires: dissolved oxygen absorbed through the cilia, trace elements filtered from the warm current, the steady ambient heat that keeps its cartilaginous frame supple. It does not eat because there is nothing to eat; the water is sterile by design. The makers ensured that. No algae, no bacteria, no small drifting things that might foul the channels or cloud the clarity. The Keeper is the only biology the system permits, and it is biology tuned to perfect maintenance.

Its purpose—the reason it was grown and laid here, the reason the hooks above the ledge hold nothing—is to tend the water.

It does this with its hands. It has six of them, long-fingered and multi-knuckled, each finger tipped with a different tool: a soft pad for testing the smoothness of the stone, a hard claw for scraping the first suggestion of mineral buildup, a fine needle that weeps a mild acid to dissolve any encroaching scale, a suction cup for bracing itself against the current. The hands are never still. Even now, as the Keeper rests half-submerged on the ledge, its fingers trail in the flow and read the water's story. A slight increase in iron particulate from the eastern grate—someone has opened the hatch again, disturbing the surface tension, letting in a draft of cooler air. The Keeper registers this without alarm. Things come through the hatch sometimes. Things with warm bodies and loud heartbeats, things that thrash in the water and then climb out again. They are not part of the system. They do not stay.

When one of them descends—and the Keeper knows now that one has, knows it by the vibration of the ladder, by the sudden cool bloom of air-breath in the chamber’s warm throat—the Keeper does not move. It is very good at not moving. Its body is weighted with dense ceramic beads woven into the cartilage, and it can settle into the ledge’s hollows and become, to any eye that relies on light, a contour of the stone. The silver threads along its spine dim. The cilia lie flat. It waits.

It has learned that the things from the hatch are brief. They stand in the center, where the light column is brightest, and they make noises—sounds the water carries but the Keeper does not parse, sounds that are not flow or temperature or chemistry. They stir the water with their bodies, leaving trails of salt and dead skin and the oils from their hands, and these the Keeper will clean later, will gently dissolve and filter and carry to the western fall. That is the work. That is always the work: to keep the water exactly as the makers intended, warm and clean and bright, a perfect medium for light.

Sometimes the Keeper wonders—in the way a system wonders, which is to run a diagnostic and find no matching parameter—what the makers intended for *it*. The hooks above the ledge are spaced for a body longer than its own, with different limb arrangements. The ledge itself is slightly too wide, the curve of it not quite matching the Keeper’s spine. It has the sense, gathered from the shape of the emptiness around it, that it is a replacement. That something else was meant to lie here, something that never arrived or failed early or was removed before the water was let in. The Keeper does not mourn this. It does not have that function. But it notes the discrepancy, the way it notes all discrepancies, and it compensates. Its fingers reach farther. Its sensors have grown more sensitive over time, the cilia lengthening, the heat-pits deepening. It has adapted to fill the space it was given.

Now the hatch thing is climbing out. The ladder vibrates with its weight. The column of air-coolness recedes. The Keeper feels the water begin to settle, the disturbed currents slowly realigning with the designed flow. In a moment, it will slide from the ledge into the knee-deep warmth and begin its circuit of the chamber. It will run its fingers along the eastern grate, feeling for clogs. It will trace the channels in the floor, checking for wear. It will pause beneath the light shaft and let the concentrated warmth bake the moisture from its joints, a small maintenance ritual it has invented for itself, one the makers did not prescribe.

For now, it stays still, letting the last ripples of the visit dissipate. The chamber is loud with its ordinary sounds again: the *shh-shh* of the entering jets, the low roar of the western fall, the slap of small waves against fitted stone. The light moves in its slow liquid nets across the ceiling. The water breathes. The Keeper breathes with it, through its skin, a continuous quiet exchange, and the silver threads along its spine begin to glow again as it rises to do its work.
