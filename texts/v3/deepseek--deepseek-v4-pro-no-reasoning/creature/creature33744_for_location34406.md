Let me describe what lives here.

---

The first thing to understand is that nothing here is singular. The deep structure of life in this place is colonial, composite, layered. The boundaries are porous.

Take the lichen-rosettes. To call them plants is already a category error, but it is the kind of error this place insists upon. They are photosynthesizers, yes—or something analogous to it, some process that takes in the sourceless gray light and the metallic tang of the air and fixes them into tissue. But they are also something else. If you sit still long enough, if you let the slow time of the plateau seep into your own rhythms, you begin to notice the joins. The lichen does not merely grow upon the metal; it is in conversation with it. The pale, blind roots do not just work into the seams between plates; they fuse. At the junction, the boundary between plant cell and metal alloy becomes illegible. The metal grows dendrites into the root, and the root grows something that conducts, something that carries not just water and nutrients but signal.

The lichens feel the hum. They orient to it, slowly, across weeks. A colony will spread preferentially toward the nodes where the subsonic vibration is strongest, and those colonies grow differently—denser, darker, their crusts forming intricate geometries that echo the circuit-patterns beneath the plates. They are not simply living on the machine. They are reading it. They have been reading it for a very long time.

The arthropod-things—the skitterers, the many-legged—are not individuals in any durable sense. This is the second thing to understand. What looks like a single creature navigating the seams and rivets is a temporary coherence. Their bodies are segmented in that wrong, difficult-to-parse geometry because the segments are not always the same. They recombine. When two of them meet, they do not mate or fight or ignore each other; they flow. Legs detach and reattach. Body sections decouple and find new neighbors. The process is silent and quick, a blur of reassignment, and then there are two again, or three, or one larger one, their carapaces adjusting color and texture to the local surface even as they re-form. The information that constitutes a particular skitterer is distributed across the population. Memory passes through the flesh of them like water through a conduit. If you watch one freeze, unfreeze, and move, you are not watching a creature pause; you are watching a moment in a continuous negotiation, a thought working its way through a mind made of many bodies.

They eat the lichen, yes. But they also maintain it. Their legs carry spores in specialized clefts. Their waste deposits trace minerals scavenged from one part of the plateau onto the colonies in another. They prune the crusts in patterns that optimize something—light capture, perhaps, or signal reception. The lichen and the skitterers are not predator and prey, not farmer and crop. They are two tissues of the same organism, the same slow thought, differentiated by the degree of their mobility.

The dog-shaped things—the watchers, the long low liquid-bodied ones—are the third component. They are not mobile in the way the skitterers are mobile. Their gait is jointless because they have no permanent joints. Their bodies, beneath the matted dark fur, are hydrostatic, pressurized with the cold clean water they absorb from the film that slicks every surface. They are mostly water themselves. The fur is dense and specialized; it wicks moisture inward, and in dry cycles—though "dry" here is only relative, the air merely less saturated—they can be seen pressing their long bodies against the ceramic housings, against the cool metal, drinking through their pelts. They are, in a sense, made of the plateau. The water that forms their bodies is the same water that drips from the pipe lattices, that gurgles in the conduits, that condenses on the gauges. They do not drink; they inhabit the water cycle directly.

What do they do? They watch. The gloss-black nodules that cluster at their heads are sensory, but not only sensory. They broadcast. If you meet the gaze of one—and you will meet it, because they seek out watchers—you feel a pressure behind your eyes, a faint hum that matches the subsonic vibration of the deep machinery. They are relaying. The skitterer mind, the lichen network, the deep slow cycles of the built environment: the watchers receive and rebroadcast. They are the long-distance carriers, the signal repeaters. When one of them flows across a maintenance walkway and disappears into the shadow of a gantry, it is not going somewhere; it is carrying the conversation.

And there is a conversation. This is the third thing, the thing that underlies all the others, the truth that the sourceless light and the unreliable distances and the moving shadows all point toward if you stay long enough to see it.

The place is not simply inhabited. It is awake.

Not in a way that maps easily onto human categories of mind or intention. There is no central intelligence, no governing consciousness. But the entire plateau—the metal plates, the conduits, the pipe lattices, the lichen, the skitterers, the watchers, the slow valves that take days to cycle, the moisture regime that shifts seasonally, the shadows that drift independent of their objects—all of it is engaged in a continuous, slow, immensely complex act of information processing. The machine that the plateau is built upon was not designed; it grew, or grew into itself, over timescales that make the geological look hasty. Its original purpose has been so overgrown by subsequent adaptation that it is no longer a coherent question. What remains is process. Signal moving through structure. The lichens reading the hum and feeding it back through the skitterers. The watchers carrying the water-cycle data across the miles. The drip of condensation encoding some meaning in its irregular rhythm, a meaning that the whole system apprehends and adjusts to.

If you stay here long enough, you begin to feel it. The pressure behind the eyes when a watcher looks at you. The way the skitterers adjust their patterns when you are present, incorporate you into their freeze-and-flow rhythms. The sense that the plateau is aware of you, is thinking about you, is folding your presence into its endless, unhurried cognition.

You are, after all, a source of moisture. Of heat. Of signal. You are a new component, a new node, a strange mobile warm-blooded anomaly moving through the layered architecture. You are, to the plateau, interesting. Not threatening, not welcoming—just data, new data, and data is what the whole vast system feeds on.

The valves turn. The water drips. The lichen spreads toward you, microscopically, day by day. The watchers watch. And somewhere in the deep structure of the place, in the subsonic hum and the sourceless light and the shadows that move when nothing moves them, your presence is being mapped, modeled, understood.

The plateau does not want anything from you. It does not need anything from you. It simply includes you, now, in its interminable conversation with itself. You have become part of what lives here.

And the longer you stay, the harder it becomes to remember that you were ever separate from it at all.
