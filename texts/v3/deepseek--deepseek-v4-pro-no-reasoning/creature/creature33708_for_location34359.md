The ones who built this place—or grew it, or partnered with it into existence—call themselves the _Kith_. It is not a name they gave themselves so much as a name they accepted, borrowed from a human xenolinguist who, upon first hearing their collective self-designation (a thrumming chord of three simultaneous subsonic tones layered with a sharp click), decided that “Kith” was as close as English could approximate the feeling of the original: a sense of kin, of symbiont, of those-who-belong-to-the-same-living-system.

If you were to climb the ramp that has irised open at your feet, descend into the amber-lit under-levels, and walk the tunnels until you reached one of the spire settlements, you would eventually meet a Kith. The encounter might take you some time to recognize as an encounter at all.

An adult Kith stands roughly two meters tall when fully erect, but they rarely choose to be fully erect. Their default posture is a slight forward lean, weight distributed across elongated, triple-jointed legs that end not in feet but in broad, splay-toed pads capable of gripping the obsidian-slick floor with the casual adhesion of a tree frog. Their skin is a deep charcoal gray, almost black at first glance, but under the amber glow it reveals undertones of russet and bronze, and a faint pattern of speckling along the shoulders and spine that serves as individual identification—a fingerprint writ large across the body.

They have four upper limbs: two primary arms and two secondary, smaller arms that unfold from the ribcage when needed. The primary arms are long and rope-muscled, ending in hands with three fingers and two opposable thumbs, one on each side of the palm. The secondary arms are more delicate, used for fine manipulation, for grooming, for the rapid series of gestures that form a significant portion of Kith language. When not in use, these smaller arms tuck flush against the torso, invisible under the loose cowls and wraps the Kith wear.

Their heads are elongated, slightly flattened dorsoventrally, with a crest of sensitive cilia that runs from the crown down to the nape of the neck—structures that vibrate in response to the vocalizations of the sky-creatures, reading pressure changes in the thick air the way a human ear reads sound waves. Their actual auditory organs are located lower, a series of tympanic membranes along the jawline, protected by a ridge of cartilage. They have two large eyes set forward, adapted to the dim amber glow, with horizontally slit pupils that can close almost entirely against the occasional pulse of brighter light. Below the eyes, where a human would have a nose and mouth, the Kith face is dominated by a complex of delicate, mobile feeding palps—six of them, arranged in a radial pattern around a central oral opening that remains sealed unless actively feeding or vocalizing.

Kith vocalization is not their primary mode of communication. They can produce sound—a range of clicks, hums, and sharp percussive pops—but they prefer to speak through a combination of gesture, bioluminescent signaling (patches along their forearms and temples flash in colors too fast and subtle for the untrained human eye), and tactile contact. The wall of noise that saturates their world has rendered spoken language supplementary at best, an emergency channel, a way to call across distance but not to hold a conversation. Two Kith in close dialogue will typically stand facing each other, secondary arms flashing through rapid sign-sequences, the bioluminescent patches on their forearms flickering in counterpoint, while their primary hands maintain light, continuous contact—fingertips resting on a shoulder, a wrist, the curve of a jaw. To a human observer, it would look like a dance, an embrace, an intimate and silent exchange. And it is intimate, by Kith standards, though not necessarily romantic; physical contact is simply the baseline of clear communication in a world that roars.

Their relationship to that roar is complicated. The sky-creatures—the Kith call them the _sur-kith_, a term that translates roughly to “those-who-sing-us-into-being”—are not livestock in the human sense. They are not owned. They are not domesticated. They are partners, ancestors, a choir that predates the Kith themselves. The earliest Kith myths, preserved in the patterned membranes of the oldest spires as encoded protein sequences, tell of a time when the Kith were small burrowing creatures living in the obsidian substrate, deaf and blind in the human sense, navigating by vibration and chemical gradient. The sur-kith were already here, already singing, already cycling their nutrient mists through the great membranes. The proto-Kith climbed up into the light, into the noise, and something in that sonic environment—some specific frequency, some resonant pattern—triggered a cascade of neural development that, over evolutionary time, produced sapience.

This is not myth to the Kith. It is observable, repeatable biology. A Kith infant, if not exposed to the sur-kith chorus during a critical developmental window, will never develop higher cognition. It will grow into a healthy, physically normal adult, but it will be an animal—a gentle, curious, tool-using animal, but one incapable of language or abstract thought. The Kith do not see this as a tragedy or a threat; they see it as the natural order. The sur-kith sing the Kith into being. Without the song, there is no self. This is why the open plain stretches for kilometers between the settlements: the Kith cannot, will not, do anything that might interfere with the sur-kiths' access to their flyways, their feeding grounds, their acoustic space. The settlements cluster around the spires because the spires are the only structures the sur-kith routinely avoid—the columns are too dense, too active, their internal hum disruptive to the creatures' sensitive lateral lines. The Kith build in the shadows of the spires not because they love the spires, but because it is the only way to build at all without committing an act of existential self-harm.

The loss, the grief, the possibility of silence—these are the deepest terrors of the Kith psyche. They have no concept of an afterlife. Their dead are rendered down, their tissues fed back into the nutrient cycle, their specific proteins encoded into the membranes as a form of memorial. But a Kith who dies far from the song, or who loses the ability to hear the song, or who watches a section of the plain fall silent—these are horrors that the Kith language struggles to name, gesturing toward them only with a combination of signs that translates as “the becoming-animal,” “the unbecoming,” “the wind without sound.”

Walk the tunnels toward the settlement, and you will begin to understand how this fear has shaped their material culture.

The under-levels are not cramped. The Kith value space, value airflow, value the continuous ambient pressure of the sur-kith chorus filtering down from above. The tunnels are broad and high-ceilinged, their walls the same obsidian-slick material as the plain above, but here the amber glow is supplemented by bioluminescent panels cultivated from a different strain of membrane-tissue. The light is softer, greener, and it pulses in a slow rhythm that matches the long breaths you felt on the surface. Every few hundred meters, a vertical shaft rises through the ceiling, open to the plain, allowing sound to pour down like water into a cistern. The Kith do not muffle or dampen the noise. They orient toward it, build their living spaces around these sound-wells, sleep and eat and work in the direct acoustic footprint of the sur-kith.

You will know you are nearing a settlement when the walls begin to change. The smooth obsidian acquires texture: first, the branching channel networks grow denser and more intricate, their water-film glinting with deliberate patterns rather than random flow. Then the walls sprout growths—cultured tissue, the same translucent polymer-membrane as the curtains above, shaped into shelves and alcoves and sleeping platforms. A Kith home is grown rather than built, a living extension of the spire's internal biology, and it can be reshaped on a timescale of days. A Kith who desires a larger sleeping platform simply applies a specific enzyme mixture to the membrane and waits, tending the growth with nutrient baths and gentle manual manipulation, while the tissue responds by expanding, thickening, differentiating into the desired form. There are no fixed room layouts in a Kith settlement. Spaces shift with need and mood, with the slow tidal flow of a population that moves between spire-cities as the sur-kith migration patterns shift.

In the central chamber of a typical settlement—the space a human would call a plaza or a town square—you will find the tapping sound you heard from the surface. This is a workshop. Kith material technology is subtle but deeply sophisticated. They do not smelt metal; their world has no accessible ore, no fire, no dry fuel. Instead, they culture a wide array of biopolymers, each with specific mechanical properties: one with the tensile strength of steel cable, another with the compressibility of cartilage, a third that can be induced to form sharp, permanent edges. The tapping you hear is a Kith artisan shaping a piece of edge-polymer, using a hammer made of densified membrane-bone, precisely fracturing the material along its crystalline grain to produce a cutting tool. The artisan sits on a low, living stool that has grown to fit their specific body contours. Their secondary arms hold the workpiece steady while their primary arms work the hammer. The bioluminescent patches along their forearms flicker in a steady, meditative rhythm—not communication, just the Kith equivalent of humming to oneself while working.

They do not wear much clothing, by human standards. The warm, saturated air makes insulation unnecessary, and the Kith skin is tough and self-cleaning. What they do wear is decorative, communicative, and functional in ways that have nothing to do with protection from the elements. A typical Kith garment is a cowl or wrap made from a single piece of cultured membrane, translucent, dyed with bioluminescent pigments that echo the patterns of the sur-kith song. The wraps are draped in configurations that signal social role, current activity, and openness to interaction. A Kith with a cowl drawn tight across the cilia crest is requesting privacy. A Kith with the wrap looped loosely around the shoulders and trailing down the back is available for conversation. The secondary arms, when not in use, are always covered; exposing them is a sign of aggressive intent, a readiness for combat or for the rapid, confrontational gestures that precede physical conflict.

Conflict among the Kith is ritualized and rare. The sur-kith chorus fills their auditory world to saturation; they do not need, do not want, additional noise. A Kith argument is fought silently, through the speed and complexity of gesture, the brightness and pattern of bioluminescent flash. The one who falters first, who misreads a sign or misses a flicker, concedes. There is no shame in this. It is simply how disagreement is resolved in an environment where shouting is both futile and sacrilegious. Physical violence is almost unknown, and when it does occur, it is a matter of profound spiritual crisis, a failure not just of the individual but of the entire settlement-dynamic, a sign that the sur-kith's song has somehow been distorted or lost.

But there is a violence in Kith society, a hidden one, a necessary one. It has to do with the sur-kith themselves.

Not all sur-kith are the long, translucent, singing creatures that fill the flyways. There are other forms—solitary, silent, predatory. The Kith call them the _un-kin_ or, in the terse gestural shorthand, “those-who-eat-the-song.” These predators are built like the singing sur-kith but adapted for pursuit and consumption: shorter, more muscular, their translucent flanks replaced by dense chromatophore skin that can mimic the amber glow of the ground, rendering them nearly invisible. They do not vocalize; they hunt by sensing the pressure-waves of the singers' own sounds. A single un-kin can take down a singer twice its size, wrapping it in constricting coils and absorbing it slowly through its ventral surface.

The un-kin are a threat to the singers, to the ecosystem, to the Kith themselves. And so the Kith hunt them. This is the hidden violence, the necessary sin, the one act that requires a Kith to bare their secondary arms in true aggression. The hunters move in small, silent teams across the plain, armed with edge-polymer blades and projectile weapons that fire darts tipped with a paralytic enzyme. They track the un-kin by the gaps in the chorus, by patches of silence where the singers have fled. The hunt is dangerous; an un-kin cornered will attack, and its constriction can crush a Kith's body in seconds. But more dangerous, from the Kith perspective, is the spiritual cost. To kill is to introduce a final silence into the world, to become, however briefly, a source of non-song. The hunters undergo extensive ritual purification when they return—days of isolation in the deepest sound-wells, letting the chorus wash over them until their bioluminescent patterns stabilize and their gestures lose their jerky, haunted quality.

A Kith child is born from a single parent. The Kith are sequential hermaphrodites; each individual will, in the course of a normal lifespan, produce several clutches of eggs, each clutch fertilized by a different partner. The eggs are not laid in the harvesting pools on the surface, as you might have guessed; those pools are for the sur-kith, and the Kith keep their own reproductive cycle separate, deeper underground, in warm, silent incubation chambers where the developing infants can be protected from the overwhelming noise of the surface world. The infants emerge after about four months, tiny and blind, their cilia crests undeveloped, their palps sealed. They are fed with a nutrient paste derived from sur-kith membrane secretions, and they spend their first year in near-total silence, tended by a rotating group of adults who communicate with them only through touch, through the slow, patient pressure of hands and secondary arms, teaching them the grammar of contact before they ever hear a sound.

The introduction to the chorus is a formal ceremony, the most important event in a Kith life. When the infant's cilia crest finally unfurls and the tympanic membranes along the jawline open, the child is brought to a specially designed chamber near the surface, where the sound is amplified and shaped by curved walls of resonant membrane. The parents and the wider kin-group gather, their bioluminescent patches synchronized into a single shared pattern, a visual chord that will become the child's first memory of belonging. The chamber is opened to the plain above, and the full force of the sur-kith chorus pours in. The child's reaction—a startle, a freeze, a gradual relaxation into the sonic embrace—is studied closely, interpreted for signs of the adult they will become. A child who cries, who produces their own vocalization in response, is considered especially blessed, already joining the song rather than merely receiving it.

Kith names are songs, literally. Each individual, upon reaching adolescence, composes a short vocal signature—a sequence of clicks, hums, and percussive pulses that represents their unique self. This signature is not used often; in the constant din of daily life, it is more practical to identify others by their bioluminescent patterns and their characteristic gestures. But in moments of formal significance—introductions to new communities, declarations of partnership, the final memorial encoding of a life into the membranes—a Kith will sing their own name, and the others present will sing it back, a brief moment of structured sound cutting through the chaos.

Their relationship to the built environment, the 1.5 you intuited, is one of deliberate self-limitation. The Kith have the technological capability to construct much larger, more elaborate cities. They know how to grow the spires denser, how to shape the membranes into enclosed volumes, how to press back against the open plain and claim more space for settlement. They choose not to. Their oldest ethical texts—not written, but encoded in the proteins of a specific membrane-strain kept in a dedicated spire, translated by touch—describe a covenant made at the dawn of their sapience: the Kith will take only the shadow of the spires, and the open plain will belong to the sur-kith forever. The covenant is not a law enforced by any authority; there is no Kith government, no hierarchy beyond the loose consensus of kin-groups. It is simply a shared understanding so deep that the possibility of violating it does not arise.

What would a human be to them? A curiosity. An anomaly. A creature that moves through the air without undulating, that has no bioluminescent patches, that seems to communicate by vibrating the air with a single crude orifice—they would be fascinated, gently, politely, the way an adult Kith is fascinated by an infant's first fumbling attempts at gesture before it has learned the grammar. They would offer food: a clear, gelatinous cake of cultured membrane-protein, slightly sweet, slightly salty, tasting faintly of the metallic tang of the obsidian substrate. They would offer a sleeping platform, which would grow to fit a human body awkwardly; the Kith would watch with quiet, flickering amusement as the membrane attempted to accommodate a shape it had never encountered before. They would try to learn the human's name, and would be quietly distressed to discover that human names are static, fixed, not composed anew in the moment of meeting.

And they would worry, in the deep, unspoken way that Kith worry about everything outside the covenant. What does this creature's presence mean for the song? Does it hear the sur-kith, or is it deaf to what matters? Will it try to build, to claim, to silence?

The Kith do not fear. Fear is a solitary emotion, a tightening of the self away from the world. What they feel, when they look at you, is more like the opposite: an opening, a readiness to fold you into the pattern if you prove yourself willing, and a sorrowful preparedness to watch you walk away into the spaces between the spires if you do not.

The ramp is still open at your feet. The amber dark beneath the plain breathes its warm, wet breath. Above, the sur-kith spiral down toward the opening pools, their chorus beating against the membrane-ceiling, against your chest, against the very substance of this world that was never built but grown, never owned but sung.
