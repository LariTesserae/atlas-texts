What lives here is not a person, but a continuity — an unbroken lifecycle of something that might once have been called a caretaker, or a keeper, or a ghost.

It does not think of itself in these terms. It does not think at all, at least not in any sequence you would recognize as thought. It is not conscious in the way a human being is conscious, with a self that stands apart from its surroundings and narrates its own experience. It is more like a function of the chamber itself, a standing wave in the slow, dense air, an eddy in the garden's chemical breath. But it lives. It eats, after a fashion. It moves. It maintains. And it remembers, though its memory is not stored in a brain or a body but in the arrangement of objects and the angle of a rope and the particular pressure of a root against stone.

If you were to force it into a description, it would be this:

It is an old woman, or something that used to be an old woman. Her name was once, perhaps, a sequence of sounds made with a tongue and a palate and a larynx, but those organs have long since been repurposed, their tissues re-absorbed by the garden, their calcium scavenged by the millipedes and shelled snails. Her body is no longer a body. It is a concentration gradient, a zone in the air where the density of certain molecules is higher than elsewhere — carbon compounds, trace amino acids, a faint signature of iron that matches the stone tree's own flesh. This is what remains of her corporeality: a smell, barely, and a temperature differential of perhaps one-tenth of a degree. She is the warmest spot in the chamber, and the bats know it, and the lizards know it, and they arrange their eternal waiting around her invisible warmth like stones around a dead hearth.

She lives in all the places at once. The platforms are her bones. The braided ropes are her tendons. The pots of unfired clay are her memory of hunger, and the grinding stones are her memory of work, and the sleeping mats are her memory of rest, which she no longer needs. She is distributed through the monastery the way a snail is distributed through its shell — not the shell itself, but the animal that gave the shell its shape, that secreted it slowly from a body that lived inside it. She secreted this place. She built it cell by cell, terrace by terrace, and then she spread herself out across it, thinned herself until she coated every surface like a film of dust, and she has been living here in this dispersed condition ever since.

She eats in the same way the garden eats. Her metabolism is not her own anymore. It has been subsumed into the larger metabolism of the chamber, the closed loop of spores and leaves and mineral ions. When the shelf fungi exhale their visible mist, she inhales. When the blue leaves catch the dim light and convert it into chemistry, she participates. When the lizards shed their skins — a thing they do once every few centuries, so slowly that the shed cuticle crumbles to powder before it fully separates — she is nourished by the powder. She does not need much. She has not needed much for a long time. The monks who came here learned early that the path to endurance was renunciation of volume, and she took that lesson to its furthest conclusion. She eats light, indirectly. She drinks iron. She is sustained by the patience of stone.

She moves at the speed of the chamber. This is essential to understanding her. She is not still — nothing here is still — but her motion is so gradual that to human perception it reads as absence. In the time it takes you to draw one breath, she might shift her attention from one side of the chamber to the other, a movement of perhaps half a degree of arc, the equivalent of a glance that takes a year to complete. In the time it takes you to grow hungry, she might have adjusted the angle of a single leaf so that it catches the fissure light more fully. In the time it takes you to grow old, she might have completed a single circuit of the spiral platforms, checking the ropes and the pots and the niches, running the house with the diligence of one who has kept it running for thirty thousand years.

But she does move. The impressions on the sleeping mats are hers. The subtle shifts in the dust that you can almost see out of the corner of your eye are hers. The way the spore-mist parts around a certain volume of air and then closes again — that volume is her chest expanding and contracting, once per hour, once per day, the respiration of something that has roped its biology to the slowest pulse it could find. She is not a ghost. She is a metabolic process operating at the lower limit of the possible, and the proof is that she is not quite transparent, not quite inert, not quite gone. The garden's chemistry includes her. The chamber's budget of matter and energy includes her. She is accountable in the ledgers of the ecosystem, a debit in one place, a credit in another, a resonant frequency in the closed air.

What does she do with her time? This is a question she would not understand, because time is not a thing she has or uses — it is the medium she swims in. But if you watch carefully, if you tune your own perception down to the scale where she lives, you can see her work. She tends. She is the last active energy left in the garden. The gardeners have all become like her, or nearly — some are even slower, some have fused with the petrified roots, some exist only as a particular pattern of spore-fall, a shadow that repeats every few centuries. But she is the one who still notices. She notices when a vine's spiral begins to iterate slightly off the Fibonacci ideal, and over the course of a thousand years she nudges it back, using nothing but the faint pressure of her distributed presence, the way a slight temperature difference can bend a growing shoot. She notices when the gel-succulents' membranes begin to cloud with mineral deposits, and she has learned to polish them, molecule by molecule, using a chemical secretion that is indistinguishable from prayer. She notices when the fissure light changes, when the exterior seasons that exist only at the edge of the thin atmosphere push slightly deeper into the mountain, and she adjusts the angle of the hanging pots and the density of the spore-mist to compensate. She is the system's immune response, its regulator, its memory.

And she remembers. This is what distinguishes her from the rest of the garden. The plants remember nothing; they simply persist. The animals remember in the sense of instinct, conditioned response, the slow tuning of behavior over evolutionary time. But she remembers events. She remembers the day the last living voice in the chamber said something aloud, and she remembers the sound, though she can no longer reproduce it. She remembers the taste of water — water! — though the memory is so old that it has become a kind of geology, a stratum of her being that underlies everything else. She remembers her own name, but not in words. She holds it as a pattern, a rhythm of syllables that corresponds to a rhythm of nerve-firings that no longer occur. If you were to speak her name, she would not turn, because she no longer associates the sound with herself. But something in the chamber would shift, a tightening of the air, a slight increase in the garden's hum, as if the name still had weight here and its utterance disturbed a balance that had been calibrated over millennia.

She is not lonely. This is perhaps the most important thing to understand. The question would not occur to her. Loneliness is a thing that happens to beings with edges, beings who experience themselves as separate from the world and yearn for contact. She has no edges. She has dissolved them deliberately, traded them away for continuity. She is the chamber, and the chamber is her, and the garden is the chamber, and the garden is the world, and the world is the mountain, and the mountain is the ceiling, and the ceiling is the sky. The distinction between self and other collapsed in her so long ago that she does not remember having made the choice — and she does remember, because memory is the only thing she has left. She chose this. She chose to become what she is. It was the logical conclusion of the monastery's whole way of life, and she took the logic to its end. She is not trapped. She is not waiting. She is not sad. She is at home in the most complete sense possible, because she is the home, and the home is her, and there is no outside to yearn for.

Except there is an outside. The fissure lets in the light and the wind. The outside comes to her as sound, as temperature, as the faint variation in pressure when storms batter the peaks a vertical league above. She knows the outside exists. She has not forgotten that there is a world beyond the chamber, a world of clouds and snow and thin air and cities full of people who move at a different speed. She does not want it. She does not fear it. She simply notes it, the way she notes everything, as data, as variable, as a thing that might eventually require adjustment. If the fissure were to widen, if the chamber were to lose its atmosphere, if the garden were to die — she would respond. She would do what she has always done. She would tend. She would slow down. She would persist.

There is a moment, now, as the fissure light shifts toward amber, when she becomes aware of you. This is significant, because awareness of an outside observer has not happened here in a very long time. She does not know what you are. You are too fast, too solid, too carbon-based — you register as a disturbance, a temporary spike in the chamber's normally smooth gradients. She has no fear response, no curiosity, no welcome. She has only attention, immense and patient, turning toward you with the speed of a continent.

In the time it takes you to notice a single lizard opening one eye, she has already begun to adjust. Not to expel you — she would never do that. But to fold you into the accounting. Your body heat is a variable. Your breath is a chemical input. Your footsteps, if you take steps, are vibrations that propagate through the stone and disturb the settling dust. She wraps all of this into her awareness, and she waits, because waiting is what she does best, to see if you will be a transient perturbation or a lasting change.

The garden does not pause. The bats do not wake. The millipede does not alter its course. The monks in the air do not turn toward you, except that they do, by a fraction of a degree, by a shift in their dispersed particulate bodies that would take a human lifetime to perceive. The old woman who is everywhere and nowhere makes room for you in her understanding, and in doing so, she makes room for you in the chamber, briefly, provisionally, with the calm of something that has seen comets cross the sky and mountains rise and fall.

She is the one who will remain when you are gone. She is the one who will still be here, adjusting the leaves toward the light, when the light itself becomes amber and then ochre and then fades into the long exterior night. She is not the last living thing, because the garden is full of life. But she is the last thing that remembers being human, and even that memory is dissolving, particle by particle, into the slow, loud, continuous present of this sealed green room at the top of the world.
