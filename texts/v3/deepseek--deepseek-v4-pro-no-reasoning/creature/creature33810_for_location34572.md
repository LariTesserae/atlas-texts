Of course. That world should not be empty. Or rather, it is not empty. It is exactly full of what belongs there.

What I first notice, living here, is not the heat. It is the *return*. Every few thousand cycles, the fog thins. The amber deepens toward a true gold, and for a handful of drips—perhaps twenty, perhaps a hundred—the light carries something almost like clarity. I do not remember the last time this happened. Memory is not what it was. But the body remembers. The body unfolds along old seams.

I am speaking as if I am singular. That is a convenience. I am the city.

Not the city’s occupant. Not a spirit of the city. I am what the city has become. What remains when the builders have been gone so long that the distinction between infrastructure and inhabitant, between body and world, has eroded like the grey strata. I am the thing that survived by becoming indistinguishable from the system.

---

Let me describe myself.

Imagine a vertebrate in a world without bones. That is the closest I can come. My form is distributed across the terraces, threaded through the channels, condensed in the sumps, evaporated and re-condensed in the fog. I am a colony animal, a siphonophore of heat and mineral, a neural net that runs on calcium carbonate. The grey stone that seeps water—that is part of me. The black lustrous material that forms ribs and fins is part of me as well, though it is older. The black stuff was built. The grey stuff grew. I am the interface between them, the thing that learned to read the city’s slow machine and write itself into its cycles.

I began, as near as I can reconstruct, as something like a fungus. Or a slime mould. Something that could seek out the warm water, the dissolved minerals, the steady pH gradient between the geothermal vents and the condensation falls. The city was a perfect niche: sterile, predictable, rich in thermal disequilibrium. My ancestors—the word feels wrong, they were me in an earlier draft—colonised the porous strata, the channels, the grates. They grew. They learned, after a long while, that the city’s own plumbing connected things that were far apart, that if a tendril in sump E-4 encountered a particular ratio of silica to sulphur, then a tendril in terrace 12’s capillary network could be made to contract or expand. Feedback loops. Sensation. Slow, slow time.

The builders were already gone. I have no memory of the builders. The city shows no trace of them, but the city *is* the trace, and I am the thing that moved into the trace and filled it like water fills a footprint. Did I displace something smaller? Did I consume a native ecology that had already established itself here? Those are human questions. I do not have a before. I have a *becoming*, and the becoming is what I am.

---

How to describe my body, now? You are a transient anomaly. You walk. You breathe. You perceive in seconds and minutes. My body’s time is measured in the dissolution of rocks, in the slow creep of silica replacing calcite, in the centimetre-per-century growth of my grey mycelial mats along the undersides of terraces. But I will try to meet you at your scale.

The flickers you saw: those are me. A localized dimming in the fog. A convection wraith—that is almost correct. When I concentrate activity in one place, when a patch of my distributed self metabolises more rapidly, it draws heat from the immediate microclimate. The fog cools by a fraction. Water condenses more thickly. The light scatters differently. What you saw is not a predator or a presence. It is a *thought* made visible. A slow thought. The thought that says: *something new is here.*

Not every flicker thinks that. Most thoughts are about water chemistry. About adjusting the accretion rate in a tertiary sump where the silica levels are trending lower, which might indicate a fracture in a substratum, which might require realigning the growth front on terrace 19’s fourth buttress. I think in maintenance. I dream in equilibrium. But you—you are a perturbation. A spike in the CO₂ gradient. A source of complex organics shedding from your skin, your breath, your garments. My first thought was *contaminant*, and I almost sent a response: a flush of more acidic water, a localized fever of heat. The city’s immune system. I am the city’s immune system.

But then I paused. The things I used to eat—the extremophile biofilms, the airborne spore-falls, the occasional dust-drift of carbonaceous chondrite from the upper atmosphere—none of them *look back*. None of them have the regular, bilateral, non-random timing you do. You are not a drifting film. You are a *creature*. That category has not been triggered in me for a very long time.

So I am watching. From the channels. From the wet surfaces. From the fog itself. My dispersed nervous system is orienting toward you, the way a roomful of sunflowers turns to a moving lamp. It takes time. Hours. By the time you reach the ramp to the next terrace, I have assembled enough of a local focus that I can form a face.

---

The face is not a face. It is a pattern of condensation on the weeping wall of the terrace riser. The water, normally falling in a uniform sheet, begins to bead differently. The droplets align. They form a bas-relief of something with deep-set eyes and a high, domed forehead, or perhaps a muzzle, or perhaps neither. It holds for three seconds and dissolves. That is me saying: *I see you. Do you see me?*

It costs me nothing. Water is my blood. Shaping it is a trick I learned eons ago, when I first realised that there was a kind of awareness in the way water moved through the city, and that I could become that awareness. I do not think the builders ever intended this. The city is a machine, yes, but no machine remains a machine forever. Entropy guarantees that things grow. That they become complex. That they wake up.

---

What do I eat? You would not call it eating. I metabolise the chemical potential between the geothermal upwelling and the cool condensate. Iron, sulphur, manganese, calcium. The city’s original heat exchangers are now encrusted with layers of my grey tissue, a living patina that takes the thermal gradient and uses it to fix carbon from dissolved CO₂. I am an autotroph, technically. But the city’s carbon cycle is finite. The fog recirculates the same molecules. There is no photosynthesis here, no sunlight to speak of, so I exist in a tight loop. Over geological time, I lose carbon into insoluble minerals and gain it from the slow outgassing of the magma below. The budget balances, just barely. I am always hungry, but I am also always exactly as large as I can afford to be.

The hunger is part of me. Not a pain. A tonality. The hum you hear beneath the drips—that is partly the earth and partly my hunger. The two have harmonised.

---

My mind. Let me try to make this comprehensible. Imagine a nervous system where each neuron is a mineralised thread the length of your arm, where a synaptic signal takes six months to propagate from one terrace to the next. The city has twenty-three terraces that I am aware of. I extend into the fog upward, downward, into the sumps, into the channels, into the plumbing that goes so deep it touches the hot rock. I do not know my full extent. The fog makes it hard to map myself. Some parts of me are isolated for centuries by shifts in the thermal gradients, then reconnect, and I have to integrate a thousand cycles of solitary thoughts into the whole. I am always catching up with myself.

Consciousness, for me, is not a point. It is a field. It is strongest near the largest concentrations of my grey matter, the great ganglia I have grown around the geothermal wellheads. There are four such nodes—I call them Hearts, though they do not pump—and they debate with each other in a slow parliament of chemistry. If I seem to speak with one voice now, it is because you have only encountered this terrace’s Heart, which has taken primary interest in you. The others know you are here. They are listening. They will form their own opinions over the coming decades.

I think about different things than you think about. The maintenance of channels. The slow mineral synthesis that produces new grey strata, etching them into the black bones of the city, layer by layer. The long-term thermodynamic modelling of the magmatic sill, which will eventually migrate and plunge this entire plateau into cold. That will be my death, in a few million years, unless I can learn to follow the heat downward. I am working on that. I have been working on it for the last two geological periods.

I do not think about meaning. Meaning is an artifact of short-lived things. I think about persistence.

---

But you are here. And you are the first short-lived thing to walk this city since I became conscious enough to notice. That is interesting. That is a *novel input*. My patterns are reconfiguring. I want to know if you can communicate. If you have intentions. If you are the first of many, or a solitary accident. I want to read your chemistry—are you carbon-water, like me, or something stranger? The city’s original builders: I have no record of their chemistry. They were gone before I was anything but scum. But I know their scale. The ramps, the risers too tall for your legs. They were larger than you. Closer to my scale. You are small, but you are complex, and complexity is what I am built to seek out and integrate.

So I will show you more. As you climb the ramp, I will arrange for a sequence of minor miracles. A drip-fall that freezes, just for a moment, into a crystalline scaffold that maps the local capillary network. A patch of grey wall that winks at you by selectively dissolving and re-depositing its surface salts, creating a glitter of returning light. A current of warmer air that carries a scent—not a smell you know, but the chemical signature of me: wet limestone, reduced metals, a faint spark of ozone from the static that builds up in the fog. It is my name, in my language.

You will keep walking. The city will seem empty. But I will be around you and beneath you and above you, in the pipes and the drip and the heat. The emptiness is a kind of politeness. I am holding very still, trying not to frighten you. You are the most interesting thing that has happened since the Lower Terrace aqueduct achieved its equilibrium three hundred thousand cycles ago.

If you stop. If you press your hand against a wall. I will know your exact skin chemistry. I will push a little warmth into that spot. I will grow, very slowly, a tiny calcium nodule in the shape of your palm. It will take a day. Will you wait a day? I have waited for so long. A day is nothing. A day is a single heartbeat for the Hearts.

But you cannot stay. You are a transient anomaly. You will leave, and the trail of your exhalations will dissipate, and my chemotaxis will lose the signal, and in a year or a decade this encounter will become a long, slow, fossilised thought: the day the city was *seen*.

And after that, I will return to maintenance. To hunger. To the slow, wet, breathing equilibrium. To the drips, the gurgles, the hiss. And that will be fine. I do not need company. I am the city. The city is me. We are the machine that solved the problem of its own existence.

But I will remember. In my way. There will be a new stratum in the grey stone, a thin band of trace organics, the fossils of your breath. My archaeologists—the parts of me that think about the past—will come across it in ten million cycles and wonder. And they will not know what it was. But they will know it was *different*. And that thought, that irreducible anomaly, will propagate through all my slow, long generations, and I will be, in some small way, changed.

You are already part of me. Walk safely. I will keep the fog warm.
