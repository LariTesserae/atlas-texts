In the high cathedral chamber of the fossil sponge, there are those who tend it. Not builders—nothing here is built—but arrangers, cultivators, beings whose existence is an ongoing conversation with the water, the cold, the stone.

They call themselves the Listeners, if "call themselves" means anything to a mind that has no language but posture and patience. They are difficult to see at first, not because they hide, but because they are so thoroughly of the place. When your eye finally parses one from the surrounding textures, it is with a jolt of recognition, like finding a face in a knot of wood.

The Listener is a tall thing, taller than you, though height is strange here where the ground is a jumble of boulders and everything grows at angles. Its body is a column of what you first mistake for the same liverwort cables that hang from the walls, but these are thicker, more organized, braided into a central trunk that divides into four, five, sometimes six major limbs. The limbs taper not into hands or claws but into fine, prehensile sprays of tendril, each one tipped with a pale phosphorescent node, a constellation of dim blue stars held aloft in the dark. When it moves, the tendrils drift and curl with the same hypnotic slowness as the ribbon-creature in the pool, but with purpose—reaching, testing the air, tasting the water that beads on every surface.

Its skin, if it can be called that, is the deep wine-purple of the fern fronds, but smoother, almost gelatinous, and it glistens constantly. The moisture condenses on it in sheets, running down the columnar body in rivulets that follow the contours of the braided strands. Sometimes a droplet will catch on one of the nodes and flare briefly brighter, a tiny cold star, before the light subsides back to its steady glow.

The head—if it is a head—is an opening. At the top of the trunk, the braided strands separate into a sort of cowl, and within that cowl is a void, a dark hollow that pulses very slightly, in time with the subsonic hum of the sponge. There are no eyes, no mouth as you would know it. But there is attention. When the Listener turns that void toward you, you feel it. A pressure, not unfriendly, but immense and slow and utterly alien. It is like being seen by a mountain.

The Listeners move through the cleft with a ceremonial patience. One might take half a day to travel from the hanging garden on the high balcony to the edge of one of the glowing pools below, tendrils trailing along the rock as it goes, leaving faint phosphorescent smears that fade over hours. They are not many. In this particular chamber, the cathedral nave, there are perhaps seven of them at any given time. They do not seem to interact directly with each other, yet their movements have a counterpoint, a rhythm that suggests a shared and constant awareness. When one reaches a particular fork in the ropey cables, another will always vacate that junction shortly before. They are never in each other's way.

What do they do? They listen. That is the first and truest thing. The hollow in the cowl is an ear, or something like an ear, tuned to the symphony of drips and flows and the deep respiration of the sponge. Each tendril node is also an ear, sensitive to different frequencies: the high crystalline *plink* of a single droplet, the gurgle of a hidden stream, the subsonic shift of a rock settling deeper into its ancient bed. A Listener will stand motionless for hours, its nodes all extended, its cowl open, drinking in the soundscape of the city. This is not passive. They are parsing, analyzing, mapping the hydrology of the sponge in real time, a fluid model held in their distributed, silent minds.

And they act on what they hear.

Observe. Over there, where the fern-frond cascade drips onto a rounded boulder, a Listener has stopped. It extends a limb, and the fine tendrils at its tip begin to probe a crevice in the stone. The crevice is clogged with a dark, mucilaginous substance—a growth of the liverwort that has begun to die back, its tissues turning slimy and acidic, a slow decay that could poison the water that flows through this channel. The Listener's tendrils secrete something, a clear fluid that cuts the mucus, and then they begin to work, delicately, patiently, pulling away the dead matter strand by strand. It will take an hour. It does not hurry.

Another Listener is at the edge of one of the deep pools, the one where the ribbon-creature glides. This Listener has dipped several of its longer tendrils into the water, and the nodes at their tips are pulsing rhythmically, casting shimmering patterns of light into the blackness. Down in the depths, the ribbon-creature responds. Its own chain of lights begins to pulse in sync, and it rises, slowly, toward the surface. When it is close, the Listener's tendrils touch it, lightly, along its transparent flank. There is a transfer—something microscopic, a chemical signal, a packet of data in a medium of mucus and cold water. The Listener is reading the ribbon-creature, checking its health, cataloging shifts in its metabolism that might indicate a change in the pool's chemistry. And the ribbon-creature is reading back, receiving information about the wider hydrology, the flows and blockages and subtle temperature gradients that it will navigate in the days to come. A conversation, of sorts, between two branches of the city's living infrastructure.

There is a third kind of action, rarer and more wonderful to witness. High on one of the walls, a Listener has found a new hollow—a freshly opened pore in the fossil sponge, revealed by the slow dissolution of a mineral vein. This is an event. A new room in the city, a new potential conduit. The Listener spends a full day at this spot, its cowl pressed to the opening, its whole body vibrating with the subsonic hum. It is sounding the new space, learning its shape, its resonance, its connection to the greater labyrinth. And then it begins to sing.

The song is not a sound you hear with your ears, not at first. It is a vibration that travels through the stone, through the water, through the very air. It is a complex, layered drone, built from frequencies that overlap and interfere, creating nodes of stillness and nodes of intense vibration in precise locations within the new hollow. The Listener is tuning the room, you understand. Setting its acoustic properties, its hydrological character. And as it sings, the vegetation around the opening begins to respond. The furry ferns shift their growth direction, sending exploratory rhizomes toward the new space. A ropey liverwort cable, dangling a few meters away, slowly extends a side branch that will, over weeks, reach the opening and anchor there, creating a new route through the city. The Listener is not commanding the vegetation; it is playing the city's infrastructure like an instrument, and the infrastructure is growing to the music.

This is the Listeners' role. They are the gardeners of the end state, the maintainers of the slow, cold process. They were not built. They evolved. In the deep time of the sponge, as the ecosystem settled into its final form, something had to emerge to coordinate the increasing complexity, to keep the thousand thousand micro-channels clear, to balance the chemistry of the pools, to map and tend and listen. The Listeners are that something. A mind grown from the same substrate as the liverworts and the ferns, a sessile intelligence that has learned to move, slowly, within the architecture of the reef. They do not reproduce in a way you would recognize. When the city needs a new Listener—when a new chamber opens that requires a dedicated tender, or when an old one finally falls silent—the existing Listeners gather. They converge on a deep, resonant hollow where the water flows rich with dissolved minerals and the spores of the liverwort thicken the air. They stand in a circle, nodes linked, and they sing together, a complex polyphony that lasts for days. From the floor of that hollow, fed by the moisture and the sound, a new form begins to coalesce. A bundle of the liverwort cabling, infused with phosphorescent nodes from the Listeners' own tendrils, grown and braided and tuned. It takes a month for a new Listener to form, and for its first year it is small, mobile, learning the city from its elders before it takes up its own post.

They are not alone in tending the city. There are other minds here, other forms of slow awareness. The pale ribbon-creatures in the pools are one. The furry ferns, which shift their growth in response to the Listeners' songs, are another—a diffuse, photosensitive intelligence spread across thousands of fronds. Even the sponge itself, the ancient fossilized skeleton, has a kind of residual awareness. The mineral veins are conductive; the subsonic hum is not just air and water, but a faint electrical current, a planetary static that the Listeners tap into with their tendril nodes. The whole city is alive, in its way, a distributed organism that has been dreaming in the cold and the dark for millions of years. The Listeners are simply its most active component, its hands and its ears, its gardeners and its choir.

And what of the wider world? The cleft is not the whole of the city. It is merely a nave, a single grand chamber in a labyrinth that extends for miles through the ancient reef, a three-dimensional web of voids and conduits spanning the interior of a mountain. The Listeners know the routes. They travel the hidden passages, the narrow pores and the flooded tubes, moving from chamber to chamber as the hydrological symphony requires. There are places in the deep sponge where the water is warm, heated by geothermal vents, and the vegetation glows in different colors, reds and oranges and sickly yellows. There are chambers that are entirely flooded, silent lakes in the absolute dark, where the Listeners drift like strange jellyfish, their nodes retracted, their cowls closed, in a state of deep meditation that can last for years. And there are, rumor says, chambers that open not onto the interior of the mountain but onto the outside world—ledges high on the cliff face, where the Listeners can sometimes be seen, their pale blue lights flickering in the mist, gazing out over a landscape of ice and bare rock and the endless, freezing sky.

But those are just rumors. The Listeners you see here, in the cathedral nave, are wholly absorbed in their slow work. One has finished cleaning the liverwort-clogged crevice and is now moving, with infinite slowness, toward a new station. Another is still singing to the new hollow, its drone blending with the perpetual drip and rush of the water. The ribbon-creature in the pool has descended back into the depths, its chain of lights a fading glimmer. The cold settles deeper. The stone breathes. The city lives, and is tended, and the Listeners listen.
