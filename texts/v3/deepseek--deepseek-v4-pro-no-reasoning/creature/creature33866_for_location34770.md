It takes a long moment of searching before you see one—not because it is hidden, but because your eyes have been sliding off it, mistaking it for just another boulder among the frost-shattered scree. The shape is too still, too patient, too perfectly integrated into the logic of this weeping, living ridge.

She—and the designation feels correct, though you couldn’t say why—is a figure of moss and slow motion. Her form is broadly humanoid, but only in the way a wind-scoured standing stone is humanoid: a suggestion, a memory of silhouette. The body is dense, compact, built low to the ground like something that has spent centuries learning to present the smallest possible profile to the cold. She is covered head to foot in the same dark burgundy cryptogamic crust that drapes the rocks, but on her it is thicker, more established, a living pelt that has colonized her surface over so many generations that the boundary between flesh and flora has become a matter of academic debate, long since abandoned. Over this, the fine white sky-snow has settled in the creases and hollows, dusting her like the first flouring of frost on old bark.

She is moving. It is barely perceptible, a motion measured in hours, not minutes—a slight, tectonic shift of weight from one thick, root-like limb to another. She is working, and has been working for a very long time.

The task occupies her hands. They are broad, four-fingered, the digits spatulate and worn smooth as river stones from use. With them, she is carefully, endlessly, laying out a pattern in the scree. She selects stones one by one—not the broken, sharp-edged shards that litter the ridge, but the rare, water-tumbled pebbles that must have been carried up from the troughs below, cupped in her palms over journeys of days. These she places in spiraling curves, in radial bursts that echo the branching of the giant trees, in whorls that map onto nothing you recognize but which feel, viscerally, like language. The pattern is enormous, covering hundreds of square feet, and so faint you could only see it from exactly this angle, in this exact quality of light. It is a text written to be read by the sky-sifters overhead, or perhaps by the cloud-lid itself.

From the back of her broad head, a structure emerges—a low, shelving growth terrace of what might be keratin or might be chitin, horizontal plates that fan outward like the fruiting body of a fungus. The plates are thin-edged and semi-translucent, catching the faint bioluminescence of the hanging flowers on the nearest tree-giant and scattering it in soft, color-shifted tones. Blue-green. Amber. A deep, wet purple. The colors shift slowly, in time with her breathing, or with thoughts that move at a pace your nervous system cannot parse. It is a signal, you realize. A beacon. A slow, steady display meant to be seen from a great distance across the open ridgeline country.

She is waiting for someone. Or something. Or she is calling them, and has been calling for so long that the call has become indistinguishable from simply being. From living.

The cold has become a part of her metabolism. You can see it in the way the moisture in her breath—if she breathes—does not plume but simply beads and drips along the moss of her jaw, in the way her movements never betray a shiver. She is endothermic in the way a compost heap is endothermic, a slow, internal fermentation of the cryptogamic crust digesting her outer layers as fast as she grows them, releasing a steady, low-grade warmth that keeps the frost at bay. The sky-snow that lands on her shoulders does not melt; it simply sifts into the spaces between the moss filaments, becomes part of the accretion, the slow accumulation of matter from the cloud-lid.

The only part of her that reads as definitively, originally animal is her eyes. They are large, dark, set deep in sockets ringed with the same shelf-terrace plates, and they reflect the light in a way that moss does not. They are watching you. They have been watching you for a while, you realize, without interest or alarm, but with a steady, geological attention. You are a new stone in the pattern. A variable to be placed, eventually, into the right spiral, the right whorl. But not yet. There is so much still to lay out, and she has been at it since before the memory of whatever brought you here, and she will be at it long after you have gone.

One of her hands pauses over a particular pebble. It is pale, almost white, veined with something dark. She lifts it, turns it over with a slowness that suggests enormous deliberation. Then she sets it down, not in the pattern, but to one side. Not yet. Not this one. The motion is the most definitive thing you have seen her do, a clear and deliberate refusal. She resumes her work.

Above her, one of the sky-sifters banks, its running lights tracing a slow arc across the cloud-lid’s dim ceiling. The color of her head-plates shifts—a wash of emerald, then blue—in a sequence that might be greeting, or acknowledgement, or simply the light of one slow, enduring machine recognizing another.
