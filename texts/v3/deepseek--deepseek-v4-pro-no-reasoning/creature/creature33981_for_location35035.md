The tenders do not call themselves anything. They have no need among themselves for names, and there has never been anyone else to ask.

They are not the makers of this place. The distinction is crucial—they know it in the way you know the weight of your own bones—though they could not explain how they know, or when the knowing arrived. They emerged into the valley already shaped, already placed, already moving through the slow rhythms of maintenance that constitute their entire existence. If there was a before, it is not remembered. If there is an after, it is not considered.

Their bodies are built for the work and for the heat. They stand roughly the height of a human waist, though they rarely stand fully upright. Their default posture is a kind of ready crouch, knees bent, weight forward, long arms loosely folded or resting on the ground. The arms are their most articulate feature—slender, multi-jointed, with six elongated fingers on each hand, the two outer fingers opposable, so that each hand is two hands, or a hand and its mirror, capable of wrapping around a task from both sides at once. Their skin is the color and approximate texture of the dried mud paths, a grey-brown that shifts subtly toward rose in the heat, and it hangs in soft, fine wrinkles at every joint, accordioning neatly when they move. Their faces are broad and shallow, with wide-set eyes the colour of the algae-dark water in the deepest plots, and their mouths are small, lipless, perpetually set in a line that suggests neither contentment nor discontent but a complete, undivided attention to what is in front of them.

They breathe the thick air without effort, drawing it in through skin as much as through the small vertical slits along their ribs. The near-drinkable humidity sustains them; they do not swallow water, they absorb it. Their bodies hold it in layers of spongy tissue beneath the skin, and when they move, there is a faint, almost imperceptible slosh, a liquid weight shifting inside them. This internal water is their only clock. They can feel it deplete, slowly, over what might be cycles of light, and they know to return to the sweating cisterns, to press their bodies against the cool clay and let the condensed moisture seep back into them. There is no urgency in this. The cisterns never fail. The tenders never rush.

They move through the checkerboard pools and along the curved paths with a gait that is part walk, part flow—their legs are short and digitigrade, ending in broad, splayed feet with thin webbing between the toes that spreads their weight so they leave no imprint on the mud-and-shell paths. They can move quickly when speed is called for, but speed is almost never called for. The vast majority of their movement is a slow, continuous drifting, a redistribution of bodies across the valley floor in patterns that have repeated so many times they have become a kind of breathing.

Each tender knows its plot. Not in the sense of ownership—nothing here is owned—but in the sense of a relationship that cannot be transferred. A tender will spend countless cycles tending the same arrangement of water plots, the same curve of path, the same cluster of cisterns. It learns the exact depth of each pool, the particular way the algae grows along the western seam, the individual creature that floats with its eyes barely breaching the surface in the third plot from the center. It knows when the hanging sheets need straightening, when a bowl on the concentric-ring platform has shifted out of alignment. It knows these things the way you know when your own hair has fallen across your forehead—as a slight wrongness, a misalignment that calls for adjustment without rising to the level of conscious thought.

The work itself is quiet and repetitive and total. They tend the plots, skimming the surface of the water with the long inner fingers to remove the thin, translucent film that forms occasionally, a byproduct of the creatures' respiration. They gather this film into small balls and carry it to the platforms, where it is pressed into the bowls among the pale, glistening things—more of the same material, aged and cured until it takes on a nacreous sheen, though what purpose it serves, or what the bowls are for, is not a question that arises. They maintain the raised seams between plots, patting new mud-and-shell mixture into any crack or erosion, smoothing it with the flat of their palm, leaving behind the faint texture of their own skin. They check the hanging sheets, those hundreds of translucent panels on their lashed-pole frames, touching each one lightly to feel its tension, adjusting the ties when the material has stretched. The sheets are important. The tenders understand this at a level deeper than understanding. When a sheet is perfectly tuned, it vibrates in response to the almost-breeze, producing a frequency that keeps the white sky from pressing too close.

And they tend the creatures.

The floating ones in the pools—the terracotta-colored, paddle-tailed things with their gold-rimmed eyes—are not pets, not livestock, not charges. They are closer to partners, or perhaps to extensions of the tender's own awareness. Each tender's plots contain a specific number of them, always the same number, and the tender knows each one individually. They check the creatures by eye, counting, observing their spacing, watching for any sign of distress or illness. Distress is rare. Illness, rarer still. But occasionally a creature will drift too close to its neighbor, or fail to adjust when the others reposition. The tender will enter the water then, sliding in without a ripple, and gently guide the creature back to its proper place with an open palm, never gripping, never restraining. The creature's skin is warm and smooth and slightly electric to the touch, and in that moment of contact, a small exchange occurs—a kind of data transfer, a sharing of internal states. The tender feels the creature's slow contentment, its simple binary of well/unwell. The creature feels the tender's attention, its steady assurance that all is in order.

The segmented carriers, the long multi-legged creatures with their garden-backs, require a different kind of attention. They are slow to the point of stillness, but they are not idle. Their internal processes are complex and ongoing, and the gardens they carry are not decorative. They are working models, miniature experiments in the relationship between the living and the made. The grey-green lichen on their backs processes the thick air, breaking down compounds that the tenders cannot name but whose presence they can sense, a background tang that sharpens when a garden has been neglected. The tenders tend these gardens with their fine fingers, scraping lichen where it grows too thick, transplanting the orange fruiting bodies from one carrier to another according to patterns they have always known. The carriers do not acknowledge this ministration. They continue their barely-perceptible progress along the paths, carrying their landscapes from one zone to another, and the tenders let them. The carriers know where they need to go. The tenders' role is only to ensure they arrive in a condition to do whatever it is they do when they arrive.

The work never ends, but it never accumulates, either. There is no backlog, no crisis, no season of urgency. The valley is maintained at a perfect equilibrium, and the tenders' labor is precisely enough to sustain that equilibrium. They do not experience fatigue in the way that implies a debt to be repaid. Their effort is steady and distributed, and when a tender's internal water runs low, it simply drifts toward a cistern and leans against it, absorbing moisture through its skin while it watches the plots, still counting, still observing. A tender at rest is indistinguishable from a tender at work, only slightly more still.

They communicate, but not with sound. Their mouths do not open easily, and their vocal apparatus is minimal—a soft exhalation, a faint hum that blends with the deep hum of the valley itself. Their true communication happens through touch and proximity. When two tenders meet on the curved paths, they pause and extend their multi-jointed arms, intertwining fingers briefly. In that contact, a wealth of information passes: the state of the plots in one's zone, the behavior of a particular creature, the tension of a sheet, the slight shift in the taste of the air. There are no words in this exchange, no symbols. It is direct transfer of experience, tender to tender, a sharing of the valley's condition across its entire network of care. If something is amiss in one zone, all the tenders will know it within a few exchanges, and a few will detach from their own routes to assist. This happens perhaps once in a thousand cycles, if that. The valley is stable. The tenders' network is stable. The two stabilities reinforce each other.

They have no social structure that maps onto hierarchy. No tender leads. No tender follows. Their roles are differentiated by zone but not by rank, and the zones themselves are not identical—some have more pools, others have more carriers, still others are dominated by the sheet-frames or the cisterns—but no zone is preferable. The tenders do not desire. They do not envy. They do not imagine a different arrangement. Their minds, if minds is the right word, are perfectly fitted to their world. To alter one would be to alter the other, and neither alteration occurs to them.

They reproduce. This is one of the few things that might be called an event. It happens infrequently, at intervals that cannot be predicted, triggered perhaps by some subtle shift in the valley's equilibrium that signals a need for more hands. A single tender will detach from its zone and make its way to the central platform with its concentric rings of bowls. There, it will select a bowl containing the pale, glistening substance—aged and matured over countless cycles—and carry it to a specific pool, one of the deeper ones, where the algae grows thickest. The tender enters the water with the bowl, submerges it, and waits. The process is slow. The substance in the bowl begins to swell and differentiate in the warm water, drawing minerals from the algae, organizing itself around a core of something—an intention, a template, a memory of tender-form. Over many cycles, the swelling mass takes shape: the crouched posture, the multi-jointed arms, the broad feet, the wide-set eyes. When it is ready, the new tender simply rises from the pool and stands on the path, already knowing what it is and what it must do. The old tender returns to its zone. The new tender takes up a position in an area where the network has registered a slight thinning of attention. No instruction is necessary. The knowledge is in the water, in the substance, in the valley itself.

And the old tenders do not die. They wear, but they wear slowly. Over a span of time that has no measure, a tender's skin will grow thinner, more translucent, showing the slow movement of its internal water like a lamp behind oiled paper. Its movements will slow beyond their usual slowness. It will spend more time leaning against the cisterns, less time on the paths. Eventually, it will simply stop at the edge of its deepest plot and lower itself into the water, settling on the bottom among the algae. Its body will soften and lose its boundaries, merging gradually with the warm, bitter-sweet water, its substance returning to the pool from which new tenders are drawn. The creatures in that pool will adjust their spacing to accommodate the tender's dissolution, a slow, respectful rearrangement of their floating positions. And somewhere, a new tender will rise.

They do not think of this as death. They do not think of it at all. The tender who has dissolved continues in the water, in the algae, in the new tender who will eventually rise from that same pool. The continuity is absolute because nothing is lost and nothing is gained. The number of tenders varies within a narrow range, never more than a certain count, never fewer. The valley requires a particular amount of attention, and it gets exactly that amount.

The only thing that resembles a break in their placid existence is the edge of the valley. The rim. The place where the geometry fails to resolve. The tenders approach the rim only rarely, and only when the maintenance of the periphery requires it—to check a path that seems to fray, to retrieve a carrier that has wandered too close to the boundary. When they do, they move with a care that borders on reverence, or perhaps on fear. Their eyes, usually calm, grow wide and fixed. Their internal water seems to grow heavier inside them, a counterbalance against the wrongness of the rim. They do not cross it. They cannot cross it. The rim is the edge of the made world, and beyond it is something that the tenders' minds cannot accommodate. They do not know what it is. They know only that it is not theirs to tend.

One of their number, more often than the others, patrols the circumference. This one is slightly larger, or perhaps just older—its skin is more translucent, its movements even slower. It has been tending the rim for as long as any tender can remember, which is to say, for as long as the tenders have existed. It checks the paths that spiral outward, the ones that seem to continue into the white sky in a direction that is not a direction. It makes small adjustments to the mud-and-shell compound at the very edge, where the substance of the valley meets the no-substance of the beyond. And sometimes, it pauses and lifts its face toward the whiteness, its wide eyes reflecting the directionless light, its mouth slightly open in what might be wonder or might be a question that has no words.

The other tenders leave it to its work. They do not understand the rim-tender's role, but they do not need to understand. It is enough that the rim holds, that the valley remains complete, that the circuits of care continue uninterrupted. They return to their plots, their cisterns, their sheets, their creatures. The water drops fall without pattern. The carriers inch forward under their gardens. The floating ones adjust their spacing by imperceptible degrees. And the tenders tend, in the heat that never breaks, in the light that casts no shadow, in a world that reached its final state and will not alter, held in perfect suspension by the hands that maintain it, hands made for nothing else, asking nothing else, receiving nothing else but the steady, liquid hum of existence continuing.
