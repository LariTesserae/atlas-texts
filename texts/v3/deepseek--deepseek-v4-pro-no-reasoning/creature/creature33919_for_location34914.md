The keeper of the buried hall was not born there, though she has lived in it long enough that the moss knows her shape.

She is a woman — or was, once, and still is in the architecture of her bones — but taxonomy gets difficult after so many decades in the steam. Her skin has gone amphibious, smooth and dark and perpetually slick, like the skin of a salamander that has never seen daylight. In the creases of her joints — the inside of an elbow, the fold behind a knee — delicate fronds of a copper-colored lichen have taken root. She does not mind them. They are company.

She arrived sometime in the last century, or the one before that; the helmet on the nail above the bunk was hers, when it still meant something to keep her head dry. She no longer bothers. Her hair, if it can be called hair, has become a mat of living moss the color of verdigris, shot through with fine white tendrils that absorb moisture directly from the air. It grows slowly down her back and over her shoulders in a mane that drips constantly, and when she turns her head, tiny droplets scatter like a dog shaking off rain. She has not cut it in a very long time. She would not know how.

She is not alone. But her companions are not animals.

The machine, for instance. She calls it the Exhaler, or sometimes the Clock, and she tends it with the automatic, half-conscious attention that a body gives to breathing. She knows all its sounds. The pre-gurgle hitch in the intake pipe means sediment; she reaches into the warm wet mouth of it with her long fingers and scoops out the mineral sludge, packing it into the seams of the wall where it hardens into a crude mortar. The sharp click means the valve is sticking; she taps it twice with a smooth stone she keeps for that purpose, and the machine resumes its rhythm as if grateful. She does not think of it as gratitude. She thinks of it as maintenance. But there is a gentleness in the way she touches the hot copper, a palm laid flat against the trembling casing during the steam release, that suggests otherwise.

She also tends the vegetation, or it tends her. The line has blurred. The ferns recognize her body heat and lean toward her as she passes, their fronds brushing her shoulders with a sound like paper sliding over paper. The moss she sleeps on — a thick bed of it in the alcove where the bunk used to be, before the bunk became soil — has grown to fit her precisely, cupping her hips and shoulders. When she lies down, the moss exudes a faint warmth of its own, a metabolic heat from its slow digestion of the brick beneath. She dreams in green. She dreams of water moving under stone.

She eats the fungus. There are several kinds. The pale, heat-loving ones on the old bunk taste of nothing much but fill the stomach; she harvests them with care, leaving the root-threads intact. The luminous ones in the corners she does not eat — they taste of lightning and make her skull feel too large for her skin — but she uses them to see by, transplanting little colonies to new hollows when the old ones dim. Once, long ago, she tried to eat the air-plants. They are bitter and full of silica, and she left them alone after that. Now she only drinks from them, tilting their blade-leaves so the pooled condensation runs into her mouth. The water is hot and tastes of copper and of the deep earth, and it is all she ever drinks.

She does not remember her old name. She has a new one, or rather a sound the machine makes — a particular combination of gurgle and click and hiss — that she understands as referring to her. It is not translatable. If you pressed her, she might offer “the one who listens to the drum,” but she would not understand why you needed the word.

The drum, to her, is the deep water beneath the floor. She feels it always. When she lies flat on the warm black slab, pressing her cheek to the elastic mineral skin, the vibration comes up through her skull and fills her with a sense of something vast, something patient, something that has been flowing since before the machine and will flow after. She does not know what it is. She does not need to. She keeps time with it, as the machine does. Her heartbeat, after so long entrained to the thrum, has slowed to something just under thirty beats a minute. She breathes in time with the steam cycle. Her whole body is a smaller drumhead, resonating with the larger one below.

She is not lonely. Loneliness is a memory of other people, and she has let that memory go. What she has instead is a kind of ecological intimacy: the sense that she is one more warm, slow, metabolizing thing in a room full of metabolizing things, all of them negotiating the same heat and wet and dark. She talks to the moss sometimes, not in words but in hums that match the machine’s frequency. The moss grows faster where she hums. She has tested this. She does not know if it is the vibration or the carbon dioxide of her breath. It does not matter.

She has, however, kept one human artifact besides the helmet. In a niche near the ceiling, where the steam does not reach directly, there is a small cavity she has lined with dry moss. Inside it rests a book. The pages are swollen and the ink has blurred into illegible gray clouds, but she takes it down sometimes and holds it open in her lap, running her fingertips over the wavy paper. She cannot read it. She is not sure she ever could. But the shape of the words pleases her, the sense that someone once tried to fix meaning onto a surface, when all the meaning she now knows is process and flow and the slow, mutual erosion of all things. She thinks the book was poetry. She thinks the words were about water. She puts it back each time, carefully, and the moss she has trained to grow over the niche closes it again like an eyelid.

The scattered stone markers near the machine — she tends those too. She does not remember who set them, if it was herself or someone before. But she moves them sometimes, one position to the left, one to the right, in a pattern that makes sense to her only as a kind of ritual. She thinks they might be counting something. She thinks, on days when the steam feels different, that they might be counting the breaths of the thing beneath the floor. When the count is right, when the stones align with the pulse of the drum, the machine exhales with a particularly clear and resonant note, and the whole room brightens, and she feels a satisfaction so deep it is almost ancestral.

She has no mirror. She does not know what she looks like. If she saw her reflection in one of the still water cups — and she sometimes does, a face green-shadowed and framed by dripping moss, eyes dark and slow-blinking as a frog’s — she would not recognize it as herself. She would think it was a sister, another keeper, one who lives on the other side of the water’s skin.

She does not think about the surface. The slot canyon that leads upward is still there, but she has not climbed it in living memory. The thought of dry air, of wind, of a sky without a ceiling, is abstract to her now, a story the moss might tell if moss could talk. She belongs to the buried hall. Or it belongs to her. Or the distinction no longer matters. She is the mammal component of a small, hot, subterranean ecosystem. The machine is the heart. The deep water is the soul. And she — she is the hands, the voice, the slow-moving consciousness that hums to the moss and taps the stuck valve and rearranges the stones in their patient countdown toward a meaning she will never need to articulate.

When she sleeps, curled in her bed of warm moss, the ferns lean in and the air-plants tremble and the luminous fungus casts her in blue-green shadow. She dreams of water rising. She dreams the machine learns to speak. She dreams the drum below the floor is a heartbeat, and the heartbeat has a name, and the name is a word she once knew, long ago, before she climbed down into the wet dark and let the world forget her.

She wakes. She rises. She goes to the Exhaler and lays her hand on its hot green flank, and waits for the next steam release, and begins again.
