There is a woman called Signe who sweeps the beetles.

She is not from here—none of them are, not really, but Signe is less *from here* than most. She came down with the third crew, a geologist by training, though no one has seen her do geology in months. What she does now is walk the main concourse with a push-broom made from a salvaged ventilation grate and the handle of a broken jackhammer, and she sweeps. The beetles part before her broom like a tide that has learned her rhythm. She does not kill them—or rather, she does not kill them *on purpose*, but the broom is heavy and the floor is rough, and some of them are always crushed into the wet stone, leaving dark smears that dissolve into the puddles within the hour. Signe has stopped noticing this. She has stopped noticing a great many things.

She wears canvas trousers stiff with moisture and a shirt that was once yellow but has become the color of old newspapers. Her hair is cut short and uneven, done by her own hand with a utility knife, because the heat makes anything longer unbearable. Her face is perpetually sheened with sweat and the mineral film that settles on everything down here, and she has developed a habit of wiping her forehead with the back of her wrist, leaving faint streaks of crushed beetle hull across her brow. She does not know this. The others do, but they do not tell her.

Signe was part of the team that mapped the first water channels—the ones that now run silent and dark behind the prefab housing, too warm to be called cold, too mineral-heavy to be called fresh. She knows the sound of every drip in the main junction, the particular *plink* of the one that falls from the ventilator louver above the third lamp, the heavier *tock* near the machine shop that lands in a puddle already dimpled by its own history. She knows which sections of the floor have grown soft, where the water table has been quietly eating the stone from below. She could tell you, if you asked, that the settlement is sinking—not quickly, not visibly, but by millimeters a year, the way a body settles into a bed. But you would have to ask, and no one does.

She sleeps in one of the two glowing housing units, the one nearer the geothermal plant, because she has learned to sleep through the turbine whine and now finds the silence of the far tunnels more disturbing than the noise. Her unit is sparse: a cot, a footlocker, a single shelf with three books she has read to the point of memorization, their pages swollen from the humidity until they no longer close properly. One is a field guide to insects. She does not know why she keeps it.

There is something else in the settlement, something that shares the space with the beetles and the workers and the slow drip of the walls.

It lives in the far end of a side passage, near the face of the excavation, where the tram-line ends and the new-cut stone is stacked in dark, wet blocks. The passage is marked with hazard tape, though the tape is old and sagging now, and the sign that says **UNSAFE** has been colonized by a crust of beetles, their bodies forming a living, shifting mosaic that obscures the letters until the word becomes a pattern, a texture, a thing with no meaning. No one goes to the face anymore—not since the ground there began to breathe. That is the word the workers use: *breathe*. The floor of the excavation face lifts and falls in a rhythm too slow to see, too steady to ignore, and the cracks in the rock exhale a heat that is not from the geothermal plant but from somewhere deeper, older, more patient. The air there is so wet it feels like drowning, and the beetles do not go there. That is the most telling thing. The beetles—those single-minded, overwhelming, indifferent billions—avoid the face entirely. The floor is bare stone, wet and gleaming, with no chitin drift, no living carpet. Just the slow rise and fall of the rock, and the sound of something shifting beneath the world.

Signe has seen it. Once. She walked to the face in the quiet hour between shifts, when the tram was still and the workers were in their bunks and the only sound was the drip and the susurrus of the beetles—which stopped, she realized, at the edge of the passage, as if a line had been drawn in the condensation. She stepped over that line without thinking, because her mind was elsewhere, because she had been thinking about the water table and the slow sinking and the way her books would never dry. And she stood at the face, where the rock was not rock but a membrane of something dark and porous, and she watched it breathe.

It breathed in, and the cracks in the floor closed like wounds healing. It breathed out, and the cracks opened, and a warmth came up from them that smelled of rain on hot stone and the inside of a mouth. And Signe understood, in that moment, that the settlement was not built *into* the rock. It was built *onto* something. Something that had been here before the drills, before the lamps, before the beetles. Something that was not angry, not patient, not aware in any way she could name—but *present*, vast and slow and alive in a way that made the word *alive* feel like a child’s drawing of a fire.

She did not tell anyone. What would she say? That the mountain breathes? That the beetles know something the humans do not? That the water table is not water at all, but sweat, or saliva, or tears—some secretion of a thing too large to notice them? No. She went back to her unit, and she lay on her cot, and she listened to the drip and the turbine whine and the dry shiver of a billion beetles, and she thought: *We are the infestation.*

Now she sweeps.

She sweeps the main concourse from the geothermal plant to the tram junction, and she does not think about the face. She does not think about the way the floor has begun to slope—imperceptibly, but *down*, always down, toward the passage where the rock breathes. She does not think about the worker named Idris who went to the face last week to retrieve a lost wrench and came back with his hands shaking, unable to say what he had seen, only that the floor had *welcomed* him, that the stone had given way beneath his boots like mud but not mud, like flesh but not flesh, and that he had felt, for one terrible moment, that he was standing in the palm of something.

She sweeps, and the beetles part before her broom, and the condensation falls from the ceiling like a blessing or a curse, depending on the hour.

The settlement is new. The walls still weep. The geothermal plant still thrumms its constant hymn. Three hundred meters above, the surface is a rumor, a memory of light and dry air and the ordinary violence of weather. Down here, there is no weather—only heat, and moisture, and the slow, wet darkness that presses in from all sides. And at the center of it, Signe sweeps, because someone must, because the beetles will not stop, because the act of pushing them aside is the only proof she has that she is still *something*—still solid, still separate, still a woman with a broom and not a ghost in a half-built tomb.

And sometimes, in the quiet hour between shifts, she stops.

She stands in the middle of the main concourse, her broom resting against her shoulder, and she listens. To the drip. To the whine. To the billions of tiny legs. And underneath it all, so low it is less a sound than a pressure, a vibration that lives in the soles of her boots and the base of her skull, she hears it:

The slow, patient, indifferent breath of the thing below.

And she thinks, *We are not the first to live here.* And then, because she is a scientist, because she cannot help herself: *We will not be the last.*

Then she sweeps again, and the beetles close behind her, and the darkness does not care.
