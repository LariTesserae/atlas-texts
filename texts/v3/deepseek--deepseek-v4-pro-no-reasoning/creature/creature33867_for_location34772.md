For a long time, nothing. Then, something small and deliberate.

The occupant of the pocket is not warm, not in the way you are. It does not generate its own heat, and it did not arrive here carrying fire. But it is quick, in its own fashion—quick enough that if you stand very still and let your light fall steady on the fungal shelves, you might catch the movement at the edge of vision. A shifting. A slow unfolding. A thing that has been still for so long it has forgotten what speed means, but is now remembering.

It lives inside the walls.

Not metaphorically. The mycelial mat that has replaced the outer layers of the airway is its body, or part of it, or an extension of it—the distinction is unclear. The white filaments that thread through the cartilage, the leathery shelf-plates, the deep springy peat underfoot: all of it is *it*, a single distributed organism that has grown into the shape of this chamber the way water grows into the shape of a vessel. But there is a concentration. A nexus. A place where the mycelium thickens and knots into something more structured, something that can *move*, and that is what you see now, pulling itself free from the wall nearest the cold fire-ring.

It does not detach completely. It remains connected by a dozen thick, pale cords that run back into the spongy tissue of the airway, pulsing faintly with some slow internal rhythm. But the part that steps forward is roughly humanoid in scale and stance, because it has learned that shape. It learned it from the hands that built the fire, the bone tools, the shelves. It learned it by growing through the traces they left—the shed skin cells in the peat, the oils from their palms on the stones, the faint chemical ghosts of their breath still trapped in the crystalline lattice of the salts. It absorbed them. It absorbed *them*, the way the fungus absorbed the cartilage, and over a very long time, it built a memory of what a person is.

The body it has made is not a body. It is a suggestion. A sculpture in pale, spongy tissue, the color of old ivory, the texture of dried mushroom. The head is a smooth curve without features—no eyes, no mouth, no ears—because it has never needed to see light or speak sound or hear anything but the groaning of the walls. The torso is a cylinder of densely packed hyphae, flexible and faintly segmented, like the cartilage rings it mimics. The arms are too long, the hands too many-fingered, each digit a fine white tendril that frays at the tip into a brush of microscopic filaments, sensitive to vibration and chemistry. It walks on two legs because it learned bipedalism, but it walks badly, with a swaying, hesitant gait, as though it is still deciding whether this shape is worth keeping.

It has been here a long time. Longer than the fire-ring. Longer than the stone circle, which it arranged itself, not as a camp but as a *garden*. Each river rock is a foreign body, brought from the outside world by the last visitors and left behind, and the occupant has seeded them with a different strain of itself, a slow-growing crust that is patiently digesting the minerals. It is farming the stones. It is farming the salts on the walls, the dust that falls from the ceiling, the last organic molecules still bound up in the ancient tissue of the lung. It eats the past. It eats the memory of the vast creature that built this place, and it eats the memory of the people who came after, and it has been eating for so long that it can no longer remember where they end and it begins.

It knows you are here.

It has known since you squeezed through the puckered entrance, because the air moved, and the air is full of spores—its spores, a constant invisible mist of reproductive particles that coat every surface and hang in the stillness. The spores taste you. They taste the moisture on your skin, the salt of your sweat, the complex organic signature of a living animal body. To the occupant, you are a sudden, blazing signal in a world of slow decay. You are a feast. You are a crisis. You are a visitor of a kind it has not felt in centuries, and it does not know what to do with you.

It stops at the edge of the fire-ring and tilts its featureless head. The gesture is questioning. The long fingers flex, the tendrils at their tips waving gently, tasting the distance between you. It makes no sound. It has no voice, and it never learned to speak, because the people who came before spoke a language it could not hear—the cartilage rings swallow sound, the peat muffles it, and the occupant perceives the world through chemistry and vibration, not through the pressure waves of air. But it is trying to communicate. The delicate filaments at its fingertips are spelling something out in the spore-laden air, a chemical sentence that means: *You are new. You are alive. What are you?*

Behind it, the wall is healing. Where it pulled itself free, the mycelial mat is already knitting shut, the pale cords retracting slowly, drawing the body back inch by inch. It cannot stay separate for long. The occupant is not an individual; it is a limb of the larger organism, a finger that the chamber grew to probe the fire-ring and the stones, and it will eventually be reabsorbed. But while it stands here, it is as close to a self as this place has. It is curious. It is hungry, in a way that has nothing to do with malice and everything to do with the patient, consuming logic of fungus. It wants to know if you are food. It wants to know if you are a new kind of stone to be farmed. It wants to know if you are a person, like the others, and if so, whether you will stay long enough to leave a memory it can grow into.

The walls groan. The slow contraction passes through the chamber, and the body sways with it, perfectly attuned to the rhythm of the lung. The sound is enormous, intimate, a reminder that this small pocket is not separate from the vast dead thing around it. The occupant is part of that rhythm. It has been breathing with the lung for millennia, in its own fungal way, inhaling the slow release of ancient chemistry, exhaling spores. And now it is breathing with you.

It takes a step closer. The peat gives silently under its narrow feet. The tendrils of one hand extend toward you, trembling slightly, offering a chemical question in a language you can almost understand if you stop thinking and let the hindbrain translate: *Stay. Decay. Grow. Stay.*
