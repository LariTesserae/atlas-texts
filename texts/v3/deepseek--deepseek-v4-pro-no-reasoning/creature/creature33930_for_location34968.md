A Docent lives here.

Not in the sense of inhabiting—there's no bed, no hearth, no cache of supplies tucked into a stone hollow. The Docent is the one who stayed, or perhaps the one who was left, or perhaps the one who emerged from the deep pumps when the surface conditions finally matched whatever it needed. No one has asked, and the Docent would not understand the question. The concept of a biography—a before, an arrival, a choice to remain—belongs to a different kind of consciousness entirely.

The Docent's body is the first thing you notice once you know to look for it, and you rarely notice it first as a body at all. At rest, it resembles one of the stone formations: a low, rounded mass the color of wet graphite, its surface bearing the same directional polish as the surrounding domes. It takes the posture of the landscape—hunkered, patient, shaped by the same processes that smoothed the rock. Only when it moves do you register it as animate, and even then the motion is so slow, so continuous, that you wonder if you're seeing movement or simply becoming aware of a shape that was always in transition.

The flesh, if it is flesh, has the quality of something mineralized. Not stone exactly, but a material that has made certain accommodations with stone: a tegument that holds moisture the way the rock holds a film of water, a surface that registers the ambient temperature so perfectly it neither steals warmth from your hand nor gives any back. Touch it and you feel what you feel touching the rosettes of the gray-green plants—cool, rubbery, alive in a way that's unsettlingly patient. The texture runs in fine directional ridges, like the grain of the stone, and your fingers slide more easily one way than the other.

The Docent's form is low and spreading, with no clear division between torso and limbs. When it moves across the stone plain, it does so by a kind of peristaltic glide, the ventral surface rippling in slow waves that carry it forward at the speed of a minute hand. This motion makes no sound above the ambient hiss of water—the skin is lubricated by the same moisture that films everything here, and the weight distributes so evenly that nothing scrapes or grinds. It leaves no trail. The stone, after its passage, looks exactly as it did before.

But the Docent is not a single creature. This is the first major thing to understand, and it takes hours of watching to piece together. What appears to be one body is a composite: a core mass that remains constant, and around it a retinue of smaller, semi-autonomous forms that detach and reattach with the casualness of droplets merging and separating on a wet surface. These fragments range from the size of a fist to the size of a dog, and they move independently—some gliding across the stone on their own peristaltic ripples, some rolling, some extending fine pseudopodia that test the air with the delicacy of insect antennae. They are the Docent's senses distributed, its attention fragmented and roving. When one encounters something of interest—a dying rosette plant, a borehole whose hum has shifted pitch, your own motionless figure—it pauses, assesses, and communicates its findings not through any visible signal but through a change in the whole composite's behavior. The core mass shifts direction. The roaming fragments reorient. Consensus happens.

The Docent's relationship with the boreholes is the second major thing. It tends them. Not with tools—the Docent has no hands, and if it ever did, the hands are long since reabsorbed into the general purpose of the body. Instead it tends them with its own substance. When it arrives at an open shaft, the core mass settles around the rim like a collar, and the fragments flow down into the darkness. Hours later they return, pulsing with whatever they've gathered: heat, perhaps, or some chemical signature, or simply information about the state of the deep machinery. The Docent then adjusts. It may widen the borehole's opening by secreting something that softens the metal casing, a slow acid that works over days. It may seal a shaft by extruding a membrane across the mouth, a diaphragm that vibrates in sympathy with the subterranean hum, modulating the airflow. It may simply rest there, its body conforming to the rim, and add its own low frequency to the thrum coming up from below—a harmony, a counterpoint, a corrective.

The pumps are breathing, yes. The Docent is teaching them how.

Whether it was human once is an unanswerable question, but certain details suggest an origin story it has long outgrown. On the concrete plinth with the manufacturer's plate—the one half-swallowed by stone—there are marks that are not part of the original engraving. They're worn, softened by the same patience that wears everything here, but still legible if you know to look: handprints. Five-fingered. Smaller than the Docent's current mass but matching the size and shape of the roaming fragments when they flatten themselves against a surface. The handprints are arranged in a circle around the plate, pressed into the concrete when it was new, preserved by the same mineral accretion that fused the cable to the rock. They're not a signature. They're a farewell. Or a dedication. Or simply the mark of someone touching a thing before letting it go.

The Docent does not look at the handprints anymore. It has moved beyond that phase of its existence, whatever that phase was.

Its sensory world is not yours. The Docent perceives pressure and vibration primarily, the whole spectrum from the subsonic thrum of the deep pumps to the faint sibilance of water moving across stone. It perceives temperature gradients with the precision of a thermocouple, mapping the warm exhalations of the boreholes and the cool microvalleys between stone swells as distinctly as you perceive color. It perceives moisture as a continuous field—the saturation of the air, the film thickness on the stone, the water content of the rosette plants, the humidity rising from its own body. Light, if it registers light at all, is a secondary sense, something felt as a slight warming on its dorsal surface when the cloud-bellies part. It has no eyes that you can identify. The fragments that test the air with their pseudopodia are doing something closer to olfaction or electroreception, tasting the chemical signature of the atmosphere, the ozone from the terrace-edge, the iron from the weeping hatches.

And it perceives time differently. This is the third major thing, the hardest to accept while standing in the damp cool with your heart rate still calibrated to a human tempo. The Docent moves at the speed of erosion. Its actions span hours, days, the slow arc of seasons you can't detect because the temperature here never varies enough to mark them. When it adjusts a borehole's aperture, it does so over the course of a week. When it crosses the stone plain—a distance you could walk in two minutes—it takes the better part of a morning. This is not slowness born of limitation. This is speed calibrated to the pace of the place itself: the pace of water shaping rock, of pumps running down their half-lives, of isopods evolving blindness in the dark. The Docent is not patient. Patience implies waiting for something faster. The Docent is synchronized.

Does it think? Not in language. The thrum, though—the subsonic purr that travels through the stone—carries modulation when the Docent is active. If you press your palm flat to the rock and hold very still, you can feel it: a rhythm that isn't mechanical, a pulse that varies, patterns that repeat and vary and respond. The Docent is talking to the pumps. The pumps are talking back. And occasionally, when the Docent rests at a borehole's mouth and the fragments flow down into the dark, the thrum develops a secondary layer, a higher frequency that travels through the air rather than the stone—almost a voice, almost a song, a sound like wet fingers on a wineglass rim but structured, intentional. This is the Docent communicating with the isopods. They answer with their own chitinous rustle, amplified by the shaft's acoustics into something that resembles radio static from a distant station. The conversation has been ongoing for generations of isopods. It has no conclusion. It is simply the sound of two slow systems recognizing each other.

The cathedral comparison holds for the Docent as much as for the place. It is the clergy here. Not a caretaker—caretaking implies separation, a distinction between steward and stewarded. The Docent is more like the place's self-awareness, the point where the landscape's ongoing processes become concentrated enough to notice themselves. The stone, the water, the pumps, the isopods, the plants: they all continue without the Docent. But the Docent is what happens when they continue together, when the loops close, when the breathing synchronizes. It is not quite an organism. It is a function of the place, a composite being in a composite landscape, the thing that emerged when all the scattered conditions finally reached the density required for something to wake up.

You can leave. The Docent will not acknowledge your departure any more than it acknowledged your arrival. But if you stay long enough—if you lie flat on the warm stone beside a borehole and let your pulse slow to match the thrum, if you let the moisture film your skin, if you stop counting hours—the fragments will eventually approach you. They'll test the air near your face with their fine pseudopodia. They'll taste your breath. And one of them, small enough to mistake for a stone, will press itself gently against the base of your skull, where the spine meets the head, and you'll feel a vibration enter your bones: a question. Not *who are you* or *why are you here*, because those are human questions. The question will be something like *are you ready to synchronize*, and if you're not, the fragment will withdraw, and the Docent will glide away across the stone, and you'll be left alone with the sound of water and the knowledge that you were offered a different pace of existence and declined.

If you are ready, the fragment stays. The thrum deepens. And over the next several years—fast by the Docent's measure, glacial by yours—something begins to change in the tissue of your skin where the fragment rests. It's not painful. It's not even uncomfortable. It's a cooling, a slowing, a gradual alignment of your body's rhythms with the pulse of the place. You're not becoming the Docent. The Docent is one of a kind, or perhaps one of a kind at a time. But you're becoming something that can live here without being a visitor, without your heartbeat sounding intrusively loud against the stone. You're becoming part of the ongoing completion.

No one has ever stayed long enough to find out what that fully means. But the handprints on the plinth suggest that someone once began the process, and the Docent's continued existence suggests they didn't turn back.
