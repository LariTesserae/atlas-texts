From the shadow of a low stone lintel, a figure emerged with the slow, deliberate economy of someone who has never known a hurried season. The movement was not a disruption of the stillness so much as an extension of it, a change in the posture of the quiet rather than an interruption. A bare foot, the sole as grey and smooth as water-worn slate, pressed against the polished tread of a step, and the person it belonged to resolved into form like a figure stepping out of a mist that was not there.

She was of this place in the way the lichen was of this place, a creature shaped by the same parameters of wet stone and saturated silence. Her skin held the same blue-grey undertone as the fractured basalt underfoot, as if the minerals of the land had leached into her bloodline over unnumbered generations and recolored the very pigment of her flesh. It was not a pallor or a sickness, but a deep, living tint, like the bloom on a plum. A tracery of darker lines mapped her limbs and torso—not tattoos in any inked sense, but a natural dappling, a kind of faint dendritic marbling not unlike the patterns of moss agate or the veining in certain leaves, which caught the diffuse light and seemed to shift her outline against the rock, making her solid one moment and a trick of the eye the next.

She was lean, built along vertical lines, with the long muscles of a climber and a walker. Her joints—knuckles, ankles, the knobs of her spine—were prominent but not stark, articulated with a grace that suggested the architecture of the place had taught her body its proportions. Long toes spread on the stone, gripping the faintest undulation. Her face was narrow, the cheekbones high and flat, the jaw a soft curve. The eyes were the most arresting feature: large, set slightly wider than a human norm, the iris a pale, luminous silver that mirrored the still surface of the pans. There was no white to speak of; the silver bled into a faint ring of darker charcoal, and the pupil was a vertical slit, like that of a gecko or a deep-sea fish. She did not blink with the reflexive frequency of a surface creature. The lids moved slowly, a long, considered sweep, moistening the eye as the air itself might moisten stone.

A hood of woven fiber, the same dark, water-resistant stuff of the bridges, lay back against her shoulders. The cloth was not dyed; it was the natural charcoal black of the roots or stalks from which it was spun, and it had the same slight, velvety nap as the lichen. Beneath it, her hair was not hair as one might know it, but a finer, denser growth, a cap of filaments that lay flat and slick against her skull, each strand a dark, translucent grey that caught the ambient light and held it like dew. When she moved her head, the light slid across it in a single, liquid sheet of silver. It never stirred in any wind, for there was no wind. It was a feature adapted for a world without breezes.

Her garment was a single, seamless tunic of the same fiber, sleeveless, falling to her knees, belted with a cord from which hung a few small tools: a shaped flake of obsidian-like glass, a rolled cylinder of what might have been resin, a small pouch that bulged with something soft. Everything she carried was minimal, essential, a part of her silhouette. She wore no ornaments, no dyes, nothing that would not return quietly to the stone and water when she was done with it.

She moved down the narrow stair with a practiced, silent tread, the ball of her foot rolling from step to step, her weight never fully settling, as if she were perpetually prepared to freeze into immobility. At the base of the scarp, she paused, the silver mirror of a pan stretching before her, and her own reflection gazed up from a handspan below her feet. She looked at herself—the grey skin, the silver eyes, the slim dark shape—and her reflection returned the gaze with the same slow blink. There was no ripple to distort it. The water was perfect. She might have been standing on the surface of a second sky, looking at another version of herself looking back.

With a movement that was both casual and profoundly attentive, she lowered herself to a crouch at the water’s edge. The lip of the pan was sharp, knife-keen, and her fingers—long, the nails trimmed but not short, and harder than keratin, a grey-blue material that seemed to be the same as the rock itself—rested lightly on it. She leaned forward and her face hovered just above the surface. Her lips, thin and the color of a rain cloud, parted slightly. She did not touch the water. She inhaled, a long, slow pull, and the saturated air just above the meniscus, the air that was thick with the exhalation of the pool, flowed into her lungs. Her chest expanded. The dendritic patterns on her ribs shifted subtly, lightening for a moment, as if the very act of breathing was a form of osmosis. She took what she needed not from the volume of the water, but from its immediate, clinging atmosphere. In a place where everything was wet, the deepest thirst was for the air just above the water, not the water itself.

This was Nera, or a name that memory of speech translates as a stone settling into a bed of moss. She was of the Unhurried People, the Vein-Dwellers, whose name for themselves was a sound not made by vocal cords, but by pressing a moist finger against a resonant piece of slate and drawing it slowly across to produce a low, vibrating hum. They had been here since the rock was young, since the weeping began, since the first drop fell into the first pool and sent out the first ring of sound into the unbroken silence. They did not build this town; they emerged into it, as the lichen emerged, as the water emerged, a property of the place given slow, breathing form. And like the place, they did not hurry. They did not age as surface things age. Their time was the time of the pans, each moment a perfect, static reflection, each life a long, slow, deliberate *tink* in the great, resonant silence of the world.
