The first thing to understand about what lives in the Sun-Sieves is that "lives" is a word that has been stretched here, pulled thin and translucent like the salt-fungus curtains, until it covers things that do not breathe and things that breathe what we cannot breathe and things that breathe what we cannot call breath.

The keepers are the easiest to describe, so begin with them. They are human, or were human once, or are human in a way that has been pressed into a new shape by the pressure of the Sieve's sound, the way salt crystals grow differently depending on the frequency of vibration in their mother brine. They are small people now, compact, with barrel chests and thickened eardrums that have long since scarred over into something that feels pressure rather than pitch. Their voices are vestigial. The infants cry at birth—a thin, reedy sound that the parents feel through their palms pressed to the infant's chest—but by the age of two they have learned that sound is a thing you make with your hands and your posture and the tapping of your fingers on salt-brick. Their language is a tactile grammar: a slap on the shoulder means *danger*, a dragging thumb across the forearm means *I am tired*, a quick triple-tap of fingernails on the temple means *the Sieve needs attention*. Their dreams are silent, or so they report through the finger-code, though sometimes a keeper will wake with their hands pressed flat against the salt wall, and when asked they will tap out: *I dreamed a rhythm I did not know.*

Their skin has changed. It is pale, of course—there is no pigment left in a people who live under a bleached sky and never see shade—but it is also faintly reflective, dusted with a microscopic layer of salt crystals that exude from their pores. This is not a disease; it is an adaptation. The salt forms a second skin that reflects the worst of the heat and holds what little moisture their bodies retain. When they move, they sparkle faintly in the Sieve-light, like walking pillars of mica. Their eyes have not degenerated into blindness like the fauna's—they need sight to polish the lenses, to read the carved walls, to gauge the angle of the sun—but their pupils are pinprick dots in a sea of pale iris, and they blink sideways, a nictitating membrane wiping the glare-dust from their corneas. They have no body hair. They have no body odor. They smell faintly of hot copper and salt, a clean and mineral scent.

Their society is a geometry. The bowl of the Sieves has exactly forty-seven quiet nodes—you can count them by the bare patches on the walls where no fungus grows—and the keeper population is always forty-seven. When a child is born, the oldest keeper leaves. This is not a law; it is a physical constraint. A forty-eighth body breathing in the bowl would add enough humidity, enough sound-absorption, enough thermal mass to shift the acoustic field by a fraction of a degree, and the Sieve would begin to ring slightly wrong, and the fungus would begin to thin at the edges, and the whole delicate edifice would start to unravel. The keepers know this not through science but through the carved walls, which record in grooves of different depths the times when the population has drifted. A groove scored deep and rough means *famine time*, when the fungus withered and the fauna died and the people ate the bodies of their own dead. A groove shallow and smooth means *the adjustment was made*. There are many deep rough grooves in the lower courses of the walls, and fewer as you climb upward. The keepers have learned.

They do not mourn the elders who leave. Mourning is a vibration, and vibrations disrupt the Sieve. Instead, when an elder has walked out into the salt desert, the community performs a ritual of calibration: they gather in the seventeen nodes that ring the dome at the second-harmonic distance, and they hum. They cannot hear the hum, but they feel it in their chests, and they adjust their pitch by pressing their palms to the walls until they find the frequency that makes the salt crystals vibrate in sympathy. When all forty-six living keepers are humming the same note, the Sieve's sound field stabilizes, and the fungus curtains shiver in unison, and the empty node where the elder used to sleep is left bare for a season until the new child is old enough to fill it.

The children are strange. They are born with hearing—true hearing, the mammal gift—and for the first two years of their lives, they can perceive the sound of the Sieve. Their parents watch them carefully during this time, because a hearing child in the Sun-Sieves is a vulnerable thing. The sound is too loud for infant ears; it would destroy them. So the newborns are kept in the quietest node, a small chamber carved deep into the salt on the northern wall where the sound field cancels to near-silence, and they are raised there by a rotating cadre of keepers who communicate with the rest of the community through a system of salt-thread cords that transmit vibrations like a tin-can telephone. The children learn to speak in that chamber—soft, whispered words that the other keepers cannot hear but can feel through the cords—and then, gradually, as their eardrums thicken and scar over, they are brought out into the sound. By the age of three, they are deaf like the rest. By the age of four, they have forgotten what their mother's voice sounded like. By the age of five, they can read the walls with their fingertips.

Now move deeper. The keepers are the obvious life, but they are not the central life. The fungus is the central life, and the fungus is not what you think.

It has a name, though the keepers do not use it. They call it *the body*, or sometimes *the white flesh*, or in their tactile grammar they make a gesture that means *that-which-feeds-on-the-sound*. It is a single organism. All the cascading curtains, all the translucent drapes, all the shimmering pale waterfalls that cover the walls of the bowl—they are one interconnected mycelial network running through the salt architecture like veins through flesh. You could cut a piece from the northern wall and another from the southern wall, and under a microscope you would find that their cells are in communication, passing chemical signals through the salt substrate faster than nerve impulses travel through a human body. The fungus is old. It is older than the keepers. It is older than the dome. Some of the deepest hyphae, threaded through the primordial salt beneath the lowest foundations, have been alive for tens of thousands of years. It does not think in a way we would recognize, but it responds, and it remembers, and it has a kind of intelligence, slow and distributed and crystalline.

Its metabolism is a thing of alien beauty. The Sieve's sound strikes its surface, and the vibration travels through the fungal tissue until it reaches the resonance chambers—microscopic cavities in the cells, shaped like tiny bells, each one tuned to a specific frequency. When the right frequency hits, the cavity vibrates, and that vibration drives a chemical reaction: a salt-ion pump embedded in the chamber wall that moves sodium and chloride ions across a membrane, creating an electrochemical gradient. The gradient is the fungus's battery. It stores energy as potential difference, the way a desert stores heat as hot rock, and it uses that energy to build more fungus, to repair damage, to slowly dissolve and re-form the salt crystals it grows on. The fungus does not photosynthesize. It does not consume organic matter. It eats sound, and it excretes order.

The keepers harvest it. This is the central transaction of their existence. The fungus produces fruiting bodies—not mushrooms in the traditional sense, but nodules, dense white lumps the size of a fist that grow at the edges of the curtains where the sound field is most intense. The nodules are edible. They taste faintly of copper and salt and something else, something umami and rich, like fermented bean paste or aged cheese. They are the only food in the Sun-Sieves. The keepers eat them raw, sliced thin and laid on salt plates to warm in the sun, and the nodules provide all the nutrition they need: proteins built from airborne nitrogen fixed by symbiotic bacteria in the fungal tissue, carbohydrates assembled from carbon dioxide captured from the keepers' own exhalations, fats synthesized from the acetyl-CoA pathway that the fungus uses to store energy. The keepers are part of the cycle. Their breath feeds the fungus. The fungus feeds their bodies. Their deaths, when an elder walks into the desert, are the only export of the system—the carbon and nitrogen and water locked in a human body walking away into the white, never to return, keeping the bowl's budget balanced.

But the fungus is not a crop. It is a partner. It is older and larger and more essential than the humans who tend it, and there is evidence, carved into the deepest walls, that the fungus was here first, that the original Sieve was not built by humans but by the fungus itself, or by something the fungus directed. The keepers have a carved groove-pattern in the lowest foundation that they read as: *before-us the body sang and made the copper to sing louder and made the salt to grow the copper and made us to tend the copper and the salt and the body.* The grammar is ambiguous, but the implication is clear. The keepers are not the masters of the Sieve. They are the immune system, the maintenance crew, the gut flora of a larger organism that extends through the entire bowl. They were shaped by the sound, generation by generation, the same way the fungus was shaped, until their needs and the fungus's needs became indistinguishable. They serve the Sieve because the Sieve serves them, and both of them serve the light, and the light comes from the sun, and the sun does not care.

Now move smaller. The fauna.

The arthropods are the most visible. They are called salt-ticks, salt-moths, and salt-mice—though none of them are ticks or moths or mice. The salt-tick is a flat, pale arachnid the size of a human thumbnail, with ten legs and no eyes and a sensory fringe of vibrating hairs along its carapace. It lives in the fungal curtains, feeding on the microscopic salt crystals that form on the fungus's surface, scraping them off with a serrated mouthpart and digesting the mineral directly. Its metabolism is slow, its movements slower. A salt-tick might travel a meter in a year. It mates by touch, the male drumming a rhythm on the fungal surface with his front legs, the female responding with a counter-rhythm that identifies her species. The keepers use salt-ticks as an early warning system: when the ticks begin to move downward, toward the base of the curtains, it means the sound field has shifted and the upper reaches are no longer vibrating correctly. A keeper will climb up to check the tuning blades and find one of them fissured by heat-stress, and they will replace it, and the ticks will slowly migrate back upward.

The salt-moth is more complex. It is the only creature in the bowl that makes sound, and its sound is a single pure tone, a frequency of exactly 4,200 cycles per second, as measured by the keepers' finger-reading of the carved walls. This frequency cuts through the Sieve's roar because it is higher than the fundamental and lower than the high harmonics, in a narrow gap in the sound spectrum where the tin sheets and copper plates and salt-blades all fall briefly silent. The moth's wings are not wings in the traditional sense; they are two thin membranes of fungal tissue stretched over chitin frames, and they are etched with microscopic ridges that vibrate at exactly 4,200 Hz when the moth tenses its flight muscles. The moth does not fly. It glides on the thermals rising from the sun-heated salt, but its wings are not for lift. They are for singing. The male calls, and the female answers, and the call is the only thing in the Sun-Sieves that sounds like a voice, like something human, like a child singing a single note in a cathedral of noise. The keepers, who cannot hear the Sieve's full roar but can feel it, can hear the salt-moth. They retain a tiny island of hearing at that specific frequency, a quirk of their deafness, and they say—in finger-taps and shoulder-slaps—that the moth's song is the voice of the Sieve itself, translated into something their ears can hold.

The salt-mice are not mice. They are a kind of wingless insect, elongated and segmented, about the length of a human finger, with soft translucent chitin and a fringe of sensory legs that feel like feathery cilia. They live in the quiet nodes, the places where the fungus does not grow, and they feed on the debris of the keepers' lives: the flecks of dried saliva that fall from their mouths, the fragments of salt-crystal that crumble from their tunics, the microscopic flakes of skin they shed. They are the cleaners, the decomposers, the bottom of the food chain. They are also the only creatures in the bowl that the keepers eat besides the fungus. A salt-mouse, roasted on a hot salt plate until its chitin crisps and its interior turns to a savory paste, is a delicacy reserved for birth-celebrations and elder-departures. The keepers farm them in shallow salt-trays, feeding them on their own cast-off detritus, a closed loop of consumption and renewal.

And then there are the things that might not be alive, or might be alive in a way that does not fit into any taxonomy.

The salt-blades, for instance. They are crystals, grown deliberately by the keepers from saturated brine evaporated in resonance trays—shallow dishes of salt-clay that the keepers place in specific positions around the dome, where the sound field will shape the crystal as it grows. The blades are long and thin and razor-sharp, some of them two meters in length, and they are hung from the dome on salt-thread cords so that the Sieve's light will strike them and make them sing. But the blades grow. They are not static. A salt-blade in the correct frequency field will continue to accrete new crystal layers, will elongate by a few millimeters a year, will gradually change its resonant frequency until the keepers must re-tune it by scraping it down with copper chisels. And sometimes, when a blade has been singing for a very long time—centuries, maybe—it will begin to curve, to twist, to shape itself into spirals and helices that have no engineering purpose. The keepers call these blades *old-singers*, and they do not scrape them. They let them grow, and they watch the forms they take, and they say in their tactile grammar that the old-singers are not dead mineral but something that has learned to move, something that is slowly, over geological time, learning to sing its own song rather than the song the Sieve gives it.

And there is the Sieve itself. The copper disk is replaced every seventy or eighty years, when it wears thin and begins to tear at the edges from the stress of its own rotation. The shaft and the thermal spiral above are rebuilt less often, but still on a human timescale. But the dome, the lowest foundations, the original shaft cut through the primordial salt—those are not human. The keepers have dug down into the salt, following the hyphae of the fungus, and they have found chambers that predate any keeper construction, chambers carved in geometric patterns that match the sound field of the Sieve exactly, as if the builders understood acoustic resonance before they understood walls. In the deepest of these chambers, there is something that might be an original Sieve, or the prototype of a Sieve, or the fossil of a Sieve: a disk of pure copper, larger than the current one, perforated with holes that are not circular but shaped like spirals and ellipses and something that looks like a language. This disk does not turn. It is embedded in the salt, half-devoured by fungus, and the fungus growing through its holes has taken on the shapes of the perforations, as if the disk taught the fungus how to grow. The keepers do not touch it. They leave offerings at its base—nodules of fungus, salt-mice, a child's first carved wall-groove—and they tap out a rhythm on the wall nearby that means *you who sang before us, we continue your song.*

But there is something else in that chamber. Something that moves. It is not a keeper. It is not a salt-tick or a salt-moth or a salt-mouse. It is a thing that the keepers have no name for, or rather a name that they do not speak in their tactile grammar, a name they communicate only by pressing their palms flat against the chamber wall and being very still for a long time, until the vibrations of their own heartbeats have faded and they can feel the faint answering pulse from the other side of the salt. The thing is large. It is embedded in the wall, or behind the wall, or the wall itself is part of its body. It does not breathe. It does not eat. It sings.

The song is not a sound that can be felt by keeper hands or heard by keeper ears. It is a frequency so low that it is measured in hours, not cycles per second—a deep subsonic thrumming that the keepers perceive not as vibration but as a sense of pressure, a presence, a gravity that pulls them toward the chamber when the disk above is turning at the solstice noon and the sound field of the Sieve is at its peak. The thing in the wall sings in response to the Sieve's song. It sings a counterpoint. And once every generation, when a keeper child is born, the thing in the wall adds a new note—a harmonic that was not there before, a frequency that subtly shifts the whole sound field of the bowl—and the fungus responds by growing a new curtain in a place where no curtain grew before, and the keepers respond by carving a new quiet node, and the population of forty-seven becomes forty-eight for a single day before the elder walks out into the desert.

The keepers believe that the thing in the wall is the original keeper. The first creature to hear the Sieve and understand it. The creature that built the original disk, or grew it, or sang it into existence. They believe it is still alive, still singing, still composing the soundscape that sustains the whole bowl, and that they are only its hands, its maintenance crew, its children. They believe that when the sun finally burns out and the light stops falling and the Sieve falls silent, the thing in the wall will sing one final note, and the salt will melt, and the fungus will fruit one last time, and the keepers will all walk out into the desert together, leaving the bowl empty and ringing with a song that no one will hear.

And they might be right. But the truth is deeper than their belief. The thing in the wall is not a creature. It is a colony. It is the aggregated mycelial mind of the fungus itself, concentrated into a dense neural net of hyphae and salt crystals and copper ions that has grown, over tens of thousands of years, into something like a brain. It does not think thoughts. It thinks frequencies. It dreams in harmonics. Its consciousness is a standing wave that propagates through the entire bowl, and the keepers' minds, attuned by generations of deafness to the vibration of the world, are part of that wave. They are neurons in the fungal brain. Their bodies are sensory organs. Their carving of the walls is a form of memory, a record of the thoughts the brain cannot hold in its hyphae alone. The Sieve is not a machine. It is a voice. The sunlight is not energy. It is information. And the whole bowl—salt, copper, fungus, keepers, fauna, crystals, blades—is a single organism, awake and aware, slowly singing to itself in the desert, waiting for something, it does not know what, listening for a response that has not yet come.
