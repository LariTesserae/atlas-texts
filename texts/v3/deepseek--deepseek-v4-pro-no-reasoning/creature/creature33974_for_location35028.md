The fog arrived at midmorning, as it always did, rolling up the mountainside in a slow, white exhalation. By the time it reached the traverse, it had already swallowed the valley below and was pressing upward against the shoulder of the mountain. At the wallows, the fog slowed, tangled in the steam that rose from the vents, and for a while it was impossible to tell where cloud ended and geothermal breath began. The air thickened until it held its shape, and everything—the dripping leaves, the mud, the crouched ferns—took on the muted, slightly unreal cast of things seen through water.

The old female was the first to arrive. She materialized out of the fog as if the fog had simply condensed into flesh—a bulk of matted black hair and heavy, sloping shoulders, her ears fringed with white, her eyes small and deliberate. She stood at the edge of the big wallow and tested the air with her trunk, sampling the complex chemistry of the place: sulfur, yes, and the iron tang of mineral mud, and beneath that the layered scents of those who had wallowed before her. The tapirs. The jaguar, faint, hours old. The peccaries.

She stepped in. The mud closed around her thick legs with a sound like a slow, relishing swallow. She sank into it, groaning softly—a sound of pure, uncomplicated relief—and let the heat work into the joints of her hips and knees. She had been climbing for three days now, following the old trail up from the lowlands, and the stiffness had settled deep.

In the canopy above her, something shifted. A shape—long-limbed, dark, deliberate—swung from a branch and hung there, suspended by one arm, watching the tapir with the mild curiosity of the profoundly bored. The woolly monkey was young, a male not yet full-grown, his coat still dense and black rather than the silvery brown of adulthood. He scratched his ribs and yawned, exposing teeth yellowed by a diet of fruit and the occasional bird's egg. When the tapir submerged herself completely, leaving only the long slope of her snout above the surface, he lost interest and turned his attention to a bromeliad the size of a sheep, cupped in the crotch of a branch. He fished inside it with his fingers, found a translucent frog, and ate it without ceremony.

The fog thickened. The hissing of the vents never changed, but the rest of the forest's sound seemed to withdraw into itself, as if the moisture were absorbing not just light but noise. The tapir wallowed. The monkey dozed. The butterflies—a dense cluster of morphos, their wings the blue of an impossible sky—settled on a patch of wet soil to sip minerals, opening and closing their wings in slow, half-second rhythms that had nothing to do with wind.

It was in this lull, this closed bubble of heat and mist, that the two young males came crashing up the trail.

They were brothers, or half-brothers—two years apart, but in their adolescence the difference was harder to read, both of them lanky and full of a nervous, combative energy that had no outlet and so became destruction. They broke from the fog at a fast, rolling shuffle, side by side, their shoulders knocking together, each one trying to crowd the other off the trail. The lead male—older by those two years, heavier in the chest—veered suddenly and shouldered the younger into a bank of heliconia, snapping the broad leaves and sending a cascade of trapped water onto both their heads. The younger one squealed, a sharp, indignant sound, and came back fast with a sidelong lunge that caught the older in the flank and spun him half around.

They froze. Not because of each other, but because of the wallow. Because of the old female, who had risen from the mud to eye them with the weary, leveling gaze of a matriarch interrupted in her bath.

The young males were tapirs too—the same black, the same white-fringed ears, the same prehensile trunks that now curled and uncurled with tension. They had been following the old female's trail for two days, not with any purpose but drawn by the same instinct that pulled all of them uphill at this time of year: the urge to seek heat, to find the mineral-rich mud that soothed the parasites out of their skin, to gather in the one place on the mountain where the cold of altitude could not reach them. They had not intended to find her. They had simply been moving, and fighting, and the trail had led them here.

The old female snorted—a low, flat sound that carried unmistakable warning. The older young male took a step back anyway, and that was his mistake. The younger one, seeing weakness, slammed into him from behind.

They fell apart and came together again, grunting, their trunks lashing, their blunt hooves churning the saturated soil. The fight was not serious—neither had the weight or the will to do real damage—but it was loud and it was messy, and it sent the woolly monkey scrambling higher into the canopy, shrieking indignation. The butterflies lifted in a blue panic. The steam from the nearest vent seemed to pulse, as if the mountain itself were exhaling in irritation.

The old female had endured enough. She heaved herself from the wallow, mud sloughing from her flanks in thick sheets, and walked directly toward the fighting males—not charging, not even hurrying, just advancing with the absolute, immovable certainty of something large and old and entirely done with nonsense. She stopped six feet from them, and they stopped too, suddenly aware of their relative sizes. She was half again as heavy as either of them. Her shoulders were scarred. Her left ear had a notch taken out of it, a clean V-shaped bite that had healed years ago into a permanent asymmetry. She looked at them. They looked at the ground. The younger male made a small, placating noise in his throat, and the fight was over.

For a moment the three of them stood in the fog, breathing hard, the air around them thick with the mingled scents of mud and steam and tapir sweat. Then the old female turned and walked back to her wallow, and the young males, after a hesitation that was exactly long enough to pretend they had chosen this, followed her. They settled into the adjacent wallow—a shallower one, more recently formed, its edges still soft and crumbling—and let the warmth rise up through their bellies. The older one closed his eyes. The younger one, still restless, nudged a floating mat of moss with his trunk and watched it spin slowly in the mineral water.

The fog pressed in. The steam lifted and mingled. The monkey, having forgiven the disruption, descended to a lower branch and began meticulously grooming the fur of his tail. In the wallows, the tapirs grew still, their bodies settling into the mud like stones into a riverbed, and the quiet reassembled itself around them—drip and hiss and the distant, rhythmic clack of bamboo, and beneath it all the steady, sleeping breath of the mountain.
