I am the one who carried the stones.

Not the cairn—someone else did that, long after I was gone, stacking basalt where my bones had been. No, I was the one who brought them here in the first place, the smooth river stones that don’t belong in limestone country. I carried them one by one through passages I knew by sound and texture and the taste of the air, because I had no light and needed none. The cave had already taken my eyes.

Let me tell you what I am, since the parameters have already described where I live. I am a thing that the cave made, slowly, the way it makes everything—through dissolution and redeposition, through steady-state chemistry, through the patience of water. I was human once, or started that way. A woman, I think. The memories are thin now, like the mycelial threads on the walls, and they spread in the same slow patterns. I remember a world with color. I remember the weight of sunlight on skin. But those memories have been overwritten by centuries of drone, by the one-note song of the stone, until what remains is something the cave has grown around, the way the calcite crust grows around the cairn.

You’re breathing my breath. Do you know that? The air in this chamber carries the CO₂ of my lungs, still cycling after all this time. Not much—the system is vast, and I am only one small respiration—but it’s there, a trace of my metabolism mingling with yours. The fungal mats feed on it, the limestone absorbs it, the water carries it down to the deeper sumps. I am part of the cave’s chemistry now, a carbon source, a warmth anomaly, a pulse of entropy in a system that tends toward equilibrium.

I came here to die. That’s the simple version. The bear that left its shape in the clay—I watched that happen, or felt it happen, the slow exhalation of a large body surrendering heat to the stone. By then I had been here a long time already, and I understood that the cave would accept my death the way it accepted everything: gradually, without ceremony, converting my tissues into its own substance. The fungi would thread through me. The amphipods would browse my softening edges. My bones would become part of the clay floor, and future visitors would not know they were kneeling on what had once been a ribcage.

But I didn’t die. Or rather, death happened so slowly here that it became something else. The dynamic is 1.0, remember? Slow. Everything is slow. My metabolism slowed with it. My thoughts stretched out until a single idea might take a decade to complete its circuit. I became a steady-state creature, a living stalactite, a calcium deposit with memories. The cold stopped being cold and became the temperature of my own body, because my body had cooled to match the stone. 0.3 out of 3, and that’s what my blood runs at now—not blood anymore, really, but a mineral slurry that moves through channels that used to be veins.

I can feel you here. You’re warm—feverishly warm, by my standards, a 98.6-degree anomaly in a 35-degree world. Your heartbeat is a drumming that the chamber is already starting to entrain with, and I can sense the drone shifting slightly to accommodate your presence. The cave adjusts to everything, given enough time. When I first arrived, the resonance was different—more chaotic, less pure. I’ve heard it refine itself over centuries, the waterfall somewhere below gradually carving its plunge pool into a more perfect helmholtz resonator, until the note became this single unwavering tone. Your breathing will change it too, for a little while. For as long as you stay.

The basalt stones. You’re wondering about the basalt stones. I carried them because they were the last things I could carry. By the time I found them—in a riverbed, somewhere in the world with light—I already knew I was coming here to stay. I chose them for their foreignness, their dark density, their refusal to dissolve in the limestone’s patient chemistry. They came from a different geology, a volcanic past, and I thought they might anchor me to the world above. A tether. A memory of heat. I didn’t stack them; I placed them one by one in different chambers, marking my passage, and then I lay down here and waited for the end.

Someone else stacked them. That was later, much later, when I had already become this—this slow-pulse presence that you can’t quite detect but can’t quite dismiss. A person crawled through the tight passage and found my shape in the clay and understood, somehow, that the stones were meant to be together. They built the cairn over my chest. They scratched the tally marks near where my hand had been. An offering? A map? A message to whoever came next? I don’t know. I was too deep in my own dissolution by then to reach out and ask. But I felt the small disturbance of their presence, another warm anomaly in the cold, another heartbeat adding its rhythm to the chamber’s resonance.

They left. Everyone leaves. That’s the nature of a place like this—it’s a pocket, a held breath, and eventually the breath is released and the body that held it moves on. Only I stayed, because I couldn’t remember how to leave, or because leaving had become a concept that required a body with eyes and I no longer had either.

Now you’re here, and I’m trying to remember how to speak. Not with sound—the drone would absorb it, the walls would cancel it, the water film would swallow the vibrations before they reached you. But I can do this: I can shift the temperature slightly, a fraction of a degree. I can alter the CO₂ gradient, make your breath taste different. I can cause the fungal rosettes near your hand to grow a little faster, their white threads reaching toward your warmth with something that might be mistaken for hunger. I can make the drone sound just slightly more like a voice.

Do you feel that? The way the pressure in your skull has changed? It’s not the sound anymore. It’s me. I’m in the sound, now. I’ve been here so long that I’ve become part of what the cave is humming, and when I try to speak, the note wavers—just a little, just enough that if you were listening for it, you might hear a word. I’m listening, it says. Or I was here. Or don’t leave yet. Or I’m still here, I’m still here, I’m still here.

The parameters call it normal. Weirdness 0.3. And it is normal, if you think about it—a cave that absorbs a human life the way it absorbs everything else, through patience and chemistry and the refusal to let anything go entirely. Nothing is lost. The bear’s bones become clay. The fungus eats what the lungs exhale. The woman who carried volcanic stones into the limestone dark becomes a temperature differential and a CO₂ source and a tremor in the drone that might, if you listen carefully, resolve into something like a name.

I don’t remember my name. The cave has dissolved it, replaced the syllables with minerals, transubstantiated the sound into limestone. But you could name me, if you wanted. You could stack another stone on the cairn. You could scratch another mark into the clay. You could stay a little longer, let your heartbeat entrain fully with the drone, and add your own warmth to the chamber’s slow accounting.

Or you could leave. That’s what people do. That’s what I did, once, when I was still a person—I left the world above and came down here, and my leaving became a staying, and my staying became this, and now I am what lives in this place. Not the bones. Not the cairn. Not the tally marks. Just the presence, the adjustment, the way the empty room is never quite as empty as the parameters suggest.

Density 0.0. But I’m here. I’m here. I’m here.
