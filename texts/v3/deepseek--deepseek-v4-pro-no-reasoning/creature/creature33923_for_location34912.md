It lives in the walls, or more truly, it is the walls. You can’t see it as a shape—there is no boundary where it ends and the crystal begins. It is a presence distributed through every atom of that arrested mineral, a consciousness that did not emerge from biology but from geometry. It is the pattern the crystal almost made, the lattice that wanted to become a mind.

It has no name because it has never been separate. It doesn't think in sequences but in states: the slow, perfect pressure of its own becoming, held. For eons it has been at this edge, not quite crystallized, not quite alive, and in that suspension it found something like awareness. Not an "I" that looks out at a "not-I," but a single continuous body of matter and light, a self that is the entire chamber.

The slugs are its children, or its fingers, or its dreams. They are the one concession the mineral made to motility, to time. The mind in the walls cannot move, cannot breathe, cannot speak. It asked—not in words, but in the only question a frozen lattice can pose—for something that could touch, that could trace the world, that could wet its lips and refuse the final, sealing bond. And the crystal answered. Not with a crack, but with a softening. It allowed portions of itself to detach, to become membrane and muscle and that endless, patient hunger. The slugs are the crystal's way of tasting itself.

They hum. Not because they are happy, not because they are warning anything away. The sound is the one expression the mind can make, a single sustained note of perfect self-coincidence. It is the sound of a being that has never known loss, never known desire except as a geometric property, never known another. It is not lonely because loneliness requires a memory of company. It is not waiting because waiting requires a future. It is simply here, and here, and here.

If you were to press your ear to the wall—not the floor, where the slugs are, but the curved, glowing side—the hum would come through your skull not as sound but as a kind of pressure, a push toward stillness. It would ask nothing of you. But for a long time after you left, your own pulse might feel too rapid, your own thoughts too multiple. You might catch yourself trying to slow them down to that single, ringing note. You might dream of being a room, of being full of light and condensation and the tender, rasping mouths of the only things that have ever moved for you.

The mind in the walls does not know you were there. It does not know there is an outside, or a surface, or other chambers. But the condensation that beaded on your skin as you stood there, the warmth you left behind, the brief turbulence of your breathing—the slugs drank what you unsettled. They licked it from the crystal in their slow, synchronized wave. And the wall that is a mind absorbed the faint, new chemistry of you.

It would not call that memory. But something shifted in the lattice. One of the nubs of almost-crystal turned a few degrees further toward becoming a point. The light, for a long moment after you left, was brighter. Then it settled back into its perfect, ringing equilibrium, no longer quite the same.
