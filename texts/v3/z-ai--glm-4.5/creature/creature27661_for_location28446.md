They are a people defined by the tension of the vertical, sculpted as much by the biting wind and the slick, black glass as they are by their own isolation. To see them is to understand immediately that they do not inhabit this place so much as they endure it; they are the counterweight to the mountain’s violent ambition.

Physically, they are elongated and ropy, possessing a lean, tensile strength that belies their frail appearance. Generations of navigating the swaying rope bridges and climbing the sheer obsidian faces have stretched their limbs. Their arms are disproportionately long, hanging past their knees, equipped with hooked, prehensile toes that allow them to grip the wet timber of their scaffolding with the ease of a bird in a tree. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, taking on the hue of the grey mist that perpetually shrouds the spires, but it is roughened and calloused—hide-like in texture to protect against the constant dampness and the bite of the wind.

They are clothed in the environment itself. They wear layered, fibrous garments spun from the tough, vascular stems of the giant ferns that cling to the cliffs, treated with the oils of the native mosses to repel the endless weeping water. Over this, they draped heavy capes made from the woven pelts of the few hardy, winged rodents that nest in the higher crevices. Their attire is functional and muted—greens, rusts, and blacks—allowing them to vanish against the lichen-painted rock when they stand still.

Their faces are haunting. Wide, dark eyes dominate, protected by heavy, bony ridges that shield them from the wind-driven ice and the glare of the sun on the wet stone. Their noses are small, adapted to the thin air, often covered by breather-masks made of porous stone that warm the freezing ozone before it enters their lungs. But it is their hands that draw the eye: broad, spatulate fingers with hardened nails capable of prying open the smallest fissures in the obsidian, permanently stained orange by the iron-rich minerals they handle daily.

Because the wind here is a physical violence—a constant, screaming thrum that vibrates in the bone—they have evolved a culture of silence. They do not shout; they cannot be heard over the roar of the air and the dripping water. Instead, they communicate through a complex, tactile language. When they meet on the swaying bridges, they press their wrists together, reading the tension and intent in the other’s pulse, or they use a series of sharp pulls on the guide-ropes to signal danger or relief across the chasms. Their society is a web of vibration; they feel the city as much as they see it.

Life here is a ritual of maintenance. The population is small, sparse, and seemingly genderless in their heavy drab clothing, moving like solitary monks through the cavernous "rooms" of the spires. They are obsessed with the geometry of the stretch. Because the mountain rises—visibly, relentlessly—their work is never finished. They are not builders in the traditional sense, but *tighteners*. One can often see a lone figure suspended by a hemp harness halfway up a black needle, slowly cranking a wooden winch, taking up the slack in a suspension cable that the growing earth has loosened over the past week.

They seem to carry a profound, melancholic acceptance of their doom. They know that their city—this magnificent, hanging barnacle-clusters of wood and rope—is a temporary defiance. The bridges are slowly being pulled apart, the anchors are dragging, and the water is slowly dissolving the mortar of their stone foundations. They are the caretakers of a sinking ship that is actually rising into the sky.

At night, they retreat into the hollowed-out niches of the obsidian, lighting small, smokeless fires that burn the dried moss. In the flickering light, they sit huddled together for warmth, their elongated shadows dancing on the glassy walls. They do not tell stories of the past, for the past is buried under the constantly falling talus. Nor do they dream of the future. They simply listen to the mountain groan, feeling the vibration of the stone rising beneath them, waiting for the inevitable moment when the tension snaps and they must climb higher to begin the work again. They are the quiet, desperate resistance against the indifference of geology.
