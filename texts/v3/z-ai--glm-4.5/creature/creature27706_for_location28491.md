To live in the Sump is to exist in a state of permanent, quiet apology. The inhabitants are not merely residents of this vertical wound in the earth; they are a biological adaptation to it, as specialized and strange as the pale crabs scuttling in the dark.

They are a people carved from the same grey slate as their surroundings—short, dense, and compact. Their limbs are disproportionately long for their torsos, arms that reach past their knees and legs bowed into a permanent crouch, evolved to bridge the gaps between the knife-sharp pinnacles without falling. They possess a simian grace, but a heavier, fluid one; they do not leap so much as they flow, draping themselves over the wet stone like spilt water.

Their skin is their primary technology. It is thick, rubbery, and entirely devoid of hair, textured with a microscopic patterning that provides friction against the slick, weeping basalt. It ranges in color from the deep charcoal of the submerged stone to the pale, sickly grey of the mist, allowing them to blend perfectly into the shadows of the overhangs. To see one standing still is to see nothing but a lump of rock until they move.

Movement is silent. Their feet are wide, flat, and splayed, possessing a natural suction that allows them to adhere to vertical faces even when coated in slime. They pad barefoot over edges that would slice through leather boots, their soles so heavily calloused they are nearly inflexible. When they traverse the stilt-roads, they do not walk with the heavy thud of a human, but with a calculated, delicate distribution of weight, testing the timber lashings before committing their center of gravity.

Their faces are dominated by sensory organs designed for the dim, damp cathedral. Eyes are large, black pools, reflecting the grey light with a wet sheen, possessing nictitating membranes that constantly sweep away the moisture dripping from above. Their noses are small, flattened slits, likely to prevent the inhalation of the clinging mist, but their ears are prominent—large, tapered shells that swivel independently. They are listeners. In the Sump, vision is limited by the veil and the claustrophobic density of the architecture, but sound travels through the stone and the water. They can hear the shift of a tectonic plate three seconds before it groans; they can hear the change in pressure as a distant sponge-rock saturates to the breaking point.

Clothing is minimal and strictly utilitarian, woven from the tough, dried moss and the sinew of the few creatures they catch. They wear harnesses made of gut, always lined with carabiners or hooks of bone, securing themselves to the rock whenever they stop moving. There is no fashion here, only hydrodynamics and safety. The smoke of peat fires clings to them, a musk that mixes with the smell of wet wool and the metallic tang of the stone.

They are a communal society born of claustrophobia. Because their homes are stacked with a density of 2.7, privacy is a foreign concept. Families live in shifts; when one sleeps, the others sit on the roof or hang from the eaves, watching the mist. They communicate through a complex language of hand signs and low-frequency humming vibrations that travel through the stone they touch, sparing them the need to raise their voices above the drip of the water.

Their diet mirrors the scarcity of the verticality. They are harvesters of the weeping. They use long, hollow reeds to suck the nutrient-rich algae from the pores of the rock, and they hunt the translucent eels with spears tipped with sharpened shale, striking with lightning speed into the shallow puddles. They do not cook the eels; they eat them raw, slippery and cold, needing the oils to keep their own skin pliable against the damp.

There is a heaviness to their demeanor, a profound melancholy that comes from living on a dying, shifting world. They know the Sump is digesting them. They spend half their lives repairing—tightening the lashings, replacing the rotting timber, reinforcing the stilts against the slow heave of the earth. They are perpetual mourners of their own architecture, knowing that the crack in the wall heard today is the sound of their bedroom collapsing next week.

They are the "Kept." They believe the earth cracked open not to trap them, but to birth them, and that the constant weeping is the sorrow of the stone at having to push them out. They do not look up at the sky a hundred feet above; that is the realm of dreams and death. They look only at the rock in front of their faces, feeling the pulse of the water through their fingertips, clinging tight to the wet ribs of the infant that holds them.
