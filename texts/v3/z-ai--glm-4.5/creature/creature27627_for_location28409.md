At first, the assertion holds true: there are no beasts here. No birds slice through the grey mist, and no insects chirp in the deep, purple ferns. But to say there is no life is to mistake the nature of the High Weeping. The silence of the animal kingdom is an illusion, a camouflage painted in shades of industrial rust and wet stone.

The inhabitants of this vertical, frozen hellscape do not live *on* the land; they *are* the land—or at least, they have evolved to become indistinguishable from the machinery that punctures it.

They are the Ferrum-Phytes, the "Iron-Flora," though biologists would struggle to classify them. They are the hidden architects of the stasis, the only organisms capable of surviving the aggressive cold and the deafening roar.

To the untrained eye, a Ferrum-Phyte appears to be nothing more than a section of decayed pipeline or a forgotten piece of heavy machinery left to rust on a precipice. They stand immense—often twenty to thirty feet tall—possessing a physiology that mimics the brutalist architecture around them. Their "skin" is not flesh, but a layered, chitinous carapace of iron-oxide and basalt deposits, secreted slowly over centuries of feeding on the mineral-rich water. This plating is constantly wet, glistening under the perpetual twilight, providing a armor that is as hard as the black glass they stand upon.

But they are not statues. If you watch a seemingly abandoned pile of girders on a cliff edge for long enough, you might see a "girder" shift. You might notice that the "rusted pipe" draped in thick mattress-moss is actually a long, prehensile limb, ending in a manipulator claw capable of crushing rock or delicately turning a pressure valve the size of a house.

Their bodies are hydraulic marvels. Instead of blood, they pump the super-cooled, pressurized water from the abyss through a complex internal vascular system. This fluid hisses violently inside them, creating the same terrifying thrum that defines the soundscape of the Weeping. When a Ferrum-Phyte moves, it does not flex; it extends and retracts with the smooth, lubricated clunk of heavy industrial machinery. Their joints do not creak with arthritis, but with the clean, metallic ring of titanium against steel.

They are solitary titans. Due to the near-zero density of the "city," a single Ferrum-Phyte might claim a territory spanning fifty miles of canyon and cliff. They wander these vast ranges slowly, their immense weight distributed across pillar-like legs that sink into the loose scree. They are the custodians of the loop. They do not build the factories, but they maintain them. You will see them perched atop the massive piston-towers, their moss-cloaked shoulders hunched against the spray, using their multi-fingered claws to clear blockages from the intakes or to tighten bolts that have shaken loose from the tectonic grinding.

They do not eat food. They are photovoltaic and kinetic. The "purple leaves" of the alien ferns are not just decoration; the Ferrum-Phytes cultivate these gardens on their own armored backs. The ferns provide a thermal insulation layer, trapping the heat generated by the creature’s internal hydraulics, while the ferns in turn feed on the iron-rich secretions of the creature’s skin. It is a symbiosis of metal and biology, a living greenhouse that walks.

Their senses are tailored to a world of noise and wetness. They have no eyes, for sight is useless in the uniform grey haze and the blinding spray of the waterfalls. Instead, they "see" through vibration. Their feet are highly sensitive seismic sensors, capable of detecting a loose rock falling miles away, or the irregular rhythm of a failing turbine in the next canyon. They communicate not through sound, which would be lost in the roar, but through heavy, rhythmic impacts of their limbs against the bedrock— Morse code hammered out on the bones of the world.

Most disturbingly, they exhibit a behavior that suggests a fractured, mechanical intelligence. They seem obsessed with the symmetry of the water. If a natural landslide diverts a flow of weeping water away from its intended channel, a Ferrum-Phyte will arrive. It will spend weeks, perhaps months, moving tons of rock, reinforcing the cliff face with secreted cement-like saliva, simply to force the water back into its original, artificial path. They are the gardeners of a chaos they cannot abide, enforcing the eternal, unchanging repetition of the High Weeping.

They are immortal, so far as death concerns them, provided the water keeps flowing. Their corpses become the infrastructure itself; when a Ferrum-Phyte finally exhausts its internal energy, it freezes into a rigid pose of maintenance, its hydraulic fluids solidifying into opaque ice, its shell rusting completely. Over time, the city grows around it, incorporating the dead giant into a bridge, a support pillar, or a dam.

So, the High Weeping is not empty. It is populated by a silent, slow-moving civilization of giants disguised as engines. They are the ghosts in the machine, the biological soul of the industrial waste, waiting in the freezing rain, turning their valves and watching the water fall, forever keeping the world exactly as it is.
