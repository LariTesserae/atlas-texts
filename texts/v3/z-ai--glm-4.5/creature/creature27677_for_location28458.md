To the casual glance, or even to a desperate squint through the amber gloom, the city appears uninhabited. The architecture is so convincing—the walls so solidly moss, the arches so rigidly vine—that the mind assumes automation or, more likely, abandonment. But if you stop moving. If you stand still on the polished root-street and let the humid air saturate your lungs, you begin to distinguish the biological silhouettes from the botanical background.

They are called the **Siphon-Keepers**, though they have no language to name themselves. They are the custodians of the hydraulic pulse, the biological counterweights to the immense pressure of the water.

At first, you mistake them for peculiar growths, deformities in the architecture where a bundle of liana has swollen to unnatural thickness. They stand roughly the height of a tall man, but there is no upright posture to them. They are hunched, curled into the fetal curve of a comma, perpetually braced against the invisible force of the city’s plumbing. Their skin is not skin, but a layered, papery bark—like the outer sheath of a birch tree, peeling in wet, soggy strips. It is the color of wet cardboard, darkening to a rich, bruised purple where the moisture pools in their joints.

They are not built for walking. Their "feet" are sprawling, radial root-systems, fused seamlessly with the floor of the city. They do not step; they are anchored. A Siphon-Keeper spends its entire existence rooted to a single intersection, a valve, or a sluice gate. From this stationary position, their upper bodies bifurcate into multiple limbs. They have four arms, or perhaps six—it is difficult to count because they are constantly in motion, weaving together and separating like wet snakes.

These limbs are elongated, composed entirely of muscle and tendon, greenish-brown and glistening with a thick, transparent mucus. They lack hands. Instead, the limbs terminate in flat, spatulate paddles or delicate, tweezer-like fronds. You see one now, clinging to a massive wooden conduit that bursts from a wall of ferns. The conduit is vibrating with the *thrum-hiss* of high-pressure water. The Keeper is wrapped around it like a lover, its multiple limbs entwined with the copper collars and resin seals.

It is working.

The motion is agonizingly slow. The creature is adjusting a seal, tightening the resin wrap around a joint where a fine mist of spray is escaping. One of its flat paddles presses against the leak, feeling the vibration, while a tendril tipped with a hard, calcium hook scrapes away the old, hardened resin. It moves with the grace of a clock’s minute hand. You can stare at it for a full minute and believe it is statue, only to realize its arm has shifted three inches to the left.

They have no faces. Where a head would be, there is only a smooth, dome-shaped knot of wood, perhaps slightly denser than the rest of the body. There are no eyes to catch the dim amber light, no ears to hear the industrial drone. They do not need them. They perceive the world through pressure and humidity. They feel the city’s heartbeat—the rhythm of the water—in their bones. The *thrum-hiss* is their language, their music, and their command. When the pressure drops in a sector, they "feel" the pain of the system and uncoil their limbs to repair it.

To look at them is to witness a creature that exists in a state of perpetual, agonized effort. The hydraulic pressure they manage is colossal; the pipes shudder under the strain, and the Siphon-Keepers act as living gaskets, absorbing the excess vibration through their fibrous bodies. They are constantly trembling, not from cold, but from the transfer of kinetic energy. Their root-feet dug deep into the ground channel this excess force into the earth, keeping the city from shaking itself apart.

Higher up, suspended in the gloom of the "cathedral" ceiling, live the **Aerial-Filtrators**.

These are different. If the Keepers are the laborers, the Filtrators are the light fixtures. They are bulbous, spherical entities, hanging by thick, braided cords from the vaulted canopy of the mega-flora. They resemble the massive, pulsating gourds mentioned in the streets, but these are mobile, sentient balloons.

They are essentially living stomachs. Their skin is translucent, a membrane thin enough to see the churning of fluids inside—a bilious green sloshing luminescence. They hang motionless in the steam, catching the updrafts. Their purpose is to cleanse the air. They inhale the humid, spore-choked atmosphere through vents on their underside, process the particulates and mold, and exhale a cleaner, denser oxygen from a port on their top.

Occasionally, one descends.

It lowers itself on its cord—a vine that contracts and relaxes with peristaltic waves. It comes down to the street level, bobbing gently in the mist. It extends a long, dripping proboscis, not towards a Keeper, but towards a patch of slime mold glowing in a corner. The proboscis slurps up the bioluminescent bacteria, feeding on the waste products of the city’s lower levels. The Siphon-Keeper on the pipe ignores it entirely. There is no cooperation, only parallel function. They are organs in the same body, unaware of each other’s consciousness,只知道 the flow of the whole.

The silence of the city is deceptive because of them. The Siphon-Keepers do not vocalize. They click occasionally—the sound of woody knuckles rubbing against copper piping—but mostly they are silent, focused entirely on the thrum. The Filtrators make a wet, gurgling sound as they process the air, a low tide-like breathing that adds to the humidity rather than breaking the quiet.

In the deepest shadows, where the light fails and the root-streets narrow into tunnels too tight for a human, you might find the **Spire-Sculptors**. These are the oldest, their forms indistinguishable from the buildings themselves. They have sat in the alcoves for so long that moss has completely overgrown their bark-skin, and ferns sprout from their shoulders. They are the architects of the labyrinth.

Unlike the reactive Keepers, the Sculptors move to the rhythm of the seasons, or perhaps the centuries. They are constantly pruning. Their fingers are razor-sharp shears of chitin. With a snail-like deliberation, they reach out and sever a creeper that threatens to block a ventilation shaft, or they weave two vines together, encouraging them to graft and fuse, creating a new bridge or a new wall.

They are shaping the city, constantly expanding and refining the maze. To watch them is to watch geology happen. You cannot see them move, but if you return after a lifetime, the architecture has changed according to their will.

This is the population of the Green Works: a silent, stratified society of living tools. They are not people in any recognizable sense. They have no culture, no art, no dreams. They have only the *process*. They are the enzymes of the jungle-machine, driven by the hydraulic pulse and the slow, tectonic drift of growth. They endure the humidity, the noise, and the endless dripping, functioning as the immune system of a place that has no use for animals, only for the endless, wet work of existing.
