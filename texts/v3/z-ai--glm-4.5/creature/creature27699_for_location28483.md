To live in the High Swell is to exist in a state of perpetual, terrified attachment. The beings here are not merely inhabitants; they are growths on the stone, stubborn barnacles of flesh and scavenged metal that refuse to be scraped away by the hydraulic pressure of the world.

They call themselves the **Clings**, a name that is less a demonym and a verb. In a landscape where "dry land" is a temporary inconvenience, the Clings have evolved—or perhaps engineered themselves—to become indistinguishable from the rugged, industrial despair of their surroundings.

**Form and Silhouette**
At first glance, seen through the shifting gray curtains of mist, a Cling looks like a pile of seaweed-wrapped debris. It is only when they move that the silhouette resolves into a humanoid shape, though one brutally modified for friction.

They are short, dense, and wide. The concept of "grace" has been bred out of them; in its place is stability. Their limbs are disproportionately thick, wrapped in layers of cured kelp-leather and oily neoprene scavenged from the ruins. They do not stand erect; to stand tall is to be toppled. They move in a perpetual crouch, their spines curved like question marks, center of gravity kept low to the Whalebacks.

Their hands are their primary interface with the world. Natural fingers have been augmented or replaced with iron hooks, rusted pincers, and gauntlets made from the rubbery baffles of kelp-trees. These are not hands for holding; they are hands for gouging. They must latch onto the polished, wet granite like ticks, and so every inch of their contact surface is textured, spiked, or suctioned.

**The Shell: Kelp and Iron**
The Clings do not wear clothing in the traditional sense; they wear architecture.
To survive the horizontal rain that falls like nails, they are encased in "Carapaces." These are constructed from the translucent, rubbery stalks of the kelp-trees, woven tightly with rusted copper wire stripped from the Intake Valve. The result is a patchwork, mottled armor that is the color of a bruise—dark greens, blacks, and the oxidized reds of old machinery.

The Carapace is slick. Water hits it and sheets off instantly. When a wave smashes over a Whaleback, a Cling does not fight the water; they go fluid, tucking limbs inside the shell, becoming a smooth stone that the washes over. They carry their own micro-ecosystems within the folds of these suits: colonies of moss that filter the air, and small, blind crabs that pick parasites from their skin, hidden beneath the layers of waterproofing.

**Sensory Adaptation: Hearing the Roar**
In the High Swell, ears are useless. The roar is a medium, a physical weight that would rupture a human eardrum within an hour. The Clings have no external ears. Their auditory canals are sealed shut by heavy flaps of skin and scar tissue.

Instead, they hear with their bones.

They have developed a form of biological seismic sensing. Their jawbones are fused directly to enhanced sinus cavities that vibrate in harmony with the granite. When a Leviathan sings in the depths below, or when the rusted hull of the trawler groans in the wind, the Cling feels it as a hum in their teeth.

To communicate, they do not speak. They cannot shout over the shriek of the wind. Instead, they use "Hammer-Talk." They carry heavy iron rods—forged from the pistons of the dead world—tapped against the stone. Complex sentences are transmitted through the rock itself, vibrations traveling through the Whalebacks to their feet. A rhythmic tapping might mean *“Safety here,”* while a chaotic, scraping staccato is the warning for *“Rogue Swell.”*

**Society and the Scavenge**
The Clings are nomadic scavengers, but their territory is vertical. They migrate up and down the Whalebacks based on the "Tide-Clock," an innate sense of the rhythmic, chaotic surges of the ocean.

Their society is centered around the "Hulks"—those rare, stable concrete bunkers and the rusted skeleton of the Intake Valve. These places are holy grounds, not because of religion, but because they offer a reprieve from the wind. Inside the Intake Valve, the air is still. The Clings gather there in the lulls between storms, huddled together for warmth, trading pieces of high-grade copper or pristine glass insulators like jewels.

They are obsessive groomers of their environment. They spend hours scraping the invasive sinew-moss off the "Tables" to keep the footholds secure, yet they cultivate the moss on the walls for insulation. They maintain the rusted pipes not to fix them, but to play them like instruments, listening to the water rushing inside to predict the surges.

**The Hunt**
They are predators of the vertical spaces. They hunt the Skimmers.

A Cling hunt is a terrifying spectacle of timing and leverage. A group will position themselves on a ledge above a cloud of the diving birds. They do not use bows or guns—projectiles are useless in the gale-force winds. Instead, they use "Webs"—long, knotted nets made of cured kelp-vines, weighted with heavy nuts and bolts from the industrial ruins.

When the wind drops for a split second, they leap. Not flying, but falling with purpose. They free-fall through the mist, colliding with the heavy birds in mid-air, their weight and hooks dragging the prey down. They slam into the water below—submerged for seconds—before using their hooked gauntlets to climb back up the sheer, wet rock face, dragging the drowning Skimmer with them.

**The Philosophy of Erosion**
Perhaps the most defining trait of the Clings is their acceptance of erosion. They know the world is grinding itself to sand. They do not try to save the Intake Valve; they merely camp in its ribs while it stands.

They view themselves not as masters of the High Swell, but as temporary rust on the surface of the stone. They have no word for "forever." Their closest concept is *“Until the Water Takes It,”* which applies to their tools, their homes, their bodies, and ultimately, the mountains themselves. In the High Swell, to be alive is simply to be the last thing to dissolve.
