The inhabitants of this rusted, submerged lung do not live; they endure. They are not creatures of action, but of presence, woven so tightly into the texture of the Sunken Gear-Deck that to distinguish them from the machinery requires a patience exceeding human measure.

Here, life has taken the form of the **Pale Floaters**.

These are the solitary, eyeless fish mentioned in the drifting silence. They are not fish as the open ocean knows them—sleek and muscular—but rather gelatinous, nebulously shaped things, resembling severed rays of moonlight caught in a jar of formaldehyde. They lack distinct scales; their skin is a continuous, translucent membrane, wet and slick, through which one can vaguely see the dark, slow-beating smudge of their organs.

They do not swim in bursts. To swim implies energy, destination, hunger. The Floaters merely *occupy* space. They hover with a neutral buoyancy, their long, tattered fins trailing behind them like wet silk in a draft, drifting centimeters above the control panels. They are equipped with sensory pits along their flanks—slight depressions that ripple like the surface of oil—that detect the thermal gradients radiating from the pipes. They congregate around the valves that spill the warm bubbles, hanging in the rising columns of heat like laundry hung out to dry, motionless save for the involuntary twitch of a gill.

Clinging to the vertical architecture are the **Rust-Weevils**.

These are small, crab-like entities, though they have abandoned the scuttling haste of their terrestrial ancestors. Their carapaces are not the hard, clicking chitin of normal crustaceans, but a soft, pitted metal that mimics the corrosion around them with flawless precision. They are the color of dried blood and iron oxide, making them nearly invisible against the pipes they traverse.

They move with a geologic friction. A single step of a Rust-Weevil—raising one of its hooked, serrated legs and placing it a fraction of an inch forward—can take minutes. They graze on the velvet moss that suffocates the dials, scraping it away with mandibles that work slowly, like the crushing plates of a garbage compactor set to the lowest speed. They leave behind clean, rivet-like trails in the moss, the only evidence of their passage, tiny geometric scratches on the organic chaos.

Deeper within the lattice, wrapped around the pistons and fused into the joints where the black kelp binds the metal, live the **Conduit-Eels**.

These are the masters of camouflage in a world without light. They are long, slender creatures, lacking the distinct definition of a head or tail, tapering at both ends into blunt points. Their skin is patterned with the exact hexagonal weave of the wire insulation they coil around. They do not hunt; they filter. They anchor their bodies in the crux of a gear or the crook of a pipe, expanding and contracting their bodies in a rhythm that matches the slow pulse of the vines.

They are part of the plumbing. As water seeps through the cracks of the Sunken Gear-Deck, it passes through the relaxed jaws of the Conduit-Eels, which strain the microscopic, luminescent plankton from the liquid fog. A Conduit-Eel might remain in the same valve, woven through the same kelp fronds, for decades, its body slowly calcifying, becoming a permanent fixture of the ruins, indistinguishable from the copper tubing it mimics until it decides to twitch once, a movement that sends a shiver through the entire plant-mechanism.

Finally, there are the **Bell-Barnacles**, sessile growths that cluster on the undersides of the massive, house-sized gears.

They look like small, deflated leather balloons, gray and wrinkled, anchored by a wide, muscular foot. They possess a single, elongated tentacle that trails upward into the gloom, waving with a current that does not exist. This tentacle is tipped with a bioluminescent bulb that glows with the same sickly amber as the dying electric lights.

They are the lighthouses of this dark place. The Floaters, blind and heat-seeking, are drawn to this faint glow. When a Floater drifts too close, mistaking the light for a warm vent, the Bell-Barnacle does not strike. It does not snatch. Instead, it opens a series of tiny, fringed slits along its side and creates a gentle suction, barely stronger than a kiss. It siphons the microscopic mucus from the Floater’s skin—a parasitic grooming. The Floater remains indifferent, allowing the cleaning, continuing its slow drift, a silent transaction in a transactionless world.

There is no predation here, only the slow, communal metabolism of decay. The fauna does not consume flesh; it consumes time, entropy, and the heat of the dying machine. They are the ghosts in the shell, the organisms that have evolved to haunt the pauses between the ticks of a clock that stopped long ago.
