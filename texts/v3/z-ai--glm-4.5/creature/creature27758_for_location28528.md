They are not distinct from the ruin; they are its consciousness, its immune system, the slow, waking dream of the place itself. To see them, you must stop looking for shapes and start looking for discrepancies in the decay.

At first, you perceive them as architectural anomalies—a pile of slag that seems to have shifted overnight, a catwalk railing that has sagged into an impossible curve. But watch the anomaly long enough, and it breathes.

The primary inhabitants are the **Calipers**, great, hunched figures that move with the agonizing grace of tectonic plates. They stand roughly the height of a man, but wide, possessing a segmented, centurion-like morphology that mimics the very pipes they tend. Their carapaces are not born of chitin, but of a composite material—a biological amalgam of calcite, oxidized iron, and the black, greasy sludge that coats everything. They look like walking industrial accidents, statues of scrap metal bound together by a thick, mucosal membrane.

They are nearly silent. In a place where silence (0.6) is a heavy presence, the Calipers move without clatter. Their joints are lubricated by the same viscous, charged fluid that drips from the ceiling, a substance that conducts the faint violet bioluminescence of the air. Where their shell plates rub together, a soft, wet light pulses, revealing that the "rust" on their backs is actually a dense colony of symbiotic, iron-eating microbes. The Calipers provide the microbes with mobility and access to fresh metal; in return, the microbes repair and thicken their shells, sealing cracks and knitting together wounds. They are self-healing armor, endlessly consuming the city to maintain themselves.

Their faces are devoid of features—no eyes, no mouths, only a smooth, sloping plate of bone-grey material. They do not need eyes in the violet gloom. They "see" through the soles of their feet and the sensitive tips of their elongated, multiple limbs. They taste the vibrations in the floor. They know when a piston is about to seize because they feel the microscopic shivering in the gantry steel seconds before it happens. They are the mechanics of the geologic timescale (0.7), positioned precisely where a pressure valve needs turning, waiting motionless for hours or days until the exact moment the machine exhales, at which point they will shift their weight, applying a ton of pressure with a single, casual shrug.

Beneath these great, silent guardians live the **Scavengers**, though to call them such is to diminish their role. They are the small, furry creatures with too many eyes, but up close, the fur reveals itself to be fine, wire-like copper filaments, caked in damp dust. They are skittish, nervous things, perpetually grooming the Calipers. They run along the massive limbs of the giants, picking away the parasitic lichens or clearing the intake vents of the Calipers’ respiratory systems. They act as hands and eyes for the blind giants, chittering in frequencies that vibrate through the metal skeletons of their hosts. When a Calipers needs to manipulate a delicate gear too small for its crushing claws, a dozen of the wire-furred ones descend, swarming the mechanism with tiny, dexterous hands, operating it like a collective extension of the giant’s will.

Then there are the **Drifters**, the gelatinous rays that haunt the air. They are not merely animals; they are the circulatory system of the hollow. They absorb the charged moisture from the air, filtering the chemical tang, and excreting a purified, nutrient-rich condensation that drips from their undersides in long, viscous ropes. This "honey" is the food source for the lower echelons. The Calipers rear up to catch the falling drips on their tongues—or rather, on the porous, sponge-like filtration patches located in their "throats." The Drifters are the gardeners in the sky, tending to the invisible flora of bacteria that keeps the air breathable, if barely.

The deepest mystery, however, lies in the **Sump-Things**. In the black water below the grates, where the mechanical limb reaches up like a drowned beggar, live shapes that are rarely seen. They are translucent, pale horrors that look like extracted spinal columns draped in wet velvet. They are the recycles. When a Calipers finally succumbs to age or irreparable damage, tumbling from a high gantry to shatter on the wet brick below, the Sump-Things rise. They do not eat; they dissolve. They envelop the fallen giant in a cloud of enzymatic solvent, breaking the complex biological machinery back down into its base components—sludge, iron, and calcite. The material is then reabsorbed into the sump, eventually to be drawn up by the pumps and rained down upon the city as the raw material for new growth.

There is no birth here, only assembly. There is no death here, only dismantling. The inhabitants do not fear the rust or the damp; they *are* the rust and the damp. They are the city’s way of walking, touching, and knowing itself. They wait in the twilight, their internal clocks tuned to the thrum of the earth, patient as the stone, content in the knowledge that as long as the walls weep, they will endure.
