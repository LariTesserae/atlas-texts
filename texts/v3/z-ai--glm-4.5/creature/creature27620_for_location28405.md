Here, the concept of "habitation" is indistinguishable from the concept of "infestation." To live in the Crèche is not to dwell in a place, but to be a constituent part of its digestion. The inhabitants are layered like sediment, compressed by the weight of their own ravenous biology.

At the very bottom, forming the literal topsoil of this hellscape, is the **Rust-Colony**. It is not a single species, but a writhing stratum of detritivores so dense they give the stone the appearance of breathing. These are the baseline organisms—pale, segmented worms the length of a forearm and translucent shelled crabs that scuttle sideways on millions of hooked legs. They exist in a state of perpetual frenzy, consuming the waste of the larger beasts, the constant rain of dead insects, and most importantly, the stone itself. They secrete a mild, foaming solvent that froths at their mouths, allowing them to lick the limestone until it is soft enough to eat. When you step on the "ground," the crunch is not rock, but the shattering of a million brittle shells and the popping of a thousand bloated, fluid-filled bodies. They are the damp rot beneath the fingernails of the world.

Rising from this sludge are the **Geomancers**, the massive, bus-length millipedes mentioned in the初步 observations. They are the tanks of the Crèche, the heavy armor pushing through the mud. Their bodies are covered in interlocking plates of dark, metallic chitin that scrape together with a sound like grinding tectonic plates, a low, bass-note rumble that vibrates in the soles of your feet. They do not see; they are blind, navigating through the tremors of the earth and the chemical signatures of the minerals they crave. Their legs are not mere limbs but hydraulic tools, tipped with diamond-hard pickaxes that tear at the earth. They are the architects of the uneven terrain, carving the valleys and crushing the smaller fauna into a nutrient paste. They are solitary engines of destruction, impervious to almost everything, yet their undersides are soft and vulnerable, constantly attended by cleaner-fish-like lizards that dart in and out to eat the parasites, risking their lives for a meal.

Above the ground, the air belongs to the **Shard-Wings**. They are not graceful; they are panicked. Resembling dragonflies but grown to the size of eagles, their bodies are faceted and jagged, constructed from translucent panels of chitin that catch the sickly green light of the ceiling, making them look like flying shards of stained glass. They move in erratic, jittery swarms, their wings humming with a high-pitched frequency that adds to the cavern’s deafening roar. They are aerial scavengers, possessing beaks that snap with the force of a bolt cutter. They dive onto the backs of the moving Geomancers, shearing off chunks of softer meat or fighting over the carcass of a fallen grazer. Their eyes are massive, black orbs that reflect nothing but greed, and they are known to turn on each other mid-flight, a chaotic explosion of wings and limbs tumbling down into the seething carpet below.

Hiding in plain sight among the jagged obsidian spurs are the **Silica-Spiders**. They are ambush predators that do not spin webs, but rather mimic the environment. Their bodies are jagged and rough, coated in a layer of dust and crushed limestone that they glue to their carapaces with their own saliva. They sit motionless for hours, indistinguishable from a rock formation, waiting for the vibration of a passing Grazer. When they strike, they unfurl with terrifying speed—legs that are mostly spike and muscle. They impale their prey, injecting a liquefying venom that turns the victim's internal organs into soup, which the spider then drinks through a specialized proboscis. They are the silence in the noise, the sudden stop in the chaos.

And then, there are **Weavers**.

The intelligence that clings to the high points is a species that has evolved not to conquer the chaos, but to curate it. They are slight, hunched creatures, standing perhaps four feet tall on digitigrade legs. Their skin is a mottled grey, lacking any pigment, a camouflage against the wet rock. They have no hair, only a series of sensory quills running down their spines that twitch with the changes in air pressure, warning them of the earth’s contractions.

Their faces are dominated by large, sensitive eyes and a complex set of mandibles that click when they are nervous—which is always. They are the architects of the crude bone-and-chitin scaffolds, structures built not for comfort, but for harvest. They are the only ones who understand the Rhythm.

When the silence falls and the pressure drops, the Weavers freeze, gripping the handholds of their high perches. They know the "Birth" is coming. As the floor heaves and new rock pushes up, steaming and rich with undiscovered minerals, the Weavers do not flee. They watch. As soon as the tremor stops, they descend. They use the chaos to their advantage, rushing down on ropes made of braided insect gut to the fresh, exposed stone. There, they harvest the *first* catch—the stunned, buried creatures exposed by the shift, and the fresh, soft limestone that hasn't yet hardened.

They are scavengers with a long view. They do not just hunt; they process. On their high scaffolds, they dry the meat of the giant grazers, curing it in the hot, rising air using smoke from fires fueled by the sulfur-rich grease of the grubs. They craft their spears from the leg-bones of the largest predators, sharpening the obsidian to a monomolecular edge. They wear armor made from the plates of dead millipedes, lashed together with the sinews of the flying things.

They are not masters of the Crèche; they are its librarians. They record the shifts in the rock, marking the passing of time by the layers of new stone. They fear the green light, worshipping it as a terrible, hungry god that requires the sacrifice of meat to keep glowing. Their lives are a desperate, bloody vigil, perched on the edge of oblivion, eating the world before it eats them. In the Crèche, intelligence is just another survival trait, like a hard shell or a poisonous sting—a tool to delay the inevitable digestion.
