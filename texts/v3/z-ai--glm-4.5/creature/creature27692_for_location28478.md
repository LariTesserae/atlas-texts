They are the children of the gear and the spark, born not of biology in the traditional sense, but of the deep, grinding pressure between industry and survival. To look upon them is to look upon a living engine, a biological interpretation of a piston.

The primary inhabitants—let us call them the **Chitin-Forgers**—are densely packed, moving not as individuals but as a singular, viscous fluid across the iron decks. They are roughly the size of a large dog or a small calf, possessed of a segmented, carapace-armored body that mimics the very obsidian rock they cling to. This shell is not merely bone; it is an alloy of keratin and ingested metal, scavenged from the city they inhabit. Over their lifespans, which are short and violent, their bodies accrete the iron filings and rust dust of the air, plating themselves in a natural armor that is dark, pitted, and scarred.

They move on six, sometimes eight, limbs. The front pair are massive, serrated pincers that function less as hands and more as industrial tools: hydraulic shears for cutting through piping, heavy hammers for riveting, and delicate clamps for threading needles through the high-pressure valves. The rear legs are hooked and spiked, evolved to grip the slick, vertical surfaces of the city. They do not walk so much as they clamp; they hook themselves onto a railing, a gear-tooth, or a rivet, anchoring against the perpetual 2.7 tremor of the earth.

Because the air is a sound vacuum (a mere 1.3 on the scale), they do not communicate vocally. To scream here is to waste breath that tastes of sandpaper. Instead, they speak through the metal. Their bodies are equipped with stridulating plates on their abdomens—rough, ridged sections of shell that they scrape against the city’s ironwork. This creates a symphony of clicks, hums, and thrums that travel instantly through the architecture. A worker on the eastern gantry does not need to see a crack form in the western pipe; it feels the vibration of a specific distress-signal traveling through the mountain’s spine, and the swarm shifts accordingly.

Their faces are hauntingly blank, dominated by two primary sensory organs. Since the light is a bruised, dim purple (filtered through industrial haze), they do not rely on standard sight. Their eyes are black, glassy orbs that dilate widely to catch the faint bioluminescence of the silicon lichen. But more importantly, they possess thermal pits along their snouts, sensing the invisible rivers of heat flowing through the pipes. They "see" the world as a map of temperatures: the boiling red of a main steam conduit, the cool blue of the outer abyss, and the warm, golden glow of their fellow workers.

Life here is a desperate, rigorous hydrology. With water at a mere 0.2, the Chitin-Forgers are masters of retention. They do not sweat; they do not exhale moisture. Their waste is a dry, crystalline dust, instantly discarded. They hydrate by "milking" the silicon lichen. Using their small, manipulator mouthparts, they scrape the gray, pulsing mats off the hottest pipes. The lichen, saturated with superheated steam and heavy metals, provides both the water and the raw mineral energy their metal-heavy bodies require. To eat is to risk burning, so they have developed a throat lining of asbestos-like tissue, allowing them to swallow boiling, mineral-rich sludge without injury.

Socially, they are a hive mind defined by utility. There is no hierarchy of royalty, only a hierarchy of function. You can tell their role by their modifications. The "Riveters" have oversized hammers for claws and flattened heads designed to fit into tight crevices. The "Scavengers" are smaller, lighter, capable of jumping across the vast, cantilevered gaps to catch falling debris or unfortunates before they are lost to the clouds below. The "Dredges" are lowest on the ladder, bloated creatures whose shells have softened from exposure to the oily rain; they scrape the decks for the viscous residue of the mist, filtering out what drinkable moisture they can.

And then there are the gliders.

The **Glass-Wings** are the shadow of this ecosystem. While the Forgers are the heavy industry, the gliders are the quality control. Resembling immense, translucent moths with a wingspan of six feet, they fill the air above the walkways. Their wings are thin as membrane, nearly invisible against the twilight, patterned with the chaotic fractals of circuitry. They possess no legs to speak of, only hooked talons meant for snatching and perching.

They are silent hunters. In the thin air, a flap of wings is a danger; instead, they ride the thermals generated by the steam vents, spiraling upward in lazy circles, then diving like falcons. They feed not on the lichen, but on the Forgers themselves—usually the injured, the slow, or the Dredges who wander too far from the safety of the swarm. A strike is instantaneous: a blur of translucence, a flash of a hooked beak, and a worker is plucked from a ledge, vanishing into the bruised sky without a sound.

The relationship is not predator and prey in the traditional sense, but a mechanic of entropy. The Glass-Wings cull the weak to ensure the efficiency of the machine. The Forgers, in turn, tolerate them, and occasionally, the largest gliders will even land on the gearing, allowing the Forgers to scrape parasites from their wings in exchange for scraps of metal.

The true horror, however—and the true genius—of these creatures is how they embody the city’s instability. When the ground shifts (as it does every few minutes) and the massive gyroscopes groan beneath the floorplates, the Chitin-Forgers do not panic. They synchronize. The vibration of the fault line is met with a counter-vibration from the swarm. Thousands of legs lock onto railings; thousands of bodies brace in unison. They become living ballast, a dense, heavy counterweight that leans *into* the sway of the buildings. They are not just living in the clockwork; they are the cogs that keep it from flying apart.

They are a miserable, beautiful race. They exist in a state of perpetual tightening, forever turning the bolt that refuses to stay tight, forever scraping the lichen that grows back in minutes, forever shivering in the glacial wind while bathed in the heat of the steam. They do not dream of the ocean miles below; they likely do not understand the concept of "softness." They are hard, sharp, and enduring—a perfect reflection of the obsidian shard they call home.
