They are a people who have forgotten the architecture of silence. You can see it in their bodies first—stocky, dense frames evolved or bred to push through the heavy air. They are broad-shouldered and thick-necked, not with the softness of sloth, but with the hydraulic density of deep-sea divers. Their skin is the colour of wet slate, perpetually glistening, coated in a thin, oily film that protects them from the ceaseless dampness. They do not shiver; they seem to emit their own low, feverish heat, a metabolic fire necessary to survive the twilight chill.

Their faces are dominated by the mouth and the lungs. Noses are wide, flared vents to suck in oxygen from the thick atmosphere, and their chests are barrel-like, expanding and contracting with the force of bellows. But it is the throat that defines them. The larynx is visibly swollen, a heavy lump of cartilage at the neck, giving them a perpetual, bulging appearance. They are a species of shouters. Their vocal cords are thick leather straps capable of vibrating for days without rest. They do not speak; they broadcast. Conversations are not exchanges of information but duels of volume, a wrestling match of sound waves where the only way to be heard is to drown out the roar of the city and the scream of the amphibians.

They are dressed in rags that have rotted into armor. Clothing here is not made; it is accumulated. Layers of scavenged fabric, heavy with water, are wrapped around their limbs and bound with wet leather cords. The outer layers are crusted with the grey mud of the basin, hardening into crude, ceramic shells that offer some protection against the sharp shale. The wood they prize—the scavenged timber from "elsewhere"—is worn as amulets or carved into heavy, rune-staffs that are thumped against the ground to create rhythm in the chaos.

Their eyes are strikingly large, black pools that drink in the meagre grey-gold light. They have adapted to a world without shadow definition; their vision is tuned to movement and contrast rather than colour. They blink rarely, staring with a fixed, unblinking intensity that suggests they are always watching for the slip of a rock or the movement of one of the beasts.

And they are always working. There is no leisure here, only the exertion that matches the roar. Their hands are scarred, wrapped in crude bandages to protect against the wet stone and the bite of the primitive tools. They move with a chaotic, jerky energy, lifting and hauling in a frenzy that feels less like construction and more like a desperate attempt to keep the city from collapsing back into the mud. They are not building a future; they are maintaining the present. Every mud-brick that dissolves is replaced instantly. Every rope that frays is spliced in a frenzy of knots. They are the antibodies of the stone, endlessly repairing the organism they inhabit.

Their relationship with the amphibians is one of brutal symbiosis. The dog-sized beasts are everywhere, draped over the shoulders of the workers like wet, smelly sashes, or clustering in the corners of the mud-brick huts. The inhabitants ignore the shrieks unless a beast comes too close; then, a heavy, calloused foot will kick it aside with the same indifference used to clear a puddle. When hunger strikes, they do not hunt; they simply reach out and snatch a nearby frog. The killing is quick—a sharp twist, a rip of the slick skin—and the eating is messy, done while continuing to work or shout. The frogs are the cattle of this place, livestock that requires no herding, simply harvested from the ambient noise.

Psychologically, they are locked in the eternal Now. Without a day/night cycle, they have no concept of sleep schedules. They do not sleep in long stretches; instead, they fade into a trancelike stupor while leaning against a wall or sitting on a wet ledge, their eyes half-closed, their bodies swaying, but their mouths never fully closing. Even in this state, they mutter and hum, contributing to the roar. They have no dreams, or at least, they do not remember them. The constant noise invades the skull, leaving no empty space for the mind to wander into fantasy. They are purely, terrifyingly present—creatures of impulse and reaction, driven by the rhythm of the hammer and the croak of the beast.

They are beautiful in a horrific way, a perfect mirror of the wet, grey hell they inhabit. They are hard, loud, and enduring, a people who have carved a humanity out of the absence of change. They are the city, and the city is them—a single, screaming organism frozen in a moment of wet, noisy eternity.
