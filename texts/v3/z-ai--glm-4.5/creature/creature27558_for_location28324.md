At first, the silence tricks you. It feels empty, a vacuum waiting to be filled. But as your eyes adjust to the blinding, monochromatic glare and the indigo shadows, you realize the "forest" is not standing still. The sentinels are breathing.

They are the Keepers of the Drip, though they have no name for themselves. They are upright, bipedal, and impossibly tall, easily matching the height of the surrounding stone pillars. To look at them is to suffer a momentary hallucination of geology; they are so perfectly camouflaged that your mind refuses to parse them as living things. Their skin is not flesh, but a dense, pliable armor of calcite and grey chitin, arranged in overlapping plates that shift silently against one another. These plates are wet, constantly slick with the same freezing condensation that coats the stone. Veins of translucent quartz run through their extremities, catching the punishing sunlight and refracting it, so that a limb might suddenly glow with an inner, luminous heat that is merely an illusion of light.

They stand motionless for hours, their legs rooted in the scree, their posture mimicking the natural slant and erosion of the mineral formations. They have no faces as we understand them. Where a head should be, the calcite armor smooths into a sensory dome, a radar dish of porous bone that reads the humidity and the pressure. They do not need eyes in a place where the sun blinds and the shadows are absolute. They "see" through vibration.

They are the architects of the acoustic weirdness.

Their presence explains the whispering gallery effect. As they stand among the pillars, they sub-vocalize—a low-frequency thrumming produced by grinding plates in their throats. This sound is below the threshold of human hearing, felt more as a pressure in the teeth than a noise in the ears. They use the stone forest as a resonance chamber, sending signals that bounce around the amphitheater, wrapping around the natural pillars to create a web of sonar. This is how they hunt. They are not hunting meat, however; there is no meat here. They are hunting *fracture*.

When the ice shatters, they move.

The motion is terrifyingly fast for something so large, then abruptly still. They feed on the kinetic energy of the breaking ice and the chemical composition of the mineral-rich tears. You will see one suddenly detach itself from the shadows, gliding over the scree on feet that distribute their weight so perfectly they do not crunch the gravel. It approaches a natural cup of stone where a fragile rim of ice has just cracked. The creature leans over, its sensory dome hovering inches above the water. It extends a long, needle-thin proboscis from beneath its armor plates and pierces the surface of the pool, drinking the melt before it can sublimate.

This is the economy of the place: speed to secure the water before the air steals it, stillness to conserve the heat.

The cairns are theirs.

The primitive stacks of slate are not ruins; they are tools, placed by the Keepers with calculated precision. Because the stone forest is chaotic, the acoustics can be muddy. The cairns act as tuning forks and diffusers. A Keeper will stack the rough slate to catch the wind, creating a specific hum that amplifies their low-frequency calls. They tend these stacks with the dexterity of a jeweler, balancing the heavy stones with a delicacy that belies their size. If a cairn falls, they stop their endless vigil and rebuild it, driven by a instinctual need to maintain their communications network.

There is a social order here, dictated by the light. The glare is their enemy, as it overheats their cold-blooded physiology. They jostle for position in the deepest indigo shadows, their tall, grey forms pressing shoulder to shoulder, vibrating softly against one another in a manner that suggests both comfort and territorial assertion. When the "white glare" becomes too intense, they enter a state of torpor, locking their joints and closing their sensory plates, becoming literal statues until the sun dips or the fog rolls in.

They are aware of you, but you are irrelevant.

You are a source of noise. Your heartbeat is a drum, your footsteps a chaotic thunder. As you stand in the geological throat, you will feel the pressure of their attention—the low-frequency hum rising in pitch, the air seeming to thicken. They do not attack; they simply turn their sensory domes toward you, triangulating your position. You are a geological anomaly to them, a moving rock that makes too much vibration. They will watch you with blind faces until you leave, waiting for the silence to return so they can go back to their slow, cold drinking of the stone's tears.
