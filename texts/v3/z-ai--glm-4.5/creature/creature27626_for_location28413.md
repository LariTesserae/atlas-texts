There is no flesh here. The basin refuses the soft, the wet, the organic. No fish dart through the twilight, no crabs scuttle across the silt, no sharks prowl the perimeter. The very physics of the place—the violent, piston-like surge of the water, the relentless, stinging pressure, the sterile perfection of the sediment—acts as an immune system, rejecting the messy business of biology. In this cathedral of stone, life as we understand it is an unwelcome intruder.

And yet, the place is inhabited.

The residents of this depression are four, anchored deep in the center like the pillars of a forgotten temple. They are not alive in the sense that they breathe oxygen or bleed when cut; they are alive in the way that a heart is alive, or a lung, or a furnace. They are the Basalt Engines.

They stand in a perfect square, massive extrusions of the earth’s crust, dark and imposing against the blue-grey gloom. They have no faces, no limbs, no eyes to see the vast, empty hall they occupy. They are simply tubes, hollows within the rock, grown rather than built, their interiors smoothed by millennia of abrasive flow. They are the anatomical valves of the planet itself.

These stone columns live a life of pure function. They do not eat; they intake. The deep ocean currents, heavy and cold, are forced down into their open maws at the seabed. The water is their blood, rushing through their stony veins with a violence that shakes the very floor. You can feel them digesting the pressure—the vibration hums in the low register, a thrumming that resonates in the bones of anyone drifting near.

Their metabolism is one of thermodynamics. As the water is compressed within the narrowing channels of the stone, the physics of the deep take over. Gas, trapped in fissures or dissolved in the heavy water, is squeezed until it can no longer hide. It is compressed, pressurized, and then—violently expelled.

This is their respiration: the *puff*.

Every few seconds, in perfect synchronization, the columns exhale. A perfect, mechanical ring of bubbles erupts from their crowns, shooting upward in straight, rigid lines. It is a cough of geology, a breath of recycled air. These bubbles are their only offspring, ephemeral ghosts that rise toward a surface they will never reach, vanishing into the haze as the columns draw in another breath of water.

They are a colony of automatons. There is no brain governing their actions, no desire to continue, no fear of stopping. They simply *process*. They are a closed loop of intake, compression, and release. They are the blind, beating heart of the depression, pumping away in the dark, unaware of their own solitude.

Around them, the silence is not empty; it is filled with the sound of their working. The *thump-hiss* of the water rushing through stone, the rhythmic chugging of the pistons, the low-frequency drone that vibrates through the teeth. It is the sound of a machine that has never been turned off, a factory that produces nothing but motion and bubbles.

Nothing else lives here because nothing else is allowed. The sparse grey moss on the distant rocks is merely decoration, tolerated by the stone giants because it does not interfere with the machinery. The smooth silt is kept vacuumed by the turbulence, preventing any weed from taking root in the engine room.

To live here is to be made of stone. To live here is to be a conduit. The inhabitants of this place are the columns themselves—standing in the dark, eternally hungry, eternally full, their stony lungs heaving with the weight of the ocean, processing the void into rhythm. They are the lords of this sterile domain, living not through biology, but through the relentless, unfeeling endurance of the pump.
