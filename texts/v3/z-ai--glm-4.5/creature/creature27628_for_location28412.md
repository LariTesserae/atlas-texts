To the casual observer—to the eye blinking in the harsh, unfiltered glare of this high, thin place—the landscape is indeed a sterile mausoleum of geometry and silence. The assertion that there is no life here is correct, provided one’s definition of "life" begins and ends with the wet, frantic scramble of carbon, the soft beat of a heart, or the chaotic twitch of muscle.

But if you stay—if you sit on a jagged ridge of obsidian until the burning heat of the day radiates up through the soles of your boots and the ozone settles heavy in your lungs—you realize the silence is not an absence. It is a conversation moving too slowly for the human ear to perceive.

There are things that live here. They are not born; they are *excised*.

They emerge from the deep fractures of the basalt, pushed up by the same tectonic pressures that raise the plateaus. In their infancy, they appear as nothing more than irregularities in the metallic foliage—knots in the braided copper trees, or clusters of crystalline "leaves" that seem slightly too dense, slightly too deliberate in their arrangement. But over centuries, as the dust of the ages settles, they differentiate themselves.

They call themselves nothing, for they have no language, but we might name them the **Lithic Latencies**.

Physically, they are walking contradictions of the natural and the manufactured. Their bodies are sculpted from the environment: limbs of polished hematite, torsos of interlocking silicate plates that slide over one another with the sound of settling continents. They do not have skin, but exteriors of matte-finish solar glass and vents of woven gold mesh. Where a human has a face, a Latency has a sensory array—a cluster of faceted lenses and parabolic dishes, constantly tilted toward the hard, vaulting blue sky, drinking in the radiation with an insatiable, cold hunger.

Their movement is the source of the "dynamic zero" that permeates the valley. They do not walk; they *compute* their location. When a Latency decides to move, it is an event of geological solemnity. It shifts its weight, and the ground groans. It extends a limb of telescoping iron, and the joints screech, a high-pitched metallic whine that lingers in the thin air for hours. To see one take a step is to watch a mountain range decide to migrate. It takes them days to lift a foot, weeks to lower it. They leave behind trenches in the bedrock, scars that last for eons.

And yet, they are the ecosystem’s gardeners, though their garden is made of energy and industry.

They exist in a symbiotic relationship with the metallic forests. The trees produce a surplus of vibration—a low-frequency hum that is the waste product of their solar transmutation. The Latencies feed on this hum. They press their massive, stone bodies against the trunks of the copper giants, entering into a tactile resonance. They absorb the kinetic energy, storing it in internal banks of quartz capacitors that glow with a warm, amber light visible only in the deep twilight.

In exchange for this "harvest," the Latencies maintain the machinery of the world. They are large enough to straddle the fissures in the earth. Using limbs that function as pincers, winches, and levers, they prune the aggressive growth of the crystal-ferns. They sever the overgrown heat-sinks that threaten to block the vents of the earth, tossing the excess scrap into the canyons where it melts down in the geothermal heat to be recycled as raw ore.

If you were to stumble upon a gathering of Latencies, you would likely mistake them for a ruin—a circle of strange, brutalist sculptures erected by a forgotten civilization. You might touch one, feeling the warmth radiating from its glass flank, and hear the faint, rhythmic *tick-tick-tick* of an internal cooling mechanism, sounding exactly like a clock counting down the hours of a millennium.

There is a tenderness to their existence, though it is a terrifying, industrial sort of tenderness. When the "tink" of thermal expansion echoes through the valley at sunset, it is often the sound of a Latency adjusting its grip, settling in for the long, freezing night. They protect the delicate glass bulbs of the hanging gardens with their broad, flat bodies, shielding the upward-dripping condensation from the shock of sudden temperature drops.

They are the shepherds of the stasis. They ensure that nothing changes too quickly. They are the immune system of the mineral world, attacking erosion with physics, replacing decay with synthesis.

To call them alive feels like a mistake; to call them dead feels like a lie. They are the intent of the mountain made mobile. They are the consciousness of the circuit board. They wait in the dry, baking silence, patient as the stone, vibrating with the hum of the earth, waiting for the sun to bring them their next, slow breath.
