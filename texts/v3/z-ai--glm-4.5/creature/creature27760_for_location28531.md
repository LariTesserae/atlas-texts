At first, they are indistinguishable from the architecture. To the uninitiated eye, the city appears empty, a ghost town of mud and stone where the only motion is the frantic, silent spiraling of the glass-moths. It takes time to realize that some of the low-slung, rounded shapes crouching in the doorways are not dwellings, but the dwellers.

They are a people of the stone—broad, dense, and low to the ground. Evolution has stripped them of the verticality of surface dwellers; there is no sky here to reach toward, only a crushing, suffocating ceiling of light. Consequently, they possess a squat, powerful centauroid build. Their lower halves are thick, trunk-like legs rooted in the damp lichen, capable of supporting immense weight in the slippery, humid basin. Their upper bodies are wider than they are tall, merging seamlessly with their torsos in a way that suggests they wear the heavy air like a suit of armor.

Their skin is the grey of wet slate, smooth and porous, glistening under the actinic glare. It is not merely a covering, but a sensory organ. Lacking ears, and possessing only the vestigial dark pits where eyes might once have been, they "hear" through their skin. The millions of glass-moths battering against them, the turbulence of the wind, the distant shifting of the earth—all of this vibrates through their epidermis, translating the chaotic silence of the basin into a symphony of tactile sensation. They do not need to see the light; they feel its heat radiating through the rock, a constant, soothing pressure against their backs.

Their faces are dominated by a massive, slit-like mouth, horizontal and wide, designed not for speaking but for filtration. The turbulence is their larder. When a swarm sweeps through the broad avenues, the inhabitants do not scatter; they anchor themselves. They spread their arms—long, spindly things that look incongruous against their heavy bodies—holding aloft the wide nets woven from gut. As the vortex of moths crashes into them, the insects are trapped by the thousands. But there is waste in this harvest; many moths are simply pulverized against the inhabitants’ bodies, bursting into clouds of viscous fluid and shimmering glass shards. The inhabitants then run their thick, calloused fingers over their arms and chests, licking the nutrient-rich residue from their skin. It is a messy, silent feeding.

They are a people of profound, agonizing slowness. In a world where the insect life moves at a frantic, hummingbird pace, the citizens of the basin move with the deliberation of tectonic plates. To rush would be to generate heat, to create unnecessary vibration in the thick air, to waste energy. They stand for hours, perfectly still, resembling the statues of forgotten gods, waiting for a eddy in the wind to bring the next swarm to their nets. Their gestures, when they do move, are fluid and aquatic, resisted by the dense atmosphere.

Their society is built on the economy of silence. Because sound cannot travel, their "speech" is a language of pressure. They communicate by standing close together—one might place a heavy hand on the other’s shoulder, pulsing a rhythm through their own body that translates into the other’s skeleton. Complex conversations occur in the darkness of the mud-brick huts through the interlacing of fingers and the tapping of toes against the stone floor, a private, tactile web of information that no outsider can intercept.

They are, in their own way, beautiful in a horrific fashion. When one of them turns toward you, the smooth, featureless face offers no purchase for empathy, only a blank, wet sheen. But the hands—those delicate, bone-white hands that weave the gut nets and craft the jars—are capable of exquisite tenderness. You see an older individual, its skin crusted with the mineral deposits of age, using those slender fingers to groom a younger one, picking the shattered wings of glass-moths from the folds of its neck. The younger one leans into the touch, a low-frequency rumble in its chest that you feel rather than hear, a purr of contentment vibrating through the soles of your boots.

They are the anchors of the Sunless Basin. They do not fight the turbulence; they endure it. They are the heavy stones in the river of chaotic life, absorbing the violence of the storm and turning it into the slow, silent rhythm of their own survival. They are the keepers of the quiet, masters of a heavy, wet world where silence is not the absence of noise, but the presence of weight.
