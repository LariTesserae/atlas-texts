They are the Unanchored, a people defined not by borders or bloodlines, but by the refusal to be digested.

To see them, you must look past the violence of the landscape, past the grinding pillars of obsidian wood and the streaming rivers of armored beasts. Look for the movement that does not belong to the wind. The Unanchored are slight, elongated creatures, built less like predators and more like spindly instruments of survival. They stand perhaps six feet tall if stretched to full height, but they rarely do; they perpetually crouch, shoulders hunched, knees bent, ready to spring or scramble at the vibration of a snapping branch.

Their skin is the color of dried parchment, tight and leathery over a framework of dense, light bone. It serves as armor against the dry heat and a barrier against the spore-clouds that drift through the amber air. This skin is not smooth; it is textured with microscopic ridges that channel the dust away from their pores. They have no hair—hair is a liability here, a handle for a passing predator or a trap for the sticky, toxic saps that drip from the canopy. Instead, their heads are crowned with plumes of coarse, hollow bristles that vibrate with the ambient thrum of the caldera, acting as a primitive early-warning system against the approach of the larger, blind herbivores.

Their faces are masks of desperation and adaptation. The nose is reduced to a series of horizontal slits, lined with thick hairs to filter the heavy pollen before it reaches the lungs. The eyes are large, shielded by nictitating membranes that allow them to see through the swirling, golden dust without being blinded. They do not blink often; in the Sunken Nursery, to close your eyes is to die. Their mouths are small, rimmed with keratinous beaks, designed not for chewing tough vegetation but for the precise, surgical extraction of fluids.

They live in the Scaffolds—massive, unstable villages suspended hundreds of feet above the churning floor. The Scaffolds are not built; they are grown and butchered. They are constructed from the slabs of calcified bark shed by the giant stalks, lashed together with the dried, unbreakable tendons of the flying leviathans. The ropes are living things, thick vines that pulse with a slow, metabolic rhythm, constantly tightening their grip on the bone-and-wood platforms. The Unanchored must constantly prune these structural ropes, paring back the seeking rootlets with obsidian blades, lest the architecture decide to consume the inhabitants.

Climbing is their first language. From birth, an Unanchored child is strapped to its mother's back in a sling of woven gut, learning the rhythm of the ascent before it learns to walk. Their limbs are double-jointed, and their hands and feet are dominated by long, hooked fingers that can lock into the smallest crevice of bark. They move through the vertical city with the fluidity of spiders, never touching the ground, never lingering in the open. To step onto the floor of the nursery is to enter the digestive tract of the world; down there, the "rivers" of migration flow.

Because there is no water, the Unanchored are hunters of hydration. Their society revolves around the "tapping" of the mega-flora. Using long, hollow probes made from the leg bones of their enemies, they bore into the pressurized veins of the obsidian trees. They must be quick. The sap is under immense pressure and is often boiling hot; it spurts out in scalding geysers that must be caught in sealed gourds. This sap is thick, syrupy, and rich with alkaloids—the fuel of their frantic metabolism. It tastes of cinnamon and copper.

The noise of the nursery has dictated their evolution of silence. They possess no vocal cords capable of speech above a whisper. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of clicks, whistles, and percussive taps made against the hollow bones of their structures. This "bone-talking" allows them to coordinate complex hunting parties without alerting the noise-sensitive predators that glide through the amber gloom.

Their primary prey are the "Sky-Stingrays," the leathery, delta-winged flyers that patrol the air currents. Hunting them is a spectacle of terrifying bravery. Hunters position themselves on the outermost edges of the Scaffolds, holding nets woven from translucent gut. They use pheromones harvested from giant glands to lure the stingrays close. When the beast strikes, the net is thrown, and the hunter must leap into the void, trusting the safety line of his tribe to hold. If the line snaps—a not uncommon occurrence given the strength of the prey—the hunter is swept away into the storm, lost to the green hell.

Life among the Unanchored is a collective, trembling vigilance. They sleep in shifts, curled together in the hollowed-out shells of giant seed pods for warmth. Even in sleep, their hooked hands remain locked onto the edges of their pods. They dream of falling.

Their mythology is simple and brutal. They believe they are the "Fever" of the forest—a temporary irritation on the skin of a waking giant. They do not speak of a future, for the future belongs only to the plants and the beasts that can survive the crushing acceleration of the Nursery’s growth. They speak only of the *Now*, of the tightness of the rope, the sharpness of the obsidian, and the sweetness of the sap.

To be an Unanchored is to be in a state of permanent, ecstatic escape. They are a people running on a treadmill made of living wood, sprinting just to stay in the same place, forever climbing the back of a beast that shakes them off with every breath. They are beautiful in their terror—lean, scarred, and luminous eyes wide open, watching the horizon for the next tree to fall, the next gap to open, the next chance to survive the digestion.
