The living things here do not distinguish between biology and industry. To exist in the Garden-Gallery is to be a component of the machine-forest, a cog made of meat, a wire made of keratin. The inhabitants are a cast of evolved specialists, creatures that have slipped through the cracks of the old world and adapted to fill the gears of the new one.

On the floor of the basin, moving through the rising steam, are the Ground-Keepers. They are the descendants of the capybara-like rodents mentioned, but evolution has refined them into something far stranger. They are low-slung, heavy-set creatures the size of oil drums, possessed of six splayed legs that end not in paws, but in dense, calloused pads that squeak softly against the wet slate. Their fur is not soft; it is a dense, oily pelt of coarse bristles that repels the condensation, shimmering with a slick, rainbow sheen of machine-cutting fluids.

Their faces are the source of the "socket wrench" comparison. Their incisors have grown together into a single, crescent-shaped beak of black enamel, designed to shear through the steel-hard bark of the pillars. When they feed, they do not graze; they sculpt. They strip away the excess layers of sclerenchyma that threaten to clog the intake valves of the living pillars. They are the maintenance crew of the understory. You can hear them working—a constant, rhythmic *crunch-scrape, crunch-scrape* that echoes through the gloom. They move with a hurried, anxious efficiency, stopping only to twitch their whiskers—long, filament-like sensors that detect the electromagnetic hum of the pillars, ensuring they never bite into a live conduit.

Above them, the air is owned by the Flock.

These are the birds of the bruised twilight, but "birds" is a generous term. They are aerodynamic veils of engineered biology. Their feathers have been replaced by overlapping scales of flexible copper and thin aluminum plating. They do not flap so much as they *displace* air, their wings moving with a metallic shimmer that sounds like pages flipping in a giant book. They possess eyes that are wide, glassy lenses devoid of pupils, glowing with the faint green of the phosphorescent veins. They see in thermal gradients, tracking the heat signatures of the pumping sap.

They are not merely nesting; they are cooling the system. When they land on the pillars, their metallic feet sink slightly into the hot bark. They splay their wings, acting as heat sinks, drawing the excess thermal energy from the plant-engines and dissipating it into the damp air. In return, they lap at the nutrient-rich resin that bleeds from the bark. It is a transaction of thermodynamics. Their murmurations are hypnotic—clouds of copper confetti swirling in the dark, blocking out the light, their calls a chaotic screeching of static and feedback, like a radio tuned between stations.

But the ecosystem requires order, and for that, there are the Enforcers.

High in the vaults, patrolling the spaces where the light fades to black, are the Winged things that hunt. They resemble skeletal pterosaurs, their leathery wings reinforced with veins of steel cable. They are silent, save for the high-pitched whistle of air passing through their hollow, carbon-fiber bones. They do not hunt for food alone; they hunt for malfunctions. They target the wounded—the Ground-Keepers who have chewed too deep and sparked a flame, or the copper-birds that have become sluggish with rust.

When they dive, it is with the speed of a falling piston. Their beaks are needle-sharp drills. They do not tear; they puncture, administering a precise neurotoxin that freezes the prey instantly. In this place, a messy kill is a leak in the system. They are the quality control, removing the faulty parts before the contagion of error can spread. They carry their prey back to the upper canopy, where they feed, and the waste falls to the floor below, instantly dissolved by the aggressive fungi that coats the wet stone, closing the loop of energy.

And then there are the unseen, the deep-dwellers.

If you were to cut open one of the massive pillars—and the sound of ripping lignin would suggest you are tearing through the hull of a ship—you would find the Sap-Swarmers. These are pale, eyeless eel-like creatures that swim through the pressurized vascular channels of the forest-god. They have hooks along their bellies to anchor themselves against the current. They filter the industrial sap, eating the heavy metals and sludge that the biological engines cannot process, excreting purified nutrients. They are the liver and kidneys of the chamber, hidden deep within the wood and metal, silent and essential.

Even the air itself is inhabited. The warm mist that rains down is alive with spores, but not just any spores. These are microscopic, electrically charged organisms—*aero-plankton*—that float in the haze. They glow faintly, contributing to the bruised twilight. When they land on skin, they tingle, a static shock that tastes of copper. They feed on the ozone and the sound waves, thriving in the acoustic pressure of the room.

In the Garden-Gallery, life is a verb defined by labor. The creatures do not live *in* the forest; they *operate* it. They are the immune system of a breathing machine, swarming, clicking, screeching, and hunting in a perfect, terrifying equilibrium, keeping the heart of the titan beating long after the world above has turned to dust.
