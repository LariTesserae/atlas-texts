They are the masonry of motion, the citizens of this calcified metropolis. To look at them is to look at the city itself, for the line between the "architecture" and the "inhabitant" has been erased by the slow, grinding hand of evolution.

The most numerous denizens are the **Pavement-Keepers**. They are the ones who give the ground its shimmering, shifting quality. From a distance, they appear to be nothing more than multi-hued pebbles or scattered jewels—obsidian, rose-quartz, and flecks of pyrite. But as the hours stretch on and the sun crawls across the sky, the "pebbles" detach themselves from the dust. They are small, flat-bodied creatures, perhaps the size of a dinner plate, with shells that mimic the angular fractures of the bedrock perfectly. They possess six legs, but they do not articulate like a crab’s; rather, they telescope, extending and retracting with hydraulic smoothness. They move by flowing over the stone, their bellies polished to a mirror sheen that allows them to glide with minimal friction.

They are the grazers of this silent world. They do not eat leaves, for the leaves are stone. Instead, they possess specialized mouthparts—a tiny, diamond-tipped drill and a rasping tongue—that bore into the waxy, coral-like bark of the living towers. They sip the metabolic vapor, the warm, humid breath that the trees exhale from their vent-spines. A herd of Pavement-Keepers will align themselves along a fracture line on a tree-trunk, a dozen living gems locked in place, feeding for days at a time without moving a muscle, indistinguishable from the mineral deposits around them.

And then, there is the apex of this stillness—the **Monolith**.

There is likely only one Monolith per plaza, and identifying it is a test of perception. The Monolith is a predator, but it does not chase. It is a master of camouflage so absolute it makes the others look clumsy. It resembles a standing stone, a jagged, solitary pillar of rough-hewn granite, often situated at a busy intersection of the "streets" where the Pavement-Keepers frequently pass.

It stands perfectly still, not for hours, but for years. Its shell is a composite of the local minerals, textured with micro-fractures that collect dust and lichen, making it smell exactly of the ancient earth. It waits for the vibrational signature of a creature moving too slowly, or perhaps for the specific chemical signature of a grazing Pavement-Keeper that has wandered just a fraction too close.

When it strikes, it is not a flurry of motion. It is a single, catastrophic event. The Monolith does not lunge; it simply *collapses* in a specific direction. Its heavy, stone-like body shifts gravity instantly, pinning the victim beneath tons of biological weight. The impact is vibrationally deafening to the small creatures around them—a localized earthquake—though to a human observer, it might look like nothing more than a rock settling.

Once the prey is crushed, the Monolith secretes a solvent acid from glands along its underside, dissolving the shell and soft tissues of the victim into a nutrient slurry, which it absorbs through its pores. Then, over the course of several days, it slowly re-erects itself, inch by inch, hydraulic fluid pumping back into its lifting columns, until it is once again a silent, innocuous boulder in the city of stone.

All of them, from the tiniest jewel-like grazer to the massive, dormant Monolith, share the same desperate conservation of energy. They are fueled by the sun’s heat and the earth’s minerals, bound by a rhythm that makes the movement of clouds seem frantic. They are a civilization of waiters, watching the world through unblinking, mineral lenses, patient enough to watch the stone grow, and quiet enough to hear the planet breathe.
