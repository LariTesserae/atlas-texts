Here, the citizens are indistinguishable from the architecture. To look at a tower is to look at a thousand sleeping faces; to walk a street is to navigate the digestive tract of something immense.

First, there are the Ossified Lords, the crabs mentioned in the passing of the currents. They are not merely scavengers; they are the masons and the demolition crews of this deep metropolis. Their carapaces are not the smooth, shiny red of the shallows, but a rough, pitted white, chemically identical to the calcium towers they scuttle over. They have evolved to mimic the city itself. When they sit motionless—a posture they hold for weeks at a time, conserving energy in the warm, heavy gloom—they look like errant boulders or the weathered statues of forgotten gods. Their claws are not designed for pinching, but for shearing. One claw is a heavy, blunt crushing tool, capable of turning the dense coral skeleton into powder; the other is a serrated needle, used for picking out the polyps that infest the structural integrity of the avenues. They move with a terrifying, hydraulic slowness, their legs scraping against the silt with a sound like sandpaper on stone. They eat the dead to build the living, processing the fallen into the grit that cements the streets together.

Living within the porous walls of the skyscrapers are the Mason-Weavers, a species of colonial crustacean that resembles a cross between a lobster and a spider. They occupy the "window" hollows, the intricate spiral staircases drilled into the coral by ancient worms. They are the city’s nervous system. You do not see them, but you hear the *click-click-snap* of their claws, a constant, morse-code chatter that echoes through the bone corridors. They spin nets of extruded protein, gossamer-thin but stronger than steel, which drape across the arches connecting the spires. These nets are not for catching large prey, but for sieving the water. They catch the "snow"—the constant, slow drift of organic matter from above—and roll it into tight, nutrient-rich pellets that drop to the streets below for the Ossified Lords to consume. It is a vertical economy: the Weavers farm the current; the Lords farm the floor.

Drifting through the mid-water avenues, filling the open plazas like slow-moving clouds, are the Glass-Ghosts. These are the gelatinous entities, vast and translucent. To look at them is to look at a living prism. They have no distinct shape; they are simply a volume of water contained within a delicate, pulsing membrane. Inside them, a galaxy of bioluminescent bacteria swirls—sickly greens, bruised purples, and the pale, funeral white of phosphorescence. They are the streetlights of the benthic city, dim and wavering. They navigate by taste and touch, trailing long, sticky filaments that can span the width of a city block. They drift blindly, engulfing whatever particles—or smaller creatures—touch their membranes. They are peaceful, omnipresent grazers, their internal glow revealing the silhouettes of the towers around them, turning the dark city into a ghostly, negative-space silhouette where the water is brighter than the stone.

 darting through the spaces between the Glass-Ghosts are the Needle-Schools. These are the blind fish, silver torpedoes that move with a frantic, synchronized energy that contrasts sharply with the glacial pace of the crabs and jellyfish. They have no eyes; their faces are smooth, tapered bone designed to slip through the water without resistance. They "see" through pressure and vibration. A school of Needles functions as a single organism, a hive mind of silver scales that flows around the pillars of the city like mercury. They are the city’s immune system. When a larger predator from the open ocean—a shark or a squid—breaches the perimeter, the Needle-School coalesces into a dense, vibrating ball. They swarm the intruder, not to bite, but to disorient. The sheer chaotic pressure of their movement, combined with the sonic confusion of their frantic swimming, drives invaders away. They patrol the currents, keeping the interior avenues safe for the slow and the soft.

And then there are the Singers in the deeps, the architects of the acoustics. You feel them more than you know them. They are immense, leviathan shapes that patrol the outskirts, too large to enter the city streets. Their skin is scarred and white, lined with the ridges of a thousand battles. They are the library of the deep. Their songs—the resonant booming that vibrates in the chest cavity—are not merely calls; they are maps. They click and groan to bounce sound off the distant spires, effectively "seeing" the layout of the entire ruin in their minds. They are the historians who remember when the city was merely a reef, and they are the catalysts for its future. Their low-frequency vibrations encourage the polyps to secrete calcium carbonate, speeding the growth of the towers. They sing the buildings into being.

Finally, in the smallest crevices, the cracks where the silt settles like grey snow, live the Stitchers. These are microscopic, parasitic shrimp that glow with a fierce, electric blue. They are thejanitors of the biological world. They swarm over the bodies of the crabs, the shells of the mason-weavers, and the membranes of the glass-ghosts, cleaning away dead skin and parasites. In return, they receive protection and scraps. They are the constant, static background noise of the city—the tiny, frantic blue sparks that drift like dust motes in a sunbeam, though there is no sun here. They bind the ecosystem together, moving from the massive Lord to the drifting Ghost, stitching the community into a single, living tapestry.

There is no individual here that stands apart from the ruin. The crabs *are* the pavement; the jellyfish *are* the light; the shrimp *are* the dust. It is a singular, breathing entity, a civilization of bone and bioluminescence, suspended in the warm, crushing dark.
