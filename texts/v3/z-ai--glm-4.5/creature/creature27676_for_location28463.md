To exist here is to be an anomaly in a system of flawless perfection. The living architecture of the plateau does not require pollinators, nor does it produce fruit to be scattered; the root-floor builds itself, the towers grow according to mathematical axioms, and the water cycles through the air without ever touching the ground in violence. Therefore, the things that live here are not participants in a chaotic food web, but rather itinerant observers, solitary components in a machine that runs without them. They are the pauses in the noise, the silent punctuation marks in a sentence of biological shouting.

The most common of these rare inhabitants—the six-legged grazers sighted in the immense distances between the towers—are biological contradictions. They do not appear to have evolved from smaller, scrabbling ancestors, but rather seem to have chipped themselves off the very architecture of the place. They are calloused and calcified, their exteriors a hard, pale ceramic that matches the color of the root-plazas. They move with a frictionless, gliding motion, their six long, spindly legs acting as calipers, measuring the distance between the dripping buttresses.

They possess no mouths that open and close, no grinding jaws. The head is a smooth, tapering curve of bone-like chitin, featuring only a series of spiracles that pulsate in time with the low-frequency thrum of the world. They do not eat; they siphon. A grazer will stand motionless for hours beside one of the deep, perfectly circular canals, or press its face against the wet, slick side of a mega-flora tower, and simply inhale the mist. Their internal biology is a labyrinth of hollow tubes and filters, designed to extract the complex sugars dissolved in the constant condensation. They are living straws, drifting through the wet air, filtering the nutritious sweat of the living planet.

Because the sound here is a physical weight—a constant, chest-vibrating roar of rushing sap and creaking wood—these creatures have no external ears. To hear such a cacophony would be fatal. Instead, they "hear" through their bones. The calcified lattice of their bodies is tuned to the resonance of the plateau, acting as a dampener. They feel the music of the world as a subtle hum rather than a deafening shout, allowing them to detect the approach of others through the vibrations in the root-floor. They are silent because silence is the only way to exist within the noise; to vocalize would be to try to shout over a waterfall.

Higher up, in the warm haze above the plains, drift the winged shapes. They are vast, leathery things that resemble manta rays more than birds, their wingspans stretching thirty or forty feet. They do not flap; the air is too heavy and still for such exertion. Instead, they ride the invisible thermal currents created by the differential cooling of the vast root-plazas. Their wings are translucent, stretched over a framework of rigid, golden veins that mimic the geometric perfection of the flora below.

From above, they are almost invisible, their pale bellies blending with the diffuse daylight (2.1) filtering through the high canopy. They hang in the sky like suspended dust motes, motionless save for a slow, spiraling descent. They, too, are grazers, but they harvest the sky. Their undersides are lined with rows of sticky, bristled filaments that comb the heavy, humid air, catching the drifting spores and moisture droplets that are too light to fall as rain. When they are full—a process that takes days—they glide down to the towers, anchoring themselves with hooked claws to the geometric arches, and use a long, proboscis-like tongue to scrape the collected slime from their own bodies.

There is a profound loneliness to them. They are not social; there are no herds, no flocks, no mating calls. The "town-or-valley" scale of the landscape ensures that two individuals might go a lifetime without seeing one another. When they do meet, they ignore each other. There is no territory to defend here—the land produces enough for everyone, and no decay means no conflict over resources. They pass like ghosts, the six-legged calipers on the ground and the silent gliders in the sky, indifferent and alone.

Most unsettling of all, perhaps, is the embryonic quality of their existence. Just as the landscape looks raw and newly created, so too does the fauna look unfinished. The grazers lack the scars of a difficult life; their ceramic shells are smooth, unblemished by injury or parasites. There are no young. No nests of chicks, no litters of young grazing near their parents. The observer sees only fully formed adults, perfect and static. It is as if they are born from the condensation itself, condensing out of the saturated air fully grown, living their quiet, siphoning lives, and then simply ceasing to function, their bodies hardening into yet another geometric statue on the plaza, returning to the "total" biology of the world without the mess of death.

They are the silent citizens of a loud world, the living statues in a garden that builds itself, breathing the wet air and waiting for a change that the eternal equilibrium promises will never come.
