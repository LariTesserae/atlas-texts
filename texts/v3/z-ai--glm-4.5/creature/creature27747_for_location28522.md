They are the Dry-Bloods, the Hollowers, a people carved from the same grey-purple stone as the bowl they inhabit. To look at them is to see the geode made flesh: they are a dense, knotted people, short of stature but impossibly broad across the shoulder, their anatomy compressed by the high altitude and the tight, vertical architecture of their slum.

Their skin is the first adaptation. It is not the soft, permeable dermis of lowlanders, but a thick, leathery hide that ranges in color from the bruised purple of the shadows to the dusty silver of the sun-beam. It is matte and textured, resembling the surface of the succulents they harvest, and it exudes a thin, oily film that seals in their precious moisture. They smell of crushed sage and stale musk, a scent that permeates their hair—which is worn long, thick, and often matted with red clay or bound with the fibers of air-plants to keep it from tangling in the thorns.

Their faces are dominated by the eyes. In the perpetual gloom of the hollow, their pupils have swollen to consume the iris, black and reflective as pools of oil, adapted to catch the faint blue-green shimmer of the bioluminescent plants. To shield these sensitive organs during the midday shaft of sunlight, they possess heavy, translucent nictitating membranes that slide across their eyes, giving them a glazed, unseeing stare when they look up toward the aperture. Their noses are small, slit-like, and lined with fine, filtering hairs to keep the pervasive rock dust from their lungs, but their mouths are wide and generous, equipped with strong, flat teeth for grinding fibrous plant matter and sharp incisors for shearing through the tough rinds of water-pods.

Movement is a language here. Because the air is so thick with the drone of a million insect wings, spoken shouts are often lost. The Hollowers have developed a dialect of elaborate hand signs and sharp, clicking whistles that cut through the ambient hum. They move with a jerky, reptilian caution, mimicking the lizards they share the space with. Their hands are their primary tools—calloused, scarred, and stained green with chlorophyll. The fingertips are rough, almost like sandpaper, allowing them to grip the smooth, curved stone of the geode walls without slipping.

Their attire is a paradox of necessity. Despite the stifling tropical heat, they cover themselves extensively. This is not for modesty, but for protection against the thorn-vines and the biting swarms. They wear tunics and trousers woven from the woody stems of dried succulents, stiff and armor-like, often reinforced with the chitinous plates of the giant beetles they harvest. A person of high status might wear a cloak made entirely of the iridescent, dusty wings of the moths, a shimmering, fragile garment that rustles like dry parchment.

Around their necks and belts hang the tools of their survival: hooked picks for prying open lithops; hollow stone needles for extracting moisture; and woven nets for catching the fat, protein-rich beetles. Most valuable of all are the *canteens*—not vessels for carrying liquid, for that would be wasteful, but sealed bladders made from the stomachs of the roosting birds, containing fresh, unspoiled pulp from the deepest, wettest cacti.

They are a people defined by the logic of the "wet." To them, the world outside is a crypt of dry death, and their humid, thorny prison is a living womb. They view the vegetation not as scenery, but as a larder of locked vaults. To be chosen as a "Harvester" is the highest honor, for these are the brave souls who climb the highest, most precarious vines to breach the water-heavy trunks of the ancient canopy plants.

The children are born with a softer skin, which hardens within their first year. Until then, they are kept swaddled in layers of soft moss and kept close to the warm stones of the floor. They learn the laws of conservation before they learn to walk: a drop of fluid spilled is a sin; a piece of fruit uneaten is a tragedy. They are taught to listen to the vibration of the geode—the "tooth-hum"—to predict storms or shifts in the insect swarms.

At night, when the blue glow of the plants is at its peak, the settlement comes alive in a different way. The noise of the insects dampens slightly, and the Hollowers sit on their woven rope bridges and stone porches. They scrape the hides of the giant birds and polish their beetle-wing armor, communicating through touch and gesture. They are a tight-knit, claustrophobic society, untrusting of open spaces, finding comfort only in the press of bodies and the solid, reassuring weight of the rock that surrounds them on all sides. They are the keepers of the warm soup, the guardians of the damp, living in a frantic, beautiful engine of survival sealed away from the world.
