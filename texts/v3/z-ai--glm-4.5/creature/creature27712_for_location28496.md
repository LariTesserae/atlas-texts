They are a people carved from the same silence as the mountains, short-limbed and barrel-chested, evolved—or perhaps worn down—by the crushing verticality and the thin, bruised air. To see them is to see a creature of dense matter; they possess a heavy, grounded inertia, their centers of gravity low to counter the eternal pull of the abyss below. Their skin is the colour of wet flint, thick and leathery, insulating a furnace of a metabolism that runs perpetually at the edge of fever to combat the absolute zero of the external world.

Their faces are largely obscured, hidden behind intricate masks of felt and leather that leave only dark, slitted eyes exposed. These eyes are adapted to the monochrome gloom—vast, pupils dilated to drink in the meagre light, reflecting the pale sun like pools of obsidian. What little skin is visible around the eyes or the hands is crisscrossed with the fine, map-like lines of frost-nip, the permanent topography of their survival.

Movement here is a study in conservation of energy. They do not walk so much as they flow, a slow, fluid deliberation that mimics the nearby glaciers. To rush is to waste heat; to stumble is to die. They traverse the slick, dripping stone with bare feet calloused into hooves, or with crampons of meteoric iron that bite into the granite. Every motion is economical, a precise placement of hand and foot on the labyrinthine scaffolding of the city.

They are swathed in layers of architecture as much as clothing. Their garments are heavy, sodden capes of felted yak wool, treated with oils to repel the perpetual damp. Around their necks hang complex seals of amber and dried bone, the keys to the heated warrens below. The scent of them hits a visitor before the sight does—a pungent, earthy miasma of unwashed beast musk, woodsmoke, and the sweet, cloying smell of the rendered fat they rub into their skins to seal in moisture.

Communication is stripped of the unnecessary. The air is too thin and the damp too absorbing for idle chatter. They speak in a dialect of glottal stops and low hums that vibrates in the chest, a language designed to travel only inches between interlocutors. More often, they converse through a complex sign language of finger-positions and shoulder shifts, silent and precise, allowing them to signal danger or affection over the roar of the wind without unmasking their faces.

Their relationship with the six-legged beasts is symbiotic to the point of identity. They do not merely herd the shaggy, moss-eating behemoths; they live amongst them. The lower levels of the towers are a shared, stinking warmth—the beasts breathe out great clouds of steam that are vented into the living quarters above, and the humans groom the tangled fur, picking out ice-mites and weaving the shed hair into new ropes and insulation. To be exiled from the heat of the beasts is a death sentence, a concept used to discipline the rare criminal.

At night, or what passes for it in the eternal twilight, they retreat into the dry-stone honeycombs. Here, huddled together in piles of fur and bodies, they generate the collective heat that allows them to sleep. They are a tactile people, unbothered by proximity, finding comfort in the press of another warm pulse. Dreams are communal, shared in the close dark, fed by the rhythmic dripping of the melting frost that is the city’s only heartbeat.

They are not inventors. They have no drive to conquer the stars that hang so terrifyingly close above their heads. Their technology is the art of maintenance—the chiseling of a new gutter to direct an ice-melt stream, the replacement of a rotted beam, the careful knapping of a meteoric blade. They are the custodians of the now, clinging to the granite spine of the world, watching the slow creep of the ice and the drift of the clouds, content simply to endure until the sun breaks through, however briefly, to glint on the frost.
