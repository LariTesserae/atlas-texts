Life here does not swim; it navigates by collision.

In the absence of light, the vast, warm bath is populated by entities that exist primarily as textures against the skin. The water is thick enough to support them like a living gelatin, reducing the need for constant motion, and as a result, the inhabitants have evolved toward a radical stillness. They are architectural in their own right, extensions of the stone geode that houses them.

Suspended in the open liquid are the **Drifters**.

These are the dog-sized isopods mentioned, the roving custodians of the jar. They look like segmented, flattened lozenges of pale white wax, carved from the same material as the specters that haunt old nightmares. Their exoskeletons are not hard shells, but thick, flexible plates of chitin that move over one another with the sound of dry leaves rubbing together.

Because they are blind, their faces are featureless smooth curves, save for a series of small, spiraling holes that exhale and inhale the thick medium. They navigate using long, delicate antennae that can extend up to ten feet in any direction, sweeping the water in a slow, rhythmic figure-eight. A single sweep of an antenna might take twenty minutes. They move by paddling with five pairs of pleopods along their undersides, legs that move with such agonizing slowness that the movement is almost imperceptible to the naked eye. If you were to watch one for an hour, you might only realize it has shifted slightly by the change in the position of its shadow against the gloom. They are scavengers, drifting until their antennae brush against the soft rot of a fallen carcass or the mineral-rich secretion of the wall-grazers, at which point they slowly unhinge their mandibles and begin a meal that lasts for days.

Among these drifters are the **Cable-Snakes**.

Long, eel-like entities that hang suspended in the gloom like abandoned rigging. They have no distinct tails or heads; they are simply muscular tubes of translucency, often thirty feet in length, filled with a fluid slightly denser than the bath. They possess a row of paddle-like limbs running the length of their bodies, useless for propulsion, but perfect for anchoring. They drift until they collide with a wall or another creature, at which point they wrap themselves around it, forming a knot. They are parasitic, feeding on the slow metabolic secretions of the wall-grazers and the Drifters, holding on with a gentle, constricting pressure that takes weeks to drain the life from their host. They do not kill quickly; they simply become part of the host, a heavy limb that was not there before.

They are the "listening posts" of the ecosystem. While they lack ears, their entire bodies are tuned to the vibration of the stone. When the megalithic blocks shift, grinding against one another in the cooling night of the upper atmosphere, the Grand Suspended Ones shudder in response. It is a sympathetic resonance, a slow-motion vibration that ripples through their gelatinous forms. Smaller creatures—the isopods and the eels—congregate on the hides of these giants, picking at the parasites that cling to the folds of their skin, creating a mobile ecosystem that drifts aimlessly through the dark.

Everything here behaves with the hesitation of the deep. There is no fleeing, no fighting, only the endless, tactile negotiation of space. To exist here is to surrender to the viscosity, to allow the warm, heavy silence to dictate the pace of your breath and the beat of your heart.
