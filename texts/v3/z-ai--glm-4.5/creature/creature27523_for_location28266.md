To live here is to be an extension of the gear, a biological cog slipping into the machinery of the iron sphere. The inhabitants are not distinct from the Heart-Measure; they are its immune system, its gardeners, and its worshippers, carved into shape by the relentless vibration and the vertical tyranny of the place.

First are **The Tuned**.

They are human, or they were once, but they have been remade by the altitude and the roar. They possess no external ears; the cartilage has long since calcified and merged with the skull, leaving only small, spiraling holes protected by flaps of thick, scarred skin. They do not hear the grinding of the metal—they feel it. Their bones are dense, unusually heavy for their slender frames, vibrating in sympathy with the Great Shafts. This bone conduction allows them to "listen" to the health of the engine by pressing their hands or foreheads against the warm copper piping. They can sense a blockage three decks down by the change in frequency humming through their molars.

They are spindly creatures, with elongated arms that end in prehensile, calloused fingers capable of navigating the slick rivets and the gripping vines. Their skin is the color of oxidized bronze—greenish-brown and textured like sharkskin to shed the constant dampness. They wear minimal clothing: harnesses of cured leather and woven black-leaf vines, not for modesty, but to provide anchor points for the carabiners they clip onto the safety lines that crisscross the void. They do not speak; vocal cords are useless here. Instead, they communicate through a complex, rhythmic percussion of clicking fingernails on metal pipes and a sign language performed against the backdrop of the bioluminescent moss, flashing their palms to signal danger or harvest.

Then there are the **Rust-Grazers**, the lemur-like creatures mentioned in passing, but stranger upon close inspection. They are the clean-up crew of the vertical city. Their metallic pelts are not fur at all, but thousands of interlocking, copper-colored scales that protect them from the abrasive grit that fills the air. They have evolved to eat the oxidation that plagues the machine. Their teeth are iron files, constantly growing and self-sharpening, designed to scrape the destructive rust from the struts and beams.

In a bizarre symbiosis, the Tuned do not exterminate them; they herd them. A Grazer perched on a dangerous, weakened joint is a sign of salvation, not infestation. The creatures scrape the metal clean, strengthening the structure, and in return, the Tuned provide them with the sweet, oily nectar that drips from the heat-exchange vents. The Grazers are fearless of the drop, able to leap across the ten-story gap of the central cylinder, using the updrafts generated by the furnaces to glide from Shaft to Shaft. Their eyes are massive, black domes that reflect the violet sky from the observation ports, adapted to see in the gloom where the high-glare lamps cannot reach.

Living within the interstices of the **Great Shafts** themselves are the **Clamp-Ferns**. These are the massive vines described earlier, but they are more than plants; they are semi-sentient tension-keepers. The vines sense the microscopic expansion and contraction of the metal as it heats and cools. When a gear heats up and expands, threatening to seize, the Clamp-Fern contracts its hold, applying hydraulic pressure from its sap-filled turgor to keep the alignment true. The Tuned harvest this sap, a thick, amber fluid that smells of ozone and sugar, which serves as their primary source of hydration and calories. They "milk" the ferns by tapping into the thick vines with hollow, bone needles, draining only enough to sustain themselves without killing the plant that holds the ceiling up.

In the hollow darkness of the floor mesh and the upper reaches of the ceiling mist dwell the **Sky-Manta**. These are the birds of the place, but they have forsaken flight for buoyancy. They have evolved vast, membranous wings filled with hydrogen gas, a byproduct of the bacteria in their gut that digests the metallic spores floating in the air. They float lazily in the humid layer above the machinery, never flapping, merely steering with slight shifts of weight. They nest in the red warning lights, drawn to the warmth. Their young are born with heavy shells to keep them anchored in the nest until their gas bladders develop. They feed on the swarms of "heat-moths"—insects with wings of transparent mica that hover over the vents, drawn to the infrared radiation.

Finally, there is the **Overseer**, or what passes for one. It is not a person, but a colony organism: a massive, slime-mold-like entity that lives inside the main control console, a glass-walled box suspended on wires. It pulses with a faint, rhythmic blue light. It feeds on the electrical currents running through the dashboard. The Tuned check on it religiously, feeding it sugar and adjusting the dials that regulate its environment. In return, the Overseer processes the data from the thousands of pressure gauges and thermal sensors, communicating corrections to the Tuned by changing the color of its bioluminescence. It is the brain of the operation, a biological computer grown in a petri dish centuries ago to manage the load that human minds could not handle.

All of them—the bone-vibrating Tuned, the iron-filing Grazers, the tension-holding Ferns, the floating Manta, and the gelatinous Overseer—are part of the single, deafening organism. They do not dominate the machine; they facilitate it. They are the grease in the gears, the oxygen in the bellows, living in the roaring belly of the world, keeping the turning from stopping.
