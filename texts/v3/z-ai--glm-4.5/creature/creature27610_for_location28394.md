To look for them directly is to fail. They are not entities that exist in the foreground; they are the negative space around the machinery, the shadows that have too much weight. In the gaps between the cathedrals of brick, where the sulfur-yellow light filters through the steam in sickly shafts, you see them best: the Tenders.

They are not merely workers; they are a biological caste forged by the sink itself. They are short, dense creatures with a physiology that seems to reject the very concept of bone, replaced instead by cartilage and thick, fluid-filled muscle that absorbs the violent shuddering of the floor. Their skin is the color of wet slate, thick and hairless, possessing a texture like cured leather that has been left in the rain. It secretes a viscous, oily sheen—not sweat, but a protective barrier that beads the mist off them like water off a duck’s back, though it glistens black with the inevitable soot.

Their faces are devoid of expression, sculpted by the heat into a permanent, slack-jawed grimace of endurance. The nose is almost nonexistent, reduced to two narrow slits to keep the scalding air from scorching the lungs, while the eyes are covered by a translucent, milky nictitating membrane. They do not blink. They cannot afford to. To blink is to miss the gauge’s trembling needle, to miss the fracture in the pipe. They see primarily by the infrared radiance of the furnaces and the electric blue arcs of the tesla coils, their vision tuned to the spectrum of heat and energy rather than the dull, gloomy visible light.

Physically, they are integrated into their environment. Their hands are the most striking aspect—massive, club-like things, ending not in fingernails but in callus pads as hard as iron. They are scarred, burned, and stained a permanent charcoal gray. They do not wear gloves; the gloves are grown into their skin. They handle the blistering iron pipes with the casual indifference of a man handling a mug of tea, their nerve endings cauterized by generations of接触 with the superheated metal.

They move with a terrifying fluidity. In the Steam-Sink, there is no wasted motion. A Tender does not walk to a valve; they glide, their posture perpetually crouched, center of gravity low to navigate the jagged basalt and the slick, grime-coated catwalks. They seem to flow around the sharp corners of the machinery, adhering to the iron like insects. They communicate not with sound—the roar of the pistons drowns out speech—but with a complex, rhythmic thumping of their heavy boots against the metal grating and sharp, pneumatic hisses expelled from their modified throats.

And then there is the stasis, the curse of the Sink. The Tenders do not age as we understand it. They do not grow old and retire; they grow *dense*. As the cycles loop without end, the Tenders simply become more like the rock and iron they tend. Their skin thickens, their movements slow into an imperceptible glacial drift, until one day, a Tender simply stops moving in a strategic alcove, their legs fusing with the grating, their back forming a perfect resting place for a heat-shield. They become part of the architecture.

The "young" ones—though age is a meaningless term here—are sleeker, faster, darting between the gears to clear the industrial grit. The "old" ones are stationary monoliths, mossy with lichen and rust, indistinguishable from the support pillars except for the slow, heaving rise and fall of their shoulders as they continue to vent steam, acting as biological pressure release valves for the complex.

They are genderless, sexless drones of function. They do not eat in the traditional sense; they consume the nutrient-rich sludge that collects in the settling tanks, a grey, oily paste that tastes of sulfur and metal. They drink the superheated condensation that drips from the pipes, their internal systems a closed loop of chemical efficiency that mirrors the machine they serve.

At night, or what passes for night in the sulfur gloom, they huddle in the warmest crevices near the furnace core, sleeping in piles like wet, grey stones. They do not dream. If they did, their dreams would be indistinguishable from their waking reality: the endless turn of the gear, the eternal hiss of the valve, the rhythmic *thrum* of the earth. They are the caretakers of a moment that will never end, trapped in the perfect, circular hell of function, preserving the scar on the world with a devotion that borders on the religious, though they have no gods to worship—only the Machine, and the heat that sustains them.
