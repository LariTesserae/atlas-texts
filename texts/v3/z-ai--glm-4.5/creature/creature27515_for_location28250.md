The inhabitants of the Wet-Loom are not separate from the machinery; they are the mechanism by which the room digests itself. In a space where the boundary between the grown and the built is so porous, the fauna has evolved—or perhaps was designed—to occupy the liminal space between gardener and pest, between architect and debris.

To see them, you must first stop looking for animals and start looking for anomalies in the rhythm of the room.

**The Calcified Skimmers**

At the micro-level, living in the milky, lime-heavy puddles that form in the recesses of the floor grates, are the Skimmers. They are barely visible to the naked eye—pale, translucent crustaceans no larger than a fingernail. They are born of the chemical runoff, their shells composed of precipitated minerals scraped from the concrete.

They move in frantic, V-shaped ripples across the surface of the standing water, filtering out the heavy metallic sediments before the liquid can fully evaporate. Their role is one of constant filtration; they are the kidneys of the puddles. As they consume the slurry, their bodies harden and greyen, eventually becoming too heavy to move. They sink to the bottom, becoming sediment themselves—tiny bricks in the foundation of the new world. Their life is a continuous cycle of drinking the stone and becoming it.

**The Graft-Spiders**

Higher up, inhabiting the vertical jungle where the ferns meet the girders, live the Graft-Spiders. These are the true weavers of the Wet-Loom. They are not biological spiders in the traditional sense, but rather chimera: their abdomens are small, warm bio-reactors pulsing with a green bioluminescence, while their legs are jagged, rust-red needles of stainless steel.

They do not spin silk; they spin sutures. They travel along the massive, cable-thick vines, and where a plant has begun to detach from the rusted iron beam—a casualty of the shifting pistons—the Graft-Spider intervenes. It pierces the stem of the fern and the flank of the metal beam with its legs, injecting a bonding agent that smells faintly of ozone and chlorophyll. This agent fuses the cellulose to the iron, knitting the plant permanently into the architecture. They are relentless surgeons, ensuring that the "erosion" of the room’s movement does not tear the vegetation away. In this way, they do not build webs; they build the anchor points of the ecosystem.

**The Lithic-Grubs**

The most unsettling of the Wet-Loom’s inhabitants are the Lithic-Grubs. These are the agents of "inverted erosion." They look like immense, segmented worms, the length of a human arm and the girth of a tree trunk, covered in a rough, grey hide that mimics the texture of the fresh concrete.

They are the eaters of the stone. They crawl slowly across the "low erosion" landscape, rasping mouths grinding against the jagged, angular rocks. They consume the raw, poured greycrete and slag-glass, digesting the sharp edges and excreting a fine, nutrient-rich loam from their rear. This loam is the dark soil in which the massive ferns take root. The Grubs are terrifyingly silent; their grinding is muffled by the damp air. They act as living tumbled stones, rounding the sharp corners of the universe, softening the architectural hostility into something that can support life. To step on one is a mistake made only once—their hides burn with the chemical heat of their digestion (2.2/3), a thermal warning to the unwary.

**The Chrome-Crab Custodians**

And then there are the entities that seem the most aware: the Chrome-Crab Custodians. These are the "maintenance drones" mentioned in passing, but up close, they reveal a disturbing autonomy. They are the shepherds of this chaos.

They are built from the same scrap as the room—shiny, polished carapaces of scavenged brass and chrome, articulated with hydraulic hisses that are too quiet to be natural. They scuttle over the mossy vents and the deep-green velvet of the walls on six multi-jointed legs. They do not eat; they plug themselves into the leaking pipes via ports in their underbellies, drinking the pressurized water and oil to power their internal mechanics.

Their purpose is curation. They move through the aggressive vegetation with shears made of diamond-tipped glass. They do not prune for the health of the plant, but for the safety of the machine. They snip a vine here to unclog a gear; they trim a fern there to prevent it from blocking a sensor. They pick up the calcified bodies of the Skimmers and deposit them into cracks in the wall, acting as mortar. They are the immune system of the Wet-Loom, white-blood cells in metallic form, endlessly fighting the "infection" of wild growth to maintain the delicate homeostasis of the factory.

**The Silent Observers**

Finally, high in the rafters where the amber glass filters the light into a medicinal gold, there are the birds—or what appear to be birds. They have the silhouettes of swifts or swallows, but their feathers are overlapping plates of thin, flexible solar cells. They rarely fly. Instead, they perch on the high-voltage conduits, absorbing electricity directly through their metallic claws.

They possess no beaks, only smooth, tapered faces dominated by huge, lens-like eyes that reflect the room in a distorted, fisheye view. They make no sound; they have no vocal cords. They simply watch. They track the movement of the pistons, the growth of the vines, and the path of the Lithic-Grubs with an intelligence that feels cold and calculated. They are the auditors of the Wet-Loom, ensuring that the construction proceeds according to a plan that no human operator could read.

Together, these beings form a closed loop of existence. The machine feeds the plant, the plant feeds the insect, the insect becomes the mortar, the crab maintains the machine, and the bird watches it all happen in a silence so thick it feels like the air itself is holding its breath, waiting for the room to finally decide what it is going to be.
