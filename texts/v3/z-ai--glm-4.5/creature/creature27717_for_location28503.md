They are the children of the shard, a people who look as if they were chiseled from the same grey, brittle rock as their mountains. There is a uniformity to their silhouette that speaks to the absolute authority of their environment: they are narrow, angular, and compressed. In a place of zero grace, the body has abandoned the curve. They are creatures of the vertical axis, with arms that hang slightly longer than the human norm, their hands spade-shaped and calloused into hard, horn-like pads capable of gripping raw stone without the need for gloves.

Their skin is the color of the mist—a translucent, silvery-grey that seems to hold the light of the phosphorescent ceiling. It is not a healthy hue; it is the pallor of the deep-rooted thing that has never known the sun. Across this pale canvas, their history is written in scar tissue. Because the world is sharp, the people are perpetually sliced. Thin, white lines map their limbs, relics of encounters with the razor-sharp terrain. There is no shame in these marks; rather, they are the texture of survival. To be unscarred here is to be newborn or dead.

They are wrapped in layers of protection. Since the ground is treacherous and the air perpetually damp, they wear clothing constructed from the only soft things available: cured hides of the blind, lizards, and vast quantities of woven lichen-fiber. Their leggings and tunics are heavily padded, quilting layers of dry moss and fur between rough hide to create a buffer against the jagged world. They look padded, awkward, almost larval in their bulk, yet when they move, they move with a startling, spider-like precision. They do not walk so much as they navigate; every step is a calculated placement of weight on a stone that might shatter or slice.

Their faces are dominated by the eyes. Large, dark, and devoid of the white sclera common to surface dwellers, their eyes are pools of obsidian designed to drink in the flat, shadowless luminescence of the dome. They do not blink often. To watch them is to watch a creature in a state of constant, frozen assessment. They are always watching the ceiling, watching the cliffs, reading the micro-fractures in the stone. The mouth is small, withdrawn, hidden behind a breathing mask of woven reeds and bone that filters the iron-heavy dust, though when they speak or eat, the mask is lowered to reveal teeth stained dark by the mineral-rich water and ground lichen.

Society here is not a democracy; it is a mechanism. The description of the city as a "hive" is biologically accurate. These people do not seem to possess the concept of privacy. They live pressed against one another, their bodies sharing warmth in the chill, their movements synchronized by the rhythm of the *thud-thud-thud*.

They divide themselves by the labor of stabilization.

There are the *Binders*, who spend their lives lashing the wooden scaffolding together. They have grotesquely enlarged forefingers and thumbs, twisted by years of pulling wet hide and vines taut. They move along the swaying log bridges with the confidence of tightrope walkers, their bodies swaying in counterpoint to the wind. They smell of tar and animal glue.

There are the *Breakers*, who shape the stone. They are the broadest of the people, muscled shoulders sloping down to narrow, agile hips. They carry tools of flint and heavy basalt. They do not build; they prevent. They chip away at the sharp overhangs, catching the falling shards in baskets woven from creepers. Their lungs are rattling with stone dust, evidenced by the low, grinding wheeze that marks their breathing.

Then there are the *Listeners*, the smallest and most nervous of the populace. They do not carry tools. They carry hollowed-out horns pressed to the rock. They listen to the settling of the earth. When a Listener raises a hand, the entire hive freezes. The construction stops, the bridges go still, and thousands of dark eyes turn upward, waiting for the crack that signifies a shift. If the shift comes, they scream—a single, high-pitched note that signals evacuation to the lower levels. If it does not, the hand lowers, and the *thud-thud-thud* resumes instantly.

Their diet reflects the scarcity of the place. They are fungivores and insect-eaters. They farm pale, spongy mushrooms in the damp, dark interiors of their mud-brick huts, harvesting them with surgical care. They keep small, eyeless lizards in clay pots, fattening them on scraps of moss. The meat is stringy and sour, but it is eaten with reverence. There is no waste here; even the bones of the lizards are ground into paste and used to caulk the gaps in the stone walls.

Emotionally, they seem stunted, compressed by the crushing weight of the cavern’s roof. They do not laugh. The sound of a shout is as rare as a bird, reserved only for the warnings of the Listeners. Their joy, if it exists, is a quiet thing—a hand placed on a shoulder, the sharing of a warm cup of mineral water, the hum of a lullaby that mimics the drone of the ceiling light. They are a people holding their breath, waiting for the world to finish breaking.

At night, if such a concept exists in this place of eternal flat light, they retreat into the honeycomb of their huts. They sleep in piles, tangled together for warmth, their breathing syncing up until the entire city sounds like a single, slumbering beast. They dream of smoothness. They dream of a world without edges, where the ground is soft and does not cut the feet. But they wake to the iron taste and the sharp stone, and they pick up their flint axes, and they begin again the work of holding the ceiling up.
