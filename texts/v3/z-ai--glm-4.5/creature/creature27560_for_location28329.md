In the absence of the beast, the plant has become the monster.

Here, where the weight of the ocean acts as a shroud, the "inhabitants" are not the darting, nervous creatures of the sunlit world. There are no eyes to track movement, no muscles to propel a hunt. The silence rules out the social chatter of pods or the solitary click of echolocation. Instead, what lives here is a solitary, sessile sentience—entities that have taken the concept of "root" and elevated it to a terrible, architectural intelligence.

They are the **Basalt-Weavers**.

To the casual glance—and glances are all the darkness allows—a Weaver is indistinguishable from the ruin itself. They exploit the visual trickery of the cyclopean blocks. A Weaver begins its life as a spore drifting in the thick, frigid currents, seeking the microscopic fissures in the tight masonry joints. Once it finds purchase in the silence between two city-block-sized stones, it does not grow outward; it grows *in*.

The creature is a composite organism, a symbiosis of a silicon-based fungus and a calcareous algae, acting as a single, sprawling entity. It does not build a shell; it *becomes* the stone. Over millennia, it secretes a slow-dissolving acid that melts the artificial masonry, drinking the minerals and elemental energy stored within the ancient bricks, and then excretes a harder, darker, glassier composite. This process is so glacial that the stone blocks do not crumble; they merely shift in texture, losing their man-made smoothness and taking on the organic, roped texture of muscle tissue frozen in panic.

A mature Basalt-Weaver looks like a rupture in the floor of the world. It is a hemispherical dome, often spanning fifty feet in height, rising from the artificial plain. Its surface is not skin, but a dense, interlocking mosaic of black plates—the "dissolved and reformed" ruins—interspersed with patches of the deep, velvet algae that bloom in the fissures. Between these armor plates, one can see a pulse, but it is not a beat of blood. It is a hydraulic shift. The creature pumps the super-cooled brine through internal canals, equalizing the immense pressure that would crush a normal shell.

Because there is no light to guide it, the Weaver has no need for a face. It has no eyes, no mouth, no distinct sensory organs. It is, in essence, a giant, exposed nerve cluster living inside a stone house. It "sees" through vibration. The immense weight of the water, the distant groan of the tectonic plates, the microscopic scrape of silt grains sliding down its flank—all of these are translated into a topographical map of its surroundings. It knows the world not by how it looks, but by how it *shivers*.

The Weaver is an ambush predator of the slowest possible order. It does not chase. It waits.

Its primary interaction with the "lush" world around it is the bladderwrack forest. The giant, rope-like kelp that rises from the joints are the Weaver’s harvest. The Weaver extends specialized, tendril-like root systems—thick, black cables that snake along the seabed for hundreds of yards. These cables are lined with microscopic barbs and enzymatic nodes. They wrap around the bases of the kelp "trees," not to strangle them, but to tap into their vascular systems.

In the eternal night, the kelp struggles to generate energy from the phantom light (0.2). It grows, but slowly. The Weaver, patient beyond measure, acts as a parasite and a caretaker. It siphons a percentage of the chemical sugars the kelp produces, keeping the kelp just healthy enough to continue growing, just weak enough to remain tethered. It is a farming practiced on a timeline of centuries. The Weaver does not eat the kelp; it milks it.

However, the Weaver’s true diet is the decay of the deep.

When the towering strands of bladderwrack finally reach their limit—when the weight of their own growth or the cessation of some deep current causes them to detach and drift down into the gloom—the Weaver reacts. This is the only time it moves with anything resembling speed. The moment a heavy kelp strand settles onto its armored carapace, the stone plates shift. A cavity opens in the dome, a maw filled not with teeth, but with grinding, mineralized surfaces and strong, corrosive fluids.

The Weaver pulls the dying vegetation in. It digests the carbon, the silica, and the trace metals, recycling the matter of the dead jungle to fuel its own expansion. The "bones" of the kelp—the indigestible fibers—are compacted and added to the Weaver’s external shell, making it grow larger, making it cover more of the ancient floor.

Perhaps the most unsettling aspect of the Basalt-Weaver is its reproduction. Because there are no mates to find, no dances to perform, the Weaver reproduces by "screeching" into the stone. When it reaches a certain critical mass, dictated by the availability of food and the stability of the ruins beneath it, the Weaver sends a deep, infrasonic vibration down through its roots and into the paved floor.

This vibration travels through the interconnected masonry for miles. It is a sound below the threshold of hearing, a physical shudder in the stone itself. When it reaches another Weaver—a distant, solitary dome resting on the horizon—the vibration triggers a response. The distant Weaver releases a cloud of genetic spores into the water. The current, driven by the deep ocean's slow turnover, carries these spores across the gloom.

They drift for years, perhaps decades, suspended in the dark, waiting to land on the shoulder of the first Weaver, or to find a new, empty patch of masonry to begin the cycle anew.

The Weavers are the monks of this submerged temple. They do not think in the rapid-fire associations of animal brains. Their thoughts are chemical tides, rising and falling over weeks. They "remember" the pressure changes of the last ice age. They "feel" the friction of the silt that has buried the ruins for eons.

They are the living embodiment of the world’s dynamic state: 0.6. They are nearly still, nearly stone, nearly dead. But inside their shells of reconstituted masonry, there is a cold, slow awareness that is dreaming its way through the dark. They are the lords of the silence, the eaters of the kelp, and the inheritors of a floor built by gods.
