They are the damp things, the pale-skinned architects of delay.

To live here is to live in a state of constant, unblinking vigilance. The inhabitants are humanoid, but stripped of any softness that implies leisure. They are gaunt, their physiology elongated and rope-muscled, evolved—or perhaps eroded—to match the verticality and the claustrophobia of the hollow. Their skin is not the flush pink of surface dwellers, but a translucent, bluish-white, the color of a fish found in a cave. It looks perpetually wet, sheened with the same oily patina that coats the fern-stalk towers, a natural adaptation to the supersaturated air that prevents them from pruning into softness in the humidity.

They do not speak. The silence of the place is not just an atmospheric feature; it is a survival strategy, and the inhabitants have evolved to respect it. Their vocal cords have atrophied, or perhaps they simply choose never to use them. Communication is a tactile affair—a complex language of pressure and gesture conducted through the soles of their feet on the swaying bridges and the interlocking of their fingers when they meet. They understand the vibration of the breathing walls; to shout would be to invite the vegetation to pinpoint your location.

They are, for all intents and purposes, naked, save for minimal loincloths woven from the desiccated silk of some dead cavern moth. Clothing would rot here in hours, weighed down by the dripping water. Instead, they wear their environment: their hair is long, slicked back with oils, often braided with small, bioluminescent mosses to provide a personal, low-level glow. This makes them look like wandering ghosts when they move across the platforms, trailing wisps of light that merge with the twilight.

Their existence is defined by the Adze.

Every adult carries one—a stone blade lashed to a bone handle, worn smooth by a grip of decades. They are a society of gardeners fighting a forest that wants to crush them. They do not build for the future; they build for the present moment. There is no art in their architecture, only the frantic arithmetic of survival. The towers are not homes; they are life-rafts. Because the "Total" plant mass is alive and intelligent in its blind, voracious way, the inhabitants are constantly trimming. They step out onto the platforms not to greet the day, but to sever the questing root-tips that seek to pry open the floorboards.

Their relationship with the isopods is a cold détente. The pale, plate-sized crustaceans are the sanitation system, devouring the off-cut vegetation and the glowing sap that bleeds from the hacked roots. The inhabitants walk carefully among them. The isopods are docile scavengers, content with the decay, but the inhabitants know that if they were to fall—if the slick bridges were to give way—they would not be killed by the fall, but rather by the sudden, frantic consumption of a thousand clicking legs. Because of this, they move with a fluid, boneless grace, never planting a foot unless they are sure of the purchase. They are light on their feet, possessing a density that seems less than that of the water-thick air.

Their eyes are vast, black pools that swallow the faint blue light of the moss. They see primarily in motion and contrast, tuned to the shifting shadows of the "deep water" dark. When they look at you, they do not look with recognition; they look with the assessment of a creature deciding if you are part of the structure or part of the infestation.

They sleep in shifts, curled together in the hollow centers of the fern-stalk towers for warmth, huddled around small pockets of heat generated by composting moss. They dream, perhaps, of a place where the floor is still, where the walls do not breathe, and where the silence is empty rather than full of crawling things. But when they wake, they pick up their adzes. They step out into the cold, chlorophyll-scented twilight, and they begin to hack away the green, knowing that they are merely a temporary scaffolding set amidst a biology that refuses to stop moving. They are the pause in the growth, the silence in the noise, living on the borrowed time of a dying fern.
