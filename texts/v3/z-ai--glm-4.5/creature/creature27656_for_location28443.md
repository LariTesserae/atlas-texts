At first, you do not see them. To look for a distinct shape against the horizon is a mistake born of other worlds. Here, the observer must look for the *wrongness* in the texture—a patch of velvet moss that ripples contrary to the slow drift of the humid air; a shadow that detaches itself from the base of a buttress; a smudge of pale gray moving across the polished grain of the floor.

They are small, diminutive flecks against the scale of the Dendritic Spires, possessed of a physiology that has surrendered to the "Wet." They are upright, vaguely bipedal, but their posture is perpetually crouched, as if in deference to the weight of the silence pressing down from the canopy. Their skin is not the opaque barrier of a mammal, but a translucent, pallid membrane, slick with a constant sheen of condensation. It looks almost fungal in texture—soft, cool, and prone to bruising purple at the slightest touch. Through this skin, one can sometimes see the slow, rhythmic pulse of a darkened circulatory system, moving sluggishly like the sap in the walls around them.

There are no hard angles to them. The "Established Erosion" has carved them just as it has carved the corners of the buildings. Their shoulders slope smoothly into necks; their limbs taper into rounded, spatulate fingers and toes that lack distinct nails. They have no hair, only a fine, downy filigree of moss-like cilia that catches the drifting spores and water droplets. Even their faces are smoothed—the nose is a mere suggestion of cartilage, the ears are small, tight holes sealed against the damp, and the eyes are vast, black pools that reflect the violet gloom. These eyes lack whites; they are lenses evolved for the starved light, dilated constantly to drink in the grayness.

They are clad in the skins of the silent, gliding things that swim in the water channels—leather that is sleek, rubbery, and repels the perpetual drizzle. But their most distinct technological signature is their footwear. To survive the polished, treacherous incline of the wood-lattice, they wear "Grip-Feet": intricate boots carved from the dense, ossified bones of the great canopy-dwellers. The soles are studded with hundreds of tiny, metal-less spikes made of sharpened stone or jagged thorn, creating a symphony of microscopic clicks that are nonetheless swallowed by the humid air the moment they are made.

In their hands, they carry the Tools of Maintenance. These are simple, elegant things. Long, flexible poles of cured wood, tipped with flint or shark-tooth, used to test the depth of the water channels and vault across the slippery gaps. They carry "Spades of Correction"—flat, broad blades of bone bound to wooden handles with woven vines, used not for digging, but for scraping.

They are the Keepers of the Flow.

To watch them work is to witness a religious ceremony that is entirely manual labor. They gather at the mouths of the engineered water grooves where the moss and detritus threaten to choke the flow. They work in silence, using their bone spades to lift the sludge with rhythmic, hypnotic precision. They do not speak; communication is achieved through a complex series of touches and taps with their poles, vibrations that travel through the wood better than sound travels through the air. When they clear a blockage and the water begins to burble freely again, they pause. They do not cheer. They simply tilt their heads, listening to the water rush away into the dark, feeling the vibration of the "city" drinking.

Their lives are spent in the Niches—the hollows at the base of the arboreal towers where the wood has curled protectively inward. Inside these warm, damp pockets, they cultivate their sustenance: vast, fleshy shelves of bioluminescent fungi that pulse with a faint, internal light. They tend these gardens with the same reverence they show the gutters, pruning the mycelium and harvesting the caps. The air inside a Niches is thick with spores, a sweet, cloying scent that clings to their skin.

They are aware of the Weirdness, but they do not fear it; they navigate it. When the water in the channels begins to defy gravity, climbing the walls of the colonnade to feed the high roots, the inhabitants do not panic. They simply adjust their rituals. They use their long poles to hook the ascending rivulets, guiding them into carved runnels, acting as midwives to the physics of this world. They seem to understand the non-Euclidean geometry instinctively; they will walk a straight line that, to an outsider, appears to curve sharply, and they will arrive exactly where they intended, as if they perceive the grid that underlies the illusion.

They are a people who know they are transient. They look at the smooth, leathery bark of the mountain-trees, and they see something eternal. They do not carve their names into the walls; to mar the surface would be a sacrilege against the continuity of the Total Built. They believe they are not living *in* the forest, but *on* the skin of a sleeping deity, and that their only purpose is to ensure the deity does not wake in irritation, or worse, suffocate in its own dream.

In the rare moments when they rest, leaning against the warm, living flank of a pillar, they close their large, dark eyes and extend their hands to rest flat against the wood. They stand like that for hours, perfectly still, feeling the sub-sonic tectonic breath of the floor beneath them, listening to the silence that is not empty, but full. They are waiting, just as the world is waiting—maintaining the gutters, tending the fungi, ensuring that when the time comes, the flow is unobstructed for whatever comes next.
