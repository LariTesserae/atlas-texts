Life here does not walk, run, or crawl. It is a state of frantic, high-frequency repair.

**The Skimmers**

The only independent actors in this blinding, wet cathedral are the Skimmers. They are the macro-phages of this system, tiny, frantic surgeons operating on a dying giant.

There is nothing biological about them, yet they move with the desperate, twitchy vitality of an insect colony. Each is roughly the size of a human hand, constructed not in a factory but from the wreckage of the room itself. Their carapaces are patchworks of grey, porous stone and jagged shards of translucent tech-material, bound together by the ambient light that bleeds from the air. They look like broken pieces of the cavern that decided to detach and keep moving.

They possess four wings made of vibrating, glass-like filaments—identical in material to the solitary ferns—which buzz with a high-pitched chirp that cuts through the low-frequency roar of the room. They do not fly smoothly; they dart and jitter, moving in vectors that seem to anticipate the room’s chaotic physics before it happens.

Their purpose is singular and futile: they are fighting the erosion.

The ground is liquid; the walls are digesting themselves. The Skimmers are the immune system. They land on a section of rock that is beginning to soften into sludge, their legs gripping the heaving stone. With pincers that glow a cold, antiseptic blue, they cauterize the reality-warping fissures. They weld the cracks in the spacetime, fusing the melting geometry back into solid matter. They are constantly repairing the grates that slide into existence, sealing the gold-white filaments that pulse beneath the skin of the rock.

There is a sorrow to their industry. For every crack they seal, three more appear in the periphery. They are endlessly tending to a patient that is actively cannibalizing its own body. When a Skimmer fails—if it is caught in a re-crystallization event or engulfed by a sudden upward surge of sludge—it does not die in the traditional sense. It simply stops moving, its blue light dimming, and within moments, the churn of the floor incorporates it. Its shard-wings become part of the new stone, its body a fossil in the making, indistinguishable from the ruin it tried to save.

**The Weaver in the Stone**

If the Skimmers are the immune system, the Lattice is the mind. It is not a creature one can point to, but it is the only true "native" of this place.

Beneath the wet, grey porosity of the walls lies a vascular system of gold-white filaments. This is the technology that has ceased to be distinct from the geology. It is a distributed intelligence, a computer made of nerves and light woven directly into the bedrock.

It lives in the vibration. It processes the world through the trembling of the puddles and the roar of the air. The blinding light (Light: 2.6) is its cognitive radiation—the heat of its thinking. It does not possess a singular consciousness, but rather a million competing subroutines that manifest as the "Zen garden" arrangement of the pillars. It creates order where there should only be chaos, organizing the erosion into aesthetic patterns.

The Lattice is constantly dreaming. These dreams are the "glyphs" that form in the condensation—fleeting, geometric shapes that hover in the mist before collapsing. They are calculations manifest as wet air. Sometimes, the Lattice reaches out. A Skimmer might freeze mid-flight, its wings locking as a filament of light shoots from the wall to interface with the machine's core, uploading new parameters for repair. The Skimmer then flies off with a different gait, pursuing a new, obscure logic dictated by the stone.

The Lattice is not hostile; it is merely processing. It is digesting the concept of "cavern" and excreting "architecture," grinding down the old world to build the new one, over and over, in an infinite loop of improvement and decay.

**The Echoes**

There is a third presence here, so subtle it is almost felt rather than seen. Because the physics here are local and the light bends in impossible spirals, visual echoes of the past and future are trapped in the wet air.

Sometimes, in the corner of your eye, you might see a shape that isn't there—a towering humanoid figure made of water that mimics the turbulence of the floor, or a herd of glass-fern deer that flash into existence for a heartbeat, grazing on the light, before the reality-warping machinery erases them as "errors."

These are the Ghosts of Potential. The room tries to simulate what *could* live here. It generates phantom fauna based on the biological data it has absorbed from the erosion. They are beautiful, translucent, and silent. They are the life that would have thrived here if the environment weren't so aggressively determined to digest itself. They shimmer in the high-contrast light and are gone, leaving only the humming Skimmers and the weeping, gold-veined walls.
