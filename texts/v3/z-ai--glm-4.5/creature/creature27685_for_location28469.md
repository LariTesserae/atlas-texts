They are known as the Tuned, though they rarely speak the name aloud. In a place where silence is the only scarcity, names are spoken through the vibrations of the metal rather than the shape of the mouth.

To look at a Tuned is to see a creature that has negotiated a peace treaty with physics. They are tall and spindly, possessing a skeletal length that speaks to a life spent climbing and reaching, their limbs elongated by generations of navigating the vertical city. Their skin is the color of deep hematite, dark and oily, not with the sheen of sweat, but with a natural secretion that protects them from the biting chemical air and the scorching radiance of the deck plates. They do not wear clothing in the traditional sense; instead, they are draped in the shed skins of the environment—loose drapes of woven wire insulation and capes made from the cured, leathery leaves of the metallic ferns.

Their faces are adaptations to the blinding, searing brightness of the High-Frame. The eyes of the Tuned are not open and vulnerable like those of a lowlander; they are heavily hooded, protected by thick, translucent nictitating membranes that act as permanent welding goggles. These lenses filter the harsh glare and the UV radiation, tinting their world in perpetual, safe shades of amber and slate. Their noses are mere slits, flared only to taste the chemical composition of the air, checking for the sudden scent of burning insulation or the sweet rot of a ruptured hydraulic line.

But it is the ears—or the lack thereof—that define them most. The auditory canals are sealed over by thick pads of scar tissue and cartilage. In this acoustic hellzone, to hear through air is to go mad. Instead, the Tuned listen with their bones. The soles of their feet and the palms of their hands are densely calloused and packed with specialized nerve endings. They are subwoofer organisms, detecting the deep, infrasonic thrum of the megastructure’s heart. They know a gear is stripping before it screams because they feel the change in the frequency of the hum through the floor grating. They feel the footsteps of a predator—or a friend—vibrating up the stanchions from three decks down.

Their society is not built on rapid conversation, but on a tactile, rhythmic language they call *Tapping*. Using heavy, knobbled rods made of ironwood or scavenged piston rods, they communicate by striking the metal skin of the world. A distinct pattern of *tang-tang-thrum* might mean "food is ready," while a resonant, deep *gong* could warn of a pressure drop in the sector above. To watch two Tuned converse is to see a duel of rhythm against the railing of a catwalk, a staccato dialogue that vanishes instantly into the industrial roar, unheard by anyone but the intended recipient.

They live in the niches of the machine, inhabiting the "negative space." Their homes are burrows carved into the accumulated mulch of decomposed leaves and rust flakes inside the hollow guts of massive ventilation columns. They sleep in hammocks woven from high-tension cable slung between the ribs of cooling towers, swaying gently in the updrafts like pods waiting to germinate.

The Tuned are omnivorous scavengers, the ultimate opportunists of the vertical strata. They harvest the "electric berries" from the invasive vines, plucking the glowing, capacitor-like fruits that crackle with stored static charge, eating them for the sugar and the jolt of energy. They fish for the silver pipe-dwelling fish using lures made of glowing filament stripped from broken circuits. They even farm: maintaining small, carefully pruned plots of moss on flat surfaces that receive just the right amount of dripping condensation and escaping steam.

Yet, their most profound relationship is with the entity they call the Great Slow. They understand the geologic timescale of the High-Frame intuitively. They do not rush; to rush is to break rhythm with the world. When a massive gear needs turning, or a rusted valve requires adjustment, they apply leverage not with bursts of strength, but with a slow, grinding persistence that matches the machine’s own inertia. They will lean against a lever for hours, their weight distributed, their breathing synced with the expansion of the metal, waiting for the metal to yield.

In their theology, they are not parasites, but enzymes. They believe the High-Frame is a living organism, a vast iron titan suspended in the sky, and their role is to prevent the "fever"—the overgrowth of the aggressive vines—from choking the titan's lungs. They prune the lethal hybrid foliage with machetes that resemble serrated shear blades. They clear the intake grates of the steam vents. They are the white blood cells of the iron lung, patrolling the humid, blinding corridors of their world, keeping the blood flowing and the heart beating, locked in a glorious, sweating, vibrating symbiosis that has lasted for millennia.
