To see them is to first mistake them for a feature of the landscape. In the blue, diffuse wash of the high-altitude light, where shadows are soft and distance is deceptive, they appear as merely another protrusion of the Oar-Stalks—pale, dormant limbs drifting in the slow, seasonal currents.

They are a people of the pause. Their bodies are long and streamlined, not with the frantic muscularity of a shark or the darting twitch of a baitfish, but with the tapered, hydrodynamic elegance of a weeping willow branch. Their skin is the color of moonlight on ice—pale, translucent, and flush with the capillary networks of a cold-blooded metabolism, visible beneath the dermis as intricate, shifting maps of deep blue and violet. They lack the fur or blubber of warm-blooded mammals; instead, they possess a dense, gelatinous layer just beneath the skin that insulates them from the glacial chill, allowing them to become one with the temperature of the water. To touch them would feel like touching polished marble left out in the snow.

They are upright, bipedal in the loosest sense, though their posture is dictated by the water column. Their spines are hyper-flexible, allowing them to stand tall upon the plateau floor or curl horizontally to sleep in the hollow cradles of the stalk-buildings. Their legs are powerful, ending in broad, rudder-like feet that can splay wide for stability against the currents or tuck tight for propulsion. But it is the arms that command attention. They are disproportionately long, spanning nearly twice the height of the body, and end in hands that are terrifyingly delicate.

The hands are their civilization. With six fingers—two opposable thumbs and four long, spindly digits—they possess the dexterity of a surgeon and the strength of a construction worker. The fingers are ridged with microscopic suckers, not to grasp prey, but to hold tools against the slipperiness of wet moss and kelp. You will often see one of them hanging suspended in the open water between two stalks, motionless, wrapped in a woven harness of dried kelp, filing a shell into a needle with a shard of volcanic glass.

Their faces are masks of serene indifference. They have no noses, only two thin, rhythmic slits that flare and contract, extracting oxygen from the water with a slow, deliberate efficiency. Their mouths are small, often concealed behind a respiratory veil of loose, cartilaginous tissue, but when they speak—or rather, when they broadcast their clicks—they expose rows of tiny, translucent teeth used not for tearing flesh, but for scraping nutrient-rich slime from the bark of the city.

But it is the eyes that hold the ancient weight of the place. They are large, black orbs, devoid of whites, designed to catch the faintest photon in the deep. They do not blink often. When they look at you, they do not look with the immediate curiosity of a human; they look through you, assessing your place in the current, your trajectory, your potential to disrupt the harmony of the grove. It is a gaze that measures time in decades, not seconds.

They are dressed not in fabric, which rots and drags in the wet, but in the city itself. They wear aprons of woven living fronds that continue to grow while attached to them, symbiotic layers that filter the water for them. Tools are hung on loops of gut and sinew: heavy stone hammers shaped like river rocks, bone chisels crafted from the ribs of the great ray-like gardeners, and adzes made of dense shell. They carry no weapons; their only defense is the city itself, and a passive biochemistry that makes their flesh foul-tasting to predators.

Movement is a ritual. They do not swim so much as they translate themselves. A kick of a leg, a subtle adjustment of a wrist, and they glide ten meters, drifting with the momentum of a falling leaf. To rush is to waste energy, and in this caloric-scarce, cold environment, energy is life. When they work—pruning the upper canopies of the Oar-Stalks or splicing a bridge between two towers—they move in a synchronized trance. A group of three will hover around a thick branch, communicating through the rapid-fire clicking of their tongue-claps, the sound bouncing off the hollow walls, allowing them to "see" the structure of the wood through vibration better than their eyes can.

They are intimately tied to the giant, grazing rays. They do not ride them, nor do they fear them. Instead, they act as shepherds and symbiotes. As a ray glides past a window, a dweller might reach out with a long arm to scrape parasites from the creature’s back, receiving in return a cloud of nutrient-rich waste that fertilizes the window-box garden of their dwelling. It is a quiet transaction, unspoken and understood.

When they sleep, they retreat into the hollows of the stalks. They do not lie down. They anchor themselves by their tails to the inner walls, hanging suspended in the center of the vertical shafts, lulled to sleep by the deep, resonant hum of the water moving through the city. In this darkness, wrapped in the breathing wood, they look less like people and more like fruit growing on a vast, submerged tree—patient, waiting, and perfectly at peace in the crushing, beautiful cold.
