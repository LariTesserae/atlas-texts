They are the Architects of the Cairn, a gaunt, stooped species of biped that seems to have evolved specifically to inhabit the cramped, interstitial spaces of the High Flat. To call them human would be a disservice to the grotesque perfection of their adaptation; they are hominids only in the loosest sense, stretched and starved by the altitude into something resembling a bundle of dried re laths wrapped in grey hide.

You spot them first not by their movement, for there is none, but by the deliberate geometry of their placement. Where the hexapods and avians exist in a chaotic, organic jam—predator tackling prey, herds pressing against herds—the Architects are frozen in acts of construction and maintenance. They stand in the tiny, rare clearings, or perch precariously atop the fossilized backs of the reptilian giants, their long, spindly arms wrapped around massive stones that they were in the process of hoisting.

Their skin is the color of the shale beneath their feet, a porous, mineral-grey that seems to absorb rather than reflect the stark sunlight. It is pulled tight over prominent ridges of bone, giving them the appearance of creatures stripped of all flesh, leaving only the essential framework. Their chests are barrel-shaped, the ribs flared outward to accommodate lungs that once had to laboriously extract oxygen from the thin, iron-tasting air. Now, those chests are held in a perpetual, silent inhale, a cavity of vacuum that waits for a breath that will never come.

They are draped in crude furs, pelts that look to have been cut from the very shaggy hexapods they stand beside. The furs are stiff with age and frost, treated with some primitive alchemy to resist the slow, grinding erosion of time. Around their necks and waists, they wear strings of polished bone and small, smooth stones—relics of a culture that valued the permanent above all else.

Their faces are masks of serene concentration. The eyes are wide open, the lids stripped back by the winds of the past, revealing irises of a pale, washed-out hazel that seems to match the sky. They do not look at the observer; they look *through* them, their gaze fixed on the eternity of the horizon. Their mouths are slightly parted, the lips cracked and dry, as if caught in the middle of a command or a prayer.

It is in their hands that you see the tragedy of their ambition.

One Architect, perched like a gargoyle on the knee of a frozen mammoth-like beast, holds a flint knife in a grip of white-knuckled tension. The blade is pressed against a length of sinew, partway through a cut that would have taken another second to complete. Another stands at the base of a crude tower of rocks, a cairn meant to mark a trail that no one travels. His arms are raised, his fingers interlocked with the jagged edges of a top stone, suspending it in the air above the tower, the final act of balance never executed.

The woven nets mentioned in the landscape are often held by these figures. A group of three stands in a semi-circle, their arms stretched taut, the fibers of a net pulled between them. The net sags slightly in the middle, heavy with the weight of a small, iridescent bird that was captured mid-flight. The Architects are not looking at their catch; they are looking outward, scanning for a threat that never arrived, or perhaps waiting for a signal from the silent heavens.

They are the interlopers who tried to tame the attic of the world. They saw the biological chaos of the High Flat and attempted to impose order upon it, building shelters from the ribs of leviathans and stacking stones to claim ownership of the dust. They built their tech low and hard: spears of chipped obsidian that retain a frightening sharpness, knives that have never known rust, and woven nets that have not unraveled in a thousand years.

Now, they are as much a part of the geology as the stones they stacked. The lichens and mosses that grow on the "soil" of the dead beasts have begun to creep onto the Architects as well. A grey, crusty mold clings to the hem of a fur wrap; a dry, needle-leaf plant sprouts from the pocket of a woven tunic. They are becoming the pillars of their own unfinished city.

To walk among them is to feel a profound sense of intrusion. They are not statues, for statues are placed by others; these beings placed themselves. They chose their postures. They chose their tools. They chose to be here, in the crushing silence, and in return, the place allowed them to stay forever. If you were to push an Architect, as the physics of this place dictate, they would not topple. They would simply slide, frictionless and rigid, maintaining that tense, ready stance, the eternal waiters of a banquet that never began.
