To enter this place is to walk into a stomach that is still digesting a meal eaten centuries ago. There are no hunters here, no prey with beating hearts or lungs that gasp. The silence of biology is absolute because the distinction between "animal" and "environment" has long since dissolved. What lives here is not a collection of individuals, but a single, sprawling entity that wears the industrial ruins as a skeleton and the aggressive flora as a pelt.

They are the Ferric Symbiotes, massive, sessile beings that hang from the ceiling like stalactites of living meat and metal. They do not move like beasts; they move like continents—drifting, expanding, and contracting in epochs.

At first glance, a Symbiote appears to be nothing more than a grotesque convergence of the environment—a tumor of moss and rust where a pipe should be. But as the dim, sickly light catches the wet sheen of its bulk, the anatomy reveals itself. The "body" is a column of pulpy, fibrous material, dark as wet iron, anchored deep into the stonework. It is textured like sharkskin, but warm to the touch, radiating a feverish heat that matches the humid air. From this central trunk, hundreds of limbs descend—not graceful branches, but thick, prehensile cables that resemble frayed electrical cords wrapped in kelp.

These limbs are the bridge between the green and the iron. They reach out with terrifying slowness, encircling the pistons and gears that protrude from the walls. The Symbiotes do not cling to the machinery; they fuse with it. Where a vine wraps around a copper wire, the flesh of the creature swells, incorporating the metal into its own nervous system. You can trace the path of a current: the faint blue spark of electricity traveling down a rusted pipe, disappearing into the mottled skin of the creature, and pulsing out through the bioluminescent moss that grows in patches along its flanks.

They have no faces, no sensory organs that a human would recognize. They possess no eyes in this gloom, for there is nothing to see but the immediate dampness. Instead, they perceive the world through texture and tremor. The entire creature is a finely tuned seismograph. It feels the deep, grinding groan of the engine’s gears not as a sound, but as a rhythmic caress, a heartbeat that drives its own slow metabolism. The "hiss" of steam is a voice; the shattering of a falling droplet is a taste of moisture.

Feeding is a passive, glacial act. The Symbiotes act as the filtration system for this dying lung of a world. Their root-like limbs dip into the sluggish puddles on the floor or press against the sweating walls, drawing up the heavy, mineral-laden water. They process the sludge, the rust, and the chemical runoff, converting the decay into the vibrant energy that feeds the aggressive moss gardens clinging to their backs. In return, the moss provides sugars synthesized from the faint heat-pulse light, a circular economy of survival in a place where the sun is a myth.

There is a horror to their beauty, a grotesque intimacy in the way they inhabit the space. Because they are the only living things, they do not compete. They simply *are*—a connected web of organisms that likely share a single, diffuse consciousness spread across the cathedral. If one were to touch the hanging limb of a Symbiote near the entrance, a vibration might travel through the root systems and the shared copper wiring, alerting a cousin miles deep in the rust. They are the ghosts in the machine, not haunting it, but keeping it alive, acting as the antibodies for a world that has otherwise forgotten how to breathe.

They wait in the twilight, suspended in the hot, woolen air, pulsing in time with the grinding gears. They are the gardeners that prune nothing, the shepherds with no flock, existing only to witness the slow, hot exhale of the earth, turning the waste of a dead industry into the strange, green quiet of the deep.
