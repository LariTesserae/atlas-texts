To speak of what lives here is to speak of a patient, silence-wearing intelligence that defies the categorization of "animal." In the absence of fish, reptiles, or insects, the ecological niche of the active, mobile creature has been usurped by something stranger: a colonial, mobile flora that has evolved to herd and harvest the very machinery it strangles.

They are called the **Curators**, though they have no name for themselves. If you were to float motionless in the warm, copper-tasting dark, your eyes would eventually deceive you. What looks like a draping curtain of kelp hanging from a rusted gantry might slowly detach itself, drifting downward with the heavy, calculated grace of a falling parachute.

A Curator is roughly humanoid in outline, though this is a coincidence of convergent evolution rather than lineage. They stand perhaps eight feet tall, their bodies elongated to suit the buoyant drift. Their "skin" is a tough, rubbery matte-green substance, translucent enough to reveal the sluggish, dark pulse of nutrient fluids running through subcutaneous veins. They possess no distinct head, only a slight thickening at the top of the torso where clusters of sensory stalks—like delicate sea-whips—wave gently in the current, tasting the chemical composition of the water.

They are not born; they are budded.

You would see them clinging to the vertical walls, their limbs adapted into distinct purposes. The upper limbs are long, multi-jointed fronds that end in manipulator tendrils fine enough to thread the eye of a needle, yet strong enough to turn a rusted valve wheel. These are their hands, their tools. The lower limbs are grasping hooks, root-like clusters that can penetrate the joins of metal pipes, anchoring the creature against the slow, thermal tides.

They are the gardeners of this industrial lung. They move with a glacial, silent deliberation, their presence masked by the constant shifting of the surrounding algae. A Curator does not swim; it navigates by buoyancy control and surface tension, drifting to a control panel and landing softly, like a leaf. Once settled, it goes to work. It uses its fine tendrils to prune the aggressive mosses that threaten to clog the pressure gauges, or perhaps it clears a vent that has become overgrown, ensuring the cycle of air and water continues. They do not worship the machine; they *farm* it. They strip copper wiring from the walls to weave into their own cellular structures, reinforcing their bodies with the conductive metal, creating a lattice of iron and chlorophyll beneath their skin.

Their mouths are not for eating, but for filtering. A slit runs vertically along their torso, fringed with cilia that constantly cycle the warm water, extracting dissolved minerals and the faint traces of ozone. They breathe the machinery as much as the water.

They are not solitary, but they are not social in the way animals understand it. They communicate through the root systems that drape like wiring between the floor grates. A Curator gripping a vine on the eastern wall can send a chemical pulse, a signal of "light" or "pressure drop," that travels through the fungal network to another Curator a mile away, deep in the dark. The silence of the chamber is therefore deceptive; it is filled with a slow, sub-lingual chatter of chemical data, a humming consciousness that permeates the green.

They are the architects of the strangeness here. They are the reason the vegetation is so lush, so integrated. They guide the vines into the cooling vents, they wrap the algae around the flywheels. They are turning the cold iron of the factory into a warm, living body.

Occasionally, one might see them sleeping. They do not lie down; they simply go rigid, anchoring themselves to a high arch or a suspended walkway, splaying their limbs to catch the faint light filtering from the surface. In this state, they resemble nothing so much as gruesome, green decorations, part of the room itself—hanging statues in a submerged temple, waiting for the cycle to turn, guarding the silence with the patience of stone.
