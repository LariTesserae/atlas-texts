The primary inhabitants of this wet, grey shelf are the **Lithic Masons**.

They are not creatures of flesh in the traditional sense, or at least, they do not appear to be. To the uninitiated eye, they are merely the animate stones of the scree itself. They possess the flattened, irregular geometry of the shards they crawl over, their carapaces a perfect mimicry of the wet, dark slate that litters the ground. They are roughly the diameter of a dinner plate, but low to the ground, possessing a density that makes them seem heavier than their size suggests. When they are motionless—which is rare—they vanish entirely into the landscape, indistinguishable from the jagged rubble.

They have no eyes. Their anterior is a smooth, sloping shield of bone-black chitin, lacking sensory organs entirely. Instead of sight, they navigate through a profound sensitivity to pressure and vibration. They feel the weight of the air, the distant press of the storm fronts rolling over the plateau, and the footsteps of the other creatures around them with hyper-acuity. This shared sensory web creates the "shared intelligence" observed in their construction projects; they do not signal one another, they simply *know* the intent of the group, flowing around obstacles and moving heavy stones in a unison that resembles a single, distributed organism rather than a colony of individuals.

Their movement is disturbingly fluid. Despite their rigid, stony shells, they do not click or clatter. The joints where their legs meet their bodies are sheathed in a thick, pale membrane that absorbs sound, making their locomotion hydraulic—smooth, silent, and relentless. They move with the urgency of flowing water, pouring over the sharp ridges and up the sides of the primitive longhouses without breaking their stride.

They are the stewards of the high shelf. They do not build nests for themselves; instead, they maintain the infrastructure of the plateau. They harvest the massive, bruised-purple fungi that cling to the cliff edges, stripping the cup-shaped caps and carrying them on their backs to the rooftops of the human hamlet, where they mash the fungal matter into a pulp that acts as a potent, water-repellent mortar. They clear the drainage channels of the narrow streets with their pincers, lifting debris with a delicacy that belies their brute strength. They reinforce the stone foundations of the huts, sliding beneath the floorboards to shore up the earth with their bodies, becoming living pillars. To them, the human architecture is just another geological formation to be curated and stabilized.

***

Sharing this damp, silent city are the **Damp-Dwellers**, the human inhabitants of the plateau.

They are a people carved from the same grey stone as their home. They are pale, their skin taking on a faint, bluish tint from the perpetual cold, and they move with a slow, deliberate economy of motion to conserve heat and energy. They dress in layers of heavy, oiled wool and felt, garments that absorb the clinging mist rather than repelling it, making them look larger and softer than they are.

The culture of the Damp-Dwellers is defined entirely by **The Truce**. They do not domesticate the Lithic Masons; they do not command them or keep them as pets. To do so would be a catastrophic breach of the plateau’s unspoken rules. Instead, they live in a state of profound symbiosis. They have learned to build their doors and windows to the specifications of the Masons—leaving gaps at the base of the walls to allow the creatures easy passage.

A Damp-Dweller might wake up to find a Mason sitting on their chest, inspecting the stitching of their blanket, or three of them lifting the corner of their bed to level it against the settling floor. The reaction is never panic, only a quiet, resigned patience. The humans simply wait, holding their breath, until the creature finishes its assessment and moves on.

Because the Masons are blind, the Damp-Dwellers have largely abandoned spoken language within the hamlet. Shouting would disrupt the hydraulic silence of the workers, and the Masons cannot hear words anyway. Instead, the humans communicate through a complex sign language performed close to the body, utilizing touch and subtle shifts in weight. Their society is a quiet one, governed by a deep, meditative rhythm.

They eat what the Masons harvest. The Masons collect the spores and flesh of the giant ferns and fungi, often leaving excess piles on the flat, scrubbed stones that serve as the village square. The humans process this harvest, boiling it into thick, nutrient-rich stews that fuel their bodies against the marrow-chilling damp. In return, the humans provide the Masons with one thing the wild plateau cannot: **Salts**. The Damp-Dwellers mine the salt deposits found deep in the fissures of the young rock and crush it into fine powder, scattering it along the paths the Masons frequent. The creatures consume the salt with gusto, their rasping mouthparts hidden beneath their shells audibly grinding the mineral—a sound, if one listens very closely, like the grinding of tectonic plates.

When the storm finally breaks, and the heavy, freezing rain begins to hammer against the thatched roofs, the distinction between the two species dissolves completely. The humans retreat indoors, sitting in the dim light of their hearths. Outside, the Lithic Masons swarm over the roofs and walls, holding the thatch down with their bodies, blocking the gaps in the mortar, anchoring the village against the wind with their combined weight. Inside, the humans listen, feeling the vibrations of the workers through the soles of their boots, safe in the knowledge that they are guests in a living house, protected by a city that builds itself.
