The inhabitants of the Hamlet of Pistons are not builders in the traditional sense, but stewards of a deep, metabolic slowness. They are a sparse, reclusive people known only as the **Keepers**.

To the uninitiated eye, they are nearly invisible. Their physiology has adapted over generations to mimic the very environment they tend; they possess a stooped, sturdy posture, with skin that takes on the texture and mottled grey-green hue of wet lichen. They are hairless, their scalps instead covered in a thin, velvet-like moss that filters the heavy, humid air.

They dress in layers of heavy, oil-treated canvas and cured leather, strapped tight with brass buckles that have long since oxidized to match the copper piping snaking around the trees. Their boots are the source of the erosion on the stone plazas—massive, iron-soled things that weigh heavy on the earth, designed to ground them against the subsonic vibrations of the groaning factories. When they walk, it is a slow, deliberate shuffle, the metal soles scraping softly against the wet stone, a sound that blends imperceptibly with the distant drip of condensation.

Their faces are obscured by intricate, brass-framed respirators that cover the nose and mouth. These masks are not mechanical filters, but symbiotic colonies of small, air-purifying ferns and mosses that breathe for them, extracting oxygen from the dense, steam-filled air and humidifying it before it enters their lungs. A mass of translucent, rubbery tubing coils from the mask down into their collars, connecting them directly to the humid, internal atmosphere of their suits. A rhythmic, wet hissing accompanies their every breath—*in-hiss, out-hiss*—synchronizing their own biology with the *thrum-hiss* of the tree-factories.

The Keepers are the technicians of the gut. They spend their "days" (for time here is measured in pressure shifts rather than sunlight) tending to the delicate interface between the mechanical and the biological. You might see one perched high on a buttressed root, using a wrench the size of a human torso to gently tighten a valve on a copper artery, stopping a leak of pressurized sap. They move with a glacial caution, aware that a sudden movement could trigger a pressure reflex in the giant tree.

They do not speak. The vocal cords have atrophied, rendered unnecessary by the thickness of the air and the sensitivity of the trees. Instead, they communicate through a complex language of taps and vibrations. Using iron rods that hang from their belts, they tap rhythms against the metal pipes—signals that travel through the vascular system of the hamlet, alerting others to a pressure drop or a blocked filter. To a visitor, the city is silent; to the Keepers, the pipes are singing constantly with a low, Morse-like chatter.

Their dwellings are not separate from the factories, but nestled within them. They sleep in hollowed-out chambers within the buttress roots, lined with soft, bioluminescent fungi that provide a warm, gentle glow. Here, the air is thickest with the tree’s exhalations—rich in oxygen and sleep-inducing scents. They curl up in hammocks woven from strong vines, rocking gently in the syncopated rhythm of the tree’s heartbeat.

Their diet is entirely derived from the byproducts of the industry. They harvest the nutrient-rich runoff that collects in the basins of the roots, boiling it down into a thick, sweet paste. They drink the distilled water that condenses on the cooler metal pipes, and cultivate small gardens of edible mushrooms in the steam vents where the temperature is highest.

Occasionally, one of the insectoid drones will descend from the canopy, whirring softly. A Keeper will extend a gloved hand, allowing the drone to land. They will exchange something—a sample of sap, a sliver of resin, a polished gear mechanism. The Keepers treat the drones with the same reverence they show the trees, viewing them as the white blood cells of the larger organism, small messengers that maintain the balance of the system.

They are ancient, or at least they seem to be. Their eyes, visible behind the thick lenses of their brass goggles, are dark and pools-deep, reflecting no impatience. They exist in a state of suspended animation, waiting, watching, and adjusting. They are the antibodies of the Hamlet, living in the spaces between the heartbeat and the breath, content to be the silent mechanics of a world that never rushes.
