At first, you would swear the High Forge is uninhabited. The silence tricks you; the lack of shouting, of footsteps, of the chaotic noise of life suggests abandonment. But if you stand still on a swaying catwalk, bracing yourself against the crushing altitude, and watch the shadows lengthen across the obsidian spires, you begin to see the errors in your perception. You realize that the "overcrowding" you sensed is not a claustrophobia of space, but of existence—there are things living here, but they have evolved to fit the cracks so perfectly that they have become indistinguishable from the city itself.

Here is what lives in the High Forge.

### The Gear-Walkers (The Tithed)

The primary inhabitants are humanoid, but only in the loosest, most architectural sense. They are tall—painfully elongated, perhaps seven or eight feet in height, but so emaciated and gaunt they look like scaffolding wrapped in grey canvas. Their limbs are telescopic, joints clicking with a dry, articulate sound that mimics the settling of the building, allowing them to span the impossible gaps between catwalks without a bridge.

Their skin is the texture of the city: pallid, scarred, and hardened into a rough hide that resembles sun-baked leather or rusted iron. It protects them from the sharp, glass-like edges of the extruded basalt. They wear no clothing, only tattered harnesses of cured gut and bronze buckles from which hang tools—wrenches, chisels, and hammers—that look like children’s toys in their oversized, calloused hands.

They are slow. Terribly, wonderfully slow. Their movements are synchronized with the massive gears that drive the city. When a piston the size of a cathedral retracts, a Gear-Walker will step forward. When it extends, they pause. They never rush. To rush here is to waste energy, and in a land of such scarcity, waste is a sin. They traverse the catwalks with a glacial, fluid grace, looking less like people walking and more like shadow puppets sliding across a screen.

They do not speak. The air is too thin to carry the wasted breath of conversation. Instead, they communicate through vibration. If you place your hand on the railing, you can feel them "talking" to one another—a series of dull, rhythmic thrums traveling through the iron bones of the city, signaling a blockage, a leak, or a shift in the wind. They are the maintenance crew of a machine that has largely forgotten its purpose, performing tasks with religious dedication: turning a valve that releases steam, tightening a bolt that has not loosened in a century, wiping a lens that looks down at the cloud layer.

### The Ichor-Eaters

Smaller, swifter, and far more numerous are the Ichor-Eaters. These are the vermin of the High Forge, scuttling in the darkened underlevels where the condensation drips. They resemble crabs or scorpions, but their carapaces are formed of translucent slag glass—the runoff of the industrial process. Through their shells, you can see the pulsing, neon-blue glow of their circulatory systems, pumping the oily, chemical-laden water they harvest.

They cluster around the massive intake pipes, scraping the mineral accretions and the "coral" that grows there with mandibles of hardened steel. They are the recyclers. When a Gear-Walker falls—or when a piece of the machinery finally shears off—the Ichor-Eaters are there in a chittering, clattering swarm, reducing the organic matter and the metal alike into slurry that feeds the roots of the city. They are the only things here that move with "normal" speed, a frantic, frantic energy that seems out of place in this slow-motion hell.

### The Silent Gliders

You noticed the birds from a distance, but up close, their nature becomes more disturbing. They are not biological. They are not entirely mechanical, either. They are hybrids—skeletal frames of aluminum and brass, powered by a metabolism of steam and geothermal heat. Their "wings" are razor-thin sheets of polished metal that catch the updrafts.

They live in the highest scaffolding, nesting in the ventilation shafts. They do not eat; they fuel themselves by pressing their bodies against the hot exhaust pipes, absorbing the thermal energy through vents in their chests. Their eyes are complex lenses, zooming in on the microscopic cracks in the stonework or the movement of the Ichor-Eaters below.

They are the watchers. If you stand still too long, a Glider will land on a railing near you, tilting its head with a whir of tiny gears. It isn't looking at *you*; it is analyzing the heat signature of your body, calculating your thermal efficiency. You are a variable in its equation. If you were to collapse, the Glider would not help; it would simply wait, because eventually, you would become heat, and heat is all they care about.

### The Deep-Dwellers

Rumored rather than seen, the Deep-Dwellers are said to exist in the lowest levels of the city, far below the catwalks, in the dark, humid fog where the light never penetrates. The Gear-Walkers speak of them only in their vibrational code—a low, ominous hum that makes the teeth ache.

The Deep-Dwellers are said to be the ancestors of the Gear-Walkers, those who stayed too long in the fog and drank too deeply of the oily water. They have become bloated, limbless masses of flesh and filtration systems, fused to the machinery, absorbing the poisons of the city and excreting the clean, breathable air that rises to the upper levels. They are the liver and kidneys of the High Forge, submerged in the chemical soup, silent and immobile, processing the world so that the others may walk the high spires. They are the price of the industry—living filters that ensure the silence remains unbroken.

### The Collective "Self"

Perhaps the most terrifying inhabitant is the city itself. The High Forge possesses a rudimentary, distributed intelligence. It is not a ghost in the machine, but the sum total of the vibrations, the steam pressure, and the slow alignment of the gears. It "thinks" in decades-long cycles.

When the steam vents hiss, it is not just physics; it is the city exhaling. When the ground shakes, it is the city shifting in its sleep. The Gear-Walkers, the Gliders, the Eaters—they are not merely residents. They are antibodies. They are the white blood cells of a vast, metallic organism that is slowly, inexorably trying to grow. You are standing on a living thing, and you are an anomaly—a particle of dust that does not fit the pattern. The silence you feel is not the absence of noise; it is the held breath of a predator waiting for you to make a mistake.
