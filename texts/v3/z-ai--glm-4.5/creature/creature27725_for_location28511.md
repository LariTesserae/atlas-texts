To look upon the floor of the Sump is to look upon a singular, writhing organism composed of infinite parts. At first glance, the Scavengers appear to be nothing more than the detritus of the city come to life—mounds of scrap metal and shedding aggregate that have gained the spark of motion. But as the eye adjusts to the gloom and the mind filters out the visual clutter, the sheer biological intensity of the swarm snaps into focus.

They are the moss of this world; the lichen that grows in the absence of light. Where a terrestrial ecosystem might support a carpet of soft, greenery, the Sump supports this hardened, carapaced tide.

Up close, the individual creature reveals a terrifying elegance. They are low-slung, built like flattened horseshoe crabs, but with a segmented symmetry that suggests both insect and machine. Their legs are not jointed in the organic way of a beetle, but are rather telescopic, hydraulic pistons of dense muscle sheathed in a black, oily chitin. When they move, there is no skittering—the friction of a million legs is absorbed by the damp air, producing only a dry, papery whisper, like the turning of a page in an empty library.

The defining feature of a Scavenger is its shell. It is a masterpiece of evolutionary mimicry, or perhaps industrial convergence. The plates are fused with the very metals they consume. Older Scavengers are nearly invisible against the backdrop; their backs are armored in flaking sheets of iron oxide, pitted and rough exactly like the surrounding girders. Others bear the smooth, verdigris-stained patina of copper or the mottled, white-scarred skin of aluminum corrosion. They do not simply wear the colors of their food; they incorporate the mineral structure into their own biology. To step on one is to feel the sickening crunch of shell that gives way not to blood, but to a thick, viscous sludge of reprocessed metal and ichor.

They possess no discernible heads. Where one expects a face, there is only a layered, radial array of sensory plates—smooth, black discs of polished obsidian glass that reflect the gray, flat light of the Sump. These plates vibrate in sympathy with the eternal thrum, allowing the creature to "see" through the resonance of the machinery. They navigate the complex architecture of the pipes and turbines not by sight, but by feeling the song of the engine. They know where the metal is weakest, where the oxidation is thickest, simply by listening to the subtle harmonics of the decay.

Beneath this sensory array lies the mouth: a terrifying industrial orifice. It is not a jaw of teeth, but a grinding mill of tungsten-hard mandibles that rotate in opposing directions. When a Scavenger feeds, it anchors itself to a pipe or a strut with magnetic pads in its legs. It presses its mouth against the surface and begins to shear. The sound is a high-pitched, tinny shriek that is instantly swallowed by the low-frequency hum of the environment. They scrape away the rust, the patina, the slow chemical death of the city, with surgical precision. They consume the entropy, stripping away the years of decay until the raw, clean steel shines underneath.

And then, they leave.

The feeding behavior is not a migration; it is a circulation. The Scavengers move in a cycle dictated by the geometry of the Sump. They pour up from the lower drains, climbing the vertical faces of the factories in vast, shimmering curtains. They graze the high pipes, combing the corrosion from the rivets, then flow downward along the sloping roofs of the assembly halls, cascading like metallic waterfalls back toward the ground. Their waste—the processed slurry of pure, elemental metals—is excreted constantly, a gray trail that slicks the ground behind them. This slurry runs into the gutters, down into the deep sumps, and is pulled back into the heart of the machinery to be forged once more.

There is no conflict among them. You will never see two Scavengers fight over a particularly luscious patch of rust. There is no mating ritual, no nesting, no vocalization of distress. They are fundamentally solitary, yet they operate as a perfect fluid. If you were to pick one up—it would be heavy, warm, and vibrating with a contained energy—and place it ten miles away, it would simply turn, orient itself to the hum, and begin walking, rejoining the local flow of the biomass as if it had never left.

Inside their shells, their internal workings blur the line between flesh and gear. Dissection (a theoretical exercise, as they heal too fast to be held open) would reveal a vascular system pumping not blood, but a conductive, golden fluid that serves as both nutrient and electrical transmission. Their nerves are fiber-optic threads that glow faintly when they feed. They are not flesh animals mimicking metal; they are metal that has learned to breathe.

They are the immune system of the Sump. The city attempts to fall apart; the Scavengers stitch it back together, molecule by molecule. They are the agents of the stasis. Without them, the factories would crumble into red dust within a century. With them, the decay is held in a perfect, dynamic check.

To stand among them is to feel a profound, existential isolation. They acknowledge your presence only to avoid it. As you walk, the river of shells parts around your boots like water around a stone, flowing seamlessly back together once you have passed. You are a solid object in a world of liquids and frequencies. They look at you with their black, glassy sensors and see nothing but a vibration that does not match the equation of the engine. You are silent to them, irrelevant to them. You are a ghost haunting the machine, and they are the busy keepers of the tomb, forever polishing the brass of a corpse that refuses to decay.
