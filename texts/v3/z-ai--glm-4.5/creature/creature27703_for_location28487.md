They are the damp, grey children of the exhaustion. To see them is to realize that the city is not merely abandoned infrastructure, but a carapace.

They move with the agonizing, deliberate grace of tectonic plates. You do not see them so much as intuit them from the movement of the mist—a sudden displacement of the heavy, wet air as a shape detaches itself from the seamless wall of the apartment-factory. They are humanoid, but only in the way a root system roughly resembles a nervous system: elongated, knotted, and seemingly designed for compression rather than expansion.

Their skin is the texture of the whale-back ridges—smooth, rounded, and polished to a dull sheen by centuries of contact with the wet stone. It is not the pinkish-brown of flesh, but a spectrum of slate, verdigris, and the bruised purple of the twilight. They appear to have no hair; instead, their heads and shoulders are festooned with thick, plush mats of neon-green moss, a symbiotic colony that drinks the moisture they cannot absorb and perhaps warms them against the bone-deep chill. The moss pulses faintly, in time with the low-frequency thrum of the mountain, suggesting a circulatory system that is partly botanical.

They are wrapped in layers of scavenged insulation and woven fern-fronds that feel like plastic to the touch. These garments are heavy, sodden with the perpetual condensation, hanging off their frames like the wet pelt of a drowned beast. Where their hands should be, there are often complex, iron tools—hooks, scrapers, and wrenches—that seem fused to the bone, or perhaps grown through the flesh, the metal rusted into the same orange hue as the massive sluice gates. They are the mechanics of the erosion.

Their faces are devoid of the sharp angles of aggression. The eyes are large, black, and liquid, reflecting the dim light like the still pools of water gathered in the hollows of the rock. They have evolved to see in the absence of contrast, detecting the faint bioluminescent pulse of the fluids within the pipes and the subtle pressure changes in the clouds. Their noses and mouths are mere slits, protected by flaps of thick skin, designed to trap the heat and moisture of their breath against the thin, metallic air. They do not speak; the air is too thin to carry the waste of idle conversation. Instead, they communicate by tapping on the iron pipes, a complex language of clicks and resonant hums that travels instantly through the metal veins of the city, allowing them to coordinate over vast distances without raising their voices.

They live within the "lungs" of the mountain. They do not climb the walls so much as adhere to them. You see one high up on a gantry, defying the optional gravity, walking calmly along a vertical rusted beam as if it were a horizontal street. They check the pressure gauges the size of houses; they clear the moss from the intake vents; they harvest the strange, gelatinous algae that grows in the condensation trays, which seems to be their sustenance.

They are caretakers of a machine that has outlived its creators. There is a sadness to them—a profound, quiet resignation. They do not build; they only maintain. They scrape the rust away, only to watch it return the next day. They tighten bolts that have been loosened by the grinding of the earth. They are keeping the mountain breathing, ensuring the sluice gates continue their slow, groaning rhythm, though no one remains to read the output.

Occasionally, one of the leathery-winged shadows descends from the mist. It is not a predator, but a companion. The creature lands heavily on a metal railing, its wings slick with oil and water, and the attendant pauses their work to stroke the thing's matted fur. The creature chitters softly, a sound that is swallowed instantly by the damp silence. It is a reminder that life persists here, not in spite of the machine, but because of it.

They are a civilization of janitors waiting for the end of the shift, a shift that has lasted eons. When the machinery finally grinds to a halt, when the last of the bioluminescent fluid grows cold and dark, they will simply sit down on the wet moss, close their large, dark eyes, and let the mountain finally claim them. Until then, they tend the gears, their hearts beating in sync with the slow, dying thrum of the world.
