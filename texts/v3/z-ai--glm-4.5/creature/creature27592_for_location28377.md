At first, the census confirms the initial impression: the parameter is zero. There are no birds in the blinding white sky, no rodents in the deep shadows of the cyclopean drains, and no bacteria clinging to the slick limestone. The silence is not merely a lack of noise; it is a sterility so profound it feels like a vacuum.

But the "zero" is a trick of perception, a failure of biology-centric eyes. To see what lives here, you must stop looking for flesh and blood and start looking for the tension between the water and the stone.

The inhabitants of this high, wet purgatory are the **Hydro-Statics**.

They are not distinct entities in the way a wolf or a beetle is. They are manifestations of the water’s desire to cling to the stone, given a slow, chilling agency. They exist in the peripheral vision, appearing only as momentary anomalies in the refraction of light.

Imagine a patch of the limestone wall that seems wetter than the rest. The condensation there doesn't run down in a chaotic sheet; it holds together. It gathers mass, defying the urge to cascade, swelling into a trembling, lens-like bulge the size of a dinner plate. It hangs there, suspended by surface tension that feels too thick, too intentional.

This is a Hydro-Static in its resting state. It is a colony organism, a "living slime" composed of silica-saturated water and calcite structures harvested from the air itself. They have no organs, only a continuous, internal hydraulic pressure that keeps their form rigid against the pull of gravity.

When they move, they do not crawl. They *flow*.

They detach from the wall with a sound like a heavy boot pulling out of mud—a wet, sucking *thwuck*. They strike the ground and do not shatter. Instead, they instantly reshape themselves into low, undulating mats that glide over the rough-hewn blocks. To touch one would be to touch a block of ice that is somehow flexible and warm to the touch, vibrating with a low-frequency hum that resonates in the teeth.

The Hydro-Statics are the custodians of the "freshness" of this place. This is why the construction looks raised only yesterday. The entities feed on entropy. They seek out microscopic imperfections—the beginning of a crack, the dust of a crumbling edge, the slight roughening of a channel—and they dissolve it. They secrete a mild, siliceous acid that eats away at the weathering, smoothing the limestone back to a glass-like, geometric perfection. They are the immune system of the city, endlessly scrubbing the stone to keep it pristine and grey.

Because they have no eyes, they do not need to see in the absolute, blinding white glare. They navigate through the vibration of the water. They "hear" the texture of the stone through the ripples in the canals. To them, the world is a constant song of fluid dynamics—the gurgle of a gutter, the hiss of a waterfall, the deep, resonant boom of a filled cistern.

The "plink, plink, trickle" is not just random condensation. It is their language.

When two Hydro-Statics meet—one gliding down a staircase, another oozing up a vertical face—they press their membranes together. They exchange chemical data: the salinity of the fog, the structural integrity of a distant ziggurat, the location of a blockage. They pulse with information, their translucent bodies briefly flashing with internal bioluminescence, a pale, cold blue that is invisible in the harsh daylight but vivid in the deep channels.

They are also the architects of the water's movement. While the stone provides the path, the Hydro-Statics provide the *will*. You may see a stream of water splitting on a rock, but look closer. The water is being *guided*. Tiny, spider-thin extrusions of the colony stretch out into the air, manipulating the surface tension, herding the droplets into the gutters, ensuring not a single molecule is wasted. They farm the fog, dragging the heavy moisture from the air and dragging it, molecule by molecule, toward the void.

They are solitary, yet omnipresent. There are no "nests." The entire city is the nest. The damp sheen on the floor is a sleeping layer of them. The fog rolling over the plateau is filled with microscopic, airborne spores of their kind, waiting to precipitate onto a dry surface and begin the work of wetting it.

To walk here is to walk through a crowd. Every time you brush against a wet wall, you touch them. Every time you slip on a slick patch of floor, you have stepped on one. They do not resent this. They simply reconstitute around the intrusion, flowing over the boot of the traveler like a cold, heavy glove, tasting the leather, analyzing the foreign biology, and then dismissively sliding away to resume their eternal task.

They are the ghosts of maintenance, the silent, wet engine that keeps the grey city from crumbling into dust. They are the reason the air tastes of wet dust; they scrub the air as voraciously as they scrub the stone.

In this absolute light, under the weight of the water, they are the only thing that endures—mindless, blind, and tireless, waiting for the builders to return, keeping the floors washed and the gutters clear, preserved in an endless, hydraulic stasis.
