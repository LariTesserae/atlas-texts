They are a people carved from the same quiet as the mist.

Given the architecture of their world—primitive stone and timber, sprawling and low—their physical stature reflects the constraints of the archipelago. They are not a tall people. Generations of navigating the fissures and the low-slung ceilings of the longhouses have produced a compact, dense physiology. Their limbs are sturdy, knotted with muscle that comes not from the lifting of heavy burdens, but from the constant, demanding negotiation of verticality. To live here is to climb; to move from the sleeping quarters to the communal plazas is to traverse ropes and ladders slick with condensation. Their hands are their most distinct feature: broad, spatulate fingers with textured pads that grip the wet obsidian with the ease of a lizard on a wall. There is no slipping for them; their understanding of friction is intuitive, honed before they can walk.

Their skin is the color of the wet basalt—a dusky, cool grey-brown that seems to absorb the dim light rather than reflect it. It is perpetually sheened with moisture, but they do not shiver. The heat of the stone has seeped into their blood; their body temperature runs high, a metabolic furnace that allows them to exist comfortably in the humid air without the need for heavy furs. They wear simple garments woven from the fibrous interiors of the giant leathery leaves, loose and breathable fabrics that hang heavy with dampness. The dyes are extracted from the deep greens of the moss, so that when they crouch motionless among the ferns, they disappear entirely, becoming nothing but another lumpy formation of stone and vegetation.

But it is their demeanor that truly marks them as children of the vertical silence. They have moved past the need for urgency. In a land where there are no predators, where food grows in abundance on the damp cliffs, and where the weather never changes, survival is not a daily calculation. It is a given. Consequently, the frantic energy of lower altitudes is absent here. They move with a glacial deliberation.

To watch one of them cross a plaza of damp cobblestone is to watch a study in stillness. They do not stride; they place each foot with precision, testing the slick surface before committing their weight. They stop frequently. They are a people who look—at the way a droplet of water gathers on the tip of a fern frond, growing heavy and pregnant before detaching into the void; at the shifting veils of mist that reveal and conceal the jagged spires. Their eyes are large and dark, adapted to the low, consistent light, possessing a gaze that feels bottomless. They do not look *at* you so much as they look *through* you, as if you are merely another formation of rock passing through their field of vision.

Communication is conducted in a frequency barely above a whisper. The architecture of their settlement, with its wide, empty plazas and huddled neighborhoods, suggests a society that values distance and privacy, yet when they meet, they do not retreat. They simply coexist. They speak a language composed of soft vowels and clicks, designed to mimic the natural acoustics of their world—the *plink* of falling water, the rustle of moss. To shout is a sacrilege; it disrupts the equilibrium of the mist. If a disagreement arises, it is settled with a slowness that makes anger seem like a tremendous waste of kinetic energy. A raised voice is the ultimate taboo, a crack in the perfect glass of their existence.

Their days are defined by maintenance rather than conquest. Since the stone is always "weeping," erosion is a constant threat, albeit a slow one. They spend their hours channeling water, carving intricate runnels into the rock face to guide the silver rivulets away from their foundations and into the basins below. They are master masons, but they do not build against the mountain; they build with it. The mortar they use is a mixture of crushed volcanic dust and fibrous moss, creating a bond that flexes with the subtle shifts of the newborn geology.

They are also collectors of the pause. You will often find them sitting on the edge of the polished walkways, legs dangling over the abyss, staring out at the sea of jagged peaks. They do not meditate in the active sense; they simply *are*. They are absorbing the silence, letting the heavy, humid air fill their lungs, syncing their breathing with the slow, rhythmic weeping of the stone.

In the evenings, as the pale dome of the sky darkens to a bruised purple but does not truly become night, they gather. The meeting halls, vast and open-air, fill with a damp silence. There is no dancing, no drumming, no singing. Instead, they sit in circles and listen. They listen to the wind moving through the spires, a mournful, lonely tone. They listen to the distant rush of water falling from the cliffs. It is a communal listening, a shared appreciation of the fact that for this moment, and the next, and the one after that, they are suspended in a world that requires nothing of them but to witness it.

They are a people who have learned that in the absence of predators and time, the only thing left to do is be careful, be quiet, and watch the water fall. They are the keepers of the pause, living their long, humid lives with the grace of moss growing on a rock—slow, relentless, and entirely at peace.
