They are not quiet because they are shy; they are quiet because silence is the only luxury they can afford. In a place where the roar is a solid state, to make a sound is to add violence to a war already won. So, the people of the Drip-Foundry—the **Soaked**—do not speak. They have forgotten how.

At first glance, you might mistake them for protrusions of the rock itself. They are a people carved from the same aesthetic as their world: pale, slick, and gaunt. Their skin is not the soft, porous skin of surface-dwellers, but a dense, seamless membrane, translucent as the shelf fungi they cultivate. Through the flesh of their arms and backs, you can see the slow, dark pulse of blood, which looks remarkably like the black water churning below them. They have evolved to be buoyant in the heavy air, their bones light and porous, honeycombed like the water-logged timber of their homes.

They are draped in layers of loose, rot-resistant fabric made from processed fungal mats, hanging off their thin frames in wet, heavy rags. This clothing serves no purpose for warmth—the climate is static—but it acts as a sponge, trapping the moisture against their bodies to prevent them from drying out, a condition they regard with a superstitious horror. To be dry is to be dead.

Their faces are the most haunting aspect. Centuries of living in the dim, phosphorescent gloom have rendered their eyes functionally obsolete. They are not blind, exactly, but their eyes are small, pinched things, hidden behind thick lenses of translucent skin that constantly weep a protective oily fluid. They do not "look" in the human sense. Instead, they possess a sensory array along their hairless forearms and the soles of their feet—lateral line canals similar to those of deep-sea fish, exquisitely sensitive to pressure and vibration.

They "see" the layout of their vertical city by feeling the hum of the wooden beams and the compression of the air currents. They know you are coming not because they see the light of your torch, but because they feel the disturbance your footsteps make in the "solid state" of the roar.

Communication is a tactile ballet. To converse is to press one’s forehead against the cheek of another, transmitting sub-vocal frequencies that travel through the bone, bypassing the ear entirely. A touch on the shoulder might mean "danger"; a grip on the wrist might mean "the wheel is stuck." Lovers embrace for hours, locked in a silent communion of hums and tremors, sharing thoughts that the roaring water cannot steal. It is an intimacy born of necessity; to be alone in the Drip-Foundry is to cease to exist.

Their society is defined by the **Rhythm of the Lift**. They are the tenders of the meaningless machines.

You will see them clinging to the wet timber of the colossal water-wheels, their bodies wrapped around the spokes like limpets. As the wheel turns—lifted by the endless cascade of black water—they move with it. They are not turning the wheel; the wheel turns them. Their job is maintenance without purpose. They scrape the mineral buildup from the gears with bone chisels. They tighten rawhide lashings that have already been tightened for a thousand years. They grease the axles with a paste made of crushed salamanders and limestone dust.

They do this because the alternative is the Stagnation. In their mythology, the water is the heart of the world, and the city is the ribcage that holds it. If the wheels stop, the heart stops, and the world falls into the abyss. They do not know they are sustaining a ghost. They believe they are sustaining the universe.

Their diet is as monotonous as their labor. They are fungivores. They farm the translucent, jelly-like shelf fungi in the darker corners of their honeycomb huts, harvesting the slime that oozes from the undersides. It tastes of iron and dust. They drink the water, of course, filtering it through the calcified shells of giant river-snails that hang in net bags from the eaves. The water is heavy with minerals, and it is this mineral intake that initiates the final stage of their life cycle.

This is the secret of the Drip-Foundry’s architecture, the reason the wood turns to stone and the city feels ancient beyond measure.

The Soaked do not die in the way surface creatures do. They do not rot and return to the earth. They calcify.

As a member of the Soaked reaches old age—perhaps sixty or seventy years—their internal filtration systems begin to fail. The minerals they consume are no longer excreted; they are deposited in the tissues. The skin hardens, turning from translucent jelly to a grey, gritty crust. The joints lock up. The movement slows.

When a Soaked one feels the stiffness of the End-Taking coming upon them, they do not fear. They travel to the lower scaffolding, the Oldest Levels, where the construction is densest and the mist is thickest. There, they find a gap in the wall, a space in the "brutish" architecture that needs filling.

They sit. They assume a posture of support—arms raised to hold a beam, back braced against a strut, legs curled to anchor a walkway. And then they wait.

The process takes weeks. The minerals crystallize. The flesh fuses with the wet wood. The blood hardens into stone.

Eventually, the person is gone. In their place stands a statue, a new pillar of the city, perfectly integrated into the structure. The wood of the shanty town is petrifying, yes, but it is petrifying *around* them. The city is not just built of wood; it is built of ancestors.

If you were to shine a light into the dark recesses of the vertical shanty, you would see them: hundreds of stone figures, locked in eternal labor, their faces frozen in expressions of serene duty. They hold up the floors. They brace the bridges. They are the mortar between the logs.

The salamanders, those pale eyeless things, sometimes scuttle over these stone figures, licking the mineral dust from their hardened cheeks. The statue does not twitch. It has become part of the wall. It has become the silence within the roar.

This is who lives here. A people who do not speak, who see with their skin, who tend to machines that do nothing, and who eventually build their own tombs into the foundations of a city that exists only to keep the water company. They are the damp in the wood. They are the vibration in the bone. They are the pause in the eternal fall.
