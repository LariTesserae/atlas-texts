To live in the High Drown is to exist in a state of permanent negotiation with the suspension. The beings here are not merely inhabitants of a place; they are appendages of the Spines, extensions of the vertical basalt towers that stab upward into the gloom. They are a people carved from the same scarcity and silence as the Needle-City itself—elongated, deliberate, and profoundly adapted to the heavy, green-gold lung of the world.

They are, for lack of a better term, the **Spined**.

Physically, the Spined are reflections of the jagged geometry that surrounds them. Generations of climbing the sheer, glass-sharp basalt have eroded the bulky curvature of the human form, leaving them tall and ionic. Their limbs are attenuated, possessing a double-jointed, fluid grace that allows them to bridge the gaps between the swaying walkways with minimal effort. In this gravity-gentled buoyancy, they do not so much walk as swim through the air. Their hands and feet are broad and spatulate, the pads roughened like sharkskin to grip the slick, oily surfaces of the rock. When they cling to the vertical faces of the towers, they merge with the shadows, their silhouettes indistinguishable from the protrusions of the stone.

Their skin is a map of the lightless deep. It is pale, bordering on translucent, lacking the melanin to protect against a sun that cannot penetrate the miles of water above. Instead, their protection is communication. Embedded in their dermis are colonies of bioluminescent bacteria that they can trigger at will. In the dim light (1.1), a conversation is a stuttering of cold blues and soft greens across the chest and throat. A flash of anxiety, a steady pulse of greeting, a slow, dimming rhythm of grief—they wear their emotions on the outside, painting the heavy air with light. This visual language is necessary because the air itself refuses to carry the weight of sound. To shout here is to feel the water in one’s lungs dampen the noise before it leaves the throat. They speak in hums and clicks, intimate vibrations meant to be felt against a collarbone rather than heard across a room.

The Spined are exclusively urban, but their city is alive in a way that defies the static nature of stone. Their homes are not built; they are excavated. Using tools made from the sharpened carapaces of the local crustaceans and the dense leg-bones of deep-swimmers, they chip away at the basalt. It is a Sisyphean existence, for the **weirdness** of this place dictates that the rock does not stay dead. The Spined are in a constant state of war with the geology. They carve a niche for a sleeping platform, and by the morning, the wound has begun to weep, sealing itself with a lattice of mineral crystals. Consequently, their architecture is jagged and temporary, a chaotic mess of scaffolding that is always being reinforced, always being chiseled back. Their most prized possessions are the wedges of hard, fossilized wood they hammer into the cracks to keep the mountain from swallowing their homes whole.

Life here is dictated by the rhythm of the **slow drift**. The Spined do not rush; to rush is to disturb the delicate balance of the buoyancy that keeps them aloft. They are a people of the "long wait." Their primary sustenance comes from the algae gardens that float in the woven baskets suspended far below the city, trailing into the abyss. Tending to these gardens requires lowering oneself on lines of water-weed rope, dropping hundreds of feet into the darker, colder currents to prune the slime and harvest the protein-rich paste. It is a solitary task, and the Spined return from it changed—quieter, eyes dilated, skin pale from the deeper dark.

They share the vertical sprawl with the **Clingers**, a species of crustacean that has evolved to mimic the architecture of the city. These creatures have shells that look exactly like the water-worn timber of the walkways, allowing them to sit motionless for weeks. They are the scavengers, the sanitation crew of the Needle-City, scuttling along the rafters to consume the organic detritus that floats upward from the depths. The Spined do not domesticate them, but they do not kill them either; they simply move carefully around them, treating the Clingers as mobile furniture.

Beneath the lowest platforms, where the artificial light of the bioluminescence fades into the tea-colored murk, live the **Glint-Eels**. These are small, translucent predators that hunt in the water lanes between buildings. They are attracted to the faint light of the Spined, and so the people wear cloaks woven from thick, oily kelp fronds to mask their glow when they travel between towers. But the true masters of this domain are rarely seen. They are felt.

The **Deep-Drifters**, the leviathans of the High Drown, are the silent gods of this ecology. To the Spined, they are not merely animals; they are moving weather systems. When a Deep-Drifter passes, the air pressure drops, and the entire city groans as the timber contracts. The Spined stop moving. They cease their bioluminescent displays entirely, darkening the city to match the gloom, becoming invisible statues of grey flesh. They hang suspended in their rope hammocks or cling to the rock faces, holding their breath alongside the planet, waiting for the massive displacement of current to subside. They believe the Drifters are the spirits of the mountains, finally freed from the stone, wandering the depths in search of the surface they will never reach.

In the end, the Spined are a civilization of survivors hanging by a thread. They are a people who have traded the ground for the grip, the horizon for the vertical drop. They are defined by the **oily sheen** on their skin, the salt crystals in their hair, and the constant, rhythmic creaking of the world that holds them. They sleep in the embrace of a healing wound, dreaming of dry land, while the green light filters down through the miles of water, turning their quiet, dreaming aquarium into a tomb that refuses to close.
