The life here begins in the breath, a cloud of biomass so dense it functions as a weather system. Before you see distinct creatures, you encounter the aerial plankton. Millions of gossamer-winged flies, no larger than a grain of rice, drift in shimmering curtains that catch the dim, gray light like suspended dust. They are the spark of the food chain, living mayflies that hatch, breed, and die within the span of a single afternoon. Their constant, falling "snow" of corpses carpets the wet ground, providing the rich, sweet smell of rotting fruit that permeates the air. This living fog is harvested by the first tier of residents: the drifters. These are translucent, balloon-like spiders the size of volleyballs that float on silken tethers anchored to the high vines, filtering the air through comb-like mandibles, endlessly trawling the humidity.

Moving lower, the "walls" of this city—the vertical faces of the great resin towers—are alive with a crawling, armor-plated infantry. These are the Mason Beetles. Imagine a Volkswagen Beetle encased in chitin the color of slate and obsidian. They cling to the smooth, eroded resin with hooked tarsi that make a sound like tearing velcro as they move. They are the scavengers and the recyclers of this metropolis. Their massive, serrated mandibles are designed not to kill, but to shave. They graze on the towers themselves, shaving away layers of ancient, softened mud and resin to get at the fungal mats that grow within the pores of the buildings. As they crawl, they leave behind trails of glistening, bioluminescent slime, creating a network of glowing neon arteries that pulse faintly in the twilight.

Hidden within the deep crevices of the calcified honeycomb ground are the architects. They are rarely seen, existing in the dark, subterranean interstices of the mega-structure, but their presence is felt. The workers are pale, eyeless things. They stand waist-high to a human, soft-bodied and segmented like a grub but possessing six powerful, spindly limbs that can punch through hardened ceramic. They communicate through high-pitched chirps and rhythmic antenna tapping that adds to the constant hum of the place. They are the masons, constantly repairing the calcified pavement with a mixture of regurgitated limestone and saliva. They move in frantic, distracted rivers, oblivious to anything that is not a stone or a fellow worker, carrying pellets of wet cement on their backs like a burden of guilt.

Then there are the shepherds. Because the air is thick with the swarming "gnats," larger creatures have evolved to herd them. The Sky-Skimmers are sleek, leathery fliers resembling a cross between a manta ray and a bat, with a wingspan of fifteen feet. They do not flap frantically; they glide on the thermal currents rising from the warm, rotting earth. Their mouths are gaping, scoop-shaped vents. They fly through the clouds of insects, filtering thousands in a single pass, their bodies rippling with the effort of swallowing. Their eyes are wide and black, reflecting the gray clouds, and they maneuver between the towering spires with a grace that belies their size, silent ghosts in the noise.

But for all the soaring giants, the true terror—and the true apex of this ecosystem—is found clinging to the heavy, ropelike vines that dangle from the unseen canopy above. These are the Weaver-Lizards. They are massive, arboreal predators, perhaps twenty feet from nose to tail, covered in scales that mimic the texture of the mossy vines perfectly—a mottled pattern of deep emerald and charcoal. They hang motionless for hours, their claws locked into the bark, indistinguishable from the vegetation they hunt upon. When they strike, it is not with speed, but with explosive power. They launch themselves into the air, extending a membrane of skin that allows them to glide between towers, snatching a Sky-Skimmer or a unwary Mason Beetle mid-flight. Their roar is a low, guttural bellow that vibrates through the soles of your feet, cutting through the insectoid hum like a knife.

Beneath the canopy, in the "plaza" itself, are the roamers. These are six-legged, boar-like creatures with shells like tortoises and snouts like shovels. They root through the shallow puddles of the honeycomb ground, sucking up the sludge of dead insects, algae, and decaying moss. They are heavy, their footsteps shaking the ground—a heavy *thump-thump-thump* that rattles the teeth. They are solitary, bad-tempered, and seemingly invulnerable, their shells calcified so thickly that not even the Weaver-Lizards can crack them. They are the ghosts of the ruin, trudging endlessly through the gloom, eating the muck of the city, indifferent to the grandeur that surrounds them.

And everywhere, everywhere, are the ants. Not the ants of a kitchen floor, but the engineers of this scale. Tiny, red, and numerous beyond counting. They flow like water over everything. They farm aphids on the sides of the Mason Beetles. They clean the teeth of the rooting boar-things. They strip the carcass of a fallen Sky-Skimmer to the bone in hours. They are the nervous system of this world, the connective tissue that binds the massive towers and the giant beasts together into a single, writhing, humming super-organism.

To stand here is to be a speck of dust in a hurricane of flesh. You are surrounded by millions of eyes that do not see you, millions of minds that do not comprehend you, driven by hungers that are older and larger than anything human.
