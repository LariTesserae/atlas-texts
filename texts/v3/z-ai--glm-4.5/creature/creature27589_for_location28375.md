The silence of nature here is not merely a void; it is a lie. To say the fauna is non-existent is to misunderstand the definition of life in a place that has abandoned the surface. What lives here does not scramble or chirp; it does not possess the frantic energy of the sunlit world. What lives here is slow, cold, and woven into the very vibration of the roar.

If you were to stand still for hours—until the damp cold settled deep into your own marrow—you would begin to notice discrepancies in the texture of the root-mass. You would see shapes that resolve themselves from the pallid, anaemic wood, distinguishing themselves from the knotted landscape only by the faintest of movements.

These are the **Resonants**.

They are solitary, towering things, standing upright on two thick, pillar-like legs that are evolutions of the very roots they walk upon. They stand easily three meters tall, their bodies elongated and gaunt, wrapped in thick, leathery slabs of skin that resemble the peeling iron of the pump-houses. They are the colour of the gloom—a mottled, bruised grey-brown that renders them invisible against the lignin walls when they remain still.

They have no eyes. In this brown, dim world, sight is a useless sense. Instead, their faces are dominated by a complex, exposed osseous structure—a radial, bony fan that stretches from where ears would be, wrapping around the back of their elongated skulls. This is their auditory sail, a membrane of bone and cartilage designed to catch the deafening roar of the machines and translate the violent noise into a navigational map. They do not hear the sound as we do; they feel the pressure waves as texture and contour. The thrum of the pistons tells them where the solid ground lies; the hum of the electricity guides them to the warmth of the pylons.

Their mouths are small, sealed tight except when feeding, hidden behind a mat of coarse, hair-like fibers that filter the freezing air before it enters their lungs. They breathe in time with the machines—a massive, rattling inhalation when the pistons fire, a slow, whistling exhalation during the reset. To sync their metabolism with the machines is to survive; to fall out of rhythm is to suffocate in the noise.

The Resonants are scavengers of the industrial bleed. They do not eat the wood; the ancient roots are too tough, too saturated with centuries of accumulated toxins. Instead, they feed on the byproducts of the parasitic machines.

You would find them clustered around the bases of the massive pump-houses, pressing their long, spindly fingers against the hot, vibrating iron. Their hands are hooked, resembling the tools of a mechanic, tipped with claws that can scrape away the accumulated sludge of carbon and oil that cakes the machinery. They scrape this black paste from the pipes and consume it with slow, meditative deliberation. It is a diet of heavy metals and hydrocarbons, lethal to anything from the surface, but to them, it is the only source of heat in the freezing dark. Their blood must be thick, antifreeze-like, carrying a dull, toxic glow through their veins.

 Occasionally, one might be seen navigating the slick wooden plains, moving with a horrifying, fluid grace. Despite their size, they do not stumble on the black ice. Their feet are broad and splayed, equipped with sensitive pads that detect the microscopic texture of the root-floor through the slime. They move only when the heavy-clanking stomp of the pistons reaches its peak, using the thunder of the machine to mask the sound of their own footfalls. In this way, they utilize the roar as a cloak of invisibility.

There is no social structure, no families. The distance between pump-stations is too great, the energy expenditure of travel too high. They are islands of life in a sea of cold mechanics. The only time they interact is during the rare moments of mechanical failure. If a pipe bursts or a piston stalls, the change in the acoustic signature—a sudden silence or a discordant screech—draws them from miles around. They gather not to help, but to witness. They stand in a circle around the wounded machine, their bony auditory sails tilted toward the sound, vibrating in sympathy with the distress. It is the closest thing they have to a ceremony, a silent vigil to the beating heart of their subterranean world.

In the hollows between the roots, where the black water pools, there lives something smaller, a parasite to the Resonants. They are the **Mute-Vermin**—pale, multi-limbed crustaceans that skitter like albino spiders across the surface of the mirrors. They are blind and eyeless, relying on a keen sense of smell to locate the waste left behind by the giants. They burrow into the insulation of the pipes, creating warm nests that hum with the vibration of the flowing water. They are the only thing that moves quickly here, darting across the slime in frantic dashes, living their entire short lives in the span of a single piston’s cycle.

And then there is the mystery of the **Warm-Spots**.

Sometimes, near the ventilation stacks that chug white steam into the freezing air, you might find patches of the root-mass that are strangely soft, yielding like rotting fruit to the touch. If you were to dig into the pulpy wood, you would find a network of thick, moist vessels buried deep within the lignin, pulsing with a slow, heavy rhythm. These are not the roots of the ancient tree, but the incubators of the Resonants. They do not lay eggs; they implant their genetic material into the living wood of the floor, splicing their DNA with the dying biology of the planet. The young grow slowly, fed by the nutrients leached from the wood and the heat of the pipes below, gestating for years until they are large enough to pull themselves free of the bark.

When a young one finally emerges, it is wet and shivering, its auditory sail soft and folded. It does not cry out. It simply presses its ear against the vibrating flank of the nearest machine, learning the song of the world, syncing its heart to the thrum of the iron, accepting the roar as the only truth it will ever know.
