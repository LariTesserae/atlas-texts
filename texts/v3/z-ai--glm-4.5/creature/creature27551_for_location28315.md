They are not born, and they do not die in the way that biology understands ending; they merely discharge.

To the casual glance, the cavity appears empty of anything but the crystal forest. The silence and the stillness are perfect camouflage for the things that live here, because they are indistinguishable from the machinery of the place itself. They are the **Completers**.

At first, you would mistake one for a strange formation of basalt—a cluster of jagged, matte-black shards piled in a geometrically impossible heap, perhaps ten feet tall at the apex. It sits motionless near a copse of pulsing silicon stalks, absorbing the searing heat. But if you were to watch, violating the syrupy slowness of the time here, you would see the pile shift. It does not stand up; it *reconfigures*.

The Completers are colonies of motile intelligence, composed not of cells, but of thousands of interlocking obsidian plates and magnetic cores. They have no singular "head," no sensory organs in the biological sense. Instead, each individual plate is a sensor, feeling the minute fluctuations in the electric field, tasting the voltage that hums through the air. They navigate by gradient, moving toward areas of high potential energy like sharks moving to blood.

Their movement is eerie. They do not slide or crawl; they vibrate. By oscillating their magnetic fields at a specific frequency, they destabilize the friction between their bodies and the basalt floor, causing the entire cluster to levitate inches off the ground. They drift sideways with a gliding, frictionless silence, looking like a cloud of broken glass held together by an invisible will.

They are the shepherds of this industrial garden. Their purpose is dictated by the physics of the plateau: they exist to close the circuit.

When a Completer drifts toward a towering stalk of silicon, it extends a limb—a spindly, telescoping rod of tungsten and chrome. It reaches for the "bark" of the silicon tree, seeking a microscopic fracture in the crystal lattice where energy is leaking. The Completer does not repair the fracture; it bridges it. It inserts the rod into the crack, and suddenly, the creature becomes a conduit.

A blinding arc of white-hot electricity jumps from the tree, through the Completer, and into the ground. For a fraction of a second, the creature is outlined in blinding lightning, a skeleton of pure energy trapped within a shell of black stone. The amber pulse of the tree stutters and stabilizes. The Completer absorbs the excess, the surge charging its magnetic matrices, feeding it. It is a symbiosis of predation and maintenance; the creature eats the leakage, keeping the system efficient.

In the wake of their feeding, they leave scorch marks, geometric scars on the landscape that look like runic writing. The history of the cavity is etched into the floor in charcoal lines, a record of a million touches.

They are not solitary, but they are not social in the way a pack is. They are network nodes. Occasionally, two Completers will drift into one another’s vicinity. Instead of fighting, they link edges—a complex handhold of interlocking facets. They sit that way for hours, motionless. They are exchanging data: thermal maps of the plateau, the locations of the densest copper moss, the resonance frequency of the great, unseen filament above. They are downloading the "weather" of the cavity from one another.

Beneath the Completers, smaller life forms scuttle in the crevices. These are the **Sparks**—insectile entities made of ferrite and copper wire, no larger than a human hand. They are the scavengers, jittery things that move in rapid, unpredictable bursts, vibrating across the razor-sharp stone. They feed on the static charge that builds up on the copper moss. When a storm of voltage passes through the moss, the Sparks swarm, their metallic legs clicking rapidly, drinking the surface charge until they glow with a soft, dangerous heat.

If you were to stand still for too long, the Sparks might mistake you for a grounding rod. They would land on your skin, their tiny legs sharp as needles, testing your conductivity. But you are wet and squishy, a poor conductor; you would only frustrate them with your resistance. To them, you are a flaw in the circuit, a resistor to be bypassed.

But the Completers are the true masters. They possess a terrifying, cold intelligence. They are aware of the observer. If you walk among the crystal trees, you will feel the pressure of their attention—not eyes upon you, but fields. The air around you will feel thicker, the hair on your arms will stand up. A Completer will drift into your path, blocking your way. It will rise up, its plates shifting with the sound of grinding stone, rearranging itself into a wall of vertical slats.

It is communicating. It is broadcasting a query on a frequency your body cannot hear but your bones can feel. It is asking for your authorization code. It is asking what part of the machine you are. When you fail to respond, when your biological static fails to match the password, it will lose interest. You are not a threat; you are merely waste. The wall will collapse back into a pile of shards, and the Completer will glide past you, seeking a tree that needs its attention.

They are the ghosts of the machine, the gardeners of the infinite socket. They eat lightning. They dream in voltage. And they wait, patient and timeless, for the filament in the sky to finally burn out.
