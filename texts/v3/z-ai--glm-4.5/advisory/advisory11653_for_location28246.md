This is a guide for the ordinary multiverse traveler preparing to enter the **Drowned Greenhouse**.

Visiting this location is not like visiting a beach or a swamp. It is an intrusion into a sterile, mechanical womb. The environment is defined by a terrifying trinity of traits: absolute submersion, suffocating bio-industrial density, and a profound, aggressive silence. You are not entering a habitat; you are entering a paused clock.

Here is how you must prepare.

### 1. Respiratory Systems: The Silence and the Taste
The first and most immediate threat is the medium itself. The air here is water—warm, heavy, and mineral-rich.

*   **Closed-Circuit Rebreather (CCR):** Do not use standard open-circuit SCUBA gear. The silence of this place is a physical weight; the roar of exhaust bubbles will shatter the atmosphere, disorient you, and echo strangely off the glass and metal ceilings. You need a closed-circuit system that recycles your breath, keeping you acoustically invisible. Furthermore, the water tastes of copper and ozone; you do not want to be coughing out dense clouds of bubbles if you inhale a sudden pocket of concentrated mineral tang.
*   **Full-Face Mask with Comms:** The water dampens sound, turning the world into a muffled dream. Without a hard-wire comms system in your mask, you will be psychologically isolated. Even if you are traveling alone, the ability to hear your own breathing amplified slightly, or to record notes for your own sanity, is vital.
*   **Dive Computer with Altitude Algo:** You are suspended "perhaps a mile" above a floor. The pressure differential might be strange, especially if the "tepid" nature of the water implies a thermal or chemical anomaly. You need a computer that can track complex pressure changes, though in a state of neutral buoyancy, your depth gauge may feel less like a tool and more like a countdown timer to the crushing dark below.

### 2. Physical Protection: The Cruelty of New Metal
The description notes that the metal is fresh, lacking the patina of age. The edges are sharp, the corners cruel.

*   **Industrial-Strength Drysuit:** The water is warm, but thermal protection is not the primary goal. You need armor. A heavy-duty canvas or Kevlar-reinforced drysuit is essential. If you brush against a rusted piston or a jagged steel girder in neoprene, you will be cut to the bone. The suit must be rated for abrasion and puncture.
*   **Cut-Resistant Gloves:** You will need to move through this environment, likely using the machinery to pull yourself along. You cannot avoid touching the walls. Your gloves must be heavy enough to grip slimy, wet kelp without slipping, but tough enough to withstand a brush with a riveted iron beam.
*   **Helmet:** A hood is not enough. A hard-shell diving helmet is required. In the event of a sudden current or a tumble from your platform, you will be surrounded by heavy metal pipes and glass panes. Head protection is non-negotiable.

### 3. Mobility and Entanglement: The Green Stranglehold
The vegetation here does not just grow; it strangles. It is packed into every cubic inch. Traditional swimming is impossible here.

*   **Leave the Fins Behind:** Fins are liabilities in dense kelp forests. They will snag on vines, wrap around conveyor belts, and tangle in the "electrical wiring" of root systems. You must rely on surface movement.
*   **Grappling Hooks and Magnetic Anchors:** You are in a state of neutral buoyancy. You are not swimming; you are flying. You need tools to pull yourself through the vegetation without touching it more than necessary. A compact magnetic grapnel will allow you to anchor yourself to the pristine steel beams above, letting you haul your body vertically through the "cathedral" without getting mired in the moss.
*   **Trauma Shears on Your Chest:** You must mount industrial-grade trauma shears directly to the front of your harness. If you become entangled in the "aggressive" vines—and you will—you will need to cut yourself free instantly. The plants are described as rubbery cables; standard dive knives might saw ineffectually. You need leverage and sharp blades.

### 4. Sensory Management: The Vertigo of the Artificial
The verticality and the lighting pose a unique psychological hazard. You are suspended in a column of light above infinite darkness.

*   **Manage Your Caustics:** The light filters down in "dancing caustics." While beautiful, these moving patterns can induce severe vertigo and nausea, especially when combined with the feeling of neutral buoyancy (which removes the sensation of "up" and "down"). Focus on the solid geometry—the metal grates, the rivets—to ground yourself.
*   **Narrow-Beam Lighting:** Do not use a wide-angle dive light. It will illuminate too much of the vast, empty darkness above and below, triggering existential dread. Use a narrow, focused beam to pick out your immediate path. You want to see the next handhold, not the mile-drop into the abyss.
*   **Tethering:** If you intend to explore away from the main platform, you must use a safety line. The visual references here are alien; without a tether, it is dangerously easy to drift "down" into the dark, thinking you are moving forward, until the light fades and you are lost in the gloom.

### 5. Psychological Preparation: The Waiting Room
This is the most difficult part of the prep. You are entering a "sterile ecosystem." There are no fish, no birds, no animals. It is a kingdom of flora without fauna.

*   **Expect the "Uncanny Valley":** Your brain is wired to expect life in a forest. The silence here—no chirping, no scuttling, just the hiss of bubbles—will trigger primal alarm bells. You will feel like you are in a place where you do not belong. Accept this feeling before you enter. Do not try to "normalize" the silence. Let it be heavy.
*   **The Watcher Effect:** The text suggests the builders may have just left, or are still watching. This is not a ruin; it is a *paused* environment. The machinery is motionless, not broken. You must prepare yourself for the possibility that the "cycle" might restart. If the flywheels begin to turn while you are tangled in the vines, you must be ready to abort immediately. Do not linger near the large pressure valves or conveyor belts.
*   **Combatting the Claustraphobia:** Despite the high ceiling, the density of the vegetation makes the space feel intimate and closing-in. The water is "heavy," adding to the sensation of being held. Practice breathing exercises. The rebreather helps here—slow, rhythmic breathing will keep your heart rate down and prevent panic from setting in.

### Summary Protocol
When you transit through the portal, you will likely arrive suspended in the warm, copper-tasting water.

1.  **Check your tether.** Confirm you are anchored to something solid.
2.  **Engage your rebreather.** Listen to the silence return.
3.  **Turn on your narrow beam.** Locate the nearest metal strut.
4.  **Do not touch the plants unless necessary.** They are the masters here; you are just a tourist in their machine.
5.  **Look up.** Remind yourself where the light is. Then, move slowly.

Traveler, this place is a memory of the future. Respect the machinery, fear the silence, and do not drift down.
