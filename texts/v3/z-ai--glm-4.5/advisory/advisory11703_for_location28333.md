**Preparation Brief: The Hydrostatic Hold**

**Classification:** Environment Type-IV (Static Liquid / Non-Newtonian Pocket)
**Threat Level:** Moderate (Psychological) to High (Environmental Stagnation)
**Primary Hazards:** Proprioceptive fluid density, hypothermia, spatial distortion, shadow dissociation.

**Introduction**
You are not going for a swim. You are going for a submersion in a pause button. This location is a physiological paradox: a fluid that acts like a solid, a dark that feels like a weight, and a silence that listens. To survive here, let alone explore it, you must prepare for an environment that resists movement in the same way a heavy dream resists waking. The key to visiting this place is not strength, but flow—moving so slowly that the medium accepts you as part of its stasis.

Below is the standard operating procedure for an ordinary human entering the Hydrostatic Hold.

### I. The Physical Interface: The "Deep-Skin" Suit

Standard diving gear will fail here. The water does not flow; it hangs. You are dealing with a substance that has a "memory." If you kick your legs vigorously, you will create a chaotic turbulence that will not dissipate, potentially trapping you in a vortex of your own making that lasts for hours.

**1. The Exosuit (Enclosed, Smooth-Skinned):**
Do not wear a wetsuit. You need a hard-shell or semi-rigid dry suit with an exterior coating of industrial-grade Teflon or silicone. The surface must be frictionless. The medium here (Water 2.9) is viscous enough to snag on neoprene or textured fabric. Every movement must feel like sliding a knife through cold butter, not churning through liquid.
*   **Why?** To prevent the "liquid scar" effect. If your gear catches, the fluid will resist violently. You need to glide.

**2. Thermal Regulation (Active Heating):**
The ambient temperature is a consistent, damp 10°C (50°F). In air, this is chilly; in this static medium, it is a bone-sucking leech. Because the water does not move, it does not carry heat away quickly via convection, but it conducts it relentlessly.
*   **Requirement:** An undersuit with integrated, battery-powered heating elements, focused on the core and the extremities. You must maintain your body heat aggressively. If you get too cold, your movements will slow further, and you risk becoming just another permanent fixture in the room.

**3. Joint Articulation:**
Ensure your suit allows for full range of motion but provides slight resistance at the knees and elbows. This acts as a physical reminder: *do not snap your limbs.* Move with the viscosity.

### II. Life Support: The Closed Loop

**1. Rebreather Only (No Open Circuit):**
This is non-negotiable. Standard scuba gear releases bubbles. In this place, bubbles do not rise. They hang like suspended pearls. If you breathe out, the bubbles will accumulate around your face, fogging your visor and obscuring the already dim vision.
*   **The Solution:** A fully closed-circuit rebreather. It scrubs the CO2 and recycles the oxygen without releasing gas. You must leave no trace in the water.

**2. The "Silence" of the Mouthpiece:**
Your heartbeat will be the loudest thing you hear. Choose a rebreather with ultra-quiet scrubbers. The sound of your own raspy breathing through a regulator can induce panic in a place where the only other noise is the groaning of the earth.

### III. Sensory Management

**1. Illumination (Low-Intensity, Diffuse):**
The darkness here is absolute (Light 0.2), save for the "diseased" glow of the flora.
*   **Warning:** High-intensity beams will act like shockwaves in the visual spectrum. They will cast harsh, sharp shadows that—given the local weirdness (2.6)—might detach from their objects.
*   **Recommendation:** Use a dim, broad-spectrum red or amber light array, mounted on your chest, not your head. Looking around with a headlamp in a place where angles shift is disorienting; keep your light source near your center of gravity. Use just enough light to see the immediate geography (5 feet), avoiding the temptation to illuminate the far walls, which seem to breathe.

**2. Visual Anchors:**
The geometry of the room shifts based on your heartbeat and focus. You may experience vertigo or nausea as the walls expand and contract.
*   **Tool:** Wear a wrist compass that is *not* magnetic (magnetic fields behave oddly in suspended pockets), but rather gyroscopic. Use it to maintain a fixed heading, regardless of what your eyes tell you.

### IV. Navigation and Movement Protocols

**1. The "Soaring" Gait:**
Do not swim. Do not kick.
*   **Technique:** Move in a slow, continuous glide. Imagine you are moving through honey. Pull yourself gently with your hands. If you sweep your arm, the fluid will remember the shape for hours; try to make that shape smooth.
*   **Pacing:** For every meter of forward progress, allow thirty seconds of travel. Rushing will trigger the "stop-motion" perception of the local fauna, and you may miss the details of the environment.

**2. The Tether (The Lifeline):**
Because the walls are covered in a suffocating layer of parasitic vegetation (Density 2.6) and the geometry is untrustworthy, you must carry a physical tether.
*   **Protocol:** Anchor a line to the entry point (assuming you can find the aperture). Do not rely on visual landmarks to find your way back. The "angles" of the room will try to trick you into thinking the exit is closer or further than it is.

**3. Interaction with Flora:**
Do not touch the walls. The vegetation is packed tight, pulsing, and pale. It looks spongy, but it likely reacts to touch.
*   **Risk:** Disturbing the vegetation might trigger a release of spores or gas into the suspension. With no current to carry them away, you would be instantly blinded.

### V. The Objective: The Central Cube

You will likely be drawn to the center of the room, to the cube of black, dense stone.

**1. Approach:**
As you near the center, you will feel the "intent" of the water increase. It will press against you with a psychological weight, not a physical one. This is the water’s proprioception recognizing a foreign object. Acknowledge it mentally. Do not fight it.

**2. The Altar:**
The cube is worn smooth by something sitting for a long time.
*   **Action:** You may sit. If you choose to sit, be aware that your suit is the only barrier between you and the absolute stillness of this place. The silence will amplify your internal rhythms. Do not stay seated for more than 10 minutes. The risk of "lucid stasis"—falling into a trance where you forget to move because the environment encourages you to stop—is high.

**3. Observation:**
While seated, observe the "eyeless things" in your periphery. They move in stop-motion. Do not try to track them with your eyes; let them come and go. They are curious, likely sensing your heat, but they are part of the suspension. They are not hunters; they are drifters.

### VI. Emergency Extraction

If you feel the geometry shifting aggressively, or if the shadows begin to coalesce into shapes that cause pain or fear:

1.  **Stop moving.** The water reacts to turbulence.
2.  **Switch off your light.** Trust your mind to adjust to the phosphorescence.
3.  **Activate the beacon on your tether.** Do not try to swim to the exit; let your partner (if you have one) reel you in, or follow the line hand-over-hand with agonizing slowness.
4.  **Breathe slowly.** Panic increases your heart rate, which the local physics interprets as a vibration, which may agitate the "shadows."

**Final Note:**
You are entering a room that has forgotten how to flow. When you leave, you may feel a phantom resistance in the air of your own world, a lingering heaviness in your limbs. This is normal. It is the ghost of the hydrostatic hold, a memory of the time you spent inside the pause button of the universe.
