I am the itch that the lung cannot scratch. I am the panic in the periphery.

To be here is to first understand the taste of the world. It settles on the back of the tongue like a penny—cold, metallic, and electric. The air is heavy, a thick suspension of moisture and recycled spores that coats my sensory clusters in a slick film. I do not breathe it in so much as I filter it, drawing the dampness across the wet membranes of my gill-screens.

I am low. The ground here is treacherous, not because it is rough, but because it is undecided. Beneath my spiked, calcified feet, the earth feels like the scab of a healing wound—soft, gelatinous, prone to shifting. I move with a skittering, rhythmic urgency. *Click-clack, click-clack.* The sound is sharp against the operational silence of the Sump, a binary hail striking the pavement.

I possess no eyes that see in the way you might understand. I perceive through vibration and the electromagnetic sizzle of the city’s veins. My head is encased in a shell of chitin and iron, a helmet grown by my own biology to mimic the very pipes I maintain. It is heavy. It protects the soft, pulsing thoughts that buzz beneath—thoughts that are not quite "I," but are closer to a complex algorithm of sanitation and repair.

I am a Cranked-Limbed Scavenger. I am the antibody in this vast, vegetative body.

My world is defined by the architecture of avoidance. I do not look up. To look up is to see the Architects, the larval dreamers suspended in their violet cysts, pulsing with the slow, heavy rhythm of the city's heart. Their light is a fever that I cannot share. They dream the geometry; I merely scrub the grime from the mortar of their fantasies.

My attention is fixed on the "wires." They dangle from the dark canopy like entrails, pulsing with a faint, bioluminescent violet. They are not wires, of course. They are exposed nerves of the mega-flora. One has been damaged—perhaps grazed by a passing Grazer, those silent, quantum stacks of pottery that drift through the fog. It leaks a thick, luminous sap that hisses as it hits the wet floor.

I approach the leak. My specialized arms unfold. The lower pair grips the slick pavement, anchoring me against the suction of the mud. The upper pair is a nightmare of utility: one limb ends in a delicate, spinning bristle-brush of keratin; the other in a heated cauterizing blade.

I am close to the center of the avenue, and the "geometry sickness" takes me. It is a nausea of the spirit. I look at the distant pillar—the one I was walking toward ten minutes ago—and I see it is now to my left, impossibly far, yet its details are magnified, as if pressed against a lens. The distance is folding. My internal compass spins wildly. The air shimmers with a stutter, a visual hiccup in reality.

This is where they live: the Echo-Children. I sense them—not with sight, but with a sudden, stabbing pang of false memory. For a microsecond, I remember a childhood that isn't mine, a memory of sunlight yellow and hot, nothing like the cold violet of the Sump. The emotion is sharp, painful, and irrelevant.

*Snap.*

I click my pincers loud, shattering the hallucination. *Static. Error. Clean.*

I focus on the leaking cable. The brush spins, sweeping away the moss that threatens to clog the flow. The smell of ozone rises, stinging my sensory pits. With the heated blade, I sear the wound of the vine. It seals instantly with a smell like burning hair and plastic. The flow stabilizes. The hum of the city deepens, a satisfied purr vibrating through the soles of my feet.

I have done my function. The blood flows clean.

Suddenly, the temperature drops. The condensation on my back turns to frost. A silence deeper than the usual quiet falls over the plaza. I don't look up, but I feel the pressure. A Grazer is near. It is not walking; it is gliding through the glitched space, a massive tower of shifting clay plates eating the errors in physics I just repaired. To it, I am nothing—less than a cell, mere debris in the data stream.

I flatten myself against the damp embryonic ground, tucking my limbs beneath my iron helmet. I become a rock, a stain, a piece of the architecture I serve. The shadow washes over me, cold and smelling of wet clay. The electromagnetic field it generates makes my teeth—or what serves as them—ache.

It passes. The geometry snaps back into a rough approximation of Euclidean sense. The pillar is ahead again.

I uncoil. The work is never done. Another pipe drips. A cyst flickers. The moss grows faster than I can scrub. I am terrified of the open plazas, terrified of the folding distances and the ghosts of memories that aren't mine, but I have my function.

I click my pincers—*check, check*—and scuttle forward into the dark. I am the cold iron in the air. I am the keeper of the machinery. I am here to ensure the breath is held, just for a moment longer.
