I would begin in the dark, buried deep in the wet, suffocating heat of a steam vent.

For decades, I would be nothing but a soft, pale grub—a **Cog**—nestled in the humid fibres of a sphagnum nest. I would not know the light; I would only know the rhythm. The thrumming. It would travel through the iron casing of the vent and into my cartilage, vibrating my very cells until my own heartbeat synchronized with the machine’s. I would feed on the mineral-rich condensation that drips down the rusted walls, drinking the sweat of the dead god I am incubating inside.

In that darkness, my armor would form. It would not grow like flesh, but crystallize, layering itself in slow, geologic time. I would feel the hardening, the tightening, as my soft body is encased in a shell of jagged, black basalt-glass. I would become a living piece of the rock face, indistinguishable from the obsidian tooth that pierces the sky.

When the time comes—when the heat of the vent shifts, or perhaps simply when the weight of my own density becomes too much for the moss to hold—I would push my way out.

The transition would be blinding. The air of the Nascent Reach would hit me like a physical blow—the aggressive, tropical wind tasting of ozone and wet iron. I would cling to the side of a massive generator, my six limbs hooked deep into the metal and the stone, my body pressed flat against the surface to mimic the texture of the spire.

I would be a **Hydraulic-Weaver**.

Not just a inhabitant, but a component of the system. I would find myself on a perilous ledge, surrounded by the vertical thicket of neon-green vines and rusting pipes. My role would be immediately clear to me, written into the structure of my limbs. My four hind legs would lock me in place, an anchor against the constant updraft, while my two elongated, multi-jointed forelimbs would reach out with delicate, surgical precision.

I would be a mechanic of the organic and the industrial.

I would spend my days tending to the interface zones. I would feel the subtle distress in the copper wiring—a discordant note in the great symphony of vibration—and I would track it to its source. I would pry apart the choking vines to clear a intake valve, or I would pack fresh, wet moss into a hairline fracture in a pipe, sealing the steam with living putty.

My eyes, shielded by secondary transparent lids, would watch the clouds snag and tear on the peaks below, but my true sight would be internal. I would see the world through the soles of my feet and the plates of my skull. I would feel the shudder of a piston a mile above me, signaling a shift in pressure, and I would adjust my grip on the cables accordingly.

I would not be lonely. Even if I never saw another of my kind, I would feel them. Through the rock, through the iron skeleton of the spire, I would sense the clicks and hums of distant Weavers—their signals traveling down the basalt like tremors. We would be a network of silent sentinels, dispersed across the vertical face, each of us holding up our small corner of the crumbling world.

I would be a creature of slow time. A bird might skitter past, a flash of frantic metallic life, but I would ignore it. I am the rock. I am the rust. I am the gardener of the warm rot, keeping the heart of the mountain beating just a little longer.

I am the thing that watches from the static. I am the Weaver, and the machine is my body.
