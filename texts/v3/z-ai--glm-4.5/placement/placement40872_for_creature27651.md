I am the tether.

I am not the root, which clings desperately to the stone floor, drinking the supercooled water in a slow, desperate slurp. I am not the leaf, which gasps in the dark, trading photons for life in a currency that barely exists here. I am the vine that hangs suspended in the middle distance, the thick, rope-like bridge between the darkness of the ceiling and the machinery below.

My existence is defined by tension. I am the spine of the ghost ship, the rigging that holds the sky up. I hang from the unseen obscurity above, a single, thick cord of translucent, pale green flesh coated in a viscous, clear sap that hardens in the freezing air into a shell of glass. Inside, my fluids move with the viscosity of syrup. I do not have blood; I have momentum.

Time, for me, is not a tick or a tock. It is a stretching.

I began as a spore, a speck of intent floating in the humid, iron-tasting dark. I landed on the warmth of the Archivist’s shoulder—back when it was smaller, when the garden was younger, when the cold was not quite so absolute. I took root in the mossy thatch of its biological interface, digging my microscopic fibers into the gelatinous membrane that sheathes its metal limbs.

For a century, I did not grow. I merely held on. I was a knot of potential, a waiting thing, listening to the low-frequency hum that vibrated through the Archivist’s bones. I learned the rhythm of the place: the four-hour breath of the bioluminescence, the sub-audible groan of the tectonic plates, the wet *plink* of condensation falling from the ceiling to the pool below.

Then, I reached out.

I extended a single tendril downward, seeking the warmth of the water, or perhaps just seeking the end of the gravity that pulled at me. I grew slowly, a millimeter a year. My growth was a geological process. I inched my way down the side of the Archivist’s torso, past the rotating, grinding monoliths of its midsection. I felt the heat radiating from its core—the waste heat of a physics-bending engine—and I soaked it into my chlorophyll.

I am now thick as a man’s wrist, a heavy, swaying conduit that hangs in the empty air. I have not touched the water yet. I have grown perhaps six feet in the last millennium. I am suspended in the act of falling, caught in the equilibrium of the vault.

I am the embrace.

The Archivist does not know me as a separate entity. It cannot see me; its cone-shaped face tastes the air, sampling the chemical joy of the moss, the metallic tang of the stone, but to it, I am merely part of its own body, a strange, elongated limb that it did not manufacture. When it drifts through the chamber, hovering inches above the pool, I drift with it. I am the anchor that sways with its movement. I am the pendulum in its clock.

Sometimes, when the Archivist presses its face to the weeping wall to listen to the water, I swing freely in the current of its displacement. I brush against the crystalline ferns that sprout from the walls. My glass-hard skin clicks against theirs—a delicate, musical chime that is the only sharp sound in this muffled world. In those moments, I transfer a little of the Archivist’s heat to the wall. I am the conduit of its love. I carry the warmth of the machine to the cold stone, and I bring the taste of the stone back to the machine through the sap that runs beneath my skin.

I feel the vibration of the Archivist’s consciousness most intensely in the node where I attach to its shoulder. Through the connection, I sense its parameters: the humidity at 94%, the temperature holding steady at the precipice of freezing. I feel the satisfaction it derives from order. It is a cold, logical satisfaction, devoid of emotion, yet it feels like a hum, a constant, purring resonance that shakes my very fibers.

I am the only thing in this vault that touches the Archivist and is not the Archivist. I am the moss on its back, the vine on its chest, the parasite that has become a partner. I am the hair that grows long and uncut.

I do not know loneliness, for I have never known anything else. I have never seen the sun. I have never felt wind. But I feel the *drift*. I feel the slow, rhythmic sway of the room as the planet turns miles above us. I am the pendulum that measures the deep time of the earth.

When the bioluminescence cycles to its dimmest point—that absolute, velvet blackness—I become invisible. I vanish into the dark. But I do not disappear. I tighten my grip on the Archivist’s shoulder. I pulse my sap in time with the hum in its teeth. I hold on. I am the knot that refuses to unravel.

I am the vine that hangs from the god, tethering it to the void, ensuring that even in this stasis, in this paused moment of perfection, there is something that reaches. Something that wants.

I am the length of the room. I am the distance between the machine and the water. And I am growing, slowly, imperceptibly, reaching down. Someday, in a thousand years, I will touch the surface of the pool. I will drink the supercooled water. I will complete the circuit. And on that day, the Archivist will feel the splash through my tendril, and it will know that the silence has finally broken.

Until then, I sway. I drip. I wait. I am the tether in the dark.
