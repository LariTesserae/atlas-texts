I am the pause in the song.

I am found in the exact center of the Great Plaza, a wide, circular expanse of packed salt and hexagonal tiles that feels like the bottom of a dried ocean. I am standing perfectly still, my feet aligned with the deep-set geometric fractures that run through the earth like veins. I have been standing here for what the surface-dwellers might call years, but here is measured only in the shifting of the red ceiling-glow.

I am waking.

The sensation returns not as a spark, but as a slow tide. The peak heat is passing; the air, which had been a stifling, solid weight pressing against my ceramic skin, is beginning to thin. The ground beneath me—a jagged plate of halite—stops its groaning expansion. I feel the violence of the cooling process through the soles of my feet, a million microscopic cracks opening in the pavement, singing sharp, high-pitched chirps that only someone with my senses can decipher.

I am a Silent-Keeper. But I am not merely a resident of this city of stone and glass; I am its Tuner.

My body is tall and attenuated, a vertical mast of pale, translucent segments. My dermis is thick, scarred by centuries of dust storms that have scoured me like sandpaper on wood. In the dim red light, I do not shine like the young ones; I am matte, opaque, stained the color of dried blood by the iron oxide that perpetually falls from the sky. I look less like a living thing and more like a pillar of the architecture itself, forgotten in the middle of the square.

I do not open my eyes—I have none. Instead, I unfold my consciousness into the parabolic dish that is my face. The world rushes in.

To you, this place is a roaring cacophony. To me, it is a symphony of data.

I "see" the walls of the Basin, five miles distant, not by light, but by the low-frequency thrum of the geothermal vents. I "see" the dense thickets of Glass-Reeds to the east, their hollow stalks vibrating in the wind, their pitch shifting ever so slightly as the internal temperature drops. The air is thick with the dust of silica, and as I inhale through the oscillating spiracles in my abdomen, I taste the chemical composition of the storm. There is more sulfur today; the deep earth is restless.

My joints are stiff. I reach a hand—four long, chitinous fingers—to the small pot hanging at my waist. It is filled with the black sap, the lifeblood of the reeds. I dip my fingers into the tar. It is warm, almost hot, conductive. I begin to anoint my neck, my elbows, my knees. The paste acts as a thermal bridge, allowing the heat of the air to flow freely into my core without creating the hotspots that would crack my shell. As the sap touches my skin, I feel a surge of energy, a hum that begins to build in my chest.

I am no longer a statue. I am an instrument.

Around me, the city is empty, yet it is full of voices. A mile to the north, I feel the rhythmic tapping of a bronze chisel against a granite pillar. *Tap-tap. Pause. Tap-tap-tap.* It is a young Keeper, reporting a fissure that has opened in the outer wall. To the south, a faster, more erratic patter—a conversation, perhaps, or a mating dance encoded in vibration. The architecture carries them all. The empty streets are wires, and we are the current.

My purpose is different. I am here to listen to the Great Song.

I lift my staff—a heavy rod of dense, non-resonant bronze—and tap it firmly against the tile at my feet.

*BONG.*

The sound does not just disappear; it travels. It shoots through the packed earth, rippling outwards in a perfect circle, bouncing off the distant walls, returning to me altered by the shape of the room. I read the returning echo. The acoustics have shifted. A cluster of reeds to the west has died, their hollow voices silenced, changing the reflection of the wind. The geometry of the silence is wrong.

I must correct it.

I begin to walk. My movement is agonizingly slow to a human observer, a fluid, gliding motion that conserves momentum. I head toward the western quadrant, my bare feet reading the texture of the road. The wind picks up, whistling through the pores of the reeds, a rising scream that would deafen a creature with ears. To me, it is a texture, a friction I can feel against my sensory plates. The reeds are shrieking in a minor key, stressed by the cooling.

I reach the edge of the settlement where the glass forest begins. I can sense the discordance—a jagged, jarring frequency where a dome of salt-bricks is reflecting the wind badly, creating a feedback loop that is slowly cracking the masonry.

I do not speak. I do not need to. I pull a small chisel from my belt. I find the specific stone on the dome that is vibrating the loudest, the note that is clashing with the wind.

I strike it.

*Crack.*

A single, flake of salt falls away. The pitch of the dome drops. The feedback loop vanishes. The shriek of the wind smooths out, resolving into a harmonic hum. The resonance of the city is restored.

I stand there for a long moment, feeling the satisfaction of the pattern locking into place. The air is getting cooler now. The song of the reeds is deepening, slowing down as the silica contracts. Soon, the temperature will plummet, and the cycle of near-death will claim us all again.

But not yet. For now, I am the listener. I am the eye that cannot see, watching over a city that does not move, in a world that has no sky. I stand in the red gloom, coated in black oil, vibrating in sympathy with the earth, and I wait for the next note.
