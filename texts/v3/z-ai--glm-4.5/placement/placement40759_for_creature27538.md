I find myself adhered to the vertical face of a "skyscraper," some three hundred feet above the "floor" of the canyon.

I am not climbing. I am not resting in the sense that a creature rests. I am *settling*.

I am a Mason-Mimic, but I am old. I have passed the age of grazing and slipping through the cracks. I have entered the final stage of my lifecycle, a transition that the others of my kind view not as death, but as a promotion. I am becoming architecture.

My body is broad, flattened, and heavy, spanning nearly eight feet across the limestone face. To the casual glance—if a casual glance could ever find me in this light—I am nothing more than a protrusion of the ancient reef, a bulging buttress or a patch of uneven erosion where the stone swelled before it hardened. My carapace, once a mottled grey to match the wet rock, has thickened. Over the decades, the mineral-rich water has deposited layers of calcite upon my back, accreting like a pearl in slow motion. The ridges of my shell have fused with the natural striations of the wall. My legs, once hydraulic and muscular, are now locked in a permanent, rigid grip, their joints calcifying into solid pillars.

I am perfectly still. Silence is not just a preference anymore; it is my structural integrity. To vibrate is to weaken the fusion between my biology and the geology.

From my vantage point, I perceive the city not as a place to move through, but as a single, immense organism to be merged with. Below me, the "street" is a slash of shadow perhaps forty feet wide. Down there, the Gutter-Weavers are tending their nets. I can feel the faint, wet *slap* of their movements through the stone. The limestone is an excellent conductor of low-frequency vibration. When a Weaver reels in a Mist-Moth, the tiny struggle travels up the wall, a tickle in my stone skin, a microscopic tremor that I file away as sensation.

I am hungry, but the hunger is distant, a dull ache buried under layers of new shell. I no longer hunt for the rust-colored lichen. Instead, I have become a garden for it. The crevices of my back, now deep and permanent, have filled with soil trapped by the wind and detritus falling from the heights. A tough, electric mint-green crust of lichen has taken root there. I am farming the very thing I used to eat. In return, the lichen aids my camouflage, masking the subtle biological warmth of my underlying flesh with the cold, chemical signature of vegetation.

Above me, the cloud ceiling drags its belly against the spires. I feel the pressure change before the mist arrives. The air grows heavier, denser with water. Then, the condensation begins.

It does not rain here; it weeps. Water seeps from the pores of the coral-city, gathering on the lips of overhangs. Directly above me, a massive stone cornice juts out. It has been dripping for a thousand years. The water hits the center of my back, a rhythmic, hypnotic *plink, plink, plink*. This is my heartbeat now. The water is saturated with calcium. As it flows over my shell and evaporates in the thin air, it leaves behind a microscopic film of white dust. I am literally being built, drop by drop, layer by layer, into something harder than stone.

Through the pearlescent gloom, I see a Spire-Shearer gliding past.

It is immense, a manta ray of the air, its wings reinforced with the same limestone that makes up my shell. It does not flap; it rides the updrafts created by the temperature differentials in the canyon. It is hunting the algae on the needle-thin spires across the way. It passes twenty feet from my face. Its eye is black and glassy, ancient. It does not see me. I am not prey; I am the cliff.

As it passes, the displaced air washes over me, smelling of ozone and wet dust. For a moment, I envy its motion. But then, I feel the deep, sub-audible groan of the Vein-Burrowers shifting somewhere in the foundations beneath us. The ground shudders—a seismic cough. The Spire-Shearer banks to correct its course, fighting the instability of the air.

I do not need to correct. I am anchored. I am the stability.

Deep inside my shell, where my living organs still float in a quiet bath of fluid, I am aware of the slow digestion of my self. The "animal" part of me is shrinking, receding, making room for the "mineral" part. My thoughts are slowing down, synchronizing with the drip of the water and the erosion of the walls. I used to think in seconds; now I think in seasons.

I am no longer a Mimic. I am the thing that is being mimicked.

A Salt-Gardener, a tiny, speck-like drone of the fungal colony, scurries across the rock face near my head. It pauses, antennae waving, testing the surface to see if I are a viable source of lime. I flex a microscopic pore in my shell, releasing a trace of the specific pheromone that says *I am not yet dead, I am not yet stone, go elsewhere*. The Gardener hesitates, then pivots and scuttles away, dissuaded.

I am not yet stone. But I will be.

The light begins to fail, not with a sunset, but with a thickening of the clouds. The pearlescent grey deepens to a bruised purple. The temperature drops. The stone contracts, and a low, resonant *hum* begins to rise from the city—the collective voice of a million fossils shrinking in the cold.

I join the hum. I vibrate with the wall.

I am here, on the forty-third floor of a building built by tides, waiting for the rain to turn me into a statue. I am the observer, permanent and immobile, watching the slow, wet decay of the world, content in the knowledge that long after the Shearers have fallen and the Weavers have dried up, I will remain—a bump on the wall, a silence in the noise, a part of the city that finally decided to stay.
