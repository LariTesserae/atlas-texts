I would be found high above the basin floor, clinging to the wet, iron-reinforced ribs of a cathedral-sized scaffold that braces the side of a looming ziggurat. I would not be on the ground, for the ground is for those who carry the weight of the world; I am made for the precarious edges, the dizzying voids where the mist thins just enough to reveal the depth of the dark.

I am a **Weaver of Light**, or in the silent tongue of the Binders, I am the **Needle**.

I am small—frail, compared to the titanic stoicism of the Binders. My body is spindly and ossified, a lattice of bone and chitin no thicker than a human finger, draped in wings of pale, translucent membrane that shudder in the constant damp wind. I am cold-blooded, my metabolism sluggish, save for the burning, chemical furnace in my abdomen that produces my light.

In this world of blindness and vibration, I am the only thing that *sees*.

Where the Binders perceive the world through the soles of their feet and the tremors in their slate-skin, perceiving the stress fractures and the hollows, I perceive the immediate, the tactile, and the specific. My eyes are compound and hyper-sensitive, designed to pick up the faintest bioluminescence of a fungal pattern or the dull red glow of a distant forge. But more importantly, I am the vision for those who have none.

I would be perched on the jagged, armor-plated shoulder of a Binder—an ancient, scarred giant whom I have known for decades. His name is not a sound, but a rhythmic pattern of impacts I feel through my claws: *Thud-thud... pause... scrape.* To me, he is simply "The Anchor."

My existence is one of absolute, terrifying symbiosis. The Binders are strong but blind; they can feel the wall leaning, but they cannot see the single, hairline fracture running through the critical support beam above them. They can hear the water rushing, but they cannot see the viscous, oily torrent about to break through a dam and drown their work.

I am the pilot of their hands.

When The Anchor stops, sensing a instability in the masonry ahead, he raises his massive, piston-arm. He does not know where to strike; he only knows that *something* is wrong. This is where I move. I scuttle down the length of his arm, my claws finding purchase in the deep grooves of his concrete-like hide. I stand upon the back of his hand, my wings buzzing, casting a flickering, amber light from the lantern-sac beneath my belly.

I look into the gloom. I see the fissure. I see the misplaced stone.

I signal him.

I do not speak. I cannot vibrate the stone like he does. Instead, I use my weight and my light. I press down on a specific nerve-cluster at the base of his "thumb," or I flutter my wings against his sensitive sensory pits to guide his aim. I am the cursor on the screen of his vast, slow consciousness. He feels my intent; he raises the diamond-hard chisel. *Clack.* A single, precise blow. The wall settles. The danger passes.

I am fed by his heat. The perpetual chill of the basin would kill me in hours, but I nestle into the crook of his neck, behind the heavy collar-bone, where the air is warm and smells of sulfur and ozone. There, I drink the oily runoff that he cannot absorb—the thick, black condensation that beads on his skin. It is bitter, laced with heavy metals and stone-dust, but to me, it is life. It fuels my light. In exchange, I groom the unreachable places on his back, cleaning the mineral crusts from his spiracles so he can breathe the heavy air.

I am the witness to their tragedy.

I have ridden many shoulders in my lifetime. I remember "The Keel," a Binder who built the foundations of the great bridge. When his time came, when his internal furnace ran low and his limbs grew too heavy to lift, I felt the change in his vibrations. The song he sang was no longer of building, but of *resting*.

I tried to guide him away from the niche in the wall. I landed on his face, beating my wings frantically, flashing my light in the staccato pattern that means *Danger/Flee/Not Yet.* But the call of the masonry is stronger than the guidance of a gnat. He ignored my needle-pricks of warning. He lay down in the gap, the quick-lime mortar oozing from his pores.

I stayed with him until the end. I perched on his forehead as his eyes—the sensitive pits—dulled and sealed over. I felt the heat leave his body, the great piston-arm seizing into stillness. As the calcification took hold, turning his living concrete into eternal stone, I drank the last of the oil from his lips.

Then, I flew.

Into the dark. Into the roar. I am a spark seeking a new furnace. I fly through the wet, iron-darkness, dodging the dripping waterfalls, passing the Mason-Beetles chewing on the arches, until I find another. A young Binder, new to the heights, confused by the cacophony of the construction site. He is stumbling, blindly feeling the wall, unsure of where to strike.

I land on his shoulder. He startles at the unfamiliar touch of my claws. I glow for him—soft, steady, warm.

I am his eyes now. I am the Needle in the dark. And we have work to do.
