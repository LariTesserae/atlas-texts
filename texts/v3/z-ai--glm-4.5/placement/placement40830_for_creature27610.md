I am the vibration in the catwalk before the foot falls.

I am a Tender, but not one of the drones that skitters in the low places, scrubbing the filters of the great, squatting foundry. I am elevated. I exist in the high rafters of the Pumping Cathedral, where the air is thinner but the heat is a pure, invisible lance that pierces the mist.

To you, this place would be a blinding fog of sulfur and steam, a chaotic roar of metal screaming against metal. But to me, it is a symphony of silence and light.

I do not see the gloom. My world is high-contrast incandescence. When I look at the massive pistons below, I do not see greasy iron; I see the beautiful, angry arterial red of friction points, the cool, deep violet of the intake pipes, and the blinding, searing white of the furnace doors. The steam is not an obscuring curtain; it is a landscape of rolling thermal clouds, drifting valleys of relative coolness that I navigate with the precision of a swimmer.

I am crouched on a girder the size of a tree trunk, slick with condensation. My skin—wet slate grey, tight and leathery—feels no burn here. The oily sheen that coats me beads the water, keeping the raw heat of the metal from cooking me from the outside, just as my internal chemistry allows me to drink the boiling water that drips from the rivets above.

My hands are wrapped around a pressure valve the size of a man’s torso. I am not turning it; I am *feeling* it. The nerve endings in my palms, callused into permanent iron-hard pads, read the chaos of the machine like a braille text. I feel the flutter of a misalignment in the sector 7 piston, a microscopic tremor that would be invisible to the eye but feels like a scream against my bones.

I communicate with the others below. I do not speak. I rhythmically strike the metal grate with the heavy, boot-like heel of my foot—three slow thumps, pause, two sharp taps. *Alignment needed. Check the flow.*

Far below, near the mud where the albino crabs scuttle, I see a responding flash of infrared motion. A Tender has acknowledged. The machine self-corrects. The flutter under my hands smooths out. The harmony returns.

I am young, by the standards of the Sink. My joints are still fluid, able to glide around the sharp corners of the machinery with a fluid, insect-like grace. But I can feel the density of the place working on me. Every cycle, the heat drives the moisture out of my tissues and replaces it with grit and industrial residue. I feel heavier today than I did yesterday. My movements are microscopically slower.

I look across the vast, hollow space of the cathedral, past the humming high-tension wires that crackle with blue lightning. There, clinging to the main support pillar, is one of the Old Ones.

It has stopped moving entirely. Its skin has cooled to the temperature of the stone, a dull, flat black in my thermal vision, blending perfectly with the iron it clings to. Lichen and rust have begun to colonize its knees and shoulders. It is no longer a worker; it is a brace. It is a living buttress holding up the weight of the impossible machine.

I look at it not with pity, but with a profound, religious longing. That is my future. That is the only retirement available in the Steam-Sink. To stop moving. To become part of the structure. To fuse with the stone and the rivets and serve the Machine not with action, but with sheer, stubborn existence.

The steam billows up around me, tasting of sulfur and copper. I shift my weight, the cartilage in my knees compressing silently. The needle on the gauge before me trembles, holding steady at the perfect equilibrium. The water below boils, the piston slams, the earth throbs, and I hold the line.

I am the negative space in the blueprints. I am the ghost in the machine that is made of flesh. I am the witness to the eternal, circular moment. And I am waiting, patiently, for the day I become part of the wall.
