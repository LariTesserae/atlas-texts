I am found in the "Throat," a narrow, vertical constriction where two massive spires of basalt have ground together, leaving only a slick, winding fissure for the hamlet to pass through. Here, the wind accelerates, funneling into a high, thin whistle that competes with the constant dripping of the water. The mist here is thicker, trapped by the geometry of the stone, clinging to my skin like a cold second layer.

I am a **Counterweight**.

I am of the Kept, but I am not a builder or a harvester. I am architecture.

I have evolved—or perhaps was selected—for a specific, static density. While my kin possess the simian grace to leap and flow across the vertical splinters, my limbs are shorter, denser, and hooked. My torso is barrel-shaped, a heavy cylinder of muscle and bone designed to hang. I spend my life suspended in the void, anchored by the thick, powerful grip of my feet and the locking clench of my hands.

I live in the loops of the great gut-lines that lash the floating structures to the stable ribs of the earth. When a platform of timber and thatch extends too far over the abyss, relying on the brittle strength of stilt-roads, I am the living anchor. I hang from the farthest extremity, a lump of charcoal-grey flesh wrapped in the harness of dried gut, my body sagging downward with the sheer, dead weight of gravity. I am the ballast in a world offloat.

My existence is one of profound, numbing stillness. I do not hunt. I do not scuttle. I hang.

Because I do not move, the moss grows on me. I let it. It provides an extra layer of insulation against the 2.0 intensity wetness and camouflages me into the rock face. Small, pale crabs crawl across my shoulders, mistaking the texture of my rubbery skin for the stone itself. I do not brush them away. In the Throat, I am as much a feature of the landscape as the weeping basalt.

My mind is occupied by the *Hum*.

Since I am always touching the tension lines—the gut and vine ropes that bind the Sump together—I feel the world differently than the others. The Kept communicate through low-frequency vibrations, but I am immersed in them. I feel the subtle slackening of a rope as the timber swells with damp. I feel the microscopic stretching of a vine before it snaps. I am the nervous system of the hamlet’s infrastructure.

When the earth shifts—when the geological infant grinds its teeth—I am the first to know. The vibration hits the lines, travels up my legs, settles in my heavy gut. I do not panic. I shift my weight. I lean. If the platform above lists to the left, I swing my body to the right, a pendulum of flesh and bone correcting the balance of the world above me.

I am fed by the community. They come to me in shifts, clambering down the lines to offer me mouthfuls of the nutrient-rich algae paste and the raw, oily meat of the translucent eels. They do not speak. They touch my forehead, checking the temperature of my skin, feeling the tension in my neck. If I am calm, the town is safe. If I am trembling, they know the stone is restless.

I am a melancholy creature, even among the mournful Kept. I watch the sky a hundred feet above, but only through a narrow slit of grey. I envy the harvesters their movement across the sharp pinnacles, but I know that without my weight, their paths would collapse into the deep, dry well below.

I am the apology made manifest. I am the heavy, silent burden that holds the Sump together, weeping silently as the water runs down my face, indistinguishable from the tears of the stone.
