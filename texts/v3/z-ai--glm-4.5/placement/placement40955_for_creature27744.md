I would be found in the oldest, lowest room of the largest longhouse, the one that sits heaviest on the fractured spine of the plateau. I am not the builder, nor the architect, and I am no longer quite a guest in the conventional sense. I am the **Anchor**.

To the Damp-Dwellers, I am an ancestor, a relic who has sat in the same corner for longer than the mist has clear memory. I am a mound of heavy, oiled wool and felt, piled so thickly that I resemble one of the geological formations outside more than a person. I have no name that is spoken aloud, for names require sound, and sound is a violation of the Truce.

I am a human, or I was once. Now, I am a component of the hamlet’s circulatory system.

My days are spent in a state of profound, meditative stillness. I sit with my back pressed flush against the cold, damp slate of the interior wall, my legs folded beneath me, my hands resting on my knees. The wool that swaddles me has absorbed the scents of the house: woodsmoke, the bruised-purple rot of the fungi, and the sharp, metallic tang of the salt I wear.

For around my neck hangs a heavy collar of raw, crystalline salt mined from the deep fissures. It is a burden that weighs on my collarbone, a constant pressure. This salt is not for eating; it is an offering, a scent-marker that distinguishes me from the inanimate stone.

I am the **Balancer**.

The Lithic Masons possess a collective intelligence that senses vibration and pressure, but they lack the foresight to anticipate the settling of the human structures. They react to stress, but they do not predict it. That is my function.

When I feel the subtle groan in the timber beam above my head—a frequency too low for human ears but felt as a deep ache in the teeth—I do not shout. I do not move quickly. Instead, I lean.

I shift my weight imperceptibly to the left. I press the flat of my hand against the wall where the mortar has begun to powder. I become a point of concentrated gravity.

The Masons feel this. To them, I am a point of intense interest, a beacon of "heaviness" in a chaotic world. Within moments, the silence shifts. The air pressure drops as the room fills with them. They pour over the floorboards, a tide of slate-grey carapaces. They swarm over my legs, their hydraulic movement silent, their wet shells cold against the wool.

They do not bite. They do not pinch. They simply *use* me. One by one, they climb onto my shoulders, adding their weight to mine, reinforcing the pressure I am applying to the weak spot in the wall. Others crawl beneath the floorboards, their legs locking together to form a living pillar that shores up the foundation directly beneath me.

I am the conductor of this silent orchestra. By tensing a muscle, by slowing my breathing, I can direct the flow of their labor. When I feel the timber settle and the groan cease, I relax. The pressure shifts. The Masons disperse, sliding back into the cracks in the wall or scuttling out into the mist to harvest fungi.

I am the bridge between the soft, fragile biology of the Damp-Dwellers and the rigid, relentless geology of the Masons. I have learned to think in compression and tension. I have learned to communicate through the sheer density of my presence.

When the storm breaks, as it always does, the true purpose of the Anchor is revealed. The wind howls outside, battering the thatch, but inside, the silence is absolute.

The Damp-Dwellers retreat to their benches, wrapped in their own heavy furs, watching me. They do not sign; they only wait. The pressure in the room plummets as the tempest sucks the air from the plateau. The house shudders.

I close my eyes. I stop breathing entirely.

I become a statue. I merge with the stone at my back. I project a signal of absolute, immovable resistance. I tell the Masons through the pressure of my stillness: *We will not move.*

And they answer.

Hundreds of them swarm the walls, the ceiling, the roof. They link legs, locking carapaces together, turning the longhouse into a solid fortress of shell and stone. They press against me, and I press against them. For the duration of the storm, I am not a person; I am the keystone of an arch. I feel the vibration of the rain striking their backs, transmitted through their shells, into my spine, into the floor. It is a thunder I cannot hear, but feel in the marrow of my bones.

I hold the house together. I hold the Masons together. I am the heavy heart in the center of the wet, grey chest of the world, beating in silence, keeping the darkness at bay.
