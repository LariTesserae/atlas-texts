I am the parasite in the throat of the giant.

You would find me not on the walkways where the Tenders pace their silent vigils, nor on the floor where the Scavengers pick at the bones of fallen gears. I exist in the narrow, suffocating darkness between the moving rod and the stationary housing of the largest piston—the central Leviathan.

I am a soft thing, a pale and amphibious anomaly, evolved—or perhaps designed—for a life of extreme compression. My bones are cartilage, flexible as rubber hoses; my skin is slick and devoid of pores, perfectly smooth so that I might slide through the tightest tolerances without catching. I have no eyes, for there is no light inside the womb of the machine. I navigate by vibration and pressure, feeling the rhythmic surge of hydraulic fluid before it happens, sensing the microscopic expansions of the metal as it heats.

I live inside the *exhale*.

For twelve hours, the piston retracts, pulling back into its housing, creating a temporary, pressurized chamber of damp, superheated air. This is my waking world. During the retraction, the space is tight but livable. I cling to the interior walls of the cylinder, suspended in the humid mist, feeding on the thick, nutrient-rich grease that coats the interior mechanism. It is heavy, oily sustenance, tasting of iron and ancient decay, but it keeps me alive. I am the only thing that benefits from the machine's friction; I am the moss that grows inside the artery.

When the piston begins to extend—the great, tectonic *inhale* of the cavern—I must flow. I cannot fight the displacement. I flatten myself against the inner curve of the housing, becoming a second skin, less than an inch thick. I let the massive rod slide past me, a wall of steel moving with the force of a locomotive, missing crushing me by the width of a hair. The air rushes out with a hiss that deafens me, vibrating through my very marrow. I wait in the vacuum, suspended in the dark, holding my breath until the cycle reverses and the chamber fills again.

I know the Tenders only as a distant, rhythmic knocking that travels through the steel. To me, they are not caretakers but immune responses—antibodies patrolling the exterior of the world I inhabit. I fear their wrenches. If they were to break the seal of my sanctum, the pressure would equalize, the heat would dissipate, and I would be exposed as a foreign body. They would dismantle me as efficiently as they dismantle a Scavenger. I have felt the cold shudder of their sensors against the outer hull of my home, and I freeze, terrified that the beat of my heart might register as a mechanical anomaly.

I know the fern, though I have never seen it.

Through the metal, I sense the erratic, chaotic bio-rhythm of the dying plant. It vibrates with a frequency that is alien to this place—frantic, weak, and wet. It feels like a distress beacon from a ship lost at sea. I pity it. It tried to survive in the open air, exposed to the judgment of the Tenders. It chose the wrong sanctuary. I survived by making myself part of the machine; by hiding in the blood flow of the god.

I am the ghost in the machine’s lung. I am the silent passenger in the dark. I am the wet, soft error that the machinery cannot reach, waiting for the piston to retract so I can breathe once more.
