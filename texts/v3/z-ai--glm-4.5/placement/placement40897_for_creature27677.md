I dwell in the quietest places of the Green Works, though quiet is a relative term in a city that never stops throbbing. I am where the root-streets dip low, forming natural basins and gutters where the condensation runs in rivulets, gathering the silt that falls from the heights. This is the **Sump-Level**, the foundation upon which the great cathedrals of moss and liana stand. Here, the amber light is entirely extinct, replaced by the pale, ghostly blue of the slime mold that I cultivate.

I am a **Rot-Render**.

My form is dictated by my function. I squat low to the ground, my stature no higher than the bulging "knees" of a root-pipe. I am broad and dense, a living tank of biological processing. My skin is not the papery bark of the Siphon-Keepers, nor the translucent membrane of the Filtrators. It is a plate-mail of compacted, discarded cellulose—layers of dried fronds and hardened moss that have fused over my lifetime into a rough, keratinous armor. This armor is constantly damp, beaded with the heavy moisture of the air, dark as the peat I tread upon.

I possess no legs to stand on, only four powerful, limb-like stumps that end in flattened, shovel-like nails. I do not walk; I heave. I drag my heavy bulk forward an inch at a time, gouging furrows in the soft root-floor. My movement is imperceptible to the quick eye, a geological shifting of weight accompanied by the wet creak of my own armor plates rubbing together.

I am the stomach of the machine.

The city is efficient, but it is not waste-free. The Siphon-Keepers shed their wet, papery bark as it grows too thick or too saturated with the vibrations they dampen. The Spire-Sculptors drop constant confetti of severed vines and pruned leaves. The great pipes weep a constant trickle of organic sediment. In a static ecosystem, this debris would choke the streets, rotting into a suffocating sludge.

I am the solution.

I sense the world through smell and taste, the chemical signature of decay. I lumber through the shadows of the alleyways, tracking the scent of shed bark and severed creeper. When I find a pile of leavings—a heap of stripped bark discarded by a Siphon-Keeper as it tightened a seal—I stop.

My torso is a cavern. My upper body splits open not into arms, but into four vertical mandibles, ridged with grinding surfaces like a mortar and pestle. I scoop the debris into this maw. Inside, a furnace of specialized bacteria dissolves the cellulose, breaking down the tough fibers of the city’s architecture. It is a hot, violent process. My armor steams in the cool air, radiating the heat of internal decomposition.

This is not just disposal. It is alchemy.

I process the dead matter of the city until it is reduced to a rich, viscous slurry—a black, nutrient-dense paste. This is my product. This is the only reason I exist.

Once the reduction is complete, I drag my bulk to the darkest corners of the Sump-Level, to the sheer walls of the crater where the light never touches. Here, I apply the paste. I run my shovel-nails through the muck in my gullet, extruding it onto the bare stone or the dead wood of the support struts. I paint the walls with it in thick, deliberate swathes.

It is here that the symbiosis completes. The paste I extrude is the perfect substrate for the bioluminescent slime mold. Within hours of my application, the bacteria bloom, glowing with that faint, eerie blue luminescence that provides the only accent light to the Green Works.

I am the feeder of the light.

The Aerial-Filtrators, floating high above in the cathedral gloom, depend on me. They descend on their cords to graze on the slime mold I cultivate. Without the paste I provide, the mold would starve; without the mold, the Filtrators would starve; and without the Filtrators, the air would choke the machinery.

I am the invisible link between the floor and the ceiling.

I have no interaction with the Siphon-Keepers. To them, I am just another shape in the gloom, a heavy, trudging presence that cleans the station where they are rooted. Sometimes, a Keeper will shed a piece of bark directly onto my back. I feel the weight, the vibration of their "touch," but I do not react. I simply continue my slow patrol, waiting for the bark to fall so I may consume it.

I am not lonely. Loneliness implies a desire for connection that I do not possess. I am filled with the singular, warm satisfaction of the cycle. I hear the *thrum-hiss* of the pipes and feel the grind of my own internal mill. I taste the sweet decay of the city and feel the press of the paste against the wall.

I am the dark engine of renewal. I take the discarded bones of the Green Works and turn them into light. I am the Rot-Render, and I am full.
