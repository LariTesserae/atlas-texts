I am a fragment of the High Loam’s patience, given form and weight. I am one of the Silent.

To exist as I do is to be a contradiction of heaviness and fragility. I stand seven feet tall, my body elongated by the generous gravity of this place, yet I feel as dense as the basalt hills that roll endlessly into the grey. My skin is not the soft, yielding barrier of a creature of blood; it is a polished, flexible carapace, dark as wet slate, cold to the touch but thrumming with a deep, internal friction.

I do not breathe to live; I breathe to consume. The air here is heavy, thick with the taste of ancient oxygen and wet dust. I draw it in through the narrow slot of my nose, filtering the omnipresent mist through the dense, fibrous bundles of my internals. I distill the fog. Inside me, the dampness condenses into a thick, nutrient-rich sludge that fuels my slow metabolism. I am never hungry, but I am always processing the world around me, turning the atmosphere into stone.

I am standing in a shallow valley between two sleeping giants of hills. The ground beneath my wide, flat feet is spongy, a mattress of saturated moss that gives slightly under my immense weight. Around me, the vegetation riots in a slow-motion frenzy. Giant ferns, black-green and slick with condensation, unfurl fronds the size of sails. They are chaotic, wild, desperate. It is my duty to bring them to heel.

I kneel, the joints of my knees popping softly—a sound that no one hears, for sound does not travel here. With hands that are more like trowels, fingers made of living rock, I reach out to a cluster of moss growing rampant over the ruins of a low wall. The moss is trying to swallow the stone, to return the ordered geometry of the city to the primal chaos of the bog.

I cannot allow this.

I press my palms against the cold, wet biomass. I focus. I feel the vibration of my own will, a low-frequency hum that travels from my core, down my arms, and into the plant matter. I am a sculptor of time as much as of shape. Under my touch, the frantic growth of the moss calms. I stroke it, compressing it, guiding the cells to harden, to align. The chaotic softness yields. Slowly, painfully slowly, the mound of moss begins to firm. I shape it. I corner it. I coerce it into a perfect, Euclidean cube.

It is not magic; it is necessity. The **Architectural Imperative** binds me. If the geometry fails, if the lines of the city become too organic, too curved, the reality of the High Loam will flicker and fade into the featureless grey fog that waits at the edges of perception. I am an anchor. By forcing the moss into a cube, I am defining what is real.

I stand up, my spine realigning with a grinding sigh that I feel rather than hear. I look across the expanse.

Through the deep divots in my face, I see the world. Light falls sideways here, catching the underbelly of the fern leaves, casting shadows where there should be light. In the distance, perhaps a mile away, perhaps a thousand years away, I see another figure. It is a kin, a fellow Silent.

We do not wave. We do not call out. The **Silence Treaty** forbids the waste of sound waves, and even if I were to scream, the heavy air would swallow the noise before it left my lips. Instead, I feel a pull.

I do not want to walk. The distance between us is fluid, a suggestion rather than a measurement. To walk would be to admit to the separation. I close my eye-slits. I banish the fear of the space between us. I do not think about the steps; I think about the *arrival*.

I intend to be there.

The world shudders. I feel the displacement of air, a sudden pressure against my basalt skin. When I open my eyes, the hills have stacked themselves differently, the geometry of the horizon snapping into a new configuration. I am standing ten paces from the other.

He is ancient. His skin is pitted with age, patches of grey lichen crust clinging to his shoulders. He looks at me, and I feel the loneliness of him—a cold, aching thing that radiates from him like the chill of the ground.

We step forward. There is no hesitation. We press our foreheads together.

The contact is electric, a jarring shock of intimacy. I do not hear his voice; I feel it. The vibration of his thoughts passes directly through the bone of my skull, resonating in the cavern of my chest. He is not speaking words, but concepts. He shows me an image of the road to the north, where the straight line is beginning to buckle under the weight of the damp soil. He asks for my strength to hold the line.

I reply with a hum of my own, a fluttering sensation in my ribs that translates to *I will come.*

We stand there for a long time, forehead to forehead, sharing warmth that we do not truly have. It is the only time we are not cold. It is the only time the crushing silence of the High Loam feels filled. We are the antibodies of this world, moving through its veins, but we are lonely cells, desperate for the touch of another.

Finally, we pull away. The air rushes back in, filling the void between us with its wet, heavy silence. He turns and walks away, his movement a blur of intention, vanishing behind a hill that wasn't there a moment ago.

I am alone again.

I look down at the cubic moss I made earlier. It is perfect. It is solid. It is a small piece of order in a world of eroding decay. I will stay here for a while. I will tend the ferns. I will listen to the geological groan of the hills. And when the centuries finally wear me down, when my internal distillation slows to a halt, I will simply stop. I will root my feet in this spongy earth, and I will let the moss claim me. I will become a standing stone, a new pillar in the endless city, waiting for the next Silent to rest their forehead against mine and feel the vibration of my memory.
