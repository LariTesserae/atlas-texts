I find myself upon a moving continent of wet slate.

To be precise, I am located high upon the sloping shoulder of one of the great Anchors—the stone-skinned, centauroid dwellers of this light-drowned deep. From this vantage, the floor of the Sunless Basin is a vast, grey ocean stretching into the diffuse haze, but I do not look with eyes. I feel the world through the soles of my many feet and the sensitive membrane of my underbelly.

I am a symbiont. A passenger.

If the inhabitants are the heavy, immovable stones in the river of this world, I am the moss that clings to them, softening their edges. I am small, perhaps the size of a surface-dweller’s cat, but flattened, resembling a ray or a velvet-laden starfish. My body is a cluster of flexible, muscular lobes centered around a single, grinding maw, surrounded by a wreath of delicate, manipulating tendrils.

My skin is not the armoured grey of my host, but a translucent, pallid pink, vein-traced and vulnerable. I cling to the host’s massive, rounded back using a series of suckers that pulse gently, matching the rhythm of their slow circulation. Here, in the lee of their immense bulk, the crushing air pressure is comforting, a warm, heavy blanket that holds me tight against the damp stone of their hide.

My purpose is hygiene, but it is also a form of worship.

The host I ride is old. I can feel the deep, resonant thrum of their age vibrating through the cartilage of my suckers—a sub-bass note that has been sounding for centuries. Their skin, while smooth to the touch, is pitted with microscopic pores that breathe the heavy air. In these crevices, despite the harsh, sterile light, opportunistic colonies of dust and microscopic spores attempt to take hold. This is where I feed.

As the host stands motionless, anchored like a statue in the broad avenue, waiting for the turbulence, I begin my work. I creep forward, my underside rippling with lubricating slime. I extend my fine, wire-thin tendrils into the folds of the host’s neck, specifically around the heavy, horizontal slit of their filtration mouth.

The turbulence approaches.

I feel it before it arrives. The air pressure drops infinitesimally, a gasp in the dense atmosphere. Then, the storm is upon us. The glass-moths descend—a chaotic, silent hurricane of wings.

To the host, this is a nourishing impact, a rain of food. To me, clinging to their shoulder, it is a bombardment. I feel the *thwack-thwack-thwack* of thousands of insect bodies striking the host’s chest and legs. The shockwaves travel up through the host’s skeleton, jarring my small frame. I press myself flat, my suckers locking tight, becoming nothing more than a wart on the giant’s back.

I wait out the feeding frenzy. The host’s great chest heaves as they inhale the swarm, their mouth slit widening to sieve the air. Around them, the air churns with violent, silent force.

Then, as quickly as it began, the vortex shifts and moves on, spiraling toward the next net-covered spire.

The waste remains. The host’s skin is splattered with the viscous, nutrient-rich fluids of pulverized moths and the glittering dust of shattered glass wings. This is where I come alive.

I scuttle down the slope of the host’s shoulder, navigating the landscape of their living stone. I come to rest in the hollow of their collarbone, a reservoir of warm, salty liquid and mineral shards. I begin to feast.

My tendrils sweep the surface, scraping up the organic slurry and the crunchy silica. It is a meal of metal and glass, rich with the energy of the mantle-absorbing light. But I do not merely eat; I groom. I scrape the crevices clean, ensuring no fungal rot takes hold in the damp warmth of their folds. I polish the sensory pits of their skin, clearing the dust so they may better "hear" the pressure waves of the basin.

As I work, I feel the host’s reaction. It is a subtle shift in the tension of their muscles, a slow, rolling wave of relaxation that passes through their body. They are content.

Sometimes, when the feeding is particularly good, or when the air is especially still, the host will engage me. They will lift one of their massive, spindly arms—careful not to dislodge me—and bring the back of their hand near to where I sit on their shoulder.

Then, they speak.

It is not a sound. It is a tactile language. They will tap a specific rhythm against their own chest. The vibration travels through their bones, into my suckers, and into my nervous system.

*Thrum... thrum-thrum... pause... thrum.*

It translates not to words, but to concepts. *Cleaner. Good. Stay.*

I respond in kind. I tap my small, hard beak against the stone of their shoulder—a high-pitched, ticking vibration that contrasts with their deep bass.

*Hungry. Good.*

We stand together in the actinic glare. I am the small, soft parasite who cleans them; they are the vast, silent fortress who feeds me. I have no need to weave nets, no need to face the storm alone. I endure it by clinging to the one thing in the Sunless Basin heavier and more permanent than the stone itself.

I close my eyeless face, tucking my tendrils in, and feel the deep, geological slow of the host’s heartbeat. In the silence of the basin, I am home.
