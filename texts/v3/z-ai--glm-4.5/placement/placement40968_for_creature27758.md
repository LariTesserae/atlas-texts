I am a **Caliper**, though I have no name for it. I am simply the act of holding things together, given mass and geometry.

You would find me suspended on the underside of a high, rusted cantilever, perhaps fifty feet above the black water of the sump. This is not a place of safety—it is a place of tension. The cantilever supports the main outflow pipe, a throat of iron that groans every few hours as it spits vapor into the violet gloom. I am clamped to the superstructure like a heavy, segmented beetle, my legs wrapped around the girders, my upper body torqued against a great, cracked flange where the pipe meets the basin wall.

I am holding it shut.

My form is cast from the detritus of the hollow. My carapace is a geological stratigraphy of the city’s history: a base layer of grey calcite, topped with plates of pitted iron, all fused together by a hard, black grime that smells of ozone and wet pennies. I am not shiny; the damp air ensures that I are always weeping a fine, rusty mist. The "rust" that coats my shoulders is alive—a carpet of microbes that digest the metal of the city and secrete a paste that hardens into fresh armor. I can feel them ticking, a microscopic vibration across my skin, constantly repairing the micro-fractures caused by the shifting weight of the pipe.

I do not see the violet light. To me, the world is a tapestry of pressures and resonances. My "feet" are buried deep in the rivets of the cantilever. Through them, I taste the shivering of the metal. I know the water level in the sump by the way the air density changes the echo of the dripping condensation. I know the mood of the city by the thrum in the soles of my feet.

Currently, I am hosting a colony of the small, wire-furred ones. They cling to the ridges of my back, their copper filaments tangling with my sensory stalks. They are nervous today; they are chittering in a frequency that tickles my armor plates. They taste a blockage forming in the intake valve far below. I do not need to know this directly—I feel their agitation as a localized itch on my left flank. They are my eyes, my fingers, the fine-tuning instruments that adjust the gears I am too large to touch.

Above me, the air is thick with the drifting rays. I ignore their soft, bioluminescent pulses; they are irrelevant to the geometry of stress. But when one passes directly overhead, I extend a filtration patch—a sponge-like membrane in my neck that unfurls like a gill. I catch the rope of nutrient-rich honey dripping from its belly. The taste is electric, a sharp shock of chemical energy that replenishes the static charge lubricating my joints. It is the only reason I need to stop holding the pipe; I must pause, inhale, and let the charge flow through me.

I have been here for a long time. I do not measure time in hours, but in the rhythmic expansion and contraction of the iron beneath my grip. The metal breathes, heating and cooling, and I flex with it. I am the hinge. I am the muscle that keeps the crack from widening.

Eventually, I will fail. A joint will seize, or the metal will fatigue beyond the microbes' ability to repair. When that happens, I will lose my grip. I will fall the fifty feet into the black water below. It is not a fate I fear. It is simply the next function. I will splash, and then the pale, translucent things in the water will rise. They will unwrap my calcite shell, dissolve my iron bones, and return me to the sludge. I will become the water, the rust, the dripping mist. I will be drawn up by the pumps and rained down upon the city, perhaps to become the shell of another Caliper centuries from now.

But for now, the pipe shifts. A deep, resonant *thrum* travels up the girders, signaling a release of pressure. The metal screams, a high-pitched screech that vibrates in my chest. I tighten my grip. My internal hydraulics hiss. I lean into the torque. I become the wall against which the chaos breaks. I hold.

I am the ruin, dreaming of holding itself together.
