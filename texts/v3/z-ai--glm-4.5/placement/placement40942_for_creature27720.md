I find myself at the **Rim of the Great Bowl**, a natural depression in the Titan's back where the plate-leaves curve upward to form a windbreak the size of a valley. The air here is frantic, a constant howling corridor of wind, but inside the Bowl, the air is stagnant and heavy, hanging with the scent of drying sap and the musk of many bodies pressed close together.

I am not a Hunter, though I have the scars of one. I am a **Weaver of Tethers**.

My form is stunted, dense, and dark, indistinguishable from the gnarled roots I sit upon. My legs are bowed and powerful, built not for running, but for locking in place—a living anchor. My arms are disproportionately long, the muscles ropy and hard, scarred from the friction of a thousand different vines and leathers.

I am sitting on a protrusion of lignified wood, a "toe" of the Titan that hangs over the terrifying emptiness. Below my heels, the world simply ceases. Three miles of air stretch down before the distant, green haze of the lower canopy becomes visible. It is a vertigo that never sleeps, a constant low-frequency hum in the inner ear.

I am weaving a **Life-Line**.

In my hands, I hold three strands of fiber. One is the dried gut of a great glider-ray, strong and elastic. The second is a vine harvested from the upper canopy, fibrous and rough. The third is human hair—collected from the brushings of the dead, woven over the course of years. I braid them together. My fingers are swollen, the nerve endings exposed and sensitive, feeling for the microscopic imperfections in the fibers that could snap under a sudden downdraft. This is not construction; it is a prayer in tactile form.

A young one approaches me. He is "Soft"—his skin has not yet weathered into the toughness of cured leather. He is strapped into a training harness, a crude thing of stiff hide that chafes his raw skin. He smells of fear.

He stops in front of me. He does not speak. Words are for the lowlanders who have walls to echo off of. Here, sound is a warning flare.

He extends his hand, trembling, and rests it on my shoulder.

Through his touch, I feel his query: *Is the air true today?*

I stop my braiding. I do not look at the sky with my eyes; my eyes are small, protected by heavy ridges, and mostly useless for the fine details of the currents. Instead, I strip off my glove and press my palm flat against the warm, bronzed skin of the Titan beneath me.

I feel the *Pulse*.

It is a slow, rhythmic thrumming, the metabolism of a mountain. The heat rises through my hand, travelling up my arm. I feel the expansion of the xylem deep beneath the bark. The Titan is inhaling, pulling moisture from the air. The ground beneath us shifts a fraction of an inch—a geological sigh.

The inhalation means a thermal is forming. The exhale will come in ten minutes. When it exhales, the heat will push upward. The air will become thin and violent.

I tap the young one’s wrist twice. *Wait.*

I return to my braiding. The rhythm of my hands matches the rhythm of the Titan’s breath. I am tying a knot known as the "Grip of the Fallen." It does not slip; it does not loosen. It is designed to hold a weight that is screaming in terror.

The noise of the sky is overwhelming. Above us, a school of needle-beaked dart-birds screeches, a tearing sound like canvas ripping. A shadow passes over the sun—a leathery glider with a thirty-foot wingspan banking hard against an updraft. The wind whistles through the hollow spires of the dead giants in the distance, a mournful, bone-vibrating song.

The young one flinches at the shadow. He grips my shoulder harder. His fear is a physical pressure, a vibration that travels through his tendons into my bone.

*I am afraid of the Fall,* his touch confesses.

I pause. I look at him, really look at him, for the first time. I see the desperation in his eyes, the need to prove he is not a parasite, but a master of the air.

I reach out and take his hand. I guide his fingers to the braided cord in my lap. I press his fingertips against the knot I have just tied. I force him to feel the tension, the interlocking strength, the way the gut fibers grip the vine.

I then press his hand against the bark of the Titan.

*The wind is the enemy,* I communicate through the pressure of my grip on his hand. *The Titan is the mother. The Fall is only death if you fight the air. Trust the Tether. Trust the Heat.*

The Titan exhales.

A sudden gust of warm air blasts up the face of the cliff, catching the loose fibers of my cloak and whipping them violently. The noise of the birds rises to a fever pitch. The thermal is ready.

I release his hand. I point to the edge.

He nods. He does not smile. There is no joy here, only the solemn duty of survival. He turns and walks to the launching lip—a flat slab of bark extending over the nothingness.

He straps on his glider, a small, fragile thing made of translucent membrane and hollow bone. He stands on the edge, his back to the abyss, facing the wind.

I watch him. I am the anchor. If he falls, I am the weight that holds the line. But today, he does not fall.

He leans forward. The thermal catches him. He is yanked upward, violently, like a leaf in a firestorm. For a terrifying second, he drops—my heart hammers against my ribs—but then the membrane catches the wind. He swoops upward, circling, riding the heat generated by the living ground.

He becomes a speck against the bronze-green sky, a tiny mote dancing in the cathedral of air.

I exhale. I did not realize I was holding my breath.

I pick up my braiding. The Life-Line must be finished before the evening rain, when the vertical rivers begin to fall and the world becomes a shimmering cage of water and noise.

I am a Weaver of Tethers. I bind the soft flesh of my people to the hard will of the Titan. I ensure that when they step into the void, they are not truly alone.
