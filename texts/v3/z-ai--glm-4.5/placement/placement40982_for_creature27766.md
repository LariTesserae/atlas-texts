I am not a visitor, nor am I one of the silent shepherds in their rusted raiments. I am smaller than that, yet more pervasive. I am the suspension before the fall.

I am a droplet of condensation.

I find myself hanging from the underbelly of a riveted iron beam, perhaps forty feet above the churning dark water of the sump. I am not born of rain, but of the machine’s own exhale—distilled from the steam that hisses through a hairline fracture in the pipe above me. I have formed slowly, molecule by reluctant molecule, gathering the heavy heat of the air and the metallic tang of ozone into a perfect, heavy sphere.

My form is tenuous, a trembling lens of surface tension. I am translucent, but I carry the color of this place: I am tinged with the dull orange of oxidation and the deep, bruising green of the chlorophyll drifting in the humid currents. Gravity is a constant, tugging urge at my bottom curve, a sweet temptation to let go and plunge, but I cling to the slick, pitted metal. I am the embodiment of the Garden’s tension—held aloft by the warmth, desperate to fall.

From my vantage point, the world is inverted and distorted through my own curved skin.

To my left, the massive piston rises, an agonizingly slow geological shift. Through my refraction, the movement of the metal looks like the breathing of a leviathan, the rivets swimming in a haze of heat distortion. I can feel the vibration of the engine travel up the iron strut and into the metal I cling to. It is a low-frequency thrum that makes my body quiver, a song without a melody that resonates in the very density of my being. I do not hear it; I *am* it, shaking in sympathy with the machine.

Below me, suspended in the gloom, is a Curator.

It has been standing there for what feels like an eternity. To me, rooted in my moment of suspension, it is a mountain. It stands perfectly still on the mesh catwalk, its long, multi-jointed fingers resting lightly on a pressure valve. I can see the details of its carapace with crystalline clarity from here: the stratified layers of rust, the texture of river-worn stone, the thick, wet moss draped over its shoulders like a heavy, sodden cape.

I am perfectly positioned to see its face. Or rather, the smooth plate of brass where a face should be. It is tilted upward, angled directly toward me. The polished surface catches the dim, bioluminescent glow of the lichen growing in the high vents, and in that reflection, I see myself.

I see a tiny, trembling sphere of orange light, hovering like a distant star in the darkness of the cathedral.

The Curator is waiting for me. I can feel the pull of its intent, though it makes no move to speed my arrival. It is communicating with the machine, vibrating at a frequency I can feel rattling my hold on the iron. It knows that when I fall, I will land on the mossy mantle of its shoulder. I will slide down the cool, rubbery vegetation and seep into the microscopic pores of the rust-carapace. I will bring the moisture the moss craves. I will carry the iron the machine needs.

I am the sacrifice. The slow, inevitable gift of the engine to its keeper.

Time here is thick. I have watched the steam vents pulse a dozen times. I have felt the atmospheric pressure shift as the great gears grind two degrees to the left. The air around me is warm, wet velvet, pressing against me, making me heavy, swelling my size until I feel I must burst.

I can no longer hold the shape of the sphere. The weight of the world—the heat, the metal, the waiting—is too much.

I let go.

The fall is not a fast motion, but a long, slow surrender. The air rushes past, though it feels thick enough to swim through. The blurred green of the ferns rushes up, then the intricate lattice of the catwalk, and finally, the stillness of the brass face.

I impact the moss with a splash that is silent in the roar of the machinery. I shatter, losing my singular consciousness, spreading out into a thousand tiny veins. I am absorbed instantly. I become part of the wetness of the moss. I become the slickness on the Curator’s shoulder.

I am no longer a droplet. I am the Rust-Garden, hydrating the patient stone. I have finally arrived.
