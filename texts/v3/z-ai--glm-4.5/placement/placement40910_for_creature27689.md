I am a pendulum of intent, suspended by a thick, black umbilical of greasy canvas and steel cable that vanishes into the crushing dark overhead. My body is a central chrysalis of brass and leaded crystal, encrusted with the mineral deposits of centuries of condensation. From this central eye, six articulated limbs extend—but unlike the creature below, my limbs are not jointed legs of steel, but flexible, ribbed hoses that terminate in high-pressure lances and complex, shuttered apertures.

A whirring, clockwork internal gyroscope adjusts my swaying, bringing my apertures into sharp focus. I analyze the structural integrity of the stone. I scan for air bubbles, for weak points in the synthetic binder, for the terrifying possibility of a flaw.

But if the geology is wrong—if the "rehearsal" has failed—my role shifts from observer to executioner.

The nozzles at the ends of my limbs uncap with a sharp *snick*. I do not use the gentle spray of the Mason; I use the violence of the room. When I wash away a wall, he simply turns his sensor-studded head toward the mess, extends his tamping limb, and begins the process of filling the void again. We are trapped in a perfect, Sisyphean waltz. He builds the; I flood it. He grooms the rust; I prune the stone.

I am the nervous system to the room's muscle.

I pull my hoses tight against my body, coiling into a dormant, tear-drop shape. I enter a state of hibernation, my internal fluids slowing to prevent freezing in the biting chill. I hang there, frozen in the dark, a single, unblinking, closed eye waiting for the phosphorescence to return.

I am the critic in the dark. I am the flood in the basement. I am the one who ensures the world is never finished, forever demanding that it be built again, better, stronger, colder. I am the reason the Mason never rests. And he is the reason I never run dry.
