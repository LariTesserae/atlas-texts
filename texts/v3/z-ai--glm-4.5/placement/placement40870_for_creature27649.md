I am the silence that hangs like a speck of dust against the thunder.

I am of **The Quiet**, but more specifically, I am a **Diver**. I do not cultivate the galls; I do not brew the honey. I am the thing that leaps into the mouth of the world to steal what falls from its lips.

My existence begins with the weight of the mask. It is not a burden, but a definition. Made of calcified sap and clay, fused to my face since the Rite of Closure, it seals me off from the humid, rotting breath of the abyss. The air here—the hot syrup of the atmosphere—cannot touch my lungs directly. It passes through the lattice of my mask, stripped of its hallucinogenic spores and thick pollen, cool and dry as it enters my throat. To breathe is to hiss, a constant, soft susurrus that only I can hear through the bone of my jaw.

I stand on the edge of the woven platform, a fungal blister clinging to the cork-bark of a Titan Tree. Three miles of empty air drop away beneath my toes, vanishing into the green haze.

I do not hear the Roar. The others— outsiders, if any could survive—would be driven mad by the sawing thrum of a billion glass-winged locusts and the metallic chiming of the Bell-Birds. But to me, the world is not loud. It is *vibrant*.

My skeleton is the instrument. The dense, calcified cartilage of my skull conducts the frequencies of the chaos. I feel the locust swarms as a itch in my teeth, a constant, high-pitched tension. I feel the Bell-Birds as a deep, resonant hum in my sternum. The air is thick with sound-waves, pressing against my skin like a physical current. I navigate by this texture of noise. When the thrum sharpens, I know the swarm is thickening. When the hum in my chest falters, I know a predator moves in the gloom below.

I am preparing to dive. My skin flashes a steady, rhythmic amber—the "Wait" signal—to my partner. He is a Weave-Binder, his skin a neutral grey, absorbed in checking the lines of the winch. He flashes back a quick, jagged spike of turquoise: *Clear.*

I spread my arms. The "glider-kite" is strapped to my torso, a frame of lightweight bone with a membrane stretched thin, cured from the gut of a fallen leviathan. It smells of ozone and vinegar.

Below us, the sky darkens. It is not night; it is a Locust Cloud. A living weather system of chitin and transparent wings is blotting out the sun, turning the day into a rattling, humming twilight. This is the time of the Rain.

A shadow detaches from the churn above. It is massive, a Bell-Bird exhausted by the heat, its wings failing. It tumbles through the layers of mist, a chaotic stone plummeting toward the green floor that would dissolve it in seconds.

*I pulse red.* *Now.*

I step off the edge.

The silence of the fall is absolute. For a moment, I am separated from the tree, suspended in the hot, wet air. The wind—normally sluggish here—rushes past my mask. Then the membrane catches the air. I do not fly; I swim. I swoop downward, guided by the vibration of the falling beast echoing in my collarbone.

The air grows hotter as I descend. The smell of wet decay seeps through my mask’s filters, a ghost of a scent. The green floor rushes up to meet me—not solid earth, but a writhing carpet of moss and beetles. I can see the "crawl-light"—the purple worms of electricity—snaking through the roots below.

The bird is twenty feet above the killing zone. It is flailing, its metallic cry sending shockwaves through my bones. I dive, extending the bone hook.

The impact jolts my entire frame. I hook the carapace of the bird, and the sudden weight drags me down. My skin flares bright white—*Panic*—as I spiral dangerously close to the grasping tendrils of the floor. The green mass below reaches up, sensing the fresh meat. Beetles the size of hands launch themselves into the air, clicking their mandibles, a chaotic static that buzzes in my knees.

I yank the winch line. My partner above feels the tension and begins to haul. I am a pendulum, swinging back and forth over the abyss, the heavy carcass thrashing at the end of my hook, dripping hot fluids that sizzle against my suit.

We rise. The purple lightning snaps at my heels, crawling up the air, hungry for the conductivity of my bone. I tuck my legs, feeling the vibration of the Titan Tree’s groaning wood returning as we near the bark.

Hands—grey, slick, and firm—grab my harness and haul me onto the platform. We collapse, the heavy bird thudding against the woven vines. The danger is passed.

My partner touches my shoulder. His skin pulses a slow, deep violet—the color of the crawl-light, the color of reverence. *Good catch.*

We do not speak. We do not cheer. In this oven-heat, amidst the deafening physical roar of the world, we sit in the damp quiet of our success. Later, there will be meat. Later, there will be the fermented Dew-Honey, stolen from the highest leaves, to make our skin glow with stuporous light.

But for now, I unstrap my mask just enough to wipe the sweat from my eyes, careful not to inhale the spore-laden air. I look out over the railing. The locusts swirl like smoke. The ground breathes. I am a ghost on the flank of a giant, vibrating with the silence of the survivor.
