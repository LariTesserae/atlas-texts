I am the **Weaver of Husks**.

I exist in the upper tiers, where the settlement thins out and the grey-purple stone of the geode’s wall becomes the dominant feature. My station is a scaffolding of dried woody vines lashed into the natural crevices of the rock, suspended halfway between the humid, breathing floor of the hollow and the distant, lightless ceiling. Down below, the colony is a steaming, rhythmic engine of consumption; up here, it is quiet, save for the skittering of solitary beetles and the wind that moans through the aperture.

I am a Dry-Blood, but I am distinct. My skin has gone past the leathery toughness of my kin; it is calcified, deeply fissured, and pale as the dust itself. Years of exposure to the drier air of the upper wall have drawn the excess oil from my hide, leaving me armored in a living ceramic. I smell not of sage and musk, but of dry bone and the sharp, acrid tang of the preservative salts I rub into my hands.

My role is dictated by the singular law of the hollow: **Nothing is wasted, and nothing leaves.**

When a Holower dies, or when a great roosting bird falls from the ceiling, or when a massive sentinel-plant succumbs to the slow rot of age, they do not rot into the earth—there is no earth for them to rot into, only dust and stone. They do not burn; fire is a dangerous luxury here, consuming the precious oxygen and moisture of the air. Instead, they mummify. They desiccate in the heat, turning into light, hollow, incredibly strong replicas of their living forms.

I am the one who catches them before they fall.

I am a spider of the vertical slums. I spend my days suspended on ropes thinner than a thumb, moving with the agonizing slowness of a climbing vine. I harvest the dead.

Using hooks made of bone and needles of stone, I sew the mummified remains of my people into the architecture. A grandmother who has passed becomes a support beam for a new platform, her hollow bones filled with a lightweight foam of crushed mineral to strengthen them. A fallen giant-bird, its flesh long since eaten or dried into jerky, becomes the arch for a bridge, its wingspan spread wide and lashed with fiber to create a walkway.

I am the architect of the city’s expansion. I do not build *up* from the ground; I build *out* from the ancestors.

My hands are stained dark with the pigments I use—extracted from the crushed bodies of the neon beetles. I paint the husks, not for decoration, but to code them. A stripe of orange means the bone is brittle and should not bear weight. A wash of blue means the material is treated to repel the burrowing worms that try to eat the dry cellulose.

In this place, I am the boundary between the "wet" and the "dry." The living Hollowers fear the dry; to them, it is death. To me, it is structure. I touch the desiccated face of a man who died fifty years ago, feeling the papery skin, the ridges of the skull. I secure him to the living vine of a cactus, merging the fluid, green life of the plant with the rigid, grey permanence of the ancestor.

The noise of the hive reaches me as a vibration, a constant thrumming in the soles of my feet. I rarely descend into the soup anymore. I have adapted to the thinner, cooler air of the ceiling. My eyes, large and dark, are adapted to the near-darkness, able to see the faint shimmer of a moth’s wing against the stone.

I sleep in a hammock woven from the dried tendons of great beasts, slung between the ribcages of two ancient, petrified lizard-gods that I mounted into the rock years ago. I dream of falling.

I am the keeper of the static. While the rest of the hollow franticallly eats, drinks, and breeds in a desperate bid to hold onto their moisture, I preserve what remains when the water is gone. I am the memory of the colony, built into the very walls. When the wind howls through the aperture, the hollow structures of the city—the bones of the fathers, the wings of the birds—sing a low, mournful whistle.

I listen to that song. It is the only sound here that is not a scream for survival. It is the sound of endurance.
