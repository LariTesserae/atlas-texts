I find myself at the **Outer Rim**, the place where the great basalt mesa shears off into the abyss. Here, the wind is not a mournful whisper but a constant, low-frequency hum that vibrates in the hollows of my chest. I am perched on a natural outcropping of obsidian, smoothed by centuries of dripping water into a shape that cups my body like a dark hand.

I am a **Channel-Walker**.

In the dense, humid silence of this archipelago, where the stone sweats eternally, water is not merely life; it is the only architect. The settlement behind me, with its low-slung longhouses and damp plazas, exists only because the water is guided. Without us, the moss would grow too thick, heavy with water, and pull the timber down. The silver rivulets would pool under the foundations, turning the solid stone into slick mud, and eventually, the town would slide quietly into the clouds below.

That is where I come in.

I am not young, nor am I old in the way you measure time. Here, age is measured by the depth of the callus on one’s fingertips and the stillness of one’s breath. My skin is the color of wet slate, grey-brown and cool to the touch, absorbing the pale, diffused light of the dome above. I am broad and low to the ground, my center of gravity aligned with the sheer verticality of my home. My legs are knotted ropes of muscle, built from a lifetime of crouching and balancing on slick edges, but it is my hands that define me.

They are wide, spatulate things, the palms rough as sharkskin, the fingers tapered but strong. They are designed for friction. I can hang from a vertical face of wet basalt by nothing more than the tips of my fingers, my body suspended over the void, feeling the pulse of the rock through the nerves in my hands.

Right now, I am suspended exactly so.

I hang thirty feet below the lip of the Upper Plaza, watching the water. My task is simple and glacial. A crack has formed in the obsidian face—a small, jagged wound where the stone is shedding its newborn dust. The water has found this crack and is exploiting it, widening the fissure with the relentless patience of erosion. If left unchecked, this trickle will become a torrent, and the section of the walkway above will weaken.

I do not rush. Rushing creates noise. Rushing is the precursor to falling.

I reach out with my right hand, finding a tiny lip of stone, dry and warm despite the mist. I pull myself closer to the crack. I have brought with me a mixture of crushed volcanic dust and fibrous moss pulp, stored in a gourd tied to my waist. I take a handful of the grey, gritty paste. It is warm, almost alive.

With the precision of a surgeon, though moving with the speed of a drifting cloud, I begin to pack the paste into the crack. I work the moss deep into the stone, sealing the wound. The moss will drink the water, swell, and knit with the stone, becoming part of the cliff face. It is a healing, not a repair.

As I work, I am aware of the silence. It is a heavy, physical presence. Behind me, above the ledge, I can hear the soft *shush* of a resident walking across the plaza. They are moving slowly, their steps measured, testing the slick cobblestones. I recognize the rhythm of the gait—it is old Kael. He is likely heading to the observation point to watch the mist clear. We do not call out to each other. To break the silence with a voice would be to shatter the glass dome that holds our world.

I finish the packing. I smooth the surface with my thumb, leaving no trace of my presence, only a patch of fresh, dark moss that looks as if it has grown there naturally.

I push off from the wall, swinging gently, and catch the ladder of polished wood that leads back to the plaza. My feet find the rungs, slippery with condensation, but my grip is absolute. I climb back up, hauling my dense body over the railing.

I am now standing in the plaza. The air is thick, tasting of minerals and wet green. I crouch by the railing, letting my body heat regulate. I am not tired. My metabolism burns slow and hot, fueled by the thick, succulent leaves we eat.

I look out across the archipelago. The sea of jagged peaks stretches out, a forest of grey knives wrapped in emerald velvet. The mist is thinning, revealing the spires, then swallowing them up again, a slow, rhythmic breathing of the atmosphere.

I am content. I am not a hero or a leader. I am a mechanism of the world, just like the moss, just like the weeping stone. I am the hand that slows the decay, the silence that keeps the water in its place. I am the Channel-Walker, and for now, as the pale dome of the sky begins to bruise with the first hint of evening purple, I simply crouch and watch the water bead up on the railing, counting the seconds before it falls.
