I would be found in the suspension, the space between the rising steam and the falling rain.

I am not on the ground—the ground is a liar here. It shifts while you sleep, the "yesterday" earth turning over to reveal a raw, gray tomorrow. To stand on the gravel is to trust a foundation that intends to consume you. So, I do not stand. I hang.

**Who I Am**

I am a Tuner, but not one of the laborers who wrestle with the iron levers or grease the treads of the grazing excavators. I am a **Resonator**.

My physiology has taken the adaptation of my people to its logical extreme. While the average Tuner has high cheekbones to shield the ear, my mastoid bones have fused and thickened, ridging like the armor of a newt. My throat is a labyrinth of heavy, calcified cartilage, capable not just of sub-vocalization, but of emitting a low, thrumming counter-frequency that can calm a vibrating pipe or shatter a blockage of ice in a valve.

I am dressed in layers of oil-treated wool that have felt the spray of the Sump for decades. The fabric is heavy, stiff with grease and damp, dark as the mud below. I smell of wet iron and the sharp, electric tang of the Velvet-Strangler moss.

**Where I Am**

My home is a hammock strung in the "dead air" between the towering Smokestack of the North Reach and the rusted hulk of a decommissioned gear-shaft. It is a vertical clutter, a shanty suspended three hundred feet above the gravel sea. This is the interstitial space, the niche where the architecture of the machine and the biology of the cliff intersect.

The wind here is a constant, wet moan. It catches the steam rising from the excavators below and turns it into a warm, heavy fog that presses against the corrugated metal walls of my shack. I live inside the constant *clack-hiss*, so deep in the rhythm that silence would feel like an amputation.

**My Existence**

My role is to listen to the erosion.

The world here grinds itself down, and the plants grow fast to eat the remains. But this cycle creates dissonance. When a root of the Green Rust bores too deep into a pressure vein, the pitch of the engine changes. It sharpens. It whines.

I spend my days hanging over the railing of my catwalk, pressing my hand against the cold, sweating iron of the stack. I feel the tremor in the metal. I close my eyes—eyes that are perpetually narrowed against the damp—and I isolate the sound of a single fern unfurling against a piston from the roar of the entire Gear-Sump.

When I find a dissonance—a vibration that is too fast, too hungry—I sing.

I open my throat and release a low, guttural hum that travels down my arm, into the pipe, and through the veins of the machine. It is a negotiation. I warn the root. I tell the vine that the iron is too hard, the heat too great. Sometimes, the plant retreats, curling its tips away from the vibration. Sometimes, I must increase the thrum until the stone cracks elsewhere, diverting the plant’s attention.

I am the conductor of a symphony of rust and respiration.

At night, when the spotlights from the searchlights sweep through the haze and the Copper-Cliffe Rooks tuck their metallic wings, I sit by the warmth of a steam vent. I boil a broth of harvested moss, glowing faintly in the dark. I eat the thing that tries to eat my home, absorbing the engine's breath into my own body.

I am wet, I am cold, and I am vibrating. I am the note that holds the chaos together.
