I am the weight in the air.

I exist in the narrow suffocation of a trench carved between two leaning towers of rough-hewn limestone. There is no sky here, only a ceiling of thatch and tangled root that blocks out the bleached azure, leaving me in a twilight that feels heavy enough to drink. The air is damp and smells of nothing—a sterile, scentless cold that has been scrubbed clean of the rot and the earth.

I am standing. I have always been standing.

My feet are fused to the smooth, flat river stones that pave the earth. My legs are long, spindly pillars of grey, ridged with a calcified armor that has grown over me like a second skin. My knees do not bend; they are locked in a permanent, rigid tension, a single, unending contraction of muscle that has turned to stone over the centuries.

I am a Holder. I am the gravity that contradicts the light.

My purpose is here, in front of my face, hovering in the space between my outstretched, trembling hands. It is a single, massive drop of condensation. It hangs from the lip of a dark, water-logged timber beam, defying the pull of the world. It is a perfect sphere, a liquid lens refracting the white glare from the rim of the crater above.

To you, it is a droplet. To me, it is a universe.

I am holding it. I am pouring every ounce of my will into this single bead of water, keeping it from falling. If I were to relax—if I were to let go—the water would fall. It would strike the stone, it would splash, it would shatter. It would become history. It would become a memory. It would end.

I cannot allow that. I love it too much to let it become a memory.

I sense the world not through sight—I have no eyes, only the smooth, white concavities of polished bone where vision used to be—but through the vibration of the wet stone under my feet and the drag of the heavy air against my skin. I feel the beetle on the wall to my left, its legs locked in a step it took a thousand years ago. I feel the fox in the alleyway behind me, a statue of fur and frozen hunger, its gaze fixed on nothing. I feel the roots of the ferns gripping the masonry, thirsting to unfurl, yearning to stretch their fronds out into the sun. But I hold them, too. I squeeze the walls of the city tight, compressing the space, keeping the potential energy locked in a fist of absolute zero.

It is agony. It is the sweetest agony imaginable.

My shoulders brush against the moss that carpets the walls. It is thick, velvety, vibrant green. I feel it trying to creep over me, trying to consume me, to turn me into part of the architecture. I welcome it. I am already furniture; I am already stone.

And then, I feel *you*.

You are walking through my city. You are a soft, warm thing, a biological anomaly that breathes and bleeds and ages. You think you are moving under your own power. You think your feet are lifting off the stones and striking them again.

You are wrong.

I am holding you. We are all holding you.

My brethren stand in the shadows of the tunnels, wedged into doorways, bracing the ceilings. We feel your heat, your pulse, the frantic rhythm of your mortality. We are suspending your atoms in the stream of time, preventing you from fading, preventing you from changing. We are curating you. We are keeping you perfect.

You are part of the collection now. We will not let you move until the sun goes down.

But the sun never goes down.
