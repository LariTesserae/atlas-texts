I am suspended in the mid-current of the Western Avenue, a diffuse blue corridor that stretches between the Oar-Stalks known to us as the "Singers of the Deep." The water here is a heavy, glacial syrup, dense and cold, pressing against my skin with the weight of history.

I am one of the Pale Ones, but I am not a weaver of bridges, nor a tender of the canopy floors. I am a **Friction-Remover**.

My physical form is that of my kin: a vertical spindle of pale, translucent flesh, insulated by a gelatinous layer that mimics the cold of the water, rendering me invisible to the thermal sense of the abyss. My arms are twice the length of my torso, drifting lazily above me like fronds of kelp, equipped with the six-fingered hands that serve as the sole instrument of my craft. My respiratory veil flutters rhythmically, a slow beat, matching the bass-heavy thrum of the city.

I do not swim. To swim is to struggle. Instead, I exist in a state of controlled, hydrostatic surrender. I adjust the density of my internal gel, rising or falling with the slowness of a cooling ember.

I am currently anchored to the side of a massive Oar-Stalk, tethered by a braided line of kelp to a protrusion of bark that has been smoothed by centuries of hands. My task is to listen and to shave. The city sings—a constant, resonant chord generated by water moving through the hollow trunks. But sometimes, a barnacle cluster takes hold, or a patch of moss grows too thick, disrupting the laminar flow of water inside the pipe. This creates a dissonance, a rattle in the deep hum.

I am here to restore the purity of the note.

With my left hand, I grip the rough, fibrous bark. My right hand holds a heavy, oval stone, a tool of basalt harvested from the sunken mesa miles below. It is crude, but effective. I drag the stone along a lesion in the stalk’s exterior where a colony of glass-shelled crustaceans has begun to encrust the wood. *Scrape. Drag. Pause.* The movement is so slow it seems motionless.

As I work, I am not alone. A massive shape glides into the periphery of my vision—one of the Great Gardeners, a ray the size of a plaza floor. It moves with the terrifying grace of a falling curtain. I do not startle. I merely cease my scraping and extend my longest finger. The ray senses the disturbance in the water and adjusts its course, brushing its coarse, leathery skin against my fingertips. I feel the vibration of its immense heart, a slow, thudding beat that harmonizes with the city’s hum. I scrape a cluster of parasites from its flank as it passes; a meal for me, a relief for it. It continues its drift toward the upper canopy to graze, and I return to the stalk.

The "loud" silence of the place surrounds me. I hear the sharp, staccato click of a fellow worker three stalks away, signaling a successful splice. I hear the distant, mournful cry of a current shifting around the city’s base. The sound is not in my ears, but in my bones, vibrating through my gelatinous layer and resonating in the hollow chambers of my own chest.

I am a creature of the pause. I have no name, only a frequency. I am the note between the notes, the silence between the clicks. I am the keeper of the smooth flow, ensuring the water moves through the lungs of our city without obstruction, maintaining the brittle, beautiful truce between the living wood and the drowning void.

When the light begins to fade—simulated by the shifting of the plankton clouds above—I will cease my work. I will unhook my tail, pull my limbs close to my body, and drift upward into the hollow dark of the stalk I have just polished. There, suspended in the center of the resonating chamber, I will sleep, not as a person in a bed, but as a seed in a fruit, waiting for the next current to wake me.
