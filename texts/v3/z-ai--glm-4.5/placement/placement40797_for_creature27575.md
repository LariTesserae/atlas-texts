I am suspended in the amber drift, a thought held in breath.

At first, there is only the sensation of the medium—the heavy, mineral warmth that presses against me from all sides. It is not a passive environment; it is a thick, slow syrup that demands surrender. To struggle against it is to exhaust oneself in seconds, so I do not struggle. I let my density equalize with the water until I am neither sinking nor rising, a distinct entity of flesh existing in perfect equilibrium with the void.

I am aware of light, but it is not the harsh, questioning glare of the surface sun. It is a filtered, cathedral luminescence. The beams strike downward in distinct pillars, illuminating the dance of the marine snow. I drift between these pillars, a shadow passing through the consecrated dust of the deep.

I feel the need for orientation, for an "up" or "down," and in seeking it, I realize where I am. I am not floating in the open blue. I am clinging to the verticality.

Beneath my belly—soft, vulnerable, spread wide like a damp canvas—is a texture that defies the slickness of the ocean. It is coarse, fibrous, and immensely thick. I am gripping the shaggy, moss-pelted back of a Velvet-Lipped Creeper. The beast is immense, a living island the size of a carriage, inching its way up the sheer face of an obsidian needle. I can feel the rhythmic, grinding contraction of its immense foot against the glass beneath us, a vibration that travels through its hide and into my bones. It is a slow, tectonic heartbeat.

*Thump. Shift. Thump. Shift.*

I am a parasite of sorts, or perhaps a symbiote. I am too small to be a gardener and too large to be an ant. I am a **Ridge-Walker**.

My form is adapted to this specific niche. I possess no shell, for a shell would be shattered by the accidental brush of a needle or the crushing weight of the Creeper if it were to roll. Instead, I am composed entirely of dense, muscular hydrostats—bundles of tissue that can firm up to become rigid or loosen to become fluid. My skin is a mirror of the obsidian, a matte, dark grey, roughened like pumice. I am camouflaged not against the water, but against the very rock my host climbs.

I have twelve limbs, splayed outward. They are not legs in the traditional sense, but rather anchors, tipped with suction discs that can adhere to the coarse fur of the Creeper or, if necessary, the glass of the rock itself. I am the spider in this mossy web.

My purpose here is singular: I am the look-out.

The Creeper is blind to the immediate danger. It senses the chemistry of the water, finding the nutrients, but it cannot see the geometric trap of the landscape. The Obsidian Needles are not just vertical; they are serrated, interlocking, and treacherous. A wrong turn by the Creeper could pinch it between two closing walls of glass, crushing the life out of it—and me along with it.

I am the eyes.

I lift my frontal lobes, extending sensory stalks that taste the pressure differential of the water ahead. I read the currents. The water moves with a "deliberate grace," yes, but when it squeezes between two tight spires, it accelerates. I feel the tug of that acceleration. If the pull is too strong, the gap is too narrow. I signal the Creeper.

How does one command a mountain? Through chemistry and touch.

I release a specific pheromone, a bitter oil that seeps from my vents directly into the Creeper’s hide. It is a signal of *Stop. Turn. Slip left.*

I feel the massive beast beneath me pause. The grinding rhythm halts. The immense muscular foot contracts, pulling away from the lethal edge of the glass shard. We drift sideways, carried by the heavy current, until the danger passes. Then, I release a second chemical—a sweet, saline compound that signals *Safe*. The grinding resumes. *Thump. Shift.*

We are climbing a spire that juts out into the open ocean, a solitary finger of black accusing the sky. Around us, the neon-kelp forest billows. It is a green storm, vibrant and translucent. Through the waving fronds, I see them: the Lantern-Jellies.

They drift past us like tetrahedral ghosts. One of them, large as a man, pulses with a soft, internal rhythm. Its light is a pale blue, contrasting with the aggressive green of the kelp. It ignores us. We are too slow, too textured, to be of interest. It is hunting the invisible clouds of plankton that swirl in the eddies.

I watch it with a mixture of envy and pity. It is free, unburdened by the need to cling. But it is also fragile. If the current shifts and slams it against the obsidian, its geometric perfection will shatter. I am rough; I will endure.

As we ascend higher, the light grows brighter. The "tropical noon" penetrates even here, turning the water into a glowing, golden haze.

Suddenly, the water changes. The "weather" is shifting.

I feel it before I see it—a displacement of mass so great it creates a low-frequency hum that rattles my insides. The heavy water begins to churn, not with violence, but with a heavy, rolling swell.

I retract my sensory stalks and flatten my body against the Creeper’s mossy back, digging my twelve anchors deep into the fur. I become a mere wart on the beast’s hide.

From the gloom below, a shape emerges. It is vast, a descending glider of hammered copper and teal—the patina of ancient bronze. It is a Copper-Back, a grazer of the deep.

It does not see us. To a creature the size of a house, we are merely part of the rock face. It glides past, inches from the spire, its wingtips brushing the ends of the neon-kelp. The sound is like canvas tearing in a windstorm—a slow, heavy rip.

As it passes, its mouth, a vacuum-like slit on its underside, opens. It inhales the cliff face. It strips the rock of the algae, the bacterial slick, and anything else clinging to it. We are safe only because we are on the vertical climb, just above its reach, and because my host’s fur is too thick and fibrous to be easily sucked away.

The draft from its passage is immense. It threatens to peel me off the Creeper’s back. I cling tighter, my muscles locking into rigidity, becoming stone myself. The Creeper halts, sensing the predator-grazer, clamping its foot down tight against the obsidian, waiting for the storm to pass.

For a long time—minutes, hours, time is fluid here—the shadow of the Copper-Back eclipses the sun. We are frozen in a twilight of metal. I can hear the grinding of its internal plates, the sound of a mountain eating.

Then, slowly, the copper giant ascends, moving toward the summit to graze on the upper ridges. The light returns. The water settles.

The danger passes.

I release my grip, softening my body back into flesh. I taste the water again. The path ahead is clear. The obsidian ridge widens into a plateau.

I release the sweet compound. *Safe.*

The Creeper begins to move again. *Thump. Shift.*

We continue our ascent. We are carrying the forest on our backs, climbing a needle of glass in a sea of sun. I am the small, rough mind that ensures we do not fall. I am the ghost in the cathedral, tending to the quiet, inevitable rising of the stone.
