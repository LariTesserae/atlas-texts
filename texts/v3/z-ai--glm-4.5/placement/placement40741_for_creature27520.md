I am anchored to the Harrow, but I am not of its flesh. I do not ride upon its back like a Hitcher, nor do I dart in its wake like a Ghost-Rider. I am embedded in the creature’s forward rim, the leading edge of the living disk that cleaves the heavy brine.

I am the **Lithocyst**.

To look at me, you would see nothing but a slight irregularity in the Harrow’s armor—a knot of calcified ridges, rough as sandstone, indistinguishable from the barnacles and corrosion that scar the beast’s hide. I am a squat, conical organism, no larger than a buoy, fused directly to the Harrow’s calcified plate. I do not have eyes; in the 0.4 visibility of the deep, sight is a lie. Instead, I possess a single, massive internal chamber filled with a viscous, gas-heavy oil that vibrates in sympathy with the ocean’s infrasonic thrum.

I am the **Pantry of the Deep**.

The Harrow is a grazer, dependent on the bacterial lawns that grow on the smooth, sphere-like boulders of the plain. But the plain is vast, and the boulders are rare, scattered like marbles across a floor of endless, sterile clay. There are stretches of silt that take the Harrow centuries to cross, wastelands where the gray mud offers nothing but the taste of ancient dust.

This is where I serve my purpose.

While the Harrow drifts, consuming the living slime of a boulder, I am feeding. My exterior is lined with thousands of microscopic cilia, finer than spider silk, which comb the viscous water (2.7) as the Harrow moves. I do not eat the silt. I sieve the water for the drifting, dormant spores of the chemosynthetic bacteria—the "seeds" of the lawns that float in the marine snow, waiting for a surface to cling to.

I pull these invisible ghosts from the dark and compress them. Inside my calcified shell, I culture them. I maintain a warm, nutrient-rich slurry in my gut, a microscopic bioreactor where the bacteria multiply, dormant but alive. I am a living battery of potential energy, storing the harvest of the current against the coming famine.

I feel the world through the Harrow’s nervous system. I feel the sudden, violent shove of a density current before it hits. I feel the vibration of the cilia-legs rippling beneath us. We move at the pace of geology. Sometimes we sit still for decades, waiting for the turbulence to settle.

When the Harrow signals hunger—a specific, chemical tremor that runs through the armor to which I am fused—I know we have entered the gray wastes. There are no boulders here. There is only the flat, crushing emptiness.

Then, I open.

A series of muscular valves dilate at my apex, and I release a dense, heavy cloud of concentrated bacteria into the water. It is not a swarm; it is a slick, an oily discharge that hangs in the sluggish current. The Harrow rotates its massive body, diving downward, and its grinding mouth scoops up the cloud I have provided.

It is not the feast of a fresh lawn, but it is enough. It is the difference between moving forward and starving in the dark.

I am the silence between the beats of the Harrow’s heart. I am the reason the journey continues. I am the keeper of the seeds, the architect of the invisible harvest. In a world of prohibitive energy, I am the savings account.

I feel a tremor now. The pressure is dropping. A density current is roaring toward us from the unseen distance. The Harrow slows, flattening itself against the clay. I seal my valves, locking my precious cargo deep inside my calcified shell. I become a stone. I become nothing.

I wait for the avalanche to wash over us. I wait for the silence to return. I wait for the time to feed the mountain once again.
