The air here is heavy, a humid, tangible blanket that sits just below the temperature of the human body. It is warm enough to encourage a constant, slick sweat, yet cool enough that the moisture in the air refuses to evaporate, choosing instead to condense on every available surface. This is a place below the true ground level, a sunken hollow or perhaps a massive artificial crater, though the edges of it are lost in the gloom and the density of the growth.

Light is dim and amber, filtering down from high, unseen sources like the glow through a stained-glass roof covered in grime. It catches on the perpetual mist that rises from the floor, creating shafts of hazy gold that illuminate a world constructed entirely of living matter.

This is not a forest in the wild sense, but a constructed city of vegetation. The "buildings" here are grown, not built. They are dense, leafy blocks shaped by centuries of pruning and grafting into rectangular, urban forms. The walls are made of woven liana and hardened moss, thick as brick and insulated with layers of fuzzy fern. The streets are not paved with stone but with interlocking root systems, packed so hard over eons that they are smooth and cool underfoot, worn polished by the slow traffic of... well, nothing. There are no animals here. No birds sing, no insects chatter. It is a biologically silent city, devoid of animal breath.

Yet it is not quiet.

It is loud, a constant industrial drone that vibrates in the chest. The sound comes from the water. This is a wet landscape, rated high for saturation, and the water is the engine of the city. Great wooden conduits—massive, hollowed-out logs resembling iron pipes—burst from the sides of the vegetative structures. They are jointed with collars of resin and wrapped in copper wire that has long since tarnished to a verdigris green. Through these pipes, water rushes with immense pressure. The sound is a rhythmic *thrum-hiss, thrum-hiss*, the heartbeat of a hydraulic system buried deep within the plant-mass.

The scale of the place is that of a cathedral or a small town square. You can see the "architecture" rising around you in a tight, claustrophobic cluster. There are no gaps between the buildings; they grow into one another, bridging the narrow alleyways with arches of ivy and curtains of hanging creepers. The density is total. In some places, the vegetation is so thick it forms a solid mass of green and brown, a labyrinth of chlorophyll and celluloid that seems to suffocate the very air.

The industrial nature of the flora is subtle but undeniable. It is not a cybernetic fusion, but a biological efficiency that mimics industry. Large, spherical gourds hang from the ceilings of the root-arches, pulsating slowly as they filter silt from the air. Ventilation shafts, carved from the trunks of long-dead mega-flora, exhale clouds of warm steam. The steam rises to join the condensation on the ceiling, eventually dripping back down in a relentless, warm rain that keeps the moss sopping wet.

Everywhere you look, water is weeping from the rock—or rather, from the plant-substitute that covers the rock. The walls of the cavern, if they exist, are entirely hidden behind a carpet of epiphytes that drink the condensation and channel it down into the city’s plumbing. The moisture level is such that slime molds thrive in the corners, glowing with a faint, bioluminescent bacteria that provides the only accent light to the amber gloom.

The sense of erosion is one of settled balance. Nothing here is crumbling; rather, everything has been smoothed into place. The edges of the leaf-walls are rounded, the root-streets are rutted not by decay but by wear. It feels as if this place has existed in this exact state for millennia, a slow-motion factory turning sunlight and water into... nothing consumable by animals. It produces only oxygen and more plant mass.

The dynamic is agonizingly slow. You can watch a large, tendril-like pulley system made of twisted vine lowering a bucket of water from a high terrace. It moves so gradually that the movement is almost imperceptible, taking perhaps an hour to descend three feet. The processes here—growth, filtration, fluid transport—operate on a geological timescale compressed into a small room.

It is a claustrophobic, loud, warm, and thoroughly vegetative pocket of industry. It feels like the boiler room of a jungle, a place where the mechanics of nature are stripped of their beauty and laid bare as a functioning, humid, and deafening machine. The air tastes of wet peat and ozone. There is nowhere to sit that is dry, and nowhere to look that does not see the intricate, packed, living mesh of the Green Works.
