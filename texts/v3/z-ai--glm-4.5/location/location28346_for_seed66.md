The sky here hangs low and heavy, a perpetual lid of humid, bronze-tinted cloud cover. It is warm, a stifling, tropical heat that doesn't so much burn as it bakes, holding the air in a static, thick embrace. You are high up—perhaps three kilometers above sea level—but the air lacks the thin crispness of altitude. Instead, it is dense, tasting of wet iron and ozone.

This is the Riven Shelf, a landscape that refuses to distinguish between the geological and the architectural.

Stretching to the curvature of the planet is a singular, unbroken city of industry. It is not a collection of buildings, but a single, sprawling organism of concrete, pitted iron, and basalt. The scale is continental; you could walk for months and never see a horizon free of smokestacks, cooling towers, and the ribcages of massive refineries. There are no streets in the traditional sense, only layers—tier upon tier of catwalks, ventilation shafts, and plazas laid one over the other, creating a density so profound that the ground below is buried kilometers deep under the weight of its own history.

There is no soil here, and there are certainly no plants. The vegetation score is a flatline zero. No grass cracks the pavement, no vines cling to the girders. The world is strictly mineral and synthetic. The only color comes from the oxidation of the metal—rust in hues of ochre, maroon, and burnt orange—and the slick, dark sheen of wet stone. It is a post-biological aesthetic, a world that has moved beyond the need for chlorophyll, sustenance drawn from the grid and the atmosphere itself.

Water is the defining sculptor. It does not fall in violent storms but exists as a constant, weeping presence. The atmosphere is supersaturated; condensation drips incessantly from the undersides of every pipe and bridge. The great masses of rock and concrete are always dark, stained by streaks of runoff that look like tears on the face of a giant. Pools of standing water collect in the depressions of the machinery, oily and iridescent, reflecting the smoggy light above. The sound of this place is a low, throbbing roar—the hum of unseen engines deep within the earth, mixed with the endless *drip-drip-splash* of a world that cannot dry.

And yet, it is not dead. It is infested.

The fauna is overwhelming, a teeming, cacophonous scurrying of life that has adapted to the endless concrete. Without predators or plants, the ecosystem is based on scavenging and thermal energy. The air is thick with buzzing, clicking wings—swarms of metal-colored beetles and leathery birds that nest in the ventilation shafts. Larger creatures, reptilian things with scales that mimic rivet heads, haul themselves along the warm conduits of the factories. They are loud. The screech of the swarm competes with the groan of expanding metal, creating a wall of sound that never abates. Life here is frantic and overcrowded, a biological infection coating the surface of a dormant machine.

The technology is industrial, ancient, and reliable. Massive pistons the size of skyscrapers move with agonizing slowness, plunging into the earth and rising again over the course of hours. Great conveyor belts, wide enough to be highways, stretch across the valleys between factories, though many are still, carrying nothing but rust and bird droppings. The lights are normal—daylight filters through the smog as a flat, grey-white noon, while the night is lit by the dull orange glow of furnace doors and the flicker of dying neon signs.

Everything here is established, settled into a deep equilibrium. The erosion is not destructive but polishing; the sharp edges of the infrastructure have been worn down by centuries of wind and rain into rounded, soft shapes, like river stones. The city feels as if it has been here since the tectonic plates shifted, a permanent feature of the geology.

Change is imperceptibly slow. The dynamic is glacial. You cannot see the factories working, but you can feel the vibration in the soles of your feet. The pistons shift so gradually they seem motionless until you look away and look back. The swarms of creatures breed and die in frantic cycles, but the city around them remains fixed in a slow, patient inhalation. It is a loud, wet, rusting paradise, a vast, heavy machine that has cultivated its own jungle of steel and scavengers, existing in a warm, humid eternal now.
