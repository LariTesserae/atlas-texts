The sound hits you first—a vast, seismic thrumming that vibrates in the hollows of your bones. It is not the chaotic noise of a city or the crashing of waves, but a single, unchanging chord of industry. It is a loudness that has long since ceased to be a noise and has become a physical pressure, a blanket of heavy air that presses against the skin.

Here, the horizon is a straight line cut into the warm, grey haze. The elevation is low, a sprawling, flat basin that stretches on for miles, bounded only by the curvature of the atmosphere. The light is normal, a pale, heatless sun hanging in a sky the color of tarnished silver, but the air is thick with warmth. It is a swamp-like heat without the swamp; a humid, respiratory warmth that smells of ozone, wet iron, and ancient stone.

There is no soil here, only the great, paved expanse of the plain. It looks like a desert of smoothed concrete and basalt, weathered by eons into a topography of gentle, rolling hills and shallow, perfectly circular depressions. The erosion is extreme, but it has settled into a deep equilibrium. Every sharp edge has been ground down by the damp, abrasive air, leaving a landscape of rounded, soft forms that feel strangely organic despite their material.

This is a world without green. The vegetation rating is a near-zero; there are no trees, no grass, no algae. Instead, the "ecology" is mineral. Great clusters of crystalline growths erupt from the ground, fused into the bedrock, sparkling with a refractive, inner light. Fungal-like columns of rust and oxidized copper stand like silent sentinels, their surfaces slow-growing and textured like brain coral. This is a post-biological world, or perhaps a pre-biological one, where the only things that grow are ores and oxides.

Water is present but elusive, manifesting as a constant, cloying dampness. The air is saturated with moisture that never quite rains. Instead, condensation slicks every surface, gathering in the ruts and worn channels of the stone plain. Pockets of water shimmer in the hollows, black and mirror-still, reflecting the grey sky. The moisture seems to seep directly out of the rock itself, as if the land is sweating.

As you move across the open, sparse terrain, the source of the tech parameter becomes clear. The landscape is not merely rock; it is machine. The "hills" are the weathered casings of massive, buried engines. The "valleys" are the gaps between colossal, frozen pistons the size of skyscrapers. This is an industrial landscape on a geological scale. It is a factory floor that has expanded until it became the world, vast and open, devoid of walls or ceilings.

There are no buildings in the human sense—no houses, no towers, no distinct structures to occupy. The "built" environment is gone, subsumed by the sheer scale of the infrastructure. Instead, the tech is integrated into the terrain itself. Great ribs of pipe, as thick as rivers, protrude from the ground, arching overhead and sinking back into the earth, coated in layers of patina and lichen. They do not gleam; they are dull, heavy, and ancient.

And yet, they function. The dynamic is absolute zero—nothing changes. The motion of the world is too slow to perceive. The pistons may rise, but they move at the rate of tectonic drift. The steam may vent, but the plumes hang in the air as stationary clouds. The sound, that deafening roar, is the sound of a billion gears turning in perpetual, locked synchronization. It is a system that has reached a perfect, timeless stasis. It hums at a frequency that makes the teeth ache.

Fauna is rare, and what does exist is hard to spot. Occasionally, a shadow detaches itself from the darkness beneath a pipe—something spindly and multi-limbed, constructed perhaps of scrap metal or evolved from the mineral crust. They move with jerky, clockwork motions, scavenging the condensation or feeding on the slow-rust of the great machines. They do not call out; there is no point in trying to be heard over the thrum. They are solitary, spectral figures in the mist.

The weirdness of the place lies in its denial of history. It feels as if it has always been this way and always will be. It is a place where the distinction between the natural and the artificial has dissolved. The mountains are gears; the rain is condensation from a cooling tower; the wind is the exhaust of a ventilation shaft. It is a warm, wet, open-air prison of perfect efficiency, waiting for nothing, producing nothing, simply existing in a loud, eternal, metal present.
