The roar does not stop. It is not the gusting, chaotic scream of a storm, nor the rhythmic crashing of ocean tides. It is a single, unwavering note—a colossal, baritone pressure that seems to emanate from the rock itself rather than the air moving through it. To stand here is to stand inside the bell of a world, pressed upon by a vibration so constant that the body quickly forgets it is hearing a sound at all; it simply becomes the texture of existence, a hum that replaces silence.

This is the High Grating, a limestone plateau elevated so high that the sky is a piercing, indigo void and the sun is a relentless, white eye that bleaches the color out of stone. There is no shade here. The light is total (2.8/3), bouncing off the wet, grey pavements with a brilliance that forces the eyes into constant squints. Despite the cool bite of the thin air (1.0/3), the luminosity suggests a burning heat, creating a dissonance between skin and retina.

The ground is a study in ancient erosion (2.0/3). The limestone has not been shattered; it has been polished. Millennia of the ceaseless wind-tunnel effect have rounded the boulders into smooth, toroidal shapes—massive doughnuts of stone and long, sweeping ribs that resemble the bleached bones of leviathans. The terrain is open (1.8/3), stretching for miles in a jagged, grey ocean, interrupted only by the occasional sinkhole dropping away into darkness.

Water here is a persistent dampness (0.6/3) rather than a flow. The air is thick with fog, but it is a strange, static fog. It does not drift; it hangs suspended in standing waves of pressure, condensing into slick sheens on the rock. Every surface is dripping, glistening, slippery. Pockets of water sit in the perfectly smooth basins of the eroded stone, stagnant and clear, reflecting the blinding sky without ripple.

Life is sparse where it roots, but abundant where it flies. Vegetation (0.9/3) clings to existence in the form of "iron-moss"—tiny, rust-colored succulents that grow only in the lee of the largest stone ribs, protected from the worst of the deafening current. There is no grass, no trees, no bushes. Just the occasional tuft of grey-green grit anchoring itself to the wet limestone.

But the sky is heavy with fauna (2.2/3). The masters of this place are the Stratus-Rays. They are vast, leathery creatures with wingspans of thirty feet or more, resembling manta rays more than birds. They possess no feathers, only skin the color of storm clouds. They do not flap in this thin air; they ride the permanent, invisible currents of the roar, gliding in immense, lazy circles. They are silent, or perhaps their calls are merely swallowed by the ambient roar.

It is in their feeding that the primitive tech (0.8/3) reveals itself. The Stratus-Rays eat the mineral-rich crust that forms on the limestone, but it is too hard for their beaks to break alone. They carry stones. Clutched in the tiny, vestigial claws at the apex of their wings are smooth, heavy river rocks—unearthed from the deep sinkholes and hoisted aloft. With precise, terrifying speed, they dive-bomb the wet crust, dropping the stones like missiles to shatter the mineral layers, then swooping down to lap up the fragments. The ground around the prime feeding grounds is littered with these broken tools, a field of shattered hammers left by a civilization that never built a house.

The weirdest aspect (0.8/3) of the High Grating is the visual nature of the sound. The vibration is so intense that it affects the condensing fog. The air is striped with visible bands of mist, frozen in place by the standing acoustic waves. To walk through the landscape is to walk through invisible corridors of hard air, where the mist hangs in vertical sheets like curtains. The density of the space feels empty (0.5/3) and hollow, yet these curtains give it a phantom architecture.

There is no time here (0.3/3). The sun arcs, but the light does not change intensity. The wind never dies. The pitch of the roar never shifts. A stone dropped from a ray’s wing today shatters the crust exactly as one did ten thousand years ago. It is a deafening, bright, frozen equilibrium, a static scream that has eroded the world into a perfect, smooth eternity.
