The light here does not fall; it diffuses. It is a dim, pearlescent gloom, heavy and warm, like the air inside a greenhouse long after the sun has set. This is a low place, a hollow in the flesh of the earth, barely an acre across, enclosed by sheer walls of stone that rise up into the mist.

The defining characteristic of this space is its newness. It is a world refusing to be rounded. There is no soil here, no deep layer of loam born of centuries of decay. Instead, the ground is a pavement of raw, jagged geology. Great plates of dark basalt and obsidian have heaved upward, frozen mid-rupture, creating a topography of knife-edges and angular slabs. The erosion score is zero; nothing has been worn down. The rocks look as if they cooled yesterday, their faces sharp enough to draw blood, their edges pristine and crystalline.

Yet, the dampness is everywhere. The air holds a humidity of near saturation—a constant, sweating warmth. Because the rock is too sharp to catch water, pools collect only in the deepest fractures, forming vein-like networks of still, black water that reflect the dim gray sky. The dampness is not enough to drown the place, but enough to slick every surface with a gleaming, hazardous sheen.

Despite the hostility of the stone, life has claimed the void in a strange, architectural pattern. The vegetation is lush but sparse, a paradox resolved by the sheer scale of the individual plants. Between the towers of black rock, solitary biological monoliths rise. These are not shrubs or thickets, but isolated trees of massive, prehistoric proportions. Each one grows alone in its own small "courtyard" of stone. Their leaves are the size of dinner plates, glossy and wax-heavy, dripping condensation onto the raw geology below. They are vibrant, impossibly green, anchored by roots that pry aggressively at the fresh rock, but they stand apart from one another, respecting the empty space.

The arrangement of stone and plant gives the impression of a city. The towering, angular rock formations look like unfinished skyscrapers or ziggurats; the massive trees are the parks planted within a planned grid. It is an "urban" density of form, but entirely devoid of intent. It is a metropolis built by tectonics and botany, not hands.

The silence is profound. This is not the empty silence of a void, but the hushed, busy silence of a library or a nursery. The air is thick with the invisible motion of abundant fauna. Thousands of small, winged creatures—lizard-birds with skin like wet slate and eyes like beads of mercury—cling to the sheer rock faces. They do not sing. They flit from ledge to ledge with a dry rustle of leathery wings, hunting the swarms of silent, fat beetles that crawl over the damp obsidian.

On the ground, sleek, fur-covered mammals move with fluid grace, navigating the razor-sharp terrain with padded, wide-splayed toes. They are the color of shadows. You can see them constantly, a river of life flowing through the stone avenues, but you never hear a chirp, a bark, or a growl. The only sounds are the soft impacts of paws on stone, the wet *plink* of condensation falling from leaf to rock, and the low, steady hum of insect wings vibrating the heavy air.

It is an active, dynamic place, but the movement happens on a micro-scale or in slow motion. Vines are visibly unravelling, inching across the rock faces, searching for purchase. Small lizards are locked in slow, wrestling matches over the best heat-retaining stones. The environment feels embryonic; it is a room where a world is currently deciding what it wants to be, holding its breath, damp and warm and sharp, waiting for the edges to soften.
