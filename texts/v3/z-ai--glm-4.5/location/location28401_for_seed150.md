The air here is heavy, not with pressure, but with a suspended, freezing mist. It is a cold that seeps through layers of clothing or bark, a damp chill (0.1/3) that settles into the joints. You stand on a high plateau (1.5/3), a shelf of the world that feels less like a geological feature and more like a bruise on the skin of the planet—young, raw, and sharp (1.3/3). The stone here, where it can be seen at all, is fractured and jagged, having only recently been thrust upward, but it is rapidly being suffocated.

This is the Green Cloister.

There is no open horizon. The scale is intimate, claustrophobic (1.1/3), defined not by distant mountains but by the enclosing walls of the immediate environment. It feels like standing in the nave of a cathedral built by a god that only understands chlorophyll.

The "ground" is a deception. It gives slightly underfoot, a composite of mosses thick as mattresses and dense, interwoven root mats that have begun the process of lithification, turning dirt into a living pavement (2.9/3). There is no bare earth. Every surface is colonized. Rocks are not merely covered; they are being digested, wrapped in sheets of lichen and bryophytes so thick that the stone’s original shape is lost, softened into rounded, organic mounds.

Water is everywhere, yet it does not flow in rivers (1.5/3). It weeps. The sharp, young stones weep constant, slow tears that slide down the green flanks and disappear into the sponge-floor. The atmosphere itself is saturated, a condensing fog that clings to the high ceiling of leaves and drips in a rhythmic, hollow *plink... plink... plink...* that is the only dominant sound in a mostly quiet world (1.2/3).

The vegetation behaves with the complexity of a city (2.5/3). It is not a forest; it is a dense, stratified sprawl. Giant, hollow-stemmed reeds rise like towers, their translucent walls glowing with the dim, diffuse light of the overcast sky (1.1/3). These towers are interconnected by bridges of vine and arching fronds, creating layers of walkways and chambers. Lower down, fungal shelves grow in deliberate, overlapping rows like the shingled roofs of a crowded market district, packed so tightly (2.7/3) that there is no space to pass without brushing against their cold, leathery surfaces. The "buildings" here grow in spirals and hexagons, biological efficiencies that mirror the architecture of termite mounds or coral reefs, but on the scale of a metropolis squeezed into a single, vast room.

There is no technology (0.3/3), only the intricate machinery of biology. The air smells sharply of oxygen, ozone, and the scent of crushed menthol—a chemical defense mechanism of the local flora. The plants here are not passive; they are active predators and competitors in a slow-motion war (1.6/3). You can see the tension: a creeper slowly throttling a fern-trunk, a patch of mold expanding with agonizing slowness to overtake a patch of moss. The dynamic is one of constant, silent encroachment.

Because the scale is small and the density absolute, the ecology feels compressed. Fauna is rare (1.2/3), likely because there is no room for large animals to move. Instead, the biodiversity is microscopic or small—tiny, segmented insects that skate across the film of water on the leaves, or translucent spiders that weave webs between the "buildings," acting as the aerial traffic control of this city. There are no birds to sing; the silence is broken only by the rustle of wet fronds and the dripping water.

It is a place of strange, wet coziness. The "weirdness" (0.7/3) lies in the familiarity of the shapes contrasted with the alien nature of the material. It looks like a ruined city, overgrown for a thousand years, but in reality, the city *is* the growth. The sharp, fractured rocks that birthed this place are now just the skeleton, long since internalized by the green, pulsing flesh that holds the cold world together. It is a hothouse on a mountain peak, a womb of wet life hovering at the freezing point, breathing slowly in the dim light.
