There is no sun here. That is the first and overwhelming law of this place. The score of light is absolute zero, a darkness so total it possesses a texture—heavy, velvety, pressing against the skin like a physical weight. In the absence of photons, the world defines itself through pressure, vibration, and the chemistry of the air.

You are standing on a high plateau, a continent of stone suspended in a void that is neither sky nor space, but simply a profound, weatherless obsidian. The elevation is immense, creating a sensation of vertigo not from looking down, but from the sheer thinness of the atmosphere, though it is improbably dense with humidity.

It is warm, a tropical, cloying heat that hovers around the body like a second skin. The air is thick with water vapor, suspended in a constant state of saturation. It is not raining, exactly, but the atmosphere weeps. The ground beneath your feet is not solid earth so much as a sponge of ancient, stratified stone, porous and drinking deep from the air. Water seeps from the rock face in continuous, silent tears, gathering in rivulets that run slick over the surface.

The sound hits you before you can reconcile the shapes of the darkness. It is loud—a cacophony of immense scale. A low-frequency thrum vibrates in the marrow of your bones, the heartbeat of colossal engines buried deep within the plateau. Overlaid on this is the chittering, shrieking, and grinding of a dense ecosystem. The air is alive with the movement of wings, the splash of heavy bodies through hidden pools, and the metallic groans of vast, shifting structures.

This is a landscape of industrial decay repurposed by an aggressive biology.

The "built" environment is sparse in terms of human intent—scattered outposts, the bleached ribs of superstructures that once pierced the gloom—but the *technology* is everywhere. The plateau is a machine. The terrain is etched with massive trenches, lined with rusted iron the size of canyons. Giant pistons, the height of skyscrapers, rise slowly from the mire with a hydraulic hiss that echoes for miles, then plunge back down, churning the wet earth.

These machines are not maintained by hands; they have been settled into. The vegetation here is lush, a verdant explosion of life that has adapted to the absolute dark. There are no leaves reaching for a sun that does not exist. Instead, the flora is bioluminescent and thermosensitive. Vast, fern-like fronds pulse with a sickly, chartreuse radiation, illuminating the wet metal around them in strobing, erratic rhythms. Trees with bark like steel plate grow through the skeletons of factories, their roots wrapped tight around gears and drive shafts, slowly crushing the mechanisms as they expand.

The density of life is stifling. Every surface is occupied. The air is thick with insects that click and buzz with electrical charge. Larger fauna moves in the shadows—you can hear the heavy, wet slap of tentacles against the stone, the guttural roar of something hunting in the rust-grass. The ecology here has evolved to feed on the ambient energy of the machines. There are creatures that graze on the heat radiating from exhaust vents, and predators that hunt by the vibration of a misstepping gear.

Because the scale is vast, weather systems form locally within the plateau’s depressions. As the massive condensers pump heat into the wet air, violent micro-storms erupt—hurricanes of steam and static lightning that are confined to a few square miles, screaming with wind before dissipating into the general gloom. These storms cycle the nutrients from the decomposing metal into the soil, feeding the weird, hybrid ecosystem.

The erosion here is established. This is not a new catastrophe; it is a mature world. The sharp edges of the industrial apocalypse have been worn smooth by millennia of dripping water and creeping moss. The machine-mountains are now rounded hills, their purposes forgotten, their movements reduced to a slow, tectonic grind. The iron is rusted into a soil that supports vast, fibrous fungi forests that smell of ozone and copper.

You walk through a corridor of dripping pipes, each one thick enough to house a family. Water pours from the joints of the metal, warm and mineral-heavy. The ground is a tangle of cables and roots, indistinguishable from one another. A shadow detaches itself from the wall—a six-limbed creature with skin like polished chrome, clicking its pincers. It ignores you, focused on scraping algae from a glowing valve.

Here, the rules of physics are bent by the sheer mass of the machinery. Gravity feels subjective, pulled in different directions by the magnetic whine of the buried dynamos. Compasses spin wildly. Time is measured by the cycle of the great Pistons—the rise and the fall, the intake of breath and the exhalation of steam.

It is a place of constant, violent activity, yet it feels static. It is a dark, warm, wet womb of metal and flesh, grinding forward without purpose, growing and dying in a lightless, thunderous equilibrium. The landscape is not dead, nor is it truly alive; it is a functioning metabolism on a continental scale, and you are merely a microbe drifting through its gut.
