The horizon does not bend here; it is a wall of shattered grey stone rising so high that the air thins into a bruised violet before tapering off into the black of space. This is a landscape of verticality, a vast, young range of granite and basalt thrust violently upward by tectonic spasms that have not yet had the time to wear the edges smooth. The peaks are jagged teeth, raw and angular, forever dusted with a frost that never melts, glinting under a high, pale sun.

Clinging to these impossible heights is the city.

It is not a metropolis of glass and steel, but a primitive, sprawling hive of dry-stone masonry, timber, and packed earth. The architecture is dense and claustrophobic, a chaotic agglutination of structures that have grown over one another for millennia. Narrow alleys winding between tenement-like towers are barely wide enough for two men to pass shoulder-to-shoulder. The buildings are stacked vertiginously, anchored by massive ironwood timbers driven deep into the rock face, creating a single, uninterrupted organism of habitation that stretches for hundreds of miles along the ridgelines. There is no open space; every flat surface, no matter how precarious, has been built upon.

The cold here is absolute, a dry, biting chill that seeps through the furs and wool wraps of the inhabitants. It is a temperature of 0—so far toward the absolute absence of heat that water freezes the moment it stops moving. Yet, the environmental parameters dictate a dampness, a paradox resolved by the ever-present mists. Clouds from the lowlands smash against the peaks, condensing into a heavy, wet fog that perpetually shrouds the lower levels of the city. The stone sweats; water weeps from the mortar and the rock faces, collecting in thousands of channels and gutters carved into the architecture. The city sounds like a quiet, rhythmic dripping—a persistent plinking that echoes in the alleyways, freezing into intricate icicles that hang like daggers over the thoroughfares.

Vegetation is sparse, struggling to exist in the thin air and freezing damp. There are no trees, save for the petrified-looking ironwoods used in construction. The only greenery is a tenacious, grey-green lichen that crusts the leeward sides of the buildings and a few patches of high-altitude moss that crowd the dripping gutters. The colors are monochrome: the black of the wet rock, the grey of the fog, the white of the frost, and the dull brown of the wooden scaffolding.

Life, however, is abundant. The city is teeming with fauna adapted to the cold and the verticality. Massive, shaggy herbivores—six-legged, yak-like creatures with thick, matted coats—wander the wider avenues, undisturbed by the humans. They are the primary source of heat and meat, and they live in the ground floors of the towers, stabled in spacious caverns carved from the mountain itself. The air is thick with swarms of small, furry rodents that scuttle along the rafters, and the skies are alive with heavy, broad-winged birds that ride the updrafts, their nests built into the crags of the highest spires. The smell is a distinctive mix wet stone, beast musk, and woodsmoke.

The technology is primitive, relying entirely on manual craft and hand tools. There is no electricity, no steam. The city is lit by torches and hearth-fires, the smoke rising to join the fog. Metal is rare and precious, mostly used for simple tools—axes, chisels, knives—fashioned from meteoric iron found in the high passes. The people wear heavy layers of leather and felt, moving with a slow, deliberate pace to conserve energy.

The atmosphere is overwhelmingly quiet, a hush born of the density of the stone and the muffling effect of the fog. Shouts are rare; communication happens through low murmurs and gestures. The dynamic is slow. The city does not change quickly. Erosion is a young force here, but the stone is hard, and the buildings are built to last centuries. The cycles are glacial: the slow drift of the seasons, the gradual accumulation of soot on the lintels, the inch-by-inch creep of the ice caps down the ravines.

Looking out from a high balcony over the vast, dense, grey ocean of rooftops, disappearing into the mist, one feels small and suspended. It is a place of endurance, a heavy, silent world where life hangs on tight to the rock, waiting for the sun to break through the clouds.
