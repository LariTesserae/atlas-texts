The air here is thin and biting, carrying the metallic taste of altitude and ozone. It is a cold place, consistently cool enough to make the lungs work harder, the kind of damp chill that settles into the marrow.

This is a landscape of raw, unfinished geology. The elevation is high, placing you among the clouds, but the terrain does not resemble the weathered, rolling shoulders of ancient mountains. Instead, the area is dominated by a cluster of jagged, obsidian spires that thrust violently upward, as if the earth beneath them had only just yesterday decided to puncture the sky. The erosion here is virtually non-existent; there are no rounded slopes, no valleys softened by millennia of silt. The rock faces are knife-edged, sharp enough to cut a careless hand, exhibiting the terrifying geometry of a fracture frozen in time. The stone is dark, glassy, and largely bare, save for where the water touches it.

Water is constant but deceptive. It does not flow in broad, muddy rivers here. It sweats from the stone. Because the geology is so fresh—so embryonic—the groundwater has yet to establish ancient subterranean rivers. Instead, pressure forces the moisture out through the microscopic pores of the obsidian spires. The rock faces are perpetually slick, glistening with a sheen of fresh condensation. Thousands of weeping fissures trace silver lines down the black glass, gathering into droplets that fall with the rhythmic persistence of a ticking clock. In the hollows between the spires, the water collects in still, black mirrors—pools so pristine they reflect the sky with terrifying clarity.

Despite the harsh stone and the cool air, the vegetation is shockingly lush. It is a primitive, voracious life that has learned to cling to the vertical. Thick mats of neon-green moss carpet the vertical surfaces where the seepage is strongest, anchoring themselves into the tiniest divots in the glassy rock. Ferns the size of wagon wheels unfurl from cracks in the cliff face, their fronds heavy with trapped mist. Lichens paint the rock in riotous oranges and rust-reds. This is a vertical forest, a hanging garden that defies gravity, drinking directly from the weeping stone.

The space feels small yet boundless—cathedral-like. You are standing in a "room" formed by five or six of these massive, needle-like spires. The floor is a chaotic jumble of fallen talus—sharp shards that have only just calapsed from the heights above. It feels like a hamlet-sized pocket of existence, a singular arena enclosed by the black walls of the earth’s newest bones.

And it is loud. The silence of the high altitude is shattered by the acoustics of the place. Because the rock is so hard and the edges so sharp, the wind does not blow around the spires; it screams through them. The air pressure funnels into the narrow gaps between the stones, creating a constant, low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the teeth. Overlaying this is the sound of the water—a million tiny drips echoing in the stone alcoves, accumulating into a roar like a distant crowd.

Nestled within this chaotic verticality is the settlement. It is an urban environment, but of a strange, primitive sort. It is not a city of sprawling suburbs, but a dense, clustered infrastructure built *into* the chaos. The inhabitants—whose presence is felt more than seen—utilize a technology of simple levers, massive timber, and woven hemp.

They have built a network of suspension bridges and platforms that cling to the spires like barnacles. The architecture is defensive and utilitarian: wooden huts perched on precipices, connected by swaying rope ladders. The construction appears primitive, using stone axes and hand-drilled holes, yet the scale of it is ambitious. They have erected massive wooden scaffolding against the sheerest faces, perhaps to harvest the lush moss or to mine the fresh stone.

The "built" environment feels empty, however. The density of the structures is high, but the population is low. You might walk for an hour across the complex network of bridges and see no one. The hammering of tools echoes from a distant tower, and smoke curls from a single hearth, but the vast majority of the platforms are vacant, gently swaying in the wind. It feels like a city built for a population that has not yet arrived, or perhaps one that has already left, leaving only the maintenance crews behind.

The weirdness of the place lies in the dynamic—the slowness. You feel unstable, as if the ground beneath your feet is not entirely stationary. Looking up at the sharp peaks, you realize the landscape is not static. The spires are still rising. It is a geological uplift visible on a human timescale, albeit slow. The earth is groaning upward, millimeter by millimeter, year by year. The wooden bridges creak not just from the wind, but from the gradual stretching of the stone they are anchored to. The city is a living thing, slowly being pulled apart by the growing mountain, requiring constant, endless repair. It is a place of perpetual construction, a civilization trying to build a home on the back of a waking giant.
