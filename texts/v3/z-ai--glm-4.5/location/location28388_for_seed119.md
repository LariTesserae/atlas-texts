The air tastes of wet iron and old dust. It is a cool, clammy chill that settles into the joints, characteristic of a place where the sun is a rumor and the water rules the stone.

You stand within the **Hollow Cistern**, a space entirely constructed, yet vast enough to possess its own enclosed weather. The floor beneath your boots is not earth, but a hexagonal mosaic of basalt tiles, fitted together so tightly over the centuries that the mortar has vanished, leaving only the grinding of stone on stone. The scale is deceptive; the ceilings are high—cathedral vaults of black granite disappearing into a gloom that feels more like a physical weight than a lack of light.

It is a **wet** place, but not submerged. The "terrain" is an architecture of reservoirs. The walls weep constantly. Massive, hand-hewn channels, carved with the primitive simplicity of the Bronze Age, line the perimeters of the room. Water trickles down the masonry in a thousand rivulets, catching the dim light before pooling in the center of the chamber in a still, black mirror. The humidity is absolute; the air is thick enough to drink. Condensation clings to every surface, turning the floor into a perpetual hazard of slick moss and slicker stone.

**Vegetation** here is an act of defiance. Life does not carpet the ground; it punctuates it. In the vast emptiness between the dripping pillars, you might find a single, solitary fern, its fronds unfurling to the size of a man, anchored in a crack in the masonry. Or a patch of lichen, grey and dry like old ash, clinging to a water pipe. It is a sparse ecology, one that survives on the margins of the artificial. The plants are lonely, isolated by the sheer scale of the empty stonework around them.

The **lighting** is dim and inconsistent. There are no electric hums here. The illumination comes from bioluminescent fungi cultivated in primitive bowls of chipped clay, mounted high on the pillars, and from the faint, milky glow filtering through translucent vents set near the ceiling. The light is enough to cast shadows, but not enough to banish them. The darkness between the pillars is deep, suggesting a void that goes on forever.

The **technology** on display is ancient and manual. Massive wooden wheels, iron-banded and rusted, jut from the walls at intervals, their spokes creaking as water currents turn them. Gears the size of cartwheels, cast in crude bronze, lock and unlock with a thunderous, metallic clatter that echoes through the chamber. This is a machine of levers and gravity, a hydraulic computer built by hands that knew only chisels and hammers.

And it is **loud**. The silence of the empty space is shattered by the roar of the water system. It is not a soothing sound; it is the roar of industry hidden within the walls. The rush of water through unseen pipes sounds like the breath of a sleeping beast. The bronze gears shriek as they turn. And layered over this mechanical din is the **fauna**.

The Cistern is **abundant** with life, a teeming ecosystem of wet things. The air is thick with "mist-skippers"—small, translucent lizards with flaps of skin between their legs that launch themselves from pillar to pillar, chittering in high-pitched, deafening waves. They swarm through the vaulted darkness like a living tornado.

In the dark pools of water that dot the floor, massive, blind salamanders the size of crocodiles haul themselves onto the basalt tiles. Their skin is slick and pale, looking disturbingly like wet rawhide. They bark—a low, resonant booming that vibrates in your chest—competing with the rush of the water and the skittering of the lizards. It is a cacophony of wet life.

The **erosion** here is settled, a partnership between water and stone. The edges of the hexagonal tiles have been worn smooth and round by centuries of dripping water and the scuttling of countless feet. The channels in the walls are not carved perfectly straight; they have been weathered into organic, kidney-like shapes by the relentless flow of water. The place feels balanced, as if the water and the stone have reached a treaty long ago, and the machinery is just the method of enforcement.

The **weirdness** lies in the purpose and the emptiness. For all its life and noise, the space feels hollow—a vast, theatrical stage set for a play that stopped running ages ago. There are no doors visible, only the water channels disappearing into the dark. The most disturbing feature is the statues: standing at irregular intervals in the vast empty spaces are figures carved from the same black granite as the walls. They are not human, but they are not animals either—limbless, smooth torsos with faces that are simply smooth ovals. They are covered in the same grey lichen, untouched by the salamanders, ignored by the lizards, weeping water from eyeless sockets. They watch the machinery turn, but they do not work.

Here, in the cool, dim dampness, you are an intruder in a factory that processes nothing, irrigates a soil that does not exist, and houses a population of things that do not care if you are there.
