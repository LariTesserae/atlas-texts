The sky here is not a ceiling but a resounding absence of color—a pale, blinding white dome that feels almost solid in its intensity. At this elevation, the air is thin and razor-crisp, tasting of ozone and pulverized stone. It is a cool world, holding a temperature that nips at the skin but never freezes, a perpetual, dry autumn maintained by the sheer height of the land above the planet’s warmer depths. The light is relentless; there are no clouds to soften it, only the stark, high-contrast glare of a sun that seems closer than it should be. Shadows are cast with absolute clarity, sharp enough to cut a finger on.

You stand upon the High Lithic Plain, a continental expanse of weathered bedrock that stretches until the curvature of the planet bends the horizon upward. The erosion here is ancient, settled into a deep, rhythmic logic. There are no jagged, raw peaks; the mountains in the distance are rounded, smooth hulks worn down by eons of slow-grinding winds. The ground underfoot is a hard-packed mosaic of shattered slate and silica, ripples of fossilized dunes that haven’t seen sand in millennia. It is a landscape of inertia, immense features locked in a stasis that feels heavy with time.

The silence is profound. It is not merely the absence of noise, but a dense, tactile quiet that presses against the ears. The wind here does not howl; at this scale, the atmosphere moves too thin to create turbulence. It flows like a sheet of invisible glass, producing only a faint, constant hiss—the friction of air moving over billions of rough mineral surfaces.

It is a desert of absolute thirst. The water parameter is near zero; this is a hygroscopic void. The ground sucks greedily at any moisture, and the chemistry of life here has long abandoned the need for liquid. Yet, the vegetation rating is lush. How does a forest grow in a void?

It grows in glass.

Scattered across the vast, grey plains are the Glass-Spires. They are the "trees" of this world, towering monoliths of translucent silicate that rise hundreds of feet into the bright air. They are spaced with mathematical precision, sparse enough that you can walk for an hour between them, dense enough that from a high vantage point, the plain shimmers like a bed of glitter.

These are not plants in the earthly sense. They are lithophytes, crystalline growths that extract their mass directly from the mineral-rich soil and their energy from the relentless sunlight. They do not have leaves; they have facets. Over centuries, they extrude complex, geometric lattices—honeycombs, spirals, fractal needles—that catch the light and refract it into a million tiny rainbows that dance across the ground. Internally, they hum with a low-frequency vibration, the metabolic sound of growing crystal, though it is too deep for human ears to hear, felt only as a tickle in the bones.

The "lushness" is vertical and optical. As the sun moves across the white sky, the spires act as prisms, bathing the shadowed ground in shifting pools of violet, emerald, and ruby light. It creates an undergrowth of light, where photosynthesis is replaced by photovoltaic charging. The ground between the spires is not bare dirt but crusted over with a bio-luminescent lichen, a dry, brittle carpet that glows faintly in the refracted colors, feeding on the sublimation of minerals.

There is movement, however slow. The ecosystem is active, but it operates on a geological timescale. You can watch a Glass-Spire grow if you are patient enough to see a micro-inch of lattice creep outward over a week. The wind is the dynamic engine; it carries microscopic spores of crystalline dust that collide and fuse, seeding new growths in the lee of rocks.

Fauna is rare, a ghostly presence in this cathedral of light. There are no herds, no swarms. There is only the Skylark.

You might see one, a mile away, a winged shape gliding on the thermal currents. It is large, perhaps the wingspan of a small plane, with a body that looks like polished ceramic. It does not flap; it rides the updrafts generated by the heat-glass of the spires. It is a solitary creature, a silent predator that feeds on the energy stored in the crystalline lichen, swooping down with the speed of a falling stone to shear off chunks of the crust with a beak of diamond-sharp obsidian. It leaves no tracks, only the sound of displaced air—a soft *whuff* that breaks the silence for a fleeting second.

The world feels like a sealed environment, a terrarium of rock and light. The scale is so vast that the spires, though gigantic, look like toys on a table. The distance between them creates a sense of profound isolation. To walk here is to feel incredibly exposed, a soft biological anomaly amidst the hard, geometric perfection of the crystal forest.

There are no artifacts. No walls, no tools, no ruins. The only structures are the natural spires, looking for all the world like the abandoned skyscrapers of a civilization that turned to stone and glass and forgot how to die. The only law here is the physics of refraction and the slow, grinding patience of the earth. It is a place of blinding beauty and total indifference, a bright, cold, and silent eternity where life has learned to build itself out of light and dust.
