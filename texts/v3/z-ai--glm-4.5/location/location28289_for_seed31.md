The sky here sits at a distance, a high, pale dome that seems to press down on the tops of the spires. This is a landscape of verticality, a high-altitude archipelago of jagged stone thrusting upward from the clouds, yet the air is not thin and cold; it is heavy, humid, and strikingly warm. It feels like breathing inside a greenhouse suspended in the stratosphere.

The geology is newborn. There is a violence to the rock faces that has not yet been softened by the centuries. The mountains are not rolling hills but jagged scimitars of grey basalt and obsidian, their edges razor-sharp, their faces raw and unweathered. These peaks tore themselves from the earth only yesterday, in geological terms, and they still bear the scars of their birth—sheer cliffs that drop away into nothingness, fractured cleavage planes where the stone is still shedding dust.

But the water is everywhere. It does not fall in rain, exactly, nor does it flow in roaring rivers. Instead, the stone sweats. The elevation traps a thick, eternal mist against the peaks, and the heat of the rock causes the moisture to condense constantly. Every surface is slick with a fine, continuous sheen of water. It seeps from cracks in the obsidian, weeping down the sheer cliffs in thousands of silver rivulets that catch the normal, steady light. Pools of impossibly clear water gather in the natural basins of the summit, their surfaces still as glass, overflowing gently into the void.

Because of this constant weeping, life has taken hold with startling speed. The vegetation is lush, a thick coat of emerald mosses, ferns, and succulents that cling to the wet stone. Great, leathery leaves unfurl over the abyss, drinking directly from the mist. The green is vibrant, almost electric against the grey stone, but it is a quiet green. There are no flowers blooming in riotous colors, just a steady, determined photosynthesis.

The silence here is the loudest thing.

There is no animal life. No birds wheel on the updrafts, no insects buzzing in the ferns, no distant calls of beasts. The ecology is entirely botanical, a kingdom of plants and stone and water existing in a vacuum of predator and prey. Because of this, the auditory landscape is defined by the physics of the water. You hear the *plink, plink* of condensation dripping from a cavernous overhang. You hear the soft, continuous shush of water sliding down moss. The wind moves through the sharp peaks with a lonely, mournful tone, but never with enough force to disturb the equilibrium.

Human presence is etched into this stasis, sparse but unmistakable. A settlement clings to the largest of the mesas, an urban sprawl that has learned to accommodate the sharp rock and the wet air. It is primitive construction—hand-hewn stone blocks dragged from the quarries, timber mortised together without the aid of complex metal tools. The architecture is sprawling and low-slung to avoid the stronger winds higher up, huts and longhouses connected by winding, slippery walkways of polished wood.

Given the sparse density of the landscape, the town is laid out with generous, almost wasteful amounts of space. There are wide plazas of damp cobblestone where nothing happens, open-air meeting halls that stand empty. The density of the buildings is low; they cluster in small neighborhoods separated by groves of giant ferns or deep fissures in the rock.

It is a place of profound stillness. Without animal movement and without the chaotic erosion of wind and ice, the world feels paused. The water weeps at the same rate it did a hundred years ago. The moss grows at the same creeping speed. The temperature holds its warm, steady grip. A person could stand on the edge of the balcony and look out over the sea of sharp, wet peaks and feel that time has stopped entirely, that the world is held in a perfect, humming suspension of wet stone and silent green.

The rules here are simple: do not rush, do not make loud noise, and watch your step on the slick stone. Life is not about survival against the elements, but about existing in harmony with a damp, eternal pause.
