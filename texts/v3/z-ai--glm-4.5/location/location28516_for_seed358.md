The air here is not something you breathe; it is something you swim through. It hangs heavy, a thick, warm suspension of moisture that tastes of iron and ancient dust. This is the Sump, a geographical bruise far below the crust of the world, a hollow space so vast that its far walls are swallowed by the gloom.

It is the sound of billions of liters of water moving under pressure, the groan of the earth settling, and the industrial clatter of massive, unseen mechanisms echoing off the stone. It is a loudness that you stop hearing with your ears after a while and start feeling in the marrow of your ribs. The dynamic here is agonizingly slow; the water levels rise by inches over decades, the rock faces shift over eons, but the noise is constant, a static scream of a world in the process of being born.

The light is dim, a suffocating twilight. There is no sun here. Illumination comes from bioluminescence clinging to the wet surfaces and the occasional, sickly amber flicker of floodlights mounted on towering, rusted rigs that stab down from the ceiling thousands of feet above. The gloom is thick, broken only by the glittering reflection off the endless sheets of water.

Erosion has not yet had time to soften the edges; the rock faces are fresh fractures, sharp as glass, towering like the walls of a split geode. There are no beaches, no smooth river stones, only shattered scree and vertical scarps. The ground is unstable, a rubble of tectonic debris that has only just fallen into being.

Water is the architect and the occupant. It scores a 2.5 on the scale—this is a drowning world. The floor of the Sump is not solid land but a maze of half-submerged pillars and archipelagos of jagged stone. Every surface weeps. Condensation drips constantly from the unseen ceiling, creating a permanent, misting rain that feels warm and greasy. Pools of standing water are everywhere, black mirrors reflecting the industrial lights, while subterranean rivers surge through channels in the raw rock, churning white foam against the unyielding stone.

You see occasional patches of slick, dark moss or mats of anaerobic fungi growing in the crevices where the water runs slowest. The rules here are simple and harsh. Gravity pulls everything down toward the water. The air carries spores that settle in the lungs. The noise never stops, drowning out thought.
