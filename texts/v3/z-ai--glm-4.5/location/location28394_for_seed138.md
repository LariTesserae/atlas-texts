The air here is heavy, a physical weight that presses against the skin, hot and utterly saturated with moisture. It does not rain so much as the atmosphere simply gives up, collapsing into a constant, sweating mist that clings to everything. This is the Steam-Sink, a low-lying basin recently cracked open by some violent, tectonic heave—a scar on the earth that has not yet had time to heal.

The ground underfoot is treacherous. Because the erosion is young, the rocks have not been rounded by millennia of wind or water. They are jagged, raw shards of dark basalt and slate, thrusting up through the thin layer of industrial grit like broken teeth. The terrain is sharp, unstable, and unwelcoming, a fresh wound in the crust that has been immediately colonized by the iron grip of industry.

Despite the heat, which hovers just below the limit of endurance, the water level never rises. It is a perfect equilibrium. The ground is weeping; water seeps perpetually from the fractures in the rock, creating a landscape of constant dampness without true flooding. Puddles form instantly, shimmering with oil slicks, yet they never overflow. The condensation drips from every surface, a rhythmic *plink, plink, plink* that underscores the deeper, guttural roar of the place.

The structures here are few but immense—cathedral-scale works of brick, iron, and rivets. This is not a sprawling city but a concentrated, hamlet-sized cluster of heavy industry. It feels like a single, sprawling machine broken into distinct buildings: a pumping station the size of a cathedral, a foundry that squats like a toad, and a maze of pipes connecting them in the open air. Because the density is low, there is space between these monoliths. You can see the gaps, the muddy pathways where the steam clears just enough to reveal the sharp, young horizon. It is an organized construction, laid out with military precision on the unstable ground, anchoring itself deep into the fresh rock.

The light is dim, filtered through a permanent ceiling of low-hanging, sulfur-tinted clouds and the thick billows of steam that vent from the ground and the stacks. It is a twilight world, illuminated less by the sun and more by the angry orange glow of furnace doors and the occasional crackle of electric discharge from high-tension wires that hum overhead.

It is loud. The silence is dead, murdered by the cacophony. Massive pistons slam with the force of pile drivers; gears the size of wagons grind and shriek; steam vents hiss like angry serpents. Yet, beneath the industrial thunder, there is the chittering, squelching sound of life. Fauna is abundant here, thriving in the thermal soup. Giant, albino crabs scuttle over the wet ironwork, their claws clicking on the metal. Bloated, heat-loving lizards bask on the warm exhaust pipes, oblivious to the vibrations. The air is thick with insects—dragonflies with metallic wings that dart through the steam. Birds, black and soot-stained, nest in the rafters of the great sheds, screaming to be heard over the engines.

But the strangest feature of this place is its absolute lack of change. The dynamic is frozen in a perfect, terrifying stasis. The machines never pause, but they never progress. They produce a thunderous, ceaseless output that is looped back into the system, burning fuel that never seems to deplete, pumping water that never drains away. The workers (if there are any, hidden in the gloom of the archways) move with a clockwork repetition. The water boils, the metal expands, the fauna hunts and breeds, but the scene at dawn is identical to the scene at dusk. It is a landscape caught in a single, screaming moment of function—a young, wet, hot scar on the world that throbs with eternal energy but goes nowhere.
