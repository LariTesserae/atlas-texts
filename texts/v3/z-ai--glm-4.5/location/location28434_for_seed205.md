The air here is thin but heavy, a paradox of altitude and heat. It sits in the lungs like hot syrup. You are high—perhaps three miles up—yet the temperature clings to a sweltering, tropical constant. There is no wind chill, only a sluggish, drifting oven-heat that smells of wet dust and blooming decay.

The horizon does not exist; it is swallowed by the sheer scale of the place. You stand on the **Green Abyss**, a vast, high-altitude plateau that stretches across the curvature of the world.

**The Substrate**
Beneath your feet, there is no stone, no soil, no bare earth. The "ground" is a total, uninterrupted vegetable mass. This is the 2.8 of vegetation made manifest: it is not that plants grow *in* the land; the plants *are* the land.

It is a dense, spongy matrix of interwoven roots, rhizomes, and pressed moss, compacted over eons into a kind of living asphalt. It gives slightly underfoot, springing back, leaving footprints that fill with sap within seconds. This substrate is ancient—erosion here has established a steady state where the landscape grows faster than it rots. The texture is uniform, rolling in immense, slow swells that look like hills but are actually the breathing of a subterranean root system the size of a continent.

**The Moisture**
Water is a thief here. It does not flow in rivers; it is stolen from the air. The atmosphere is saturated—damp to the point of hallucination—but the water never falls as true rain. Instead, a heavy, incessant condensation clings to everything. The heat draws moisture up from the vegetative depths, and the elevation chills it just enough to create a permanent, clinging fog.

Every surface slick with sweat-like droplets. Pockets of clear, viscous water pool in the natural dips of the root-mat, hot to the touch and tasting of minerals and pollen. It is a world of intermittent drowning and constant thirst; the air is wet, but the water is hard to catch.

**The Pillars**
If the ground is a continuous green sea, the vertical structures are the scattered archipelagos. True macro-flora—the Trees—are incredibly sparse (1.3/3 density). They do not grow in forests. They stand alone, separated by miles of empty, humming sward.

These are not mere trees. They are biological skyscrapers, titans of cork-like bark and broad, canopy-spanning leaves that create their own microclimates. They are spaced so precisely that no two shades overlap. Because the land between them is empty of vertical landmarks, the distance feels deceptive; a nearby Tree might actually be five miles away, its sheer height making it seem close.

**The Roar**
The defining characteristic of this place is the noise. It is deafening (2.6/3), a wall of sound that presses against the skin as physically as the heat.

It is the fauna (2.7/3). The ecosystem does not just contain animals; it is *made* of them. The air between the sparse Trees is thick with life—billions of creatures filling the void. There is no silence. The sky is a churn of wings.

The primary inhabitants are the **Glass-Winged Locusts** and the **Bell-Birds**. The locusts move in clouds so dense they darken the sun, a living weather system of chitin and transparent wings that creates a constant, sawing thrum. The Bell-Bbirds perch in the infinite miles of root-ground, emitting resonant, metallic chimes that carry for miles. The combined effect is a cacophony: a low-frequency vibration of the atmosphere pierced by screaming trills and the clicking of a billion mandibles eating the green floor.

**The Scattered Works**
Human presence—or whatever intelligence built here—is insignificant, a mere ghost in the machine (Built 0.8, Tech 1.4).

There are no cities. There are only attachments.

Clinging to the bark of the titanic Trees, thousands of feet above the green floor, are small, primitive structures. They are not constructed of metal; they are grown. Woven from living vines and hardened with clay, they look like fungal growths on the side of a giant. These are the platforms of the harvesters.

They use simple tools: long hooks made of bone, woven nets, and stone blades. They do not farm; they scavenge. They climb down on ropes made of hair-fine moss to collect the carcasses of the giant insects that litter the floor, or to harvest the "dew-honey" that condenses on the upper leaves.

**The Weirdness**
There is a rule here that defies standard biology. The vegetation and the fauna are locked in a hyper-accelerated cycle of consumption. The green floor grows visibly; you can watch the moss creep if you stand still for an hour. The locusts eat it down to the nub.

The weirdness lies in the **Spores**. Because the vegetation is total and the fauna is overwhelming, the air is a thick soup of reproductive dust. It coats everything. It creates a static charge in the air—lightning that doesn't strike, but crawls along the ground in slow, purple worms of electricity. This "crawl-light" is the only illumination at night, a ghostly, bioluminescent strobing that syncopates with the deafening rhythm of the insects.

**The Experience**
To stand here is to feel small, wet, and loud. You balance on the spongy, infinite green. The heat makes your head swim. A mile away, a Titan Tree rises into the haze, a tiny speck of a woven nest clinging to its flank.

Around you, the ground shimmers—sometimes from the heat, sometimes from the crawling purple electricity, and sometimes because the "ground" itself is moving, a carpet of beetles undulating beneath the moss. A cloud of locusts descends, blotting out the normal light (1.5), turning the day into a rattling, humming twilight. You wipe the thick condensation from your face, and your hand comes away green with pollen. You are a visitor in a stomach that is constantly digesting itself.
