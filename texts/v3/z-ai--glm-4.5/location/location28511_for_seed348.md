The sound is not a noise; it is a medium. It is a low-frequency thrum, pitched just at the edge of hearing but felt deep in the cartilage of the ribs and the teeth. It is the sound of the world’s engine, a steady, deafening roar that has not fluctuated in volume, pitch, or timbre for a thousand years. It is loud—painfully so—but because it never changes, the mind eventually edits it out of consciousness, leaving only a ringing silence where the ear should be.

You stand in the Sump.

This is a low place, a shallow bowl of geology several miles wide, perhaps a crater or a sunken continental shelf. The elevation is low enough that the air hangs heavy and cool, trapping a permanent, thin mist against the ground. It is not raining, but everything is wet. The dampness is atmospheric, a physical pressure that sweats from the riveted iron plates underfoot and beads on the cold flanks of the machinery.

There is no green here. The vegetation parameter is a flat zero. The concept of "leaf" or "root" has no purchase in this soil. Instead, the ground and the structures are coated in a slow, shifting mosaic of mineral oxidation. Copper patinas in poisonous greens, rust in flaking oranges, and pale, lichen-like patches of aluminum corrosion cover every surface. It is a chromatic world of metal and stone, wet and slick, smelling of ozone and old oil.

The architecture is urban but sparse, a dispersed city of industrial function. The scale is small enough that you can see the boundaries of the zone—distant cliffs of black basalt that wall in the Sump—but the structures within are colossal. They are cathedral-sized factories, refining plants, and assembly halls, standing apart from one another in the damp gloom like monoliths on a plain. They are not clustered tightly; there is room to breathe, vast open courtyards of cracked concrete and grated decking between the buildings.

These structures are "established." They show no signs of recent construction, nor are they crumbling. They have settled into a perfect, eroded state. The sharp edges of the girders have worn smooth, like river stones, and the concrete is stained with centuries of runoff, yet they stand in perfect equilibrium. Nothing is being built, nothing is falling down. It is a frozen tableau of industry.

The tech level is high and heavy. Massive pipes, thick as subway tunnels, weave through the air like the exposed intestines of a leviathan, venting steam that instantly condenses into the clinging fog. Turbines the size of houses spin behind grating, their motion a blur, yet they produce no variation in the eternal hum. Electricity arcs silently between brass terminals, lighting the scene with a "normal" light—flat, shadowless, and gray—that seems to emanate from the moisture in the air itself.

And then there is the life.

If the industry is the skeleton of this place, the fauna is its flesh, and it is overwhelming. There is no ecosystem of predator and prey here, no cycle of hunt and flee. The fauna is a single, overwhelming biomass: the Scavengers.

They are not animals as we understand them. They are bio-mechanical grazers, hexapodal and shelled with plates of chitin that mimic the rusted steel they crawl over. There are millions of them. They coat the floors, they cling to the ceilings of the pipelines, they swarm over the massive turbines. They are the size of dogs, with mandibles designed to shear metal and suck oil.

They are everywhere. You cannot step without crunching through a shell, yet there is no aggression. They move in a single, flowing river of flesh, a constant, roiling tide that pours from one machine to the next. They eat the corrosion. They consume the slow decay of the city, converting oxidized metal back into raw, slurry-like waste that drips down into the Sump’s drains to be recycled.

This is the weirdness of the place: the stasis.

Despite the overwhelming number of creatures, the environment is in a state of absolute dynamic zero. The Scavengers move, but they do not migrate. They flow in a closed loop. The machines spin, but they produce no new product; they simply grind the air. The water condenses, drips, and evaporates in the same rhythm, beat for beat, day after day.

There are no seasons. The temperature is a perpetual, clammy cool. The light does not dim. The sound does not cease. It is a pocket universe trapped in the moment of its own function.

To stand in the Sump is to stand inside a clock that has stopped moving but continues to tick. The Scavengers sweep past your legs, their clicking legs adding a textural rattle to the bass hum of the engines. They ignore you completely. You are a glitch in the geometry, a temporary variable in a math problem that was solved long ago.

You touch a railing. It is slick with cold condensation and vibrating with the eternal thrum. Underneath the film of water, the metal feels warm, as if the city itself is a living thing, sleeping a sleep that it will never wake from. The dampness seeps into your clothes. The silence of the noise fills your head. Here, in the rusted hollow of the world, nothing ever happens, and everything happens, forever.
