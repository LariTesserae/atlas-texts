The descent is deceptive. The passage down is tight, constricting, a throat of dry, dusty stone that forces you to exhale just to squeeze your ribs through. But as the walls fall away, the oppression does not lift; it transforms into exposure.

You emerge not into the dark, but into a blinding, suspended noon.

This is the Cyst. It is a subterranean hollow, a spherical pocket of existence buried deep within the crust, yet it glows with the ferocity of a desert at high noon. The light does not come from a sun, nor from a ceiling. It seems to bleed out of the very matter of the place. The walls, curved and seamless, are composed of a dense, milky quartz that has absorbed and refracted the planet’s geothermal energy for eons, radiating it back as a cool, blinding white luminescence. It is a brightness that hurts the eyes, flattening depth and erasing shadows until the world feels two-dimensional, a cut-out of stark white and vibrating heat haze.

The scale of the place is intimate yet vast—a cathedral of air. The floor is a polished, convex disc, smooth as a lens, sloping gently down to a single point in the center. There are no pillars, no jagged stalactites, no corners to hide in. The erosion here has finished its work long ago; it has achieved a perfect, terrifying equilibrium. Every angle has been planed away by the slow, patient movement of air and water. It is a room that feels as if it was polished by giants and then left to gather dust for a million years.

The air is the first thing that claims you. It is dense, heavy with water. The temperature is a stifling, tropical warmth, a blanket of humid heat that sits immediately on the skin. The humidity (0.7) manifests not as rain, but as a constant, trembling sweat. The condensation does not drip; it hangs in the air, turning the blinding white light into a soft, prismatic fog before your eyes. The walls weep silently, a thin film of water covering the quartz in a slick, glassy sheen, making the floor treacherous and frictionless.

Here, the emptiness (0.0) is a physical weight. The space is so devoid of clutter that the distance between you and the curved wall feels like a vacuum. There are no rocks, no debris, no piles of guano. Just the white curve of the floor and the white curve of the ceiling meeting in a distant, blurred horizon. It creates a vertigo of openness, a sensation that you are floating unprotected in a void.

In the exact center of this convex dish, where the floor dips to its lowest point, lies the only anchor in the world: The Colony.

It is the sole vegetation (1.4), a sparse, singular growth that defies the sterility around it. It looks like a grotesque, oversized anemone made of translucent, pale-green flesh and fibrous, crystalline spines. It is perhaps ten feet tall, pulsing slowly with a rhythm that takes minutes to complete a cycle. This is the heart of the ecology. It feeds on the minerals that seep up through a tiny, invisible vent in the floor’s center and drinks the heavy condensation from the air.

And it is here that the fauna (1.7) thrives.

The "animals" are not beasts in the traditional sense. They are small, crab-like entities, no larger than a human hand, possessing shells of translucent glass and legs of articulated, dark iron-oxide. They are abundant, swarming over the central anemone like bees on a hive, though they move with a horrifying, jerky slowness (1.1).

They are the source of the primitive tech (1.4). They are tool-users. They do not build; they do not alter the landscape. But they carry things. Each glass-crab clutches a shard of sharper stone or a sliver of metal scavenged from the deep earth. They use these implements with deliberate, surgical precision to scrape the thick, nutrient-rich mucous from the skin of the central anemome. They are farmers of sweat, cultivating the giant plant, harvesting its slime, and carrying it in small, folded leaf-constructs back to the crevices in the floor where they burrow to sleep.

The weirdness (1.7) lies in their silence and their light. They do not chitter. They do not click. They move in a near-total vacuum of sound (1.1). The only noise in the Cyst is the soft, wet *schlepp* of a crab leg moving on the wet quartz, or the occasional heavy *drip* of condensation falling from the high, invisible ceiling to shatter on the floor. It is a quiet that feels loud, a pressure in the ears.

As your eyes adjust to the glare, you realize that the glass-crabs are not just harvesting. They are arranging. On the slick, empty white floor surrounding the central plant, they lay out the harvested slime in intricate, geometric patterns. Spirals, concentric circles, fractals that stretch for yards across the convex floor. They are terraforming the void, turning the emptiness into a canvas of slow-drying biological ink.

Why do they do it? Is it worship? Is it agriculture, letting the slime dry into a consumable wafer? Or is it communication, a message written in glycoproteins to the blinding light above?

There is no wind to disturb their work. The dynamic is glacial. The patterns take weeks to form. The light never shifts intensity. The heat never breaks. It is a room of arrested time, a bright, hollow lung buried under miles of rock, breathing slowly, wetly, filled with tiny, blind gardeners tending to a god made of slime and glass in the center of the world. To stand here is to feel naked, a single, dark imperfection in a blinding, perfect machine of slowness.
