The horizon does not curve; it simply creates a ceiling. This is a low, vast basin, a continental depression where the air is warm, thick, and motionless. It smells of baking clay and cinnamon dust.

Here, the concept of a "forest" has been reinterpreted by nature as a city.

The ground is raw, sharp-edged gravel—geology that has not yet been softened by the passage of time or the lubricant of water. There are no rounded pebbles here, only the jagged shrapnel of a freshly broken planet. This is an emerging land, raw and embryonic, yet it is consumed by life.

Because there is almost no water, the vegetation did not evolve the soft tissues of leaves or stems. Instead, the basin is packed with the "Fenestration." These are towering, monolithic stalks that rise hundreds of feet into the warm air. They are not wood, but a dense, fibrous mineral composite, dry and brittle to the touch. They stand in straight, tight phalanxes, creating deep, narrow canyons between them. The density is absolute; there is no open ground, only a labyrinth of verticality.

From a distance, the Fenestration looks like an endless, abandoned metropolis of brown and rust-red skyscrapers. The "lushness" here is a matter of sheer volume of biomass. The stalks grow so closely together that they support one another, weaving their upper branches into a perpetual, porous ceiling that filters the normal, bright sunlight down into a dim, amber gloom.

It is a quiet place.

Without water to act as a conductive medium, sound dies instantly. There is no splashing, no rustling of soft leaves, no croaking of frogs. The silence is heavy and textured. Occasionally, a stalk will settle under its own weight, sending a sharp, singular *crack* through the air, like a gunshot in a library. The only other sounds are the dry, clicking sounds of the inhabitants.

The fauna is abundant but invisible to the casual glance. This is a society of chitin and armor. Because the environment is dry and sharp, life here is hard-shelled. Thousands of species of insects, armored arthropods, and dry-skinned reptiles move through the canyons. They are the citizens of this biological city. Massive, multi-legged grazers crawl up the sides of the stalks, using powerful mandibles to shear off the dry, fibrous bark. They move with a slow, deliberate grace, their hooked feet finding purchase on the mineral surfaces without making a sound.

Predators are ambush hunters, flattening their segmented bodies against the jagged rocks at the base of the stalks, waiting for the dry-clatter of a grazer to pass by. There is no screaming, no wet tearing of flesh; the hunt is a silent, clicking affair of sudden motion and crushing force.

The atmosphere is dynamic in a geological sense. The ground is alive. New spires of the Fenestration push up through the gravel floor every day, shattering the stone and slowly shouldering aside the older towers. It is a slow-motion demolition and reconstruction project. Old stalks die and fossilize in place, turning into the bedrock for the next generation. The city is always growing, always expanding, consuming the raw mineral earth and turning it into vertical, dry architecture.

There is no weather here, not in the way of rain or storms. The "weather" is a shedding of dust. As the sun beats down on the mineral forests, the stalks expand and contract, shedding fine layers of cinnamon-colored powder. It drifts down through the canyons like dry snow, coating the armor of the beasts and filling the cracks in the ground.

It is a place of intense, silent industry. A biological superstructure built on a desert foundation, immense, crowded, and totally devoid of fluid. It is a masterpiece of dry preservation, a world that blooms not with flowers, but with endless, rigid endurance.
