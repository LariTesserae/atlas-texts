The sound hits you before the air does—a roar not of wind or machinery, but of living things compressed into a container too small to hold them. It is a cacophony of screeches, splashes, chitters, and the wet, heavy slap of bodies moving through the mire. This is the High Rookery, a place that feels less like a geography and more like a fever dream of biology.

You stand on a root-broadened ledge, a "cathedral" scale space that nonetheless feels claustrophobic. The light is normal—daylight, grey and diffused by a perpetual, cool-hanging mist—but it struggles to penetrate the density of the space. The air is heavy and cool (0.7), tasting of wet moss, iron-rich soil, and the sharp, ammoniac tang of guano.

Here, the city is not built; it is grown. The urban environment (1.8) is a verticality of living architecture. The buildings are immense, bulbous trunks of petrified-soft wood, fused together by centuries of parasitic vines and epiphytes. There are no sharp angles here; everything is rounded, swollen, and slick with moisture. The "walls" of the structures are actually layers of thick, leathery bark that have been carved—hewn with crude bone chisels and stone adzes (1.3)—to create hollows. These hollows glow from within with the orange warmth of hearth-fires, the smoke curling out to join the wet ceiling of the world.

The vegetation (2.8) is total. It is not that plants grow here; it is that the space *is* plant. The ground is not soil but a writhing mattress of mycelium and root-hairs, spongy and treacherous. Mosses hang in thick, wet curtains from the eaves, dripping constantly. Every surface is colonized. Ferns unfurl from the thatched roofs, and lichens crust the walkways. The distinction between "street" and "garden" has long since dissolved.

Water (2.5) is the blood of this place. It doesn't just rain; the atmosphere condenses on the cool surfaces of the high-altitude (1.5) plateau, causing the very stone to weep. Waterfalls cascade from the upper terraces, unplanned and erratic, crashing down into the central "plaza"—a flooded, marshy depression where the runoff collects. The sound of falling water is a constant bass note underlying the shrieks of the fauna. Puddles never evaporate; they only merge, forming slick mirrors on the floorboards of the walkways.

But the true engine of this landscape is the fauna (2.8). The density is overwhelming. It is a vertical city of creatures. Thousands of large, raucous birds—something like gulls mixed with primates, leathery and loud—claw at the architecture. They nest in the carved hollows, displacing the inhabitants constantly. The ground level is a riot of amphibious scavengers: sleek, otter-like lizards and heavy, mud-colored toads that thrash in the shallow canals.

The dynamic is turbulent (2.6). There is no stillness. The rookery is in a state of chaotic flux. A fight breaks out in the upper canopy, raining debris and downy feathers into the streets below. A section of a root-bridge, weakened by the rot of the damp (erosion 1.7), collapses into the central swamp, causing a splash that sends a thousand small creatures into a panic. The inhabitants, clad in furs and simple woven fabrics, move with a frantic energy, repairing fences with fresh-cut timber while shooing aggressive nesters from their porches. It is a bazaar of survival: shouting, trading, running, hammering.

The rules here are simple: hold your ground, keep your fire lit, and never stand still. To stand still is to be covered, to become substrate. The erosion is established (1.7); the stone steps are worn smooth, concave by millennia of wet feet and claws. The place feels old, settled into its own damp misery, a balanced ecosystem of noise and decay where the city is slowly being digested by the forest it was carved from.

You duck as a flight of something heavy and squawking wings through the mist, the downdraft chilling your face. The water drips from the rim of your hat. Everywhere you look, there is an eye, a wing, a leaf, a drop of water, all vibrating with the terrifying, holy energy of being overcrowded, wet, and alive.
