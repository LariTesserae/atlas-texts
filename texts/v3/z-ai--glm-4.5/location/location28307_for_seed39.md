The air here tastes of iron and ozone, sharp on the tongue, thin enough to make the lungs work for every breath. It is a high, cold world, a vast, open tableland of grey stone that sits far above the cloud line, though the clouds frequently rise to meet it. The sky is not the blue of the lower altitudes but a perpetual, slate-grey twilight, dim and diffuse, as if the sun is permanently filtered through a thick pane of frosted glass.

The geology is raw, embryonic. This is a landscape that has only just decided to be land. There are no smooth rolling hills, no valleys carved by the slow patience of millennia. Instead, the terrain is a chaotic jumble of sharp-edged scree, jagged uplifts of basalt, and great fissured plates of slate that grind slowly against one another. The rock feels fresh, almost bloody in its newness; the edges are knife-sharp, untouched by the rounding hand of wind or time. It is a violent, unfinished place, emerging from the planetary crust like a broken bone piercing skin.

Water is everywhere, yet it is a landscape without rivers. The dampness is atmospheric, a heavy, clinging wetness that condenses instantly on every surface. The rocks weep; water seeps continuously from the fractures in the stone, gathering in slick, black pools that reflect the dim light. The ground is sodden and treacherous, a mixture of frost-slick mud and loose stone. Every step squelches, the water rising immediately to fill the footprint. It is a cold, pervasive wetness that chills the bone, soaking through fur and fiber alike.

Vegetation struggles here. It is sparse, clinging to existence in the lee of the larger rock formations. There are no trees, only thick, leathery mats of lichen the color of bruised iron and stunted, thorny bushes that grow low and flat against the ground, huddling together for warmth. They are tough, silica-heavy plants, adapted to scrape nutrients from the bare rock rather than rich soil.

But where the plants are scarce, the fauna is overwhelming. The silence of the high plateau is broken not by wind, but by the sounds of industry.

This is an ecosystem of engines. The dominant life forms are massive, slow-moving behemoths that drift across the scree like biological excavators. They are quadrupeds, the size of warehouses, with hides that are not flesh but interlocking plates of chitin and keratin that clank and grind as they move. They possess no grace, only a terrifying, deliberate momentum.

They are the industry of this world. As they walk, massive mandibles hanging low beneath their heads scrape the raw rock, shaving off layers of stone and mineral. The beasts consume the geology itself. Inside their immense, barrel-like bodies, the rock is processed—heavy, guttural vibrations rumble from their flanks, the sound of massive grinding mills and chemical furnaces reducing basalt to slurry. They are living refineries.

Steam vents from ports along their spines, hissing into the cold air and adding to the perpetual fog. Their waste is superheated slag, deposited in neat, steaming piles that harden into unnatural, glassy spires. The air is filled with the low-frequency thrum of their digestion, a rhythmic, industrial *thrum-hiss-clank* that echoes across the empty plateau.

Smaller creatures—scavengers with pick-like beaks and metallic feathers—dart around the legs of the giants, feeding on the parasites that clean the beasts’ grinding teeth or snatching up chunks of slag. It is a predator-prey dynamic driven by the consumption of the earth itself.

There are no buildings here, no cities, no hand-crafted tools. The "tech" is entirely biological, an evolutionary arms race that has produced organisms capable of heavy industry. The plateau is a factory floor without a roof, a wet, cold, dim expanse where giant living machines slowly dismantle the mountains, turning raw elevation into energy and heat.

Despite the abundance of life, the density feels low. The giants need space. They are solitary wanderers, visible miles away as dark, hulking shapes against the mist, separated by vast distances of wet, grey rock. The space between them is hollow and echoing, emphasizing the sheer scale of the enterprise.

It is a dynamic, active place. The ground tremors periodically with the movement of the giants. Slides of wet scree tumble down the fresh slopes. The weather shifts rapidly, pockets of freezing mist giving way to sudden, localized downpours of warm, oily rain generated by the collective breath of the herd. It is a world in the process of being made and unmade simultaneously, a cold, wet construction site where the workers are beasts, the materials are mountains, and the shift never ends.
