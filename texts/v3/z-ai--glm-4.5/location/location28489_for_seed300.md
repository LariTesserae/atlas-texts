It has the proportions of a cathedral nave—high, but narrowly enclosed (1.4/3). Within these walls, the ground is a dense, chaotic mosaic (2.1/3). There is no open soil; every inch is contested. Smooth, river-worn cobbles lie jammed against jagged shards of iron, and both are overgrown with a lacework of rust and lichen.

These artifacts are scattered (1.2/3), single features dotting the wet waste, half-drowned in the mire. Only low, hardy clusters of silver moss cling to the cooler sides of the iron pipes, and occasionally, a pale, fungus-like stalk rises from a crevice in the rock, only to collapse into black slime within a day. The flora is secondary to the mineral; it is a parasite on the machine of the world.

The landscape is digesting itself, a turbulent, silent stomach breaking down the iron of the past into the rounded stones of the future.

Sound travels only a few feet before being swallowed by the damp moss and the hanging fog. You might hear a distant, rhythmic *clunk*—a rock striking a hollow pipe from somewhere deep within the churn—but it is muffled, sounding as if it is coming from underwater.

There are things in the water—dark, slick shapes that dart between the submerged gears—but they rarely break the surface.

This is a place of transition, a waiting room for matter. The iron is becoming stone; the stone is becoming sand; the water is turning everything to rust. It is a beautiful, terrifying rot, silent and still, yet moving with the inexorable force of a glacier.
