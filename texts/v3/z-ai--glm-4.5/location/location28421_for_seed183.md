The light here is a physical weight. It is not merely bright; it is oppressive, a bleached, magnesium-white deluge that seems to hammer the landscape flat. At this extreme elevation, the sky is less a ceiling and more a vacuum of pure radiance, stripping shadows of their softness until they are merely sharp, black cuts in the fabric of the world.

You stand on the Ridge of Tears, though there are no tears, and no one to weep them. The ground is dense—a cluttered, chaotic saturation of stone forms that stretches to the horizon. It is an open, regional expanse, yet every inch of it is occupied by the mineral clutter. There are no plants. The score of 0.2 means the Green never happened here; instead, the world has colonized itself with silica and salt.

Where biology would have placed ferns or moss, this place puts "glass-bristle." The ground is carpeted in a crystalline fuzz, millions of tiny, translucent quartz fibers that grow directly out of the basalt. They are not alive; they are merely accretions of the world’s breathing, mineral exhalations caught in the act of freezing. In the crevices between the fibers lie "salt-hearts"—globular clusters of translucent pink crystal that pulse faintly with a geo-thermal rhythm.

The atmosphere is wet, a 1.7 on the scale, but it is a wrong kind of wet. The air is heavy with suspended moisture, a cold, clinging fog that does not rise but hangs like wet wool. The rocks are weeping, perpetually slick with runoff that appears to condense directly out of the solid stone. Water does not just flow here; it bleeds. The sheer cliff faces are streaked with black rivulets of mineral-rich water that never seem to dry, a constant, silent perspiration of the mountain.

The temperature is cool, a biting 1.2 chill that seeps through the dampness. It is the cold of a tomb that cannot decide if it wants to freeze or rot.

The sound is loud. It is not the roar of a waterfall, but a constant, high-frequency keening—the friction of the wind against the glass-bristle, the harmonic resonance of the salt-hearts, and the echoing *drip-drip-drip* of a billion weeping rocks amplified by the high altitude. It creates a tinnitus that never fades, a background static of geophysical processing.

And then there is the weirdness. Reality here is weary of holding its shape.

This is a landscape of non-local water. You can watch a droplet of condensation slide down a shard of obsidian, detach from the tip, and vanish before it hits the ground, only to reappear as a sudden splash in a pool ten yards away. The hydrology is broken; the water is taking shortcuts through dimensions. The erosion is established and settled, yet the landforms look unfinished. Great, arching bridges of stone span valleys without support, their ends fraying into smoke.

Scattered among the glass-bristle are the marks of the built—primitive, clumsy things. Simple stacks of rough-hewn stone, arranged in spirals or crude hovels, built by hands that likely did not understand the geometry of this place. The tech level is 1.1: stone tools, no metal. But looking closer, the tools are left where they fell, half-melted into the rock, as if the mountain decided to digest them. These structures are not ruins of a civilization; they are abortive attempts by something to anchor itself in a world that refuses to stay still.

There is no animal life. The silence of living things is total, replaced by the mechanical noise of the environment. But the ecology is dynamic. The glass-bristle grows visibly as you watch; the salt-hearts expand and contract with a sucking sound. The land is alive in the way a furnace is alive—active, hungry, and entirely inorganic.

The dynamic shift is in the light cycles. The sun does not set; it simply *dims*, a flicker of a failing bulb, and the shadows lengthen and detach from their objects, drifting across the valley floor like black oil. During these "twilights," the water flows uphill, chasing the receding heat, climbing the cliffs in defiance of gravity, only to crash down in a torrent of confusion when the bright returns.

It is a post-biological garden. It is a refinery where light is smashed into stone and water is the waste product. It is beautiful in the way a broken algorithm is beautiful—terrifying, precise, and utterly devoid of mercy.
