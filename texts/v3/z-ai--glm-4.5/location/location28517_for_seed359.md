The air is thick and hot, tasting of chalk and old copper, a dry heat that doesn't so much burn as it suffocates. There is no humidity here; the concept of moisture has been excised from the local physics, leaving the atmosphere static and heavy, like the air inside a sealed kiln.

You stand in the Courtyard of the Inner Sun, though there is no sun and no true sky—only a high, distant vault of stone, rough-hewn and suffering from a lack of polish. The scale is deceptive, claustrophobic yet vast, like the nave of a cathedral designed for giants who crouched. This is a "small" world only in the sense that it has boundaries, walls that close in not with pressure, but with the sheer density of their presence.

Everything is built. There is no geology here, only architecture. The ground beneath your feet is not soil but a tessellated pavement of fitted basalt and slate, locked together so tightly that a knife blade couldn't find the seam. It is a "total" construction, a prison or a monument raised by hands that clearly did not understand the right angles of Euclid. The masonry is primitive—hammer-dressed stone, stacked without mortar, leaning against itself in defiance of gravity. Yet, the construction is absolute. Every niche, every column, every shelf is hand-carved, suggesting a labor that spanned generations of obsessive, silent stonemasons.

But the stone is being eaten.

The "lushness" here is a profusion of glass and silica. Where water should be, there is vegetation made of sharp, transparent clarity. Crystal ferns unfurl from the joints in the pavement, their fronds brittle and refracting the normal, flat light into jagged rainbows. Towering stalks of hollow quartz rise like trees, their interiors filled with a shifting, dry gas that hisses faintly when the stalks sway. It is a garden of hazard. The ground is carpeted in shards of obsidian moss that crunches underfoot, a dense, overgrown thicket of mineral life that chokes the narrow avenues between the walls.

There is no erosion in the traditional sense—no wind to sand down the edges, no rain to soften the corners. The place is "young" in its agony. The edges of the stone blocks are still knife-sharp; the crystal points on the "leaves" are unblemished. However, there is a different kind of decay at work. The world is fresh, but it is wrong. The reality here is breaking down, peeling like wet paint, though there is no water to cause the rot. In the corners of your vision, the stone walls seem to fold in on themselves, a perspective error that hurts the eye if stared at for too long. A column might stand perfectly straight, yet its shadow falls diagonally across the room, stretching into a geometry that implies a fourth dimension.

The fauna is abundant, skittering through the glass undergrowth. They are things of chitin and clay, small crab-like creatures and multi-legged insects that click-clack over the basalt. They don't seem to breathe; they have no mouths. They simply exist, consuming the silica or perhaps the very space around them. A bird-like construct, made of woven twigs and bone, perches on a high quartz limb, watching with eyes that are simply polished stones. It does not sing; the silence here is "mostly quiet," broken only by the brittle tinkle of glass foliage brushing against stone, and the slow, grinding sub-audible rumble of the walls shifting.

The dynamic is slow, agonizingly so. This is a place of patient processes. If you were to stand still for an hour, you might see a crystal fern extend a millimeter, or a clay-crab slowly carve a new groove into the pavement. But weirder things happen slowly, too. A corridor you just walked down might gently shorten, the walls inching inward like a closing throat. A doorway might migrate three feet to the left while you blink. It is the movement of a dream trying to stabilize itself, and failing.

It is warm, stiflingly so, radiating from the stone and the living glass. The heat traps the scents—dust, dry minerals, and the faint, sharp smell of ozone that accompanies the reality breakdowns. It is a sealed ecosystem, a terrarium built by madmen or gods, where life has adapted to thrive on the absence of water, feeding on light and stone and the errors in the code of the universe. It is lush, it is crowded, it is entirely artificial, and it is fiercely, dangerously alive.
