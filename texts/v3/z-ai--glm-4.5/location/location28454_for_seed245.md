The air here is heavy, a humid, particulate soup that clings to the skin like wet silk. It is hot, a stifling, blanket heat that doesn't so much radiate from a sun as it does seem to exude from the ground itself. The light is dim, a perpetual twilight perhaps born of a thick, condensing atmosphere that diffuses the source into a bruised, violet-grey gloom. You cannot see the sky, only a gradual fading of the murk into a darker ceiling of vapor.

The landscape stretches out endlessly, a basin of low elevation that feels less like a valley and more like the floor of a universe that is still deciding how to solidify. This is a "Total" environment, a constructed place, yet it bears no mark of precision or industry. There are no seams, no sleek metals, no glowing conduits. Instead, the world feels crudely built, as if fashioned by hands the size of continents using tools that are primitive and immense.

Everything is built, but everything is raw.

You stand on a surface that feels like masonry, but it is masonry gone wrong—or perhaps, gone right in a way that defies standard engineering. The blocks are immense, irregular slabs of a grey, porous stone, stacked dry and without mortar. Yet, they are not settled. The "Erosion" here is negative; the land is not wearing away, it is actively emerging. The ground is embryonic. Gaps between the massive slabs weep water (1.7), a constant, slow drainage that collects in shallow, mirky pools on the uneven surface. The stone itself is sweating. Condensation slicks every surface, making the world glisten with a greasy sheen in the dim light.

There are no plants, no animals (0.4/0.5). The silence (1.4) is profound, but it is a tense silence. It is the quiet of a held breath.

The scale is vast (2.6). In the distance, spires rise up—not skyscrapers, but crude ziggurats of stacked, wet stone, miles high. They lean precariously, defying gravity through sheer stubbornness of mass. Between these monoliths lies the sparseness (1.4). Vast, empty plains of nothingness stretch out, interrupted only by the occasional solitary geometric shape—a perfect sphere of granite ten stories tall sitting in a puddle, or a single, immense wooden post driven into the "soil" that extends up beyond the visibility range.

The turbulence (2.6) is the heartbeat of this place. It is not a storm of wind, but a storm of matter. Without warning, the ground somewhere in the middle distance will shift. A section of the plain, miles wide, will suddenly decide to become a mountain. The great slabs will grind against one another, heaving upward with a slow, tectonic groan that vibrates in the teeth and bones. The sound is low, infrasonic, felt rather than heard. Elsewhere, a towering structure might simply lose coherence, dissolving back into a slurry of wet gravel and clay, collapsing into the weeping marshes below.

This is a world of primitive tech (1.2) operating on a god-scale. It looks as though giants attempted to pave a swamp using only stone knives and bear skins, and never stopped. You see the marks of crude tools—chisel marks the size of football fields, hack-saw gouges on canyon walls. The construction is manual, brutal, and ceaseless.

The weirdness (2.8) lies in the physics of the construction. The materials do not always behave like stone. Sometimes, a pillar will act like soft wax, bending in the heat before snapping back into rigid rock. The water flows uphill, defying the low elevation, siphoning into the sky via capillary action in tubes made of woven reeds the width of rivers. The geometry is non-Euclidean; a straight line walked toward a distant peak often brings you back to your own starting point, or deposits you instantly miles away, a glitch in the fabrication of reality.

It is a place of "wetness" without biology. The water supports no life; it is merely a lubricant for the grinding of the world. It is mineral, tasting of salt and iron. The atmosphere is thick enough to swim in, a dense fog that carries the scent of wet dust and ancient, unhewn clay.

Here, the world is not finished. It is being born around you, violently and clumsily. It is a nursery for geology, a chaotic workshop where continents are slapped together out of wet mud and giant bricks, left to dry in the dim, oppressive heat, only to be torn down and rebuilt before the sweat has even evaporated. There is no life to witness it, only the vague sense that the observation itself might be what keeps the bricks from falling.
