The ground does not offer a simple flat path. Instead, the terrain is a forest of stone—a chaotic, dense scattering of verticality. Thousands of jagged pillars rise from the earth, ranging from knee-height splinters to monolithic obelisks that loom thirty feet high. They are packed tightly together, a "dense" architecture of mineral obstacles that forces any traveler to weave and scramble. There are no plants here (0.1). No green softens the harsh lines. The "young" nature of the erosion is evident in the violence of the shapes. These pillars have not been smoothed by time. The spaces between the pillars are a labyrinth of razor-sharp ridges and fallen scree, unstable and treacherous underfoot.

Water is the dominant sculptor and tenant, scoring a 1.6 on the scale, but it behaves strangely here. It does not flow in rivers. It weeps. Droplets form at the tips of the jagged spires and fall with maddening regularity—*plink, plink, plink*—joining the shallow, oily puddles that stagnate in the shadows.

The wind, though cool and slow-moving, is forced through the tight corridors of the pillar forest, creating a low, mournful keening that vibrates in the chest. Overlaying this drone is the ceaseless dripping of water and the delicate, terrifying sound of the rock itself "singing." The rules of this world are slow and patient. Erosion is not a grinding down but a weeping out. The land is dissolving, drop by drop, even as it pushes upward. The dynamic is one of stasis; nothing happens quickly, yet everything is always moving—the water, the slow creep of the pale leather-beasts, the settling of the sharp stone. It is a loud, wet, sharp, and incredibly lonely place, a young world screaming in the dark while waiting to be worn smooth by the tears of the sky.
