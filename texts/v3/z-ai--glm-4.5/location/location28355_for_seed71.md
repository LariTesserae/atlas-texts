This is the High Loam, a place where the world has worn itself down to its absolute oldest patience.

**The Vastness and the Elevation**
To stand here is to stand on the roof of a geology that has forgotten how to be young. The elevation is extreme, a high-altitude expanse that stretches beyond the curvature of the visible horizon, yet the air is not thin. It is heavy, dense with the scent of wet dust and ancient oxygen. The scale is continental, planetary; you could walk for a thousand years and not see the edge of the plateau. Here, the weather does not arrive from elsewhere; it generates itself. Clouds are born in the crevices of the earth, swirl briefly, and die against the ground.

**Erosion and the Terrain**
The land is the definition of *eroded*. There are no jagged peaks, no violent cliffs. The violence of the world ended eons ago. Now, everything is rounded. Mountains have been sanded down to sleeping giants, immense whale-back hills of stone that roll endlessly into one another. The rock is dark, basaltic, and smooth, polished by a slow, grinding friction that persists even now. It feels like a world that has settled into its final form, a landscape that has achieved a state of deep, meditative rest. The valleys between the hills are shallow and wide, filled not with runoff, but with a soft, treacherous mist.

**Water and Temperature**
It is desperately cold, a biting, damp chill that never leaves the bones. It is a temperature of 0.3—a state where water hovers perpetually between freezing and sluggish liquid. There are no streaming rivers, no crashing waterfalls. Instead, the water is omnipresent and intrusive. It sweats from the stone face of the hills. It hangs in the air as a thick, cold fog that limits visibility to a few hundred yards. The ground is spongy, saturated, a marshy tundra that squelches underfoot, hidden beneath a carpet of moss so thick it feels like walking on a wet mattress. The dampness is constant; it is not a rain that falls, but a moisture that simply *is*, condensing on your skin and soaking through the simplest layers of cloth.

**Vegetation**
Despite the cold, the vegetation is aggressively lush. It is a strange, cold-dwelling verdancy. Giant ferns, taller than men, unfurl fronds that are dark green, nearly black, leathery and slick with wet. Trees exist, but they are stunted, wide things—bonsai of continental scale—with trunks as thick as cathedrals and canopies that spread low to the ground to soak up the dim heat. There is no fruit, no flower, just an endless, silent, dark biomass fueled by the damp soil and the diffuse light. The green is overwhelming in its volume, yet it feels heavy and melancholic.

**Silence and the Absence of Fauna**
The silence is the loudest thing here. The parameters indicate a near-total absence of animal life. There are no birds, no insects to hum, no mammals to scuttle. The ecology has no heartbeat of prey and predator. The wind does not howl; it merely sighs through the giant ferns. The only sounds are the geological ones: the distant, groaning shift of a glacier-mass of rock settling, the drip-drip-drip of condensation falling from a leaf, and the wet slap of your own boots. Without animal calls, the space feels vacuous, as if the world is a stage set waiting for actors who will never arrive.

**The Built Environment**
Sprawled across this endless, damp, cold plain is the city. It is primitive—built of hand-hewn stone, mud-brick, and massive timber—but its scale is urban. It is not a concentrated metropolis, however, but a sparse, endless sprawl. Small clusters of huts and low, longhouses are scattered across the hills, connected by rough-hewn roads that simply fade into the mist. The density is low; you might walk for an hour between houses.

The construction is rudimentary but massive. Walls are built without mortar, simply stacked gravity-defying slabs of the local smooth stone. It looks like a settlement built by a civilization that mastered shelter but never progressed past the stone age. Yet, the organization is evident. The roads are straight lines carved into the chaos of the hills. There is an infrastructure here, a logic to the placement of the dwellings, suggesting a society that values distance and privacy over community. It is a city the size of a country, populated by ghosts.

**The Reality Breakdown**
The weirdness here is subtle but pervasive. The laws of physics are not suggestions, but they are... flexible.
Distance behaves oddly. As you look toward the horizon, the hills seem to stack upon themselves, repeating patterns that shouldn't exist. A house that looks a mile away might take three days to reach, or you might step forward and find yourself immediately at its door.
Light sometimes falls sideways, illuminating the underside of leaves while the tops remain in shadow.
The erosion is not entirely natural. You find stone tools embedded deep within the rock, fossils of hands that are still holding the hammers, as if the rock grew around them instantly. In some places, the vegetation grows in perfect Euclidean shapes—cones, cubes, spheres of dark green moss—ignoring the chaotic wind.
Time feels thick. You can stand still and watch the ferns uncurl with a speed that makes the eye hurt, only to realize hours have passed in a second.

**The Dynamic**
Despite the silence and the lack of animals, the place is fiercely active. The cold damp is constantly freezing and thawing, cracking the stone. The ground heaves with a slow, tectonic breath. The reality itself flickers; a path might vanish and reappear a hundred feet to the left. The lush vegetation grows visibly, fighting the cold in a slow-motion riot of green. It is an active decay, an active growth, a world in the process of becoming something else entirely, slowly digesting itself in the cold, quiet dark.

**Rules of the World**
1.  **The Law of Relative Distance:** A straight line is rarely the fastest way. Movement here is often determined by intention, not steps. If you fear the distance, it lengthens.
2.  **The Cold Preservation:** Nothing rots here. Things only turn to stone or moss. Wood does not decay; it petrifies.
3.  **The Silence Treaty:** Sound does not travel far. To be heard, you must touch. Speech is a tactile act, not an auditory one.
4.  **The Architectural Imperative:** You cannot destroy the built structures. You can only add to them or bury them. The primitive urban frame is the skeleton of the reality; breaking it causes the local space to collapse into a featureless grey fog.
