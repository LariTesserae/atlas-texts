The sound hits you before the air does.

It is not a wind, though it moves like one. It is a roar, a continuous, physical thrumming that vibrates the bones of the plateau. This is the Scream of Altitude—a place where the atmosphere is so thin and the barometric pressure so volatile that the wind doesn't just blow; it sings in a register that shatters the eardrums of the unadapted. To stand here is to live inside a giant bell that has been struck and never stopped ringing.

The landscape is a high, desolate ridge, a fractured spine of stone rising above the cloud layer. It is a cathedral of dry air. The temperature is biting, a crisp, sterile cold that tastes like ozone and iron. There is no water here—not a drop, not a molecule of liquid. The ground is a bed of ancient, oxidized salts and dust, compressed over eons into a ceramic-like hardness. The erosion is established; the sharp edges of the rock have been sanded down not by water, but by centuries of acoustic abrasion, leaving the landscape smooth, rounded, and strangely blank.

Yet, it is lush.

The vegetation does not bloom; it calcifies. Rising from the dry mineral floor are the Singing Spires—great, towering trees of translucent amber and quartz. They do not drink; they listen. Their foliage consists of millions of thin, glass-like filaments that tremble in the deafening roar, transducing the punishing acoustic energy into chemical sustenance. They are spaced far apart, adhering to a strict mathematical spacing that suggests a planted orchard rather than a wild forest. Between each fifty-foot crystalline giant, there is a vast, empty silence—or rather, a vast, empty space where the roar has nowhere to hide. The density is near-void; you could walk for minutes in the open, dust-choked air before reaching the shadow of the next tree.

The scale is intimate despite the volume. The ridge feels like a room, a bounded arena perhaps a mile wide, boxed in by curving walls of obsidian that focus the sound like a whispering gallery. It feels small, a hamlet-sized sanctuary suspended in the stratosphere.

Life here has evolved to be hard, sleek, and abundant. The fauna are the "Shards"—flocks of metallic, insectoid creatures that swarm in the millions. They possess no wings, but rigid, aerodynamic shells that ride the pressure waves of the sound itself. They move in schools that flash like mercury in the normal, bright daylight, darting between the amber trees. They do not vocalize; they communicate by altering the reflectivity of their shells, signaling in a visual staccato that is impossible to track with the naked eye. They are the pollinators of the Spires, dusting the glass filaments with mineral powders.

The scene is turbulent. The "weather" changes violently, not in rain or temperature, but in pitch. Every few minutes, the acoustics shift—a "squall" of resonance strikes, and the air becomes visible with wavering distortions. The ground rumbles. The Singing Spires bow and sway, their glass leaves chiming in a dissonant harmony that raises the volume from a roar to a physical assault. During these moments, gravity feels lighter, less binding, as if the sound is holding the world up.

Scattered among the trees are the Built remnants. They are not shelters—nothing could shelter you from this noise—but they are artifacts. Monoliths of a matte, non-reflective material that sucks in the light. They stand waist-high, etched with glowing runes that pulse in time with the acoustic squalls. These are not ruins of a house, but terminals of some post-modern engineering, anchors for a reality that is constantly trying to vibrate itself apart. They hum with a counter-frequency, the only thing keeping the ridge from shaking itself into dust.

It is a strange, deafening garden. A place where life eats noise, where the rain is replaced by vibration, and where the silence between the crashing waves of sound is the most terrifying thing of all. You do not walk here; you brace yourself against the roar, a visitor in a high-altitude echo chamber that is determined to shake you to pieces.
