The air here is thick enough to chew. It hangs heavy and warm, a suspended breath that smells of wet copper, crushed ferns, and the sweet, cloying scent of decaying nectar. This is the Sump-Garden, a low-lying depression in the earth that the rest of the world seems to have forgotten, or perhaps intentionally avoided.

You don't walk on the ground here; you walk *on* the garden.

**Terrain and Substrate**
The concept of "soil" has been abolished. At an elevation barely above the water table, the earth has surrendered entirely to biology. The ground is a dense, knotted mattress of roots—pale, arterial cables that pulse with a slow, rhythmic turgor. They layer over one another in a chaotic felt, compressing centuries of growth into a spongy, unstable floor. Every step yields, sinking a few inches before the interlocking mat catches your weight. Water doesn't pool in puddles; it is squeezed out like sweat from the very ground you walk on, welling up between the toes of your boots.

Water is constant but patient. It is not a flood, but a weeping. Every vertical surface—the trunks of trees, the rusted stanchions of forgotten machines, the hanging vines—is slick with moisture. The atmosphere condenses on every available surface, creating a perpetual, fine rain that falls upward from the breath of the plants.

**Vegetation: The Total Mass**
The vegetation rating here is absolute; it is not a collection of plants, but a single, continuous architectural tissue. There are no distinct trees, only pillars of the same massive, woody vine-flesh that support the canopy. These pillars are fused together where they touch, welded by years of humidity and parasitic mosses, forming arches and buttresses that mimic the layout of a ruined cathedral.

The space is packed. There is no "undergrowth" because there is no gap between the floor and the ceiling. Ferns the size of billiards tables grow directly out of the vertical trunks. Orchids with pneumatic-looking bulbs hang in chains, their roots tangled with power lines. The canopy above is a solid sheet of leathery, emerald-green leaves, fracturing the "normal" light into a diffuse, aquatic gloom. It feels less like a forest and more like the interior of a lung—wet, organic, and enclosing.

**The Industrial Scars**
This is where the weirdness creeps in. The biological mass has not just grown over the industrial past; it has digested it. Scattered throughout the grove are the rusted, skeletal remains of a pumping station, likely a century old.

Massive iron pipes, now burst and Corroded into lacework, pierce through the root-floor. They don't lie on the ground; they *are* the ground. The roots have grown around the pipes, gripping them with the same tension they use to grip rock, but in places, the roots have infiltrated the pipes. You can peer into the flared mouth of an exhaust vent and see, deep inside, a tangle of pale roots drinking from the sludge at the bottom.

A massive flywheel, half-swallowed by a bole of wood, stands in the center of the clearing. It no longer spins, but the heat of the decomposition and the warm water pooled in its hub creates a thermal current that makes the vines above it shiver. The technology here is repurposed: rain gutters become sluices for sap; gear-teeth serve as perches for sluggish, moisture-loving fungi.

**Atmosphere, Sound, and Dynamic**
The sound is a low, deafening thrum. It is the sound of billions of leaves transpiring, the sound of water moving through xylem the size of sewer pipes, and the distant, metallic groan of the old settling machinery. Occasionally, a drop of water hits a resonant metal plate buried in the vegetation with a loud, musical *ping*. It is a loud place, but the noise is ambient and constant, a pressure against the ears rather than a series of distinct events.

Everything moves slowly. The dynamic of this place is geologic. You cannot see the plants grow, but you can feel the tension. The vines are slowly, inexorably crushing the iron beams. In a decade, the flywheel will be gone. In a century, the pipes will be dust. It is a slow-motion violence.

**Fauna and Isolation**
Life here is rare because the competition for space is too fierce. Birds cannot fly through the density; they would be tangled in the hanging roots. The only visible fauna is a single, solitary entity—something that looks like a large, arboreal sloth, but with fur that mimics the texture of moss and bark. It hangs motionless on a pipe-arch, blinking once every ten minutes, indistinguishable from the industrial decay it inhabits. It does not call out; it simply exists, another piece of furniture in the green room.

**The Rules of the World**
To exist here is to submit to humidity. Metal rusts in days; leather rots in hours. Electronics fail. The only things that persist are stone, glass, and the relentless, hungry plant-mass. It is a closed ecosystem, a hamlet-sized terrarium that is slowly tightening its grip on itself. The boundary is sharp—step five feet up the slope, and the trees thin out. Step down, and you are in the belly of the beast.

It is a place where the machine died and the plant kept the engine running, turning the mechanism of industry into a temple of slow, wet, suffocating life.
