The sky here is a blinding, unyielding sheet of white—heat made visible. It is not merely hot; the air has weight, a thick, pressurized haze that presses against the skin like a damp woolen blanket, though there is no moisture to be found. We stand on a high, ancient plateau, a vastness of baked earth and oxidized mineral that stretches endlessly toward a horizon that seems to vibrate with thermal distortion.

The ground underfoot is not soil, but a planetary scab. This is a landscape of extreme desiccation (0.2), a place where the concept of "wet" is a geological error from a previous epoch. The terrain is a cracked mosaic of polygonal plates, the fissures between them plunging deep into the crust. Here, erosion has not worn things down so much as it has smoothed them into a terrifying, featureless uniformity (2.4). The wind, when it comes, does not carry sand; it carries dust so fine and electrostatically charged that it behaves like a gas, hissing over the polished, rounded basalt hills.

It is profoundly empty (0.4), a hollow cathedral of stone where the distance between objects is measured in miles rather than meters. Yet, it is loud (2.2). The silence of the desert is a myth; this place roars with a low-frequency thrumming, a constant, seismic industrial grinding that vibrates in the marrow of your bones. There are no birds, no insects, no rustling of grass—because there is no grass (0.3). Vegetation is an impossibility here. The only green is the oxidized copper shimmer of the stratified cliffs.

The source of the noise is the life here, if it can be called that.

The fauna (2.6) does not roam; it processes. This is the industrial biological engine of the world. Dominating the open plains are the Great Fermenters—colossal, slow-moving arthropods the size of cathedral ruins. They are not built, yet they possess the terrifying geometry of heavy industry (2.4/0.5). Their carapaces are interlocking plates of natural steel and silica, venting steam from spiracle stacks along their flanks. They move with agonizing slowness (0.9), a geological pace that renders them almost motionless to the casual observer. A single step of a massive, piston-like leg might take a decade to complete.

They do not eat. They mine. Huge, mandibular drills rotate at the front of their heads, boring into the crust to extract raw minerals and thermal energy from the planet's deep heat. As they process the earth, they excrete vast, trailing ribbons of superheated, refined slag—their "waste"—which glows dull cherry-red before cooling into the black glass that litters the valley floors. This is the ecosystem: a conversion of raw stone into energy and structure, performed by living locomotives that have existed for eons.

The scale (2.2) is deceptive. A "herd" might consist of three individuals spaced five miles apart, yet their presence dominates the region. Between them move the scavengers—shoals of flat, metallic ray-like creatures that glide through the superheated air just inches above the ground, feeding on the magnetic disturbances left in the wake of the giants.

But the strangest aspect of this place is the friction between the living and the physical laws (2.9). The heat here is so intense, and the biological processing of the fauna so energetic, that reality begins to shear. The air shimmers not just with heat haze, but with spatial distortion. Looking at a distant Fermenter, its silhouette often fractures, overlapping with itself in a time-delay echo. You see the beast where it is, and faintly, where it was ten seconds ago, and where it will be in ten seconds, all superimposed.

Sometimes, the "slag" they excrete defies gravity, drifting upward in floating, jagged islands before cooling and plummeting. The sound of the land loops—a grinding screech might hang in the air for minutes, repeating like a scratched record, even after the creature that made it has ceased its motion. It is a world where the boundary between machine and organism, and between solid and energy, has melted in the crucible of the high, dry oven.

Time here is not a flow; it is a slow, crushing accumulation. The sun beats down, indifferent and normal (2.1), illuminating a scene that looks like the aftermath of a factory that ate the world. The Great Fermenters continue their endless, slow harvest, the living engines of a high, hot, hollow purgatory, turning the silence of the stone into the roaring song of industry.
