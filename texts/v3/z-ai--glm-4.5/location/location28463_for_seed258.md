The landscape sits high, suspended in the warm, still air of a perpetual plateau. It is not a mountain range that pierces the clouds, but rather a singular, vast shelf of land that floats above the low-lying mists, extending to the horizon in a stark, uncanny grid.

Here, the distinction between geology and biology has collapsed. The ground is not soil, nor is it stone in the traditional sense. It is a construction of living material—a dense, interwoven lattice of calcified roots and petrified woody tissue that forms the bedrock. This substrate is "total," a pure plant-architecture that has grown into the shape of the world itself. It looks manufactured, with vast, flat plains of rhizome-matter stretching out like paved plazas, yet every inch is biological, pulsing with a slow, deep sap that mimics the function of tectonic plates.

The atmosphere is heavy and wet (2.0). The warmth (1.8) holds a humidity that feels like breathing through a warm washcloth. Water does not fall as rain here; instead, the condensation is constant. The air is so saturated that moisture beads on every surface, dripping from the towering, arched buttresses of the mega-flora like weeping rocks. The "condensing atmosphere" ensures that the landscape is always slick, shimmering under a normal, diffuse daylight (2.1) that filters through the high haze.

Despite the lushness of the medium, the density of the features is sparse (0.6). The vast, open plain of the root-floor is interrupted only by immense, singular structures—living towers that rise hundreds of feet into the air. These are not forests, but isolated pillars of biological architecture, spaced far apart like the temples of a ruined civilization. Between them lies the emptiness, the open "town-or-valley" scale (1.8) that makes a traveler feel small and exposed.

The "emerging" (0.4) nature of the erosion is the most unsettling aspect. Everything looks raw, as if the world was created yesterday and has not yet weathered. The edges of the root-plazas are sharp and splintered, not rounded by wind or time. There is no soil, no dust, no decay. It is a embryonic landscape frozen in the moment of its completion. This newness is preserved by the absolute stillness (0.5). Nothing changes. The water drips, but the pools never overflow; the plants live, but they do not grow or bloom. The world is in a state of frozen equilibrium, a dynamic of zero entropy.

The sound (2.0) defines the experience. It is loud, a constant, low-frequency thrum that vibrates in the chest. It is the sound of the world’s metabolism—millions of gallons of sap rushing through the hidden veins of the root-floor, the creaking of the woody towers as they settle imperceptibly, and the endless, echoing splash of condensation falling into the deep, perfectly circular canals that traverse the plains. It is a mechanical noise produced entirely by organic means.

Fauna (1.3) is rare. Occasionally, a solitary, pale shape—something like a winged drifter or a silent, six-legged grazer—can be seen moving in the immense distance between the towers, but they are few and far between. They do not call out; they simply exist within the noise.

The "built" (2.8) environment is total, yet devoid of technology (0.4). The "construction" is instinctual, a hive-mind of flora that has grown arches, bridges, and domes where needed, perfectly geometric and possessing a weirdness (2.5) that unsettles the human eye. Angles that should be organic are forced into perfect right angles; spirals follow the golden ratio with mathematical precision. It is a garden built by a god with a compass, empty of people, full of water, and eternally, loudly, wetly alive.
