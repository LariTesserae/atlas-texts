You do not look up at the sky here; you look up at the rim, a jagged collar of grey stone and twisted metal that cuts a dim circle against the fog. The light is weak, a watery, bruised violet that seems to emanate from the damp air itself rather than any sun. It is a twilight that never breaks, a perpetual, cool gloom.

The condensation is so dense that water beads on every surface, hanging like sweat on the cold iron of the railings. It is a clammy, chill cold (1.1), the kind that seeps through wool and settles into the joints, a dampness that presumes a right to your skin.

It is an urban ruin, or perhaps a ruin that has become a city. The scale is deceptive; at first, it feels like a vast cathedral, a single echoing vault, but as you move, you realize it is a small, enclosed hamlet of industry (0.6). The structures are built on a human scale, but pressed tight against the walls and clustered around the central depression. Great flywheels, taller than a man, are locked in place, caked in grey calcite. It looks like a factory that has been praying for centuries. Yet, it is not dead. It is merely slow. The machinery is not stopping; it is operating on a geologic timescale.

Vegetation is sparse (1.3), struggling against the chemical tang of the air. There are no trees, no broad canopies to catch the dim light. Instead, the ecology is tenacious and low. Tough, leathery lichens cling to the pipes, colored the dull orange of oxidized copper. Occasionally, a single, fern-like frond unfurls from a crack in the concrete, its leaves black and glossy, drinking the condensation directly. The green is muted, greyed-out by the lack of sun, fighting a losing battle against the encrusting rust.

But where the plants fail, the fauna thrives (2.3). The damp and the warmth of the slow-moving engines have birthed a strange, teeming ecosystem. This is the core of the weirdness (2.2). The boundary between the mechanic and the organic has eroded. The rust on the pipes moves under its own power; it is a colony of microscopic iron-eating grazers. The erosion here is established (2.3). Nothing is sharp. The iron has rounded off like river stones. The brickwork is worn smooth by centuries of trickling water and the passage of countless soft bodies. It is a landscape in equilibrium. The decay has stopped because the material has found a new purpose. The city is not falling down; it is digesting itself, transforming into a habitat.

A mechanical limb, skeletal and worn, reaches up from the water, draped in moss that looks like grey hair. A small, furry creature with too many eyes scampers along the limb, pausing to groom itself.

There is no wind here, only the circulation of air driven by temperature differentials. The sound is the lapping of water against metal, the distant, rhythmic groan of settling stonework, and the soft, wet patter of a thousand tiny feet moving in the dark. It is a quiet, wet, room-sized world, sitting below the earth, dreaming in slow motion.
