The air here is heavy, a physical weight you have to push through, warm and thick with suspended moisture. It is a twilight world, lit by a diffuse, low-hanging luminescence that suggests a sun obscured by perpetual, immobile cloud cover. The light never brightens to noon and never fades to night; it simply is, a constant dim grey-gold that makes shadows bleed softly into the wet stone.

This is a place of raw edges. The geology feels embryonic, as if the mountains and valley floors were churned up from the deep crust only a moment ago and then instantly frozen in time. There are no rounded hills here, no valleys smoothed by the patience of rivers. The terrain is a chaotic, jagged sprawl of shale and basalt spires, sharp enough to cut, thrusting upward from a shallow basin. The scale of it is regional—a vast, open plain of broken stone extending to the horizon—but the sheer density of what covers it makes the horizon feel close.

Water is the master of this place, but it does not flow. It weeps. Every outcrop of black rock is slick with condensation; droplets constantly form on the mineral surfaces, blooming and sliding down in endless, unchanging rivulets. The ground squelches underfoot, a layer of saturated gravel and grey mud that never dries. Puddles are permanent features, mirrors that catch the dim light without rippling, held in perfect stasis by the lack of wind and the saturated air.

There are no plants. The landscape is entirely mineral, save for a slick, microbial sheen that coats the wetter rocks—a primitive, fungal-orange slime that represents the only "vegetation" this world allows. It is silent in terms of growth; nothing rustles, nothing photosynthesizes.

Instead, the space is urban. Rising from the jagged stone floor is a city, or something that resembles one, constructed entirely from the raw materials at hand. It is a primitive architecture of rough-hewn blocks, mud mortared with the grey sludge, and timber scavenged from... elsewhere. There are no metal beams, no glass windows, no synthetics. Just splintery wood and heavy, dark stone. The structures are packed tight, a dense warren of multi-story tenacles stacked haphazardly upon one another, bridging the sharp ravines with ropes and planks. It feels less like a designed city and more like a sprawling infestation, a dense hive of humanity clinging to the raw geology.

And it is loud.

The sound is deafening, a roaring wall of noise that never ceases. It is not the chaotic clamor of battle, but the relentless, rhythmic din of life compressed too tightly. It is a cacophony of voices—shouting, chanting, conversing at the top of their lungs—overlaid with the sounds of labor: the rhythmic *thud-thud-thud* of stone against stone, the shriek of saws cutting wet timber, the clatter of primitive tools. There is no attempt to dampen the noise; the stone reflects it, amplifying the roar until it vibrates in your chest.

But the source of the loudest noise is not the people. It is the fauna.

The city is infested. Abundant, teeming life swarms through the streets and buildings. These are creatures of the wet stone—amphibian monstrosities the size of dogs, with slick, wet skin and oversized, bulging eyes. They congregate in thousands on every rooftop, every ledge, every muddy street. They are the dominant presence here, outnumbering the human inhabitants ten to one. And they scream. It is a constant, high-pitched croaking and barking, a tidal wave of biological noise that merges with the human clamor into a single, oppressive roar.

Nothing changes. This is the strangest rule of this world: it is locked in a perfect, deafening equilibrium. The rain does not start or stop; the moisture level is constant. The sun does not set; the light remains fixed. The noise does not abate; it holds at exactly the same decibel level, hour after hour, year after year. The people work, the amphibians scream, the water weeps down the raw, un-eroded stone, and the haze hangs low. It is a snapshot of a moment stretched into eternity, a loud, wet, crowded forever.
