The air here is not empty; it is merely spacious, a vast, warm cathedral of atmosphere where the distance between the floor and the ceiling is measured in miles.

This is the High Strata.

**Terrain and Vegetation**
The concept of "soil" has vanished. At this extreme elevation, the ground is a singular, continuous expression of plant life. Here, vegetation is not a collection of organisms inhabiting a landscape; the vegetation *is* the landscape.

You stand upon the back of a Titan. The "mountains" you see in the distance are not tectonic stone, but the petrified, lignified crowns of arboreal colossi that died standing upright millions of years ago, their roots interlocked so deeply that the distinction between individual trees has dissolved. They have settled into their final form—a state of ancient erosion where the biological has become geological.

The surface underfoot is warm, fibrous, and tough as cured leather. It radiates a gentle, primal heat, a residual metabolism of a world that has slowed down but not stopped. The color is a uniform, desaturated bronze-green, worn smooth by eons of wind and friction. There are no leaves in the traditional sense, only vast, plated sheets of photosynthetic material that overlap like dragon scales, covering the rolling hills of plant-matter.

**Atmosphere and Water**
The air is warm (1.5/3), a perpetual, breezy spring that carries the scent of ozone, drying sap, and the musk of biology. It is damp (1.2/3) not with rain, but with the breath of the world. Moisture gathers on the immense, plate-leaves above and occasionally releases in sudden, heavy cascades—vertical rivers that fall for thousands of feet before shattering into mist on the canopy below. When the sun hits the falling water, rainbows are not arcs but complete, rings of color standing upright in the air.

The humidity clings to the skin, a thin film of sweat that never quite dries, kept in check by the constant, drying wind.

**Density and Scale**
The defining characteristic of this place is the terrifying ratio of size to population. The scale is open (2.5/3) and aggressively empty (0.5/3). You could walk for a day across the ridged back of a single ancient tree-trunk without seeing a vertical horizon.

The space between the "ground" and the "sky" is a sheer vertical drop of three miles. Between the rolling hills of this arboreal plateau and the distant, nebulous ceiling of the upper atmosphere, there is nothing but clear, warm air. This void gives the landscape a feeling of terrifying exposure. There are no walls, no caves, no dense thickets to hide in. You are always visible, always exposed to the sun and the sky.

**Sound and Fauna**
If the visual world is empty, the auditory world is a riot (2.7/3). The silence of the void is broken by the overwhelming presence of aerial fauna (2.9/3).

Because there is no ground-level terrain to support terrestrial walkers, the ecology has taken to the air. The sky is thick with life. Great, leathery-winged things with wingspans of thirty feet ride the thermals generated by the heat of the living ground. Flocks of smaller, needle-beaked dart-birds move like schools of fish, their millions of wings beating in a unified rhythm that sounds like a roaring ocean.

The soundscape is deafening: the shriek of raptors, the low-frequency thrum of giant gliders, the incessant chatter of millions of insects drifting in the upper currents. It is a wall of noise that never ceases, day or night. Even the wind makes noise here, whistling through the hollow, petrified spires of the dead Titans, creating a mournful, low-grade drone that vibrates in the chest.

**The Built Environment**
Amidst this grandeur, the marks of habitation are faint (1.4/3). The inhabitants—presumably human, though they seem small and fragile here—are not masters of this domain; they are parasites.

Living in the "scattered" (1.4/3) pockets where the massive plate-leaves create natural bowls or sheltered depressions, they exist using primitive tech (1.3/3). Their homes are not built; they are grown or carved. They use stone tools to hollow out blisters in the bark, creating communal huts that hang precariously over the vertical drop.

They do not work metal. They use bone and woven fiber. Their bridges are not of rope, but of long, dried vines braided together, stretching across the immense canyons of empty air between plant-masses. To travel between the "islands" of foliage, they use gliders made of stretched hide and light wood, riding the updrafts like the birds they hunt. They are a people of the air, terrified of the fall, inextricably bound to the deafening, living sky.

**Dynamic and Rules**
The world is active (1.8/3) but slow in its geological movements, fast in its biological ones. The "ground" heaves slowly, a breathing motion that can cause nausea in newcomers. The weather cycles are violent and sudden; a clear morning can turn into a windstorm within an hour as the heat of the plant-mass clashes with the cooling upper atmosphere.

The rule of this world is simple: *Do not fall.* There is no bottom to catch you, only a fall through miles of aerial predators and a final impact on a canopy layer so far down it is invisible from here.

**Summary**
You are standing on the roof of the world. It is a warm, damp, endless plain of living wood, stretching to the horizon in rolling, bronzed hills. Above, the sky is a chaotic, deafening storm of wings and motion. Below, there is nothing but three miles of empty air. It is beautiful, ancient, and profoundly lonely, despite the noise.
