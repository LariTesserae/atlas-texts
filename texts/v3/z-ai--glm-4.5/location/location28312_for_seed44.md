The air here is heavy enough to wear. It hangs in the lungs, a humid, thermal soup tasting of wet iron and crushed ferns.

This is the Boiler-Grove, a singular, concentrated pocket of low-lying industry where the distinction between the factory and the forest has been eroded by centuries of rust and root. It sits in a shallow depression in the land, elevation low enough that the water table doesn't just exist beneath the soil; it is the soil. The ground is not solid earth, but a raucous, spongy mat of roots compressed so densely by the weight of the machinery above that they have fused into a kind of woody pavement. Here, every step sinks slightly, the water weeping from the ground with a wet *shhh-thuck* sound.

It takes a full minute to complete a single revolution. The steam rises in languid, lazy columns that drift upward to condense on the leafy ceiling, raining back down in a perpetual, warm mist.

The vegetation has adapted to this throttled tempo. The leaves are thick, leathery, and dark green, designed to catch the dim, filtered light that trickles down through the dense canopy above. They are coated in a layer of grease and soot, yet they thrive. Moss grows in thick, velvety pads over the moving gears, untroubled by the friction, acting as a lubricant. The local ecology has replaced animals with mechanisms. There are no birds, only the automated clatter of control valves. There are no insects, only the steam-whistles that signal the shift changes.

Water is the blood of this place. It is everywhere. It drips from the joints of pipes, it weeps from the seams of the brick furnaces, and it stands in stagnant, oily puddles on the root-floors. The humidity is such that condensation constantly forms on the cold metal surfaces, feeding the algae that carpets the lower rungs of the infrastructure.

The silence of nature is total. The only "animals" are the distant, shadowy shapes of maintenance workers moving along the high gantries—small, ant-like figures in oilskins, tending to the symbiosis of iron and chlorophyll. They move with the same slow, deliberate pace as the machines, unbothered by the noise, which has long since ceased to be a sound and has become simply another quality of the atmosphere, like the heat or the wet.

It is a place of arrested decay and aggressive growth. The erosion is established; nothing here is new, and nothing is dead. The rust is just another type of bark; the steam is just another type of breath. The Boiler-Grove is a stable, noisy, wet lung, breathing in the swamp water and exhaling industrial heat, trapped in a slow, timeless cycle of production and respiration.
