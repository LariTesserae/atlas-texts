The Dimina Peaks. The name is a half-forgotten echo from a people who are gone, or perhaps never were. It clings to the place like the rime-ice to the black, basalt cliffs. Peaks they are, but cleaved by valleys that are less safe passage than wind-tunnels, funnelling glacial gusts between the stone teeth of the mountains.

Water is here, but not in abundance. It weeps from the rock, a constant, melancholic sweat that freezes almost as soon as it emerges. Miniature curtains of ice hang trembling, blurring the razor edges of the cliffs. Sometimes, in sheltered pockets, it gathers into tarns so black and still they seem to swallow the weak light. Once a year, a thin rain falls, a mournful drizzle carried on winds that scream of places further north, places perpetually drowned under grey skies. But the rain is brief. Mostly, it is the creeping damp, the frost that flowers in the deepest shadows, the perpetual slickness of the stone.

Vegetation clings to life with grim determination. Lichens are the dominant flora, crusting the rocks in shades of grey, black, and virulent green. A few tenacious mosses find purchase in crevices where the wind cannot entirely scour them away. Even more rarely, stunted, ground-hugging shrubs twist out of the stone, their leaves thick and waxy, their branches gnarled by the ceaseless wind. They huddle together, seeking warmth in numbers, but they are always alone in the end.

The air bites with a cold that settles deep in the bones. It is a cold beyond temperature, a cold of being. It is the cold of a dead god’s stare, the cold of absolute, uncaring space. It is a cold that seems to amplify the silence, drawing the heat from any sound, leaving only the wind's screaming.

The elevation here is not just high, but savage. The mountains rise in impossible angles, defying both gravity and reason. The paths, if they can be called that, are narrow ledges carved by the wind and the freeze-thaw cycle, clinging to the cliff-face like the desperate thoughts of a dying man. There is a sense of being above the world here, beyond its concerns. The air thins; the sky presses down, a low, oppressive ceiling.

Erosion is a slow, grinding force here, but not a chaotic one. It is the sculptor of ages, patiently refining a masterpiece of desolation. The stone is smoothed, weathered, but solid. It has reached a state of near-perfect equilibrium. This close to the sky, where gravity is the master, loose debris will quickly fall, but the stone itself will remain so long as pressure and temperature remain. The mountains are old, worn like worry beads by a god who has forgotten what he prays for.

The scale is vast, but strangely intimate. You can see for leagues, across valleys that plunge into shadow, to peaks that pierce the clouds. Yet, at the same time, the sheer overwhelming violence of the landscape collapses in on itself, so only the single step in front of you remains.

Empty. That is the defining characteristic of the Dimina Peaks. Empty of life, empty of warmth, empty of hope even. The land has a silence that is not peaceful but oppressive, the silence of a tomb waiting for its dead. It is the silence of the universe, a vast, uncaring void stretching in every direction.

There is evidence of something else, though. Scattered stones arranged with unnatural regularity, half-collapsed cairns bleached white by the sun and scoured by the wind, and the occasional single walls, stark and jagged, cutting across the valleys in defiance, are all that remains. They are small against the hugeness of the land, easily missed, and nearly lost to the elements, but they are there. Whispers of a time when others walked these slopes. Now, there is only the wind murmuring through the stones.

No technology remains. Only raw materials arranged and rearranged. Nothing could survive such a place for long without being dismantled by the wind.

The light in the Dimina Peaks is meager. The sun, when it appears, is pale and weak, a ghost of warmth in a bone-chilling world. Mostly, the mountains are shrouded in a perpetual twilight, a dim, grey half-light that seems to drain the color from everything it touches. A perpetual lunar eclipse hangs over the valleys even when the sun is high.

Rare fauna ekes out an existence here. A handful of hardy mountain goats, their fur thick and matted, cling to the precipitous cliffs. A few ravens, black as sin, haunt the sky, their cries lost in the wind. They are the scavengers of this brutal land, feeding on scraps of anything they can find. But mostly, there is nothing.

And then there is the *weirdness*. The very *texture* of reality seems frayed here. The wind, for example, does not just blow; it *speaks*. Groans, whispers, laughter, shouts, sometimes forming half-remembered words in languages that should not exist. The shadows stretch too long, too thin, and they move with a life of their own. The stone seems to shift when you are not looking directly at it, rearranging itself into impossible geometries. Objects occasionally vanish, only to reappear days later miles away. Time flickers, with moments stretching like taffy and others collapsing to a single burst. The valleys open and show you cities of cyclopean stone, that disappear the moment the fog moves, and the sun shines again. The Dimina Peaks are a place where the laws of nature are… suggestions, rather than hard rules.

The sound here is the wind. It is a constant, relentless presence, howling through the valleys, shrieking around the peaks. It is a sound that drives men mad, a sound that strips away sanity, a sound that embodies the emptiness and the desolation of this place. It is also, sometimes, joined by the grinding of stone against stone or the deep crack of ice as another section of a hanging glacier works loose on the cliff face.

The dynamic of this place is like still life in a world of motion. The movement of time is almost an abstract concept here. You could sit here for a year and see virtually no change. The Dimina peaks are in cold lock, an eternal, frozen equilibrium. This is not a place of becoming, but a place of being: an artifact left on its own for a long, cold time. A monument to something no one remembers.
