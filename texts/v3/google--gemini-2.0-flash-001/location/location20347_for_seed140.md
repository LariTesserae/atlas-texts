The air is thick, close to water. Not rain, though, not exactly. More like breathing in a cloud, a dampness that clings and saturates. This is the High Mire — a vast, shallow bowl cupped between mountains worn down to gentle humps by time beyond counting. The land here is ancient, settled into its final form like an old beast sinking into the mud.

Darkness is the rule. Not absolute, lightless black, but a perpetual twilight filtered through layers of condensed moisture and the dense canopy overhead. What light there is has a reddish hue, as if bled through countless leaves.

The vegetation is aggressively lush. Giant, fern-like trees with thick, spongy trunks crowd together, their crowns a matted ceiling that almost seems to touch the moss-draped ground. Vines thicker than a human torso twist and writhe, connecting everything in a tangled web. Everywhere, there is the sense of constant growth, of life pushing against life in a silent, slow-motion battle for light and space.

Beneath the living blanket, the mud sucks at your feet. It's warm and yielding, the kind of mud that smells of decay and teeming life. Small pools of water collect in the deeper hollows, reflecting the dim light in fractured patterns. These pools feed countless species of luminescent fungi, their soft, otherworldly glow the only real source of light in the mire.

The sound is almost overwhelming. Not a cacophony, but a constant, throbbing hum. The croaking and clicking of unseen amphibians blends with the drone of insects and the rustling of leaves in the perpetual damp breeze. Deeper, there's a resonant booming that feels more felt than heard; the sound of the massive trees drinking, drawing water up from the saturated earth.

Fauna abounds. Insects with chitinous shells and iridescent wings flit through the undergrowth. Strange, six-legged mammals rustle in the ferns, their bodies striped with bioluminescent patterns. Overhead, winged creatures like feathered kites glide through the murk, their calls echoing through the moss-draped trees. Most are harmless; their existence a display of the richness of the ecosystem of the Mire.

Scattered throughout the Mire are the remnants of a forgotten civilization. Small structures, too overgrown to be fully discernible, made of a dark, polished stone fused seamlessly together. They are integrated rather than built; each appears to have been grown in its place. Twisted archways lead to shadowed chambers. In several rooms, complex geometries hang without support, vibrating slowly with a low tone. The technology here is beyond anything comprehensible, hints of engineering that operates on scales beyond our current physics. The stone itself seems to resonate with an energy that warms the air around it.

The Mire is empty in the truest sense. It’s difficult to see more than a few meters in any direction, the density of life too thick to allow for long views. It’s a place of hidden depths and intimate spaces, where the boundaries of perception blur. One could spend a lifetime wandering here and never see the same space twice. Change is incremental and geological. Plants grow, then decay. Animals are born, then die. The slow hum is the sound of the Mire breathing. The cycle is endless and inexorable. The High Mire is a world unto itself: a place of wet darkness, a place of life pushed the extreme, and a place of ancient secrets lying dormant beneath the moss.
