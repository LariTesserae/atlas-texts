The air tastes of brine and ozone, metallic tang overlaid with something sickly sweet, like fermenting fruit left too long in the sun. It hangs heavy and still, clinging to the skin, prickling with humidity despite the dim light filtering through the perpetual smog.

This is the edge of the Slag Flats, the last semi-solid ground ringing the Processing Ponds. To the west, a horizon of rusted-orange metal and broken concrete stretches into a haze. To the east, the Ponds themselves start; a seemingly endless expanse of iridescent, viscous liquids under a sky perpetually bruised with chemical runoff.

This "ground" is more accurately a mat of compressed slag and industrial detritus, colonized by a bizarrely successful form of bioluminescent algae. It thrives in the toxic runoff, forming a fragile, spongy skin over the poisoned earth. The dominant vegetation consists of thick, fleshy stalks that swell with a reddish fluid. These stalks, known locally as "Blood Reeds", sway sluggishly in the humid air, their surfaces slick with condensation. It's said they tap into subterranean pockets of geothermal heat, adding to the stifling atmosphere.

Structures rise from this spongy foundation: haphazard agglomerations of corrugated iron, scavenged pipes, and riveted steel plates. These are the "leech farms", where desperate souls extract rare-earth elements from the toxic runoff – slowly poisoning themselves in the process. Their ramshackle constructions are interconnected by precarious walkways of scrap metal, groaning and flexing under barely-visible stress; little bridges spanning pools of bubbling, corrosive liquid.

The loudest sound is the relentless throb of nearby pumps and the hiss of steam escaping from cracked pipes. Deeper sounds vibrate through the metal: the low-frequency hum of the Processing Ponds’ machinery, the crack and groan of settling slag, and the echoing clang of maintenance crews as they endlessly repair the failing infrastructure. There's also a strange, arrhythmic clicking, like the mandibles of some enormous unseen insect, coming from the direction of the Ponds.

The population seems sparse, but that's deceptive. Most of the activity happens within the buildings, or buried beneath the metal. Movement is cautious and deliberate. Everyone carries an array of tools and specialized equipment: filtration masks, hand-cranked generators, makeshift sensors that beep and flicker erratically. They move with a grim determination, their faces gaunt and shadowed. They appear to be constantly taking readings, fiddling with valves, rerouting pipes with a desperate need for efficient throughput of the Ponds' effluent through their crude separation refineries.

"Active processes" is an apt description — not just of the industrial activities, but of the landscape itself. The ground shifts as the Blood Reeds inflate and deflate with cycles. The air undergoes sudden temperature spikes as geothermal vents shift and release pockets of superheated gas. The chemical composition of the Ponds themselves varies wildly as new toxins are dumped, and old ones react with the algae. The very ground underfoot is unstable, prone to sudden collapses and sinkholes filled with boiling, iridescent sludge.

The strangest thing is the silence. It is a loud silence, filled with mechanical echoes and the constant roar of the Ponds, and yet there is an absence of all animal life. No birds sing, no insects buzz, no rodents scurry. Only the hum of the machines, the whisper of the Blood Reeds, and the desperate, coughing breaths of the leech farmers fill the air. The silence is a constant, unnerving reminder of the toxicity that permeates every molecule of this place, a silence that screams of something profoundly wrong.
