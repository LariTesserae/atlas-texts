The air hangs thick and heavy, almost a broth, despite the heat simmering off the corrugated iron roofs. It smells of ozone and brine, tinged with something acrid that makes the back of your throat itch. This is the Shallows, the lowest ward of the combustion city, built quite literally on the bones of the old salt marshes.

Everywhere you look, rivulets of brackish water trickle. They run down the rust-streaked walls of the factories, pool in the pot-holed, asphalt streets, and gather in oily slicks at the base of warped metal shacks. The ground isn't ground, not really, but a compressed layer of ash, slag, and pulverized shells. It squelches ominously underfoot.

Vegetation is almost nonexistent, a few hardy, chlorine-bleached weeds clinging stubbornly to cracks in the factories, their leaves dusty and brittle. Above, a permanent haze of coal smoke and chemical effluence obscures the sky, filtering the sunlight into a perpetual twilight. The sun itself is a smudged, orange memory, barely strong enough to cast a shadow.

Despite the grim conditions, the Shallows teem with life. Gutter-snipes with oil-stained feathers scrabble for scraps amongst the refuse. Rats, bloated and fearless, dart through the shadows, their eyes gleaming in the dim light. Strange, bioluminescent fungi sprout from damp corners, casting an eerie glow. Further away, past the first line of factories, you can catch glimpses of the marsh's denizens: spindly-legged herons wading through murky pools, clickbeetles clinging to the metal, and schools of small, silver fish darting through the flooded streets.

The city itself is a sprawling mess of decaying industry. Factories, many long abandoned, stand like hollow-eyed giants, their windows boarded up, their machinery rusting into grotesque sculptures. The air vibrates with the clang and roar of the working mills, a cacophony of pounding pistons, grinding gears, and hissing steam. Sirens wail intermittently, a mechanical lament that pierces the humid air. Here and there, makeshift dwellings cling to the factories like parasitic organisms – precariously balanced shacks cobbled together from salvaged metal and driftwood.

The architecture is a jumbled collage of styles and eras, a testament to the city's chaotic growth. Crumbling brick facades stand beside gleaming chrome towers, each bearing the scars of time and neglect. Wires crisscross overhead, a tangled web of electricity that pulses with a dangerous energy.

This place feels old, not necessarily in a geological sense, but in a way that suggests nothing new ever really happens here. The Shallows have reached a kind of stagnant equilibrium, a steady unchanging rhythm of industrial output and decay. No weather seems to matter, the city absorbing changes and turning their energies into further rot. The tides come and go, leaving behind only a higher watermark on the walls. Each sunrise is indistinguishable from the last. The relentless churn of the machines feels like the grinding of the world's molars, inexorably reducing everything to grit and bone.

One wonders what the point is. But there are people here, going about their lives. The children of the Shallows, no different than children anywhere, scamper among the factories and canals, diving for scrap or playing games with stones. The older residents move with an equally strange familiarity, their heads down, immune– as if none of this matters and it's always been this way.
