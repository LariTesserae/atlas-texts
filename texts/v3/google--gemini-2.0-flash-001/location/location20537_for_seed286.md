The world is a single, planet-spanning mangrove. Its roots are sunk in cold, abyssal water, its canopy interwoven above the clouds. 

The water is a perpetual twilight zone. Far below, thermal vents hiss and pop, supporting bizarre chemosynthetic life, bioluminescent anglerfish cousins with dragging, cable-like feelers. Closer to the roots, filter feeders cluster, sifting out detritus raining down from above. It’s a slow, icy rain, a constant snowfall of decaying leaves and the corpses of creatures too slow or too weak to climb back up. Currents are sluggish and predictable, moving in enormous, slow loops around the planet.

The mangrove roots are colossal — each one the size of a mountain range — and are themselves a complete ecosystem. Encrusting every surface are layers of calcareous algae, tube worms, and barnacles. Tunnels bore into the roots, carved by generations of industrious, beetle-like creatures. These tunnels extend for miles, creating entire underground cities, pulsating with the light of fungal gardens. 

As one ascends the roots, the temperature slowly rises. Giant snails with armored shells graze on the algal mats. Schools of bioluminescent fish, adapted to the dim light, dart through the tangled masses of rootlets. The constant, rhythmic whooshing sound here is the respiration of the mangrove itself, drawing water and nutrients up from the depths.

Higher still, the roots begin to branch and mingle, forming enormous platforms and bridges. Here, the true scale of the mangrove becomes apparent. The light grows stronger. The water is far below. The air is thick with the scent of decaying vegetation and the metallic tang of industry. Perched upon these platforms are the great engine-trees: colossal, hollow trunks that house the workings of the mangrove's pneumatic system. 

For this is no ordinary living thing. The mangrove is, in truth, a vast, planet-spanning machine.

Generations ago, a forgotten civilization engineered the mangrove to purify the poisoned atmosphere and stabilize the planet's geology. They built into it a network of pumps, filters, and valves, all powered by the plant's own growth. These engine-trees are its heart, driving the circulation of water and nutrients, regulating the planet's temperature, and maintaining the atmospheric balance.

The industry is subtle. The engine-trees are woven into the mangrove's natural structure, their metallic components camouflaged by layers of bark and leaves. It's a quiet, efficient, and utterly relentless process. Steam hisses from vents hidden amongst the foliage, and the rhythmic thumping of colossal pistons resonates through the wood.

The canopy is a blinding, green world of endless leaves and branches. Here, the light is intense. The air is hot and humid. The sound becomes a deafening roar, a cacophony of rustling leaves, screeching insects, and the distant thunder of the engine-trees.

The dominant fauna of the canopy are colossal insects. Giant mantises stalk unsuspecting prey. Butterflies with wings the size of sails flutter through the air. Enormous, spider-like creatures spin webs of incredible strength, trapping unsuspecting creatures. 

The weirdest denizens are the arboreal whales. These massive, filter-feeding creatures float amongst the branches, their vast bodies supported by specialized air bladders. They feed on pollen and spores, their calls echoing through the canopy like mournful foghorns.

Everything here is adapted to the mangrove. Nothing changes. Seasons are nonexistent. The mangrove thrives, indifferent to the passage of time. It is a planet-sized clock, ticking in perfect synchronicity, its gears grinding with a low, constant rumble. It fulfills its ancient programming, a perfect, unchanging, and alien machine.
