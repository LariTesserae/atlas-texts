The Sky-Skerry. That's what the cloud-herders call it. Not that they visit often. Too steep for grazers; too cold for comfort, even in the heart of summer.

Imagine a single, monstrous tooth, jutting from the perpetually bruised gums of the lower sky. That's the Skerry. At its base, where the cold stone bleeds into the swirling mist, is a shallow tarn – more of a persistent puddle, really. It's fed by countless trickles that weep from the porous rock face. The water is the color of iron and tastes faintly of copper.

The air is thick, perpetually damp. A fine, cold spray dances in the wind, coating everything in a glistening sheen. Visibility rarely extends beyond a few dozen paces. The Skerrry *roars*. Not with a single, unified voice, but with a chaotic chorus. The wind howls around the jagged peaks and through the narrow ravines, a constant, bone-chilling lament. Waterfalls, some no thicker than a strand of hair, plunge down the cliffs, adding their high-pitched screams to the symphony. And then there are the Kryl – the dominant fauna here.

The Kryl are...strange. Picture a cross between a mountain goat and a lichen colony. Their bodies are covered in a thick, shaggy coat of what looks like fur, but is actually a dense mat of symbiotic fungi. These fungi are bioluminescent, casting an eerie, pulsating glow in the perpetual twilight. The Kryl are constantly on the move, scraping the rock face with their hardened hooves, feeding on mineral deposits and the sparse, sturdy grasses that cling to life in the crevices. Their bleating echoes across the Skerry, a high-pitched, almost metallic rasp that cuts through the wind and water.

Vegetation is tenacious, but abundant in its own right. Cushions of emerald moss cling to the dampest surfaces. Twisted, stunted trees – more like shrubs, really – claw at the rock with gnarled roots. They bear strange, bulbous fruit that bursts with a sour, intoxicating juice. Patches of vibrantly colored fungi erupt from the decaying wood, adding splashes of unnatural hues to the monochrome landscape.

High on the Skerry, where the wind is a screaming gale, are the ruins. Not grand palaces or imposing fortresses, but simple, circular stone shelters, their roofs long collapsed. They are built with the same porous stone as the rest of the Skerry itself. Scraps of woven grass matting lie scattered amongst the rubble, along with crude stone tools and the worn, bone needles used to stitch together hides. The cloud-herders say these were built by the "Sky-Singers" – a tribe said to have communicated with the weather itself. They vanished long ago, driven away by a plague, or a snowstorm, or perhaps simply the unbearable loneliness.

The most prominent feature of the ruins is a single, upright stone, taller than any of the shelters. It's worn smooth by wind and water. Its surface is covered in shallow carvings. Some look like stylized renderings of the Kryl; others seem to depict swirling cloud patterns. A shallow depression at the top of the stone always holds a puddle of rainwater. The water quivers even on still days, in a way that feels... significant.

The Sky-Skerry is a place of relentless activity. Rain falls constantly, eroding the already-weathered stone. The Kryl graze and migrate, their droppings fertilizing the meager soil. Predatory birds – massive, raven-like creatures with metallic blue plumage – circle overhead, their sharp cries adding to the din, always searching for a stray Kryl lamb. The bioluminescent fungi pulse with a slow, mesmerizing rhythm. The wind howls. The waterfalls scream.

This isn't a static place. It is forever changing, forever eroding, forever alive. A small, isolated world, clinging to existence in the face of overwhelming forces. A lonely monument to the forces of nature. And perhaps, just perhaps, when the wind is right, and the light is dim, you can still hear the faint echoes of the Sky-Singers, calling to the clouds.
