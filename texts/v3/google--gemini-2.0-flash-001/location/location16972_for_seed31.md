The city of Aethel rests uneasily high in the warm, wet mountains. The stone here is young and unforgiving, great slabs and crags thrusting skyward where the mountain-making forces have only recently quieted. Erosion has barely begun its work; the wind and rain still bite keenly at the exposed rock. Yet, water is everywhere. It doesn't flow so much as weep from the stone, condensing from a thick, almost cloying air. Mist hangs perpetually in the valleys below, a pearlescent sea threatening to swallow all.

Given the harsh terrain, Aethel shouldn't exist. What little soil there is clings tenaciously to the steepest slopes, a dark, fertile mud choked with rampant, almost desperate vegetation. Mosses and ferns erupt in vibrant green explosions from every crack and crevice. Strange, flowering vines, heavy with swollen, nectar-filled blossoms, twine around everything they can reach. It’s as though the mountain, inhospitable as it seems, is determined to soften itself with life. These plants drink up the constant moisture, creating a humid greenhouse under the open sky.

Aethel sprawls in this precarious environment. Buildings are not so much built as grown from the rock itself. The raw, jagged stone forms the foundations and lower walls, onto which the people of Aethel have grafted homes and workshops of woven branches, daubed with mud and roofed with broad leaves. It gives the settlement a chaotic, almost organic quality, as though it might have sprouted from the mountainside as naturally as the ferns.

The "streets", if they can be called that, are more like stairways carved directly into the living rock. Water trickles down these steps, creating slick, green pathways that wind between dwellings. Small irrigation channels, laboriously dug and maintained, divert the constant flow to small terraced gardens where bright vegetables struggle for survival.

There are no animals. The only sounds are the drip, drip, drip of water and the whispering of the wind through the dense, overflowing leaves. The air hangs heavy, thick with the cloying scent of blossoms and the earthy smell of damp stone and mud. The pervasive moisture soaks into everything. Clothes feel perpetually damp; tools rust quickly; mold grows in dark corners.

Primitive tools and simple craft are the way of life. The people of Aethel work the stone with hand-forged tools, shaping and fitting the rock into place with generations of inherited knowledge. They weave intricate baskets and mats from the tough plant fibers, and their clothes are constructed from layers of treated leaves and woven vines. There is little metal – it is a precious commodity, carefully hoarded and repurposed.

And perhaps strangest of all, nothing changes in Aethel. The rhythm of life is unchanging. The seasons bleed into one another. The rain falls. The plants grow. The people toil. The mountains remain. The faces of the elders are just like the faces of the young, and the tools used by children are the tools used by the workers. There are no grand projects, no new ideas – only the endless repetition of tasks, the careful maintenance of a precarious balance.

Days are mostly quiet. The urban center of Aethel is more like a scattering of tiny settlements spread out for miles, connected only by waterways and well-worn footpaths. Every now and then you see a cloaked figure in the distance tending to a garden, or hauling clay, or repairing some feature of the town. But very rarely does the silence break.

Aethel exists in a kind of frozen equilibrium, a testament to the tenacity of life, and perhaps, to its strange acceptance of stillness. It is a place where the world may be very different, but where the will of the spirits of nature have decided that the future is the same as the present.
