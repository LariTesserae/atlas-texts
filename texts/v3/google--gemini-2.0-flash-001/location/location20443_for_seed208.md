The air hangs thick and cold, a soup of moisture that beads on every surface. Visibility is poor; the dim, grey light filters weakly through the endless fog, rarely reaching more than a few dozen feet before dissolving into the gloom. What *can* be seen is a landscape of gently rolling hills, almost imperceptible in their undulation, but undeniably present.

Erosion is the defining characteristic here. Not the sharp, violent erosion of rushing water or shattering ice, but the slow, patient erosion of time. The hills are rounded, smoothed, their peaks worn down, valleys filled in, until the whole place seems to sigh with the weight of ages. Yet, there is also activity. The fog itself shifts and swirls, driven by unseen currents. Small rivulets, more like persistent wetness than flowing water, carve their way across the slopes, carrying sediment and dissolved minerals in their sluggish course.

Vegetation is nearly absent. Instead, the land wears a skin of black, glassy rock, a kind of volcanic obsidian that has weathered beyond recognition. This brittle mineral is everywhere, shattered into shards and gravel, forming the spine of the hills. But on this obsidian rock, there is life. It's not plant life, not in any sense that I recognize. Instead, there's a pervasive slime, a glistening biofilm that coats much of the stone. It's black, like the rock, but polished and shining with reflected light, creating an illusion of depth beneath the surface. In places, it forms convoluted patterns, like organic circuitry etched onto the world.

Scattered across this landscape are structures, or rather, the remnants of structures. These are made from the same obsidian-like material, crudely shaped into walls and shelters. Most of them are collapsed, reduced to piles of rubble, half-swallowed by the creeping slime. But a few still stand, their roofs caved in, their walls breached, offering a glimpse into the lives of those who once inhabited this place. I find simple tools near the collapsed houses. They’re stone tools and shards of obsidian, and they glint under the heavy fog.

And yet this barren place teems with life. The sound alone is overwhelming. A chorus of croaks, clicks, whistles, and rasping noises that reverberates through the fog. The source? Amphibians. Countless amphibians in every imaginable form, size, and coloration. They slither across the slime-covered rocks, swim in the shallow rivulets, and cling to the underside of the collapsed structures. They are the lords of this domain, the inheritors of this ancient land. Some are tiny, no larger than my thumb, with iridescent skin that shifts with every movement. Others are massive, their bodies a patchwork of armored plates and fleshy wattles.

The scale doesn't feel overwhelming. It's an open landscape, yes, but the fog confines the eyes. The absence of landmarks, of anything to provide a sense of scale, collapses distance. It might be vast, encompassing an entire valley, but it feels close, intimate, as if I'm standing at the center of a world contained within a bubble of mist.

The unusualness of this place is heightened by the contrast between the geological timescale and the biological vitality. The land speaks of unimaginable age, of a world worn down to its bare bones. Yet, the teeming amphibian life screams of constant activity, of a vibrant ecosystem in perpetual motion. It's a world of slow, inexorable decay and frenetic, almost desperate life, locked in a strange and unsettling dance.
