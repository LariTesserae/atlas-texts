The High Vats. That’s what the maintenance logs call them. Colloquially, some of the line-workers call them the Hanging Gardens of Hydroponics, but that’s a joke, mostly. There are no gardens.

Imagine, if you can, a cube roughly the size of a town square—maybe two acres. This cube is completely filled with water, a near-opaque green shot through with a latticework of metal struts, pipes, and ceramic… well, not quite *bricks*. More like fluted columns, some intricately spiraled, others deeply pocked and seemingly organic, like petrified coral. These columns are the literal *cells* of the structure. They form a packed, interlocking matrix. The water itself, warm and viscous, flows *through* these structures, driven by hidden pumps.

The whole thing hangs suspended—somehow—high above a featureless plain of dull grey metal. You can’t see the plain directly; the air is thick with clinging moisture, close to saturation, and the cube itself casts a heavy shadow. The lighting is industrial but diffuse, as if coming from within the water itself. It's neither day nor night, just a perpetual, even illumination within the green depths.

The struts and pipes are undeniably industrial: rusted iron, bolted together with enormous rivets, encrusted with minerals precipitated from the water. But the ceramic columns… those are something else. They pulse faintly with a bioluminescence muted by scale. If you put your hand on one (through a specialized glovesleeve, of course—the water itself is toxic), you can feel a faint vibration, a low hum that resonates deep in your bones.

No animals live here. Not a single one. The water is too caustic, the environment too demanding, or perhaps something else is at play. There’s a kind of… vigilance to the silence. This isn’t a natural lack of life. It’s a suppressed absence. No birdsong, no rustling of leaves (there are no leaves), no buzzing of insects. Just the distant, persistent hum of the pumps and the deeper thrum of the ceramic structures... and the drip... drip... drip... of condensation falling from the cube's underbelly into the abyss below.

The water is *dense* with vegetation—but not vegetation as you know it. There are no leaves, no roots in the traditional sense. Instead, the structure of the water is permeated with tendrils and colonies of luminescent algae, glowing kelp-like structures that drift with the currents, and vast blooms of floating, fleshy… things. Some are bladder-like and translucent, pulsing gently. Others are stringy and tangled, like underwater tumbleweeds made of living light. And still others are shaped like complex geometries, perfect tetrahedrons or dodecahedrons of shimmering green.

The water is always shifting, slowly, almost imperceptibly. The currents are weak, the flux minimal. It takes days, weeks, sometimes months to notice a change in the overall pattern of the algae blooms. The ceramic columns grow imperceptibly, accreting minerals and organic matter from the water. They whisper to each other, the line-workers joke. They are building whatever wants to be built, in its own time. "The place where the building builds itself," someone wrote, in the maintenance logs.

The purpose of the High Vats is unknown. Theories abound. Some say they are a water filtration system, purifying vast quantities of contaminated water from the plains below. Others believe they are a food production facility, growing algae for protein synthesis. Still others whisper about something far stranger—a bio-reactor, a self-replicating ecosystem, a living computer grown from ceramic and algae. Whatever its purpose, it has been running for centuries, perhaps millennia. The High Vats exist in slow time.

The "total" built environment is a puzzle. The lack of access points or visible maintenance hatches makes it almost impossible to describe how to it's possible to get inside, or to maintain and observe the systems within from the narrow metal walkways, suspended by more struts, that skirt the structure. It must use a total system of self-repair, the rare replacements or fixes performed by automated drones.

And the weirdness… that’s the hardest thing to describe. It's not just the strangeness of the environment itself, or the unnerving silence. It’s the feeling that you are not seeing *everything*. That there are patterns within patterns, geometries within geometries, purposes within purposes that you cannot even begin to comprehend. The High Vats are a puzzle box made of water, ceramic, and light. And nobody has yet figured out how to open it.
