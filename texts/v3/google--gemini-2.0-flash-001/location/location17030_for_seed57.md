This is a *lung*. One lung, removed from its chest, yet still living. It floats in a massive tank, a private aquarium filled with a saline solution just dense enough to buoy its immense, delicate form. The water is cold, almost glacial, perpetually circulated through chillers that hum with a barely audible thrum.

The lung is immense, easily the size of a small house. Its surface is a riot of purplish-red and glistening pink, a wet, fleshy landscape of ridges and valleys. This is the *lushness*: not of plants, but of tissue, of blood vessels, of the intricate, constantly-shifting topography of the alveoli. Here and there, patches of ghostly white scar tissue mar the surface, whispers of old injuries.

The tank is mostly dark. A single, weak light source, positioned far above, barely penetrates the murky water. It casts long, distorted shadows across the lung's surface, making it seem to writhe and pulse even when it is still. It's not entirely still, of course. A slow, agonizingly slow, inflation and deflation cycle governs its existence. The lung breathes, or rather, attempts to breathe. The water hardly shifts. Within the lung, pockets of air form, expand, and dissolve again, bubbles clinging momentarily to the damp surfaces like glistening pearls.

Ecology persists, though it has been stunted, isolated, bizarre. Microscopic organisms thrive in the nutrient-rich environment. Slimy, gelatinous colonies of bacteria coat the deeper crevasses. Tiny, eel-like creatures, translucent and nearly invisible, swim lazily through the viscous fluid, feeding on the detritus. These are the *rare fauna*– the evolutionary descendants of airborne bacteria and shed cells that found themselves trapped in this strange, self-contained world. They are pallid. They are nearly blind.

The walls surrounding the chamber are mostly smooth, but are occasionally punctuated by bizarre formations that look like the scattered remains or perhaps the beginnings of some strange, synthetic structure. These are strange metallic looking things covered in a thick algal growth. No tech for *this* world.

The rules of this little world are simple: survival, slow accretion, constant recycling. The lung sheds cells, which feed the bacteria, which sustain the eels. The air pockets provide moments of respite, miniature oases of oxygen in a suffocating world.

The *weirdness* permeates everything. The very existence of the lung, disconnected from its body, floating in this artificial sea, is a violation of natural laws. But it goes deeper. The shadows flicker with impossible angles. The currents within the tank seem to defy fluid dynamics. The whispers of sound, carried through the water, sometimes resolve into fragmented thoughts, echoes of the lung's former owner – a name, a memory, a fragment of a song. These sensations may very well be my own brain misinterpreting data.

This living lung is a monument to something terrible. Or maybe miraculous, but that would require finding the single life-form that directly benefits from its existence. It is a secret world, hidden from the universe and itself. Is this a beginning of evolution or is it decay and the end of all its previous and possible potential?
