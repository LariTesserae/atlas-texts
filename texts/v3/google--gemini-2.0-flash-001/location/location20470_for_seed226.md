Okay, let's see. A cool, wet, lush, and high place. Newly forming, but already settled. Small in scale but urban in nature, though urban at a primitive tech level. Empty but loud. Rare fauna. Unusual somehow, but in a slow-moving way with normal light. Interesting…

The Skytowers of Xyl stand upon the Weeping Plateau – a highland shelf of porous, newly-heaved stone that bleeds meltwater year round. Even in the short summers, when the thin, cold sunlight manages to burn through the clinging mists, rivulets carve their way down the raw, pale rock faces, feeding the riotous carpets of moss and strange, pale fungi that cling to every surface.

“Towers” is, perhaps, a generous term. They are more like stacks of huts, or cliff-side warrens built upwards rather than into the stone. Xyl’s people, the Whisperfolk, construct them slowly, patiently, one mud-brick and woven-thatch section at a time. They use a simple post-and-lintel system, levering enormous flat stones into place with wooden cranes and ramps that are dismantled and rebuilt higher as the construction progresses. The oldest towers are perhaps twenty stories high, the newest barely reaching above the tallest of the wind-stunted trees on the plateau.

The emptiness is deceptive. Though one can often see for great distances across the fog-choked valleys – the plateau is mercifully free of obstructions – Xyl feels strangely claustrophobic. This is partly because the sounds are amplified in the near-constant mist. The drip of countless tiny waterfalls, the rasping calls of the Stoneclutch birds that nest in the tower crevices, the perpetual *thrum* of the wind through the high passes, all combine into a deafening cacophony. But it is also because of the Whisperfolk themselves, or rather, what they have become.

They were once, according to fragmented oral histories, nomadic herders following the giant, shaggy sky-cattle that roamed these highlands. But as the plateau rose – a geological hiccup rather than a true, slow upwelling – the cattle migrated away, unable to navigate the steeper, slicker terrain. The Whisperfolk, trapped, adapted. They learned to quarry and build. They learned to cultivate the strange, phosphorescent fungi for food and light.

But something else happened too. Exposure to the dripping stone – rich in rare, heavy minerals – subtly altered them. Their bones became denser, their skin paler, almost translucent. They began to flicker.

It's hard to describe. The effect is most noticeable in the low light; a shimmering around the edges of their bodies, a momentary displacement, as if they are being selectively erased and redrawn by a faulty hand. Some say they are slowly becoming one with the stone itself, their bodies dissolving into the mineral matrix of the plateau. Others claim it’s a consequence of their strange diet, the glowing fungi somehow disrupting their cellular structure. Whatever the cause, the result is unsettling. They appear to be fading, fragmenting, constantly on the verge of disappearing.

The rare fauna consists mostly of insects adapted to the mineral-rich environment, and the aforementioned Stoneclutch birds – large, flightless avians with powerful beaks used to chisel nesting cavities into the sheer rock faces. The birds are both revered and hunted; their eggs are a vital source of protein. Their bones are ground and added to the mud-bricks, giving them a faint, ethereal glow in the dark.

The laws of Xyl are simple: *Silence the Well, Mend the Stone*. It is forbidden to intentionally block or divert the myriad streams that trickle across the plateau. To do so is to invite drought, famine, and the wrath of the Stone itself. *Mending the Stone* refers to the constant, painstaking process of repairing cracks and fissures in the towers. The Whisperfolk believe the towers are a single, living entity, symbiotic with their own existence. When the towers crumble, so too will their fragile hold on this unforgiving landscape.

Their technology is basic, limited to stone tools, woven baskets, and simple carpentry. But their understanding of the peculiar properties of their environment is profound. They know which fungi are edible, which are poisonous, and which can be used to dye their clothes. They understand the subtle shifts in the wind that presage a coming storm. They can navigate the fog by the feel of the moisture on their skin.

The "weirdness" is the slow, patient process of their transformation, and their deep, almost instinctive connection to the land. It lies in their shimmering forms and the echoing drip of water. It lies in the silent reverence they pay to the Stone, and the unending, futile task of mending what is already dissolving. It lies in the unsettling juxtaposition of primitive industry and an ethereal, fading future.
