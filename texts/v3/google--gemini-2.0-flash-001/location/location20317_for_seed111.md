The air hangs thick and cold, clinging to the rough, newly-split surfaces of the rock. It’s a dampness that chills you to the core, a constant film that coats everything – not quite rain, but a perpetual exudation from the stone itself. The elevation is breathtaking, not in a soaring-vista way, but in the tightness of the space, the oppressive weight of the mountains looming close on all sides.

This isn’t a valley, not exactly. It’s more like a raw wound in the earth, a gash ripped open and still bleeding. The granite is raw, pink and grey, fractured and edged into knife points. Scree slopes plunge down at terrifying angles, unstable underfoot. The freshness is palpable; you can almost hear the echoes of the cataclysm that birthed this place, the rending and grinding of unimaginable forces. And yet, the pace of change is slow, glacial. The sun, rarely seen through the perpetual twilight, creeps across the rock faces, each day indistinguishable from the last.

Vegetation is a brutal afterthought. Patches of lichen cling to the rock, desperate for purchase in the mineral-rich wetness. Here and there, clustered in the most shielded crevices, are clumps of coarse, dark moss, almost black with moisture. A few skeletal shrubs, twisted and stunted by the elevation and the cold, claw at the stony ground. They seem more mineral than vegetable, extensions of the granite itself.

This close in, the scale is all wrong. One could walk from one side of the space to the other in an hour. And yet, it feels vast, infinite. The sheer number of things occupying every crack and crevice gives it that feeling. The area is dense with unseen presences.

These mountains are screaming, always. And it's not a quiet scream. It is so intensely loud that it's hard to believe, at first, one is actually hearing anything. The source of the sounds are myriad; the rasp of scree, never silent, constantly readjusting itself in minute shuffles; the rush of water, surprisingly abundant, trickling and gushing through hidden channels in the rock; the cries of birds, so strange and piercing as to be almost metallic. But these are but hints of the true noise, the real noise which is produced by the fauna, and seemingly the granite and rock, groaning and resonating.

The fauna. Ah, the fauna. This place belongs to them. The granite is literally _alive_. Every surface teems. Not with teeming maggots or swarming insects; not exactly. But the surfaces themselves are the life. The granite breathes with strange lifeforms one might call lichen, perhaps, but are in fact far more mobile, far more intentional. They are capable of forming structures with themselves, of communicating and rearranging. They cover the surface of the stone, sometimes with such density that they resemble a moving carpet of color and activity.

Then the birds. Such impossible, angular shapes they have. Black, mostly, with flashes of iridescent color. They are harsh, unmusical. They swoop and whirl in the dim light, their calls the sharp edges of razors. They seem drawn to the strange organisms, devouring them bit-by-bit. This gives the surface of the stone the appearance of living, pulsating as patches are devoured and consumed.

It's clear that someone has carved the odd mark, the unique engraving, on the walls of this place. What appears to be a stone tool, or a primitive metal tool, has been used to create primitive art. It marks territories, or maybe rituals. It adds to the cacophony, as now the land is shouting with sound as well as color.

This is a place of primal energy. A place where life is relentless, brutal, and fiercely strange. A place that whispers of secrets best left undisturbed, of ancient forces that slumber just beneath the surface, waiting for the right moment to be unleashed. The longer you remain, the harder it becomes to distinguish the living from the non-living, the sane from the mad, the waking from the dreaming. The whispers of the mountain start to seep into your soul.
