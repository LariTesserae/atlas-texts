The air is thick and warm, heavy with the scent of wet metal and the insistent, pervasive aroma of some flowering vine that chokes the lower slopes. Water trickles down every surface – not a downpour, but a constant weeping, like the mountain itself is sweating. Here and there, a thicker stream gushes forth from a crack in the rock, plunging into a tangle of emerald-green ferns and moss that clings to the cliff face. 

We’re high up, impossibly high. The valley floor, if there even *is* one, is lost in swirling mist far below. What we can see is a relatively small plateau, perhaps a few hundred meters across, perched almost impossibly on the edge of a colossal peak. The rock is dark, basaltic, sharp-edged and relatively unweathered, giving the impression that the whole thing was only recently heaved up from the depths. Despite the steepness, vegetation has taken hold wherever it can, creating a riot of green against the black stone.

The ferns are remarkable. Some are delicate and lacy, almost translucent, while others are broad and leathery, their fronds glistening with captured moisture. The flowering vine, the source of that overwhelming scent, is everywhere, its thick tendrils wrapping around everything, its vibrant magenta blooms adding splashes of color to the green monotony. It hums with some unseen energy.

What's most striking, though, is the machinery. Dotted across the plateau are the remnants of old industrial works. They're not sprawling factories or anything, more like a collection of specialized devices, each seemingly devoted to a particular task. A towering steel frame supports a massive water wheel, slowly and deliberately churning as it is driven by a waterfall emerging from a fissure in the cliff above. Cables, thick as a person's torso, snake across the ground, disappearing into half-collapsed stone structures that may have once housed generators or control rooms. The air vibrates with the low, rhythmic thrum of hidden engines.

The sound is remarkably loud. Not just the rushing of water, but the constant groaning and grinding of machines. A deep, resonating hum permeates everything, punctuated by the occasional clang of metal on metal. You get the distinct impression that many of these machines are still running, even if their purpose has long been forgotten.

There are other sounds too. A strange, high-pitched whine that seems to emanate from deep within the rocks, and the rustling of unseen things moving through the dense vegetation. Despite the abundance of plant life, animal life is surprisingly scarce. I spot a few large, iridescent insects flitting among the flowers, and once I think I see a flash of movement – something sleek and dark – disappearing into a shadow. A dark, oily fluid also appears in small cracks in the rocks, and flows into some of the machinery. 

Even though the machines are active, the whole scene feels steeped in a slow kind of time. The erosion on the stones is minimal, the vegetation spreads with a steady perseverance, the machines churn in a slow, patient rhythm. The cycles are long, the changes subtle. This place feels like it's been operating this way for centuries, maybe millennia, slowly, inexorably transforming the landscape.

Amidst the ferns sits the scattered debris of occupation — corroded metal tanks, broken glass vials, and the occasional ceramic, brick-like block assembled into a small wall. These walls don't appear protective, and are only a few courses high. These blocks appear to be the primary construction material, being of a neutral color and of the length of an arm. One wall section sits next to a small outcropping of rock, near a dense patch of the flowering vine.

The place is weird, but only subtly so. There's nothing overtly fantastical or magical about it. It's more the feeling that things aren't quite right, that something is just slightly off-kilter. The way the vines seem to pulsate with a faint, internal light. The way the water flows uphill in certain places. The way the machines seem to operate without any discernible input or control.

Whatever this place was, whatever its purpose, it’s clear that it's been abandoned for a long time. But it hasn't stopped working. It continues to grind and churn, to weep and hum. A forgotten monument to some forgotten industry, slowly being swallowed by the relentless embrace of the mountain.
