The air hangs thick and heavy, each breath a conscious act of pulling humidity into parched lungs. It’s a warm, cloying warmth, like a damp cloth pressed against the face. The light is filtered, muted by the sheer density of the vegetation, a perpetual twilight clinging to the ground even when the sun, a hazy disc, manages to pierce the canopy.

Beneath your feet, the earth is a sponge, a yielding mat of mosses and decaying matter. Water is everywhere. Not in raging rivers or placid lakes, but as a constant presence. It glistens on broad, waxy leaves, beads on the rough bark of the colossal, fungal-encrusted trees, and pools in every depression. Trickles and rivulets weave unseen paths through the undergrowth, their presence betrayed by the rustling of water-loving plants.

This is a city built of plants, for plants. Not in the sense of manicured gardens or pruned hedges, but a sprawling, chaotic, organic metropolis where the very architecture is alive. The trees themselves are the skyscrapers, their trunks impossibly wide, their bark a tapestry of clinging vines, epiphytes, and iridescent fungi. Bridges of woven roots arch between them, forming pathways for… well, for something. Tracks worn into the thick layers of moss suggest the passage of heavy things, but the rare fauna of this place are mostly hidden, shy creatures that disappear at the slightest disturbance.

The buildings, such as they are, are carved from living wood or woven from giant leaves sealed with sap. They are not perfectly square, nor perfectly round, but bulge and sag in natural, organic ways. Some are clearly dwellings, others perhaps workshops, with openings that vent steam and strange, sweet smells. The whole city seems to breathe, to grow, to shift imperceptibly with the slow, relentless cycle of life and decay.

The industrial element is woven into this natural framework. Instead of cold steel and concrete, the factories are living structures. Water-powered mills, their wheels fashioned from petrified wood and vines, groan and grind, processing the raw materials of the forest. Great bellows, made of stretched hides and powered by clockwork mechanisms crafted from bone and wood, sigh with a rhythmic whoosh, feeding air into furnaces fuelled by phosphorescent fungi. Copper wires, crudely insulated with tree resin, snake haphazardly through the undergrowth, bringing power, or something like it, to the various workshops.

The sound is overwhelming. A cacophony of dripping water, rustling leaves, buzzing insects, the rhythmic thud of water wheels, the hissing of steam. It is a loud, constant hum that vibrates in your chest, a low frequency drone that seems to resonate with the very bones. It is a sound that fills the senses and leaves no room for silence.

Evidence of civilization is intertwined with the jungle. One can see the clear signs of organized construction, but the lines are blurred, softened by the sheer force of the vegetation. The buildings are in a state of perpetual flux, constantly being consumed by the plants, only to be rebuilt and reclaimed. The whole city is a slow, patient dance of creation and destruction, a testament to the power of nature and the resilience of those who have learned to live within its embrace. The erosion and weathering are ancient and established, a testament to the age of the place. Everything feels settled, in its own state, in balance, however strange that balance might seem.

The edges of the city are hard to define, as it gradually merges into the untamed wilderness. Beyond the last dwelling, the trees stand even closer together, the undergrowth becomes even more impenetrable, and the sounds of the city are swallowed by the rustling of the forest. There, in the deep, shadowed heart of the wilderness, one suspects, the true secrets of this place are hidden. But stepping into that wilderness feels like a transgression, a violation of a carefully maintained equilibrium. The city, for all its strangeness, feels like the safer place.
