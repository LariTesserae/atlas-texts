The air thins, prickling the skin, as the cog-road grinds upward. It clings to the mountainside, a greasy iron serpent scaling a petrified green god. Before it, tangled, bright. Behind, the valley falls away – a patchwork of cultivated fields, now distant enough to seem planned and orderly. Here, though, all order breaks down in a riot of chlorophyll.

The track is relatively recent; a few decades at most. It cuts through a landscape far older, where the only building is becoming. The rock itself is barely rock at all. It's more like petrified moss, a gigantic sponge of compressed vegetation, striated with quartz veins and laced with underground springs. The mountain is rising, not through volcanic upheaval or tectonic shift, but through the inexorable accumulation of plant matter, layer upon layer, century upon century.

Water is *everywhere.* It doesn’t so much *fall* as *weep* from the stone. Every surface glistens. Mist curls perpetually around the towering stalks and fronds which form the bulk of the vegetation. The temperature is cool, even bordering on cold; the thin air holds little heat, and the perpetual moisture chills to the bone. The entire place feels like walking through the inside of a gigantic, damp lung.

The plants here are… unusual. They resemble earthly forms, but distorted, magnified, and combined in impossible ways. Towering, fern-like trees with trunks of braided vines reach for the sky, their fronds forming a dense, sun-drenched canopy far above. Closer to the ground, thick mats of luminous moss cover everything, punctuated by clusters of bulbous, pitcher-like plants that drip with digestive enzymes. The ground is never solid; it gives way beneath your feet with a soft squelch, a slurry supporting this dense jungle.

Then there's the *noise.* The constant drip of water is overlaid with the drone of archaic machines. Further up the track, clinging precariously to the mountainside like mechanical ticks, are the condensers. Towering steel structures, they’re powered by wood-burning steam engines, and their purpose is to wring moisture from the already saturated air. They work tirelessly, and against nature itself, to feed the industrial works higher up the mountain. Steam hisses, gears grind, and exhaust pipes cough plumes of sooty smoke into the pristine air, the cacophony echoes across the sparse landscape.

There's very little animal life here. The ecosystem is too new, too specialized. You occasionally spot pale, sightless grubworms and millipedes writhing amongst the moss, and high above, a few large, moth-like insects flit amongst the fronds. But mostly, it's just the plants, the machines, and the endless, deafening *drip*.

Scattered among all the greenery are remnants of human activity, of the machines that humans imposed on the land. Along with the cog-road, there are the rusted remains of early experiments, abandoned by the engineers as they were replaced by later models. There are broken aqueducts overgrown with moss, the skeletons of failed distillation devices, and the cracked foundations of buildings lost to the relentless green tide. The humans are not gone however. The main facility lies higher up, a cluster of industrial buildings and rail lines surrounding the mountain’s peak. It has been built to make the most of the mountain's abundant resources, yet its presence feels temporary, like a scar that the wilderness will eventually heal.

The slowness of things permeates everything. The mountain is rising, yes, but imperceptibly. The erosion, while minimal, is constant, a slow wearing away of the nascent rock. The machines, though noisy, are also slow, their processes measured in tons rather than kilowatts. Change comes in geological time, in the lifespan of forests, not in the frantic bursts of innovation or collapse one might expect from an industrial undertaking. It is an ecosystem and industry operating on a timescale that is difficult for the human mind to grasp.

And the light. Even on the cloudiest days, the diffused light through the vegetation is startlingly bright. The moss glows faintly, the leaves shimmer, and even the darkest corners of the undergrowth pulse with a soft, ethereal radiance. It’s a light that feels almost alien, a light born of pure, unadulterated plant life.

It's a place of contradictions: a young landscape shaped by ancient forces, an industrial outpost in a primal wilderness, a bright, wet world that somehow feels cold and lonely. It is, above all, a place that is becoming, a place caught in the slow, relentless process of its own making, indifferent to the noise and ambition of those who attempt to harness its power.
