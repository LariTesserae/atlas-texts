The sun is a hammer. It beats down on the chrome-lacquered ziggurats of the Hydroponic Quarter, each tier gleaming with a sweaty brightness that glances off the perpetually damp air. The air itself shimmers, thick with the effluvium of accelerated growth: the sweet rot of compost, the metallic tang of nutrient solutions, and the cloying perfume of genetically-engineered blossoms.

This is the Shale Flats Reclamation Project, Sector 7. Or, as the worm-farmers call it, the "Great Greenhouse." Generations ago, they say, this was a wasteland, pulverized by something called "tectonic upthrust" and then poisoned by something else called "legacy toxins." Now, nothing is wasteland. Every square meter is meticulously engineered, a symphony of pipes and pumps and precisely calibrated sunlight.

The ground, if you can call it that, is a lattice of molded polymer. Underneath, the bones of the old world - the fractured shale bedrock - are cradled in a network of canals. These canals, choked with bright green algae and buzzing with artificial life, feed the hydroponic farms that rise up in stepped terraces. It's less like a landscape and more like a gigantic, intricately-plumbed lung.

The plants are unnatural, almost caricatures of themselves. Giant taro leaves, each the size of a small hoverpad, drip condensation into collecting troughs. Fruits, bloated and glistening, hang heavy from engineered vines. Everything is cultivated for maximum yield, for efficient conversion of sunlight and nutrients into edible biomass. There's a frantic, desperate energy to the abundance, a sense that if the pumps stopped even for a moment, the whole system would collapse in on itself.

Fauna…that's something of a loaded term here. You won't find flocks of birds or herds of grazers. Instead, the air hums with the chitinous clicks and whirs of bio-engineered insects. These six-legged workers are the backbone of the system: pollinators, composters, pest control. They scuttle through the foliage, tending to the plants with a tireless, programmed efficiency. And then there are the worms. Giant, glistening earthworms, bred for their voracious appetites and their ability to transform bio-waste into fertilizer. They churn through the canals, blind and purposeful, a living river of soil-enrichment.

The weirdness is baked into the environment. It's in the unnaturally bright colors of the flora, in the geometric perfection of the ziggurats, in the very air itself, which seems to hum with a low-frequency vibration. The laws of nature seem… malleable here. One can sometimes see the sky flicker, as if the very rendering of the firmament is straining at the limits of its processing power. And sometimes, if you stare at the taro leaves long enough, you might catch a glimpse of the patterns shifting, reconfiguring into something… else.

The sounds are minimal. The gentle hum of the pumps, the clicking of the insects, the drip-drip-drip of condensation. There are no natural sounds, no breeze rustling through trees, no birdsong. The artificial quiet is almost oppressive, broken only by the occasional clang of a hand-operated pump or the slosh of water being carried in buckets.

And yet, despite the heat, the humidity, the artificiality, there's a strange kind of contentment here. The worm-farmers, clad in their patched-up overalls and wide-brimmed hats, move with a slow, deliberate grace. They tend to their crops, they repair their pumps, they tell stories in hushed voices around sputtering oil lamps. They know the rules of this world, they understand the rhythm of the cycles. They have built a life here, a precarious but undeniable existence, atop the ruins of the old.

The never-changing aspect is perhaps the most unsettling thing of all. The Shale Flats Reclamation Project has been in this state for generations, an equilibrium achieved through constant effort and meticulous control. The sun rises, the pumps hum, the insects work, the crops grow. Tomorrow will be the same as today, and the day after that. There is no change here, only maintenance. A perpetual present, suspended in a haze of humidity and artificial light.
