The air hangs heavy and damp, a constant, clinging presence that beads on exposed skin and slicks the cobblestones underfoot. It's a low-lying district, a cleft in the larger sprawl of the city carved by a slow, persistent seep of water from some unseen source higher up. "District" might be too grand a word; it's more like a tightly-packed ganglion, a knot of buildings squeezed between the steeper slopes above and the unseen edge of the mire that swallows the city further down.

The buildings are uniformly dark, built from a close-grained, black stone quarried locally. It almost seems to sweat moisture, nurturing a film of green algae in the deeper crevices. Single-storied structures, mostly, with steeply pitched roofs of the same stone, overlapped like scales to channel the frequent rain. Gutters, crudely carved and often overflowing, divert water into a network of equally crude drainage channels running along the uneven streets. These channels, rather than proper sewers, are the lifeblood and occasionally the bane of the district. They carry refuse and rainwater intermixed, a slick, black current that gurgles constantly.

Every surface is occupied. The low buildings abut each other, sharing walls and sometimes even roofs cobbled together with clay and what looks like woven reed mats. Balconies, barely wide enough to stand on, jut out precariously, draped with drying fabrics or stacked with fuel – peat bricks harvested from the margins of the city. Every available space is utilized, crammed with workshops, stalls, and living spaces. The air is thick with the smell of damp stone, woodsmoke from perpetually smoldering braziers, and the acrid tang of whatever dyes and fixatives are used in the district’s primary trade: tanning.

Vegetation is scarce, confined primarily to the tenacious algae and mosses clinging to the stone. A few hardy, stunted reeds push up through cracks in the paving near the water channels, their pale green a stark contrast to the prevailing black. Sometimes, a desperate attempt at a window box will appear, invariably wilting under the humid gloom and neglect.

The technology is brutally practical. Tools are mostly stone and bone, supplemented with simple copper implements – knives, awls, and the occasional clumsily-made saw. Hides are stretched on wooden frames, scraped with sharp flints, and soaked in vats of foul-smelling concoctions. The whole process is labor-intensive, reliant on manual strength and a deep, passed-down knowledge of the materials.

The soundscape is muted. The constant drip and trickle of water is a background drone, overlaid by the rhythmic scraping of tools on hides, the low murmur of voices, and the occasional splash as something – or someone – falls into the drainage channels. There’s a pervasive quietude, a sense of the district holding its breath, as if fearing to disturb the precarious balance it maintains against the encroaching water.

Fauna is limited. Rats are ubiquitous, sleek and well-fed on the refuse that accumulates in the channels. Occasionally, a lone frog will hop clumsily across the cobblestones, disappearing into the shadows. Flies are a constant presence, drawn to the decaying organic matter. But mostly, it’s a place of people, tightly bound together in a claustrophobic, inescapable embrace.

The weirdness stems from the constant battle against the elements. The residents are fiercely pragmatic, bordering on fatalistic. Superstitions cling to the edges of their lives – offerings left at the mouths of the drainage channels to appease the water spirits, charms woven from reeds to ward off illness, and a deep-seated fear of the unseen source that feeds the constant seep. The tanning process itself has an alchemical air, a mingling of natural processes and human intervention that feels somehow unnatural. The hides, stretched and manipulated, seem to take on a life of their own, whispering secrets of the creatures they once belonged to.

The district is dynamic in a slow, grinding way. The water levels fluctuate with the rainfall, sometimes flooding the lower workshops, forcing hurried evacuations and repairs. The tanning vats must be constantly tended, the chemical balance maintained. The rhythm of life is dictated by the availability of hides, traded from hunters in the uplands, and the demand for finished leather from crafters higher within the city. The seasons bring subtle shifts – the water grows colder in winter, the smell of tanning intensifies in summer – but the underlying sense of a struggle against entropy remains constant. It is a place on the edge, clinging to existence, a testament to the tenacity of life in the face of relentless pressure.
