The highlands are a tapestry of saturated green, woven so intricately that the underlying stone—if stone it truly is—has long surrendered to the relentless embrace of life. This is not a forest of individual trees, but a single, sprawling organism expressed through countless forms. Imagine terrestrial mangroves, but instead of tangled roots reaching for the sea, they are knotted aerial roots reaching for the next stratum of vegetation, spanning chasms so slowly that no one who begins the journey will live to see its end.

Water is everywhere, not as a rushing river or a placid lake, but as a constant presence. Clouds form and dissipate within the dense canopy, releasing their moisture in gentle, perpetual drizzles. Waterfalls weep from moss-covered cliffs, feeding shallow pools choked with bioluminescent fungi, their soft blue light the main source of illumination in this perpetual twilight. The air itself is heavy with humidity, clinging to the skin like a second, damp garment.

The temperature is a constant warmth, a humid embrace that encourages growth without end. Decay is swift, but the rate of new growth outpaces it, creating a cycle of birth and death that feeds the ever-expanding green. The altitude lends a certain coolness despite the warmth, a sharpness to the air that stings the lungs if one breathes too deeply.

Erosion is a forgotten concept here. Time has sanded off any sharp edges, dulled any potential conflict. Everything is smoothed, rounded, worn down not by wind or ice, but by the sheer weight of living matter. The landscape is near-flattened into gentle, rolling hills blanketed in endless vegetation, a topography so gentle that it's easy to believe that it was always this way, that the mountains themselves were born soft.

The scale is vast, almost incomprehensible. This isn't a valley or a mountain range, but a continent-spanning garden, a single ecosystem that seems to defy any boundaries. The weather here is a local affair, generated by the immense transpiration of the plant life itself, creating localized storms and pockets of dense fog that perpetually alter the view.

The density is remarkable. One is never alone, not really. Life presses in from all sides: countless insects crawl along the vines, unseen creatures stir in the undergrowth, and the air hums with the silent symphony of a million unseen processes. Layer upon layer of plant life creates a labyrinthine world, an endless maze of green and brown, of living matter and decaying matter, a single, sprawling city built from the flora of the world, where entire ecologies live in the span between two leaves.

Traces of a prior presence are scarce and subtle. Here and there, overgrown stone structures peek through the vegetation, their purpose lost to time. They are small and dilapidated, more like natural formations than deliberate constructions. Perhaps they were shelters, or perhaps the remains of some forgotten experiment, long ago yielded to the inexorable advance of the plants.

Technology is nonexistent. The inhabitants, if they exist, live in harmony with the ecosystem, neither shaping it nor being shaped by it, but simply a part of the great green machine. Tools are unnecessary; everything needed is provided by the landscape itself. Water drips from the leaves, food grows in abundance, shelter is woven by the vines.

The silence is almost deafening, broken only by the occasional rustle of leaves or the chirping of unseen insects. There are no large predators, no fierce winds, no jarring sounds. Here, the silence is so complete that it becomes a presence in itself, a reminder of the slow, inevitable processes that govern everything.

The fauna is teeming, but mostly small and shy. Insects, arachnids, amphibians, reptiles, and avian creatures abound, each filling a unique niche in the complex ecosystem. Larger creatures exist, but they are elusive, rarely seen. The abundance indicates a healthy, stable ecosystem, but also one that is easily disrupted.

This place is strange, alien, unlike anything found on Earth. It's a world where the rules of biology have been rewritten, where plants have become the dominant force, shaping the landscape and creating their own reality. Everything has been terraformed to perfection for botanical splendor.

Change is slow, measured in geological time. The cycles of life and death continue, but the overall form of the landscape remains largely unchanged. The processes are patient, the gradients weak. The whole place breathes on a timescale that is imperceptible to most creatures. This is a world that has settled into its final form, a place where time has lost its meaning and life has become its own reward.
