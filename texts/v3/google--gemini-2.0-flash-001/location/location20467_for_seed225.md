The sun blasts down.

Not directly, though. The place is underground, but the ceiling is close enough, and porous enough, that it might as well be open sky a lot of the time. This close to the surface there is no pressure. No heat differential. The place breathes. Warm, thin air moves gently, carrying the smell of stone and something else...something green trying.

It is a network of *nurseries*. That is the simplest way to say it. Chambers, maybe twenty or thirty feet across, connected by narrow passages. The ceiling is riddled with holes. Not jagged breaks, but neat perforations, as if something bored carefully through limestone. The light pours through these holes, illuminating particular spots on the floor, the walls, the damp, sweating ceiling.

The rock is pale, almost white, but stained with rusty orange and streaks of black. The floor is mostly bare stone, but in the places where the light falls, things grow. Sparse, yes, but abundant in variety. Fat, succulent things that look like pebbles. Ground-hugging leaves veined with red. Thin, spidery stalks topped with a single, iridescent blue petal. There is no soil, exactly. Just a thin layer of something like powdered chalk mixed with moisture.

The sound is mostly quiet. The buzz of insects. The drip, drip, drip of water. A rustling that could be wind, or something else moving through the sparse vegetation. Occasionally, a low, guttural croak that seems to vibrate through the very rock.

The architects -- for these are built spaces, despite the lack of formal construction -- are the *gloamtoads*. Toad is a vast oversimplification. Imagine something like a toad, yes, but the size of a dog. Translucent skin stretched over a network of glowing blue veins. Huge, bulging eyes that seem to absorb all available light. The gloamtoads move slowly, deliberately. They have no need for haste. Time here is a slow, patient process.

Their technology is primitive, but effective. They use sharpened stones to bore the holes in the ceiling, angling them carefully to catch the light at different times of the day. They cultivate the plants, moving them from chamber to chamber to optimize their growth. They use the plants for food, for medicine, and for the creation of pigments that they use to paint intricate patterns on the walls.

The patterns are the key to understanding this place. Each chamber is decorated with a unique series of symbols, geometric shapes, and stylized depictions of plants and animals. These are not mere decorations. They are maps. Records. Prayers. They are the language by which the gloamtoads communicate with each other and with the world around them.

And the strangeness...The most noticeable feature would be the blue worms. They are about the thickness of a human finger, and grow to be about four feet long. During the day, they appear sluggish, and almost inert. But as the sun is setting, they wake up from their torpor. While still underground, the worms come above ground and glow in the dim chamber. No one knows if the glow is to attract mates, or if it is used for something else. The worms are not easy to catch, due to being very smooth and slippery. They will even break off a piece of their body if being held.

The worms are not native to this system. There is a way to know this; the gloamtoads have not depicted a worm in their art. Everything else has been memorialized. The worms have only been around for a few generations of gloamtoads. There are signs that gloamtoads have been attempting to depict the worms...but nothing yet has been set in stone.

The nursery system is a delicate balance. The gloamtoads tend to it, watching the cycles of light and water, coaxing life from the rock. They are the keepers of this strange, sunlit underworld, and their fate is inextricably linked to its own. There is no knowing what would happen if they stopped tending the garden.
