The Foundry Pylon stood against the sky, a perfect cube of riveted steel that hummed with contained energies. The air was thin and cool, bright with a light that felt both natural and aggressively artificial. It wasn't the sun, not exactly, but a web of high-altitude reflectors, catching and focusing irradiance down onto the sprawling metalscape below. Below stretched Manufactory Facet 784, a dizzying expanse of platforms, pipelines, and interconnected industrial structures. Built, utterly. Not a trace remained of the original substrate, whatever mountain peak it might have been. Here, elevation was just another parameter to be managed, another resource to be plumbed.

This high, the wind was minimal, almost nonexistent, due to some interplay of shielding and static charge. What was audible was the dense drone of industry, layered and modulated into a low, almost soothing hum. It was a deceptive quiet, though. A *busy* quiet, full of the minute clicks and whirs of countless automated processes. Steam hissed faintly from vents, quickly condensing into a fine mist that clung to the metal surfaces. The dampened metal gleamed in the bright light, reflecting the harsh angles and repeating geometries of the engineered landscape.

Vegetation was sparse, but present. It wasn't *natural* vegetation, of course. Genetically-engineered mosses and lichen clung to the vertical surfaces, absorbing condensation and ambient heat, serving some obscure purpose in the manufacturing process. Their color ranged from a metallic silver-green to a startling electric blue, depending on their proximity to the pipelines. Further down, clinging to the lower levels of the platforms and the outer walls of towers, were hydroponic farms – tiered arrays of vats where algae and fungi grew in nutrient-rich solutions. Nourishment for the fauna: swarms of bio-engineered moths, their wings shimmering with interference patterns, clustered around the hydroponics vats. They were designed to pollinate the metallic 'trees', the towering heat exchangers that rose from the platforms filtering the emissions. The slow, deliberate cycles, the barely-changed routines of Facet 784 had carved a niche not only for the moths; every cranny hosted micro-fauna bred for some specific, obscure, function.

The weirdness came in waves, shimmering distortions in the air, visual glitches as if reality were momentarily struggling to maintain its consistency. These were localized manifestations of the "Flux", unpredictable energy surges that rippled through the grid. The workers—or "servitors," as they were officially designated—had learned to anticipate the signs: a sudden flicker in the reflectors, a high-pitched whine in the power conduits, a momentary shift in perceived gravity. Most found some way to live parallel with the weirdness, accepting the effects as part of their daily grind. Some were assigned to monitor and contain the effects, some to study and leverage them, but most to simply survive.

Erosion was an artificial process here, a planned form of decay. The surfaces were designed to degrade at a predetermined rate, releasing elements required elsewhere in the manufacturing cycle. Pipelines corroded, shedding metals into collection trays. Platforms cracked, exposing internal structures. But decay was part of the design. Nothing was truly lost, only transformed, recycled, and reintegrated into the system.

Everything here felt both hyper-modern and strangely ancient. The technology was advanced, but its application was oddly ritualistic. The servitors followed complex, repetitive routines, their movements dictated by algorithms and ingrained habit, less like operators and more like cogs in an immense machine. They were immersed in the dense grid of production, and yet their existence was punctuated by small anomalies and quiet mysteries. The Flux, the moth swarms, the hydroponics, and the endless, humming industry: a cathedral of calculation, a dense and living monument to a forgotten purpose.
