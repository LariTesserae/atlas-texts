The air hangs thick and warm, a humid blanket that clings to skin. It's the kind of warmth that seeps into your bones, drawing out a languid contentment, if not for the disquieting feeling that you are not supposed to be here. Not in this exact spot, at this exact moment. Not yet.

You stand on a patch of what could generously be called land. It is more accurately an aggregation of mud and root, hardly more than a large hummock. Below, the mud gives way to dark, still water. Above you is a cathedral of green – though it's a young cathedral, gothic arches still taking shape, flying buttresses only barely supporting the vaulting ceiling. The "city" here is not built in stone or steel, but woven from living things.

"Sparse" is the wrong word for the vegetation. It's not that there isn't much of it; it's that what *is* here is so concentrated, so brimming with vitality. Broad, fenestrated leaves drip condensation onto the slick mud. Vines, thick as pythons, coil around the trunks of slender, pale trees that seem to be reaching for a sky that is mostly obscured by the density of the canopy. Everything is dripping, seeping, oozing with moisture; the air itself feels like a damp cloth pressed against your face. It is not raining, but there is the constant sound of water trickling. Channels form in the soft earth as the tiny streams trickle downhill, some disappearing into the murky water, others feeding pools of algae.

The scale is deceptive. At first glance, it seems limited, contained. A small, self-enclosed pocket. But the deeper you look, the more it expands. The hummock underfoot seems to be connected to another, and another again, each a tiny island in a shallow, interconnected web of waterways. It's a floating city, a collection of miniature ecosystems clinging together, slowly coalescing. Urban, yes, but in the organic, emergent way that a coral reef builds itself from the skeletons of tiny creatures.

"Erosion" is also inaccurate. It’s not crumbling away that you witness, but a building-up, a constant cycle of creation and decay, happening in accelerated time. Roots that sink into the earth also rise from the mud, arching in the air to create living bridges. Fallen leaves don't decompose so much as melt back into the system, providing fresh nutrients for the rapidly growing flora. The entire place shimmers in the humidity, as if the very air is a solvent, dissolving and reforming everything in perpetual motion.

You haven’t seen any people, nor any trace of them. No tools, no structures beyond the living architecture. The city is biological. Yet, there are small pathways through the undergrowth — barely visible, easily missed — and resting places where the fronds of giant ferns have been flattened. The inhabitants here are small, quick, and quiet.

The silence is broken only by the hum of insects. They are not annoying, swarming things here, but individual presences, each with its own distinct buzz or click. Beetles with iridescent shells crawl along the stems of orchids. Dragonflies with wings like stained glass hover over the pools of water. And in the distance, barely audible, a chorus of croaking frogs resonates.

Every surface crawls with life. Fungi bloom in vibrant colors on rotting wood. Tiny snails leave glistening trails on the leaves. Everything is connected in a vast, unseen network. Predator stalks prey, and nutrients from a fallen branch become life for the soil and for the tree it once fell from.

The light is dim, diffused by the dense canopy. It filters through the leaves in shifting patterns, creating an ethereal glow that dances over the landscape. The air smells of sweet decay and the tang of standing water. There's a heaviness to the atmosphere, a sense of oppressive fertility.

This is a world in its infancy, a city still gestating. It has its own rules, its own rhythms, barely perceptible but undeniably present. You are an intruder, a premature visitor to a place not quite ready for your presence. But the invitation has been given, and you are here now, in the heart of its slow, green emergence.
