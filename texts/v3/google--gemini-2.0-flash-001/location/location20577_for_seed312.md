The wind sighs, a drawn-out lament that claws at the edges of the city. Not wind as we might know it, a scouring force, but something thicker, laden with moisture that beads on exposed skin and clings to rough-hewn stone. It tastes of iron and something else, something ancient and metallic, like the blood of the world weeping through cracks in its skin.

This is Orok-Sul, the Spine City, and it clings to the face of the God-Mount like a scabrous growth. We are high. Terribly, impossibly high. Below, the land falls away in a dizzying swirl of greys and whites – the grey of bare rock, the white of perpetual snow that dusts the lower slopes. The *scale* is the thing that truly crushes you. One could walk for days, weeks even, *down* the mountain and still be dwarfed by its immensity. The air thins and bites here, and the sun, when it deigns to break through the perpetual shroud of cloud, feels sharper, crueler. The light is generally normal, but seems amplified to an almost painful quality.

Orok-Sul itself is a warren of structures built from the very rock it occupies. Sharp, recent erosion has created jagged stone features that are readily incorporated into their designs. Buildings are piled atop each other; a haphazard, organic accretion. Stone bricks and roughly shaped timbers form the bones, bound together with a mortar that seems to bleed from the mountain itself – a dark, viscous substance that smells faintly of sulfur. Everything is damp. Water seeps from the stones, trickles down walls, and gathers in stagnant pools in the uneven streets. The condensation is everywhere.

Vegetation is sparse, almost cruelly so. Patches of hardy moss cling to north-facing walls, and tough, wiry grasses scratch at the thin soil in sheltered crannies. You might find a stunted, wind-blasted bush clinging to the edge of a precipice, its branches gnarled and twisted by the constant pressure of the damp wind.

The soundscape is dominated by the wind, a low, mournful drone that seems to penetrate the very bones. Intermittent dripping of water. The scree of loose rocks dislodged on the mountain’s flanks. Occasionally, a deeper, resonant hum vibrates through the city, a sound that the inhabitants attribute to the mountain itself, shifting in its slow, geological slumber. Sound is mostly quiet, but the vastness and the echoes amplify even the smaller noises.

This city *teems* with life. Not human life, though they are present in significant number, scratching a meager existence from the harsh environment. But the *fauna*… Here, the mountain gives birth to creatures unlike any found in the lower lands. Giant, shaggy spiders spin webs that shimmer with captured moisture. Birds with leathery wings and bone crests soar through the narrow streets. Insects, armored in chitin and fueled by the mountain's strange energies, crawl over every surface. Every nook and cranny is alive, an ecosystem compressed into the impossible confines of Orok-Sul. The place throbs with unseen life, a high *density* in every direction.

The people of Orok-Sul are a hardy folk. Their tech is primitive, the tools of survival hammered from rough iron, their clothes woven from the wool of mountain sheep and the silk of giant spiders. They are builders, constantly patching and expanding the city, fighting a slow, losing battle against the relentless elements, and erosion. They understand the mountain, its moods, its whims. They appease it with offerings and rituals, hoping to stave off the next rockslide, the next flood of icy water, the next… hum… from the depths.

There's a sense of *slow* time here. The mountain changes on a scale that dwarfs human lifetimes. The people adapt, endure, and perpetuate in a rhythm that mirrors the age of stone itself. Each new generation is another layer in this complex, dense city. Building upon the old infrastructure, literally.

The overall feeling is of an alien place, a precarious balance, an entire civilization built on the back of something ancient and immense. A place where the boundaries of existence blur, and where the line between city and mountain, between life and rock, becomes almost imperceptible.
