The Miresing.

The Miresing is a hanging valley, choked with moss and choked with life. It is not a *deep* valley, not a scar in the world's face, but a wide, shallow declivity between two shoulders of ancient, rounded mountains. Water drips, flows, and oozes everywhere. If a stone is turned, a film of moisture coats its underside; kick at any patch of what appears to be solid ground, and your boot will sink into cold mud. The air itself is damp, on the edge of mist but rarely quite crossing over. The light is permanently dim, filtered through a canopy of saturated green.

It is not a forest in any conventional sense. The trees are stunted things, their growth checked by altitude and the waterlogged soil. They huddle together, their roots a tangled mat struggling for purchase on the thin soil. Lichens cling to their bark, and moss grows everywhere – on the branches, on the trunks, on the ground, even on the larger animals that move through the undergrowth. The scale is intimate; one could cross the entire Miresing on foot in a day, skirting the bogs and picking a careful path through the dense vegetation. It feels like a single, enclosed space despite its lack of walls. The surrounding mountains serve as a constant reminder of limits.

The dominant sound is the drip. A slow, steady plink-plink-plink that permeates everything. Occasionally, it is punctuated by the croak of a frog, or the rustle of something moving through the undergrowth, but these sounds are quickly swallowed by the all-encompassing damp. Silence, in the Miresing, is just a different kind of wet sound.

Despite the gloom, life explodes here. Insects swarm in clouds, their iridescent wings flashing in the dim light. Fungi of every shape and color sprout from the damp earth and rotting wood. Strange, slug-like creatures leave glistening trails across the moss. This is not a place of grand, majestic beasts, but of countless small lives intertwined in a complex web. The sheer *density* of the life is staggering. Every possible niche is filled, every scrap of organic matter recycled with astonishing speed.

And then there are the Troglodytes. *Homo palustris*. They are small, hunched figures, their skin slick with a layer of mud and algae. They are perfectly camouflaged in the dim light, almost indistinguishable from the rocks and tree roots that surround them. They are not builders; they do not shape the land. They live *in* it, in the hollows of trees, in the overhangs of rocks, in the barely-drained 'caves' that pockmark the hills through which the few, slow-moving streams find hollow routes. Their tools are crude – sharpened stones, woven reeds, bones lashed together with sinew – but they are enough. They are hunters and gatherers, living in small, nomadic groups, following the seasonal cycles of the Miresing. Their existence is difficult, precarious, but they are intimately connected to this place. They are as much a part of the Miresing as the moss and the water.

The weirdness is subtle, almost imperceptible at first. It is in the strange bioluminescence of some of the fungi, in the way the insects communicate with a series of clicks and whistles, and in the haunting, melodic calls of the Troglodytes, which mimic the sounds of the Miresing itself. There's a sense that the normal rules of the world don't quite apply here, that the veil between realities is thin. It is not a place of overt magic or fantastical creatures, but of subtle anomalies and uncanny echoes.

Perhaps the most striking thing about the Miresing is its stillness. It has existed in this same state for millennia, a stable, self-contained ecosystem. The slow processes of decay and renewal have reached a perfect equilibrium. Change is measured in geological time, not in human lifespans. The mountains erode at a glacial pace, the bogs slowly fill with sediment, and the Troglodytes continue their ancient way of life, undisturbed by the outside world. It would take an eon for sunlight to dry the Miresing out. It is a place where time seems to stand still, where the past, present, and future blur into a single, endless moment of damp, green, teeming life.
