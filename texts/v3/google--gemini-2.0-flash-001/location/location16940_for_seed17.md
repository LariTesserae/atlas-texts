The abyssal plain stretches, cold and dark, further than any eye could see, even if eyes could see in this lightless place. It is not the familiar dark of an ocean floor, silted and teeming, but something else entirely. Here, the water itself is the world, a vast suspension not of salt but of something far denser, and colder than any terrestrial sea. The density is nearly imperceptible, an immense stillness against all senses, but it is there, giving the water a weight unlike any known liquid.

Jagged, recently-formed peaks of black, volcanic glass rise from the plain, remnants of some cataclysmic event that shook the liquid world and spat these structures out, still sharp and untouched by erosion. They are as numerous as teeth, jutting against the currents, forming a mineral archipelago in this strange, submerged realm. No plant life clings to these obsidian spires. No soft algae or coral dulls their razor edges. This is a world of pure geometry, of angles and force, of heat expunged and matter abruptly frozen.

Scattered across the abyssal plain, clinging to the black glass cliffs, are the remains of immense, metallic structures. These are more than mere buildings: they are colossal shells of factory complexes, long-silent and corroded by the hyper-dense waters. Their architecture is brutally functional, all right angles and vast, echoing chambers that once housed machinery now lost or dissolved. Here and there, a single corroded pipe snakes across the obsidian. A colossal, toppled engine, encrusted with strange, geometric barnacles, lies half-buried in the plain. 

This was once a place of thunderous industry. The black glass itself may have been the very product of it, expelled in superheated clouds and then frozen by the icy water. Now, only the faintest ghosts of that time remain: a rust-colored stain blooming slowly outwards from a shattered vent, the echo of a grinding piston that reverberates down long-sealed corridors.

And yet, life teems here. Not plantlife. No, but there is an abundance of fauna. They seem to thrive on the industrial detritus, on the metallic salts dissolved in the water. Strange, bioluminescent creatures flit through the wreckage. They are slender, almost invisible threads of light, weaving complex patterns through the ruined machinery. And amongst all the light there is always something that creeps in the corner of your vision that slithers and slides against your mind.

The landscape is also deafening. Not in a sudden, violent way, but with the deep, resonating hum that penetrates every molecule of the liquid world. The frequencies are so low that they are felt rather than heard, a constant vibration in the bones. This is not a natural sound. This is the vibration of immense machines still functioning, deep within the world, grinding on in eternal, pointless labor. When the hum falls away, every now and then, there is still the sound of some echo from the past. The hiss, the steam, and the clatter of a machine or two.

For all its strangeness, there is a profound stillness to this place. The temperature is constant, unwavering. Currents do not shift. The composition of the water never varies. The endless hum remains the same, the vibrations ever-present. It is a system in perfect, frozen equilibrium.

But not perfectly. Not entirely.

There is a hint of unusualness on the edges of perception. An unplaceable color to the ever-present bio-luminescence. A subtle warp in the geometry of the black glass. A sense of patterns that are not quite patterns. This can be found at the base of the massive spire like buildings. They are perfectly engineered, but there is little room for design flaws, only minor mistakes and errors. These errors can be found on most surfaces. It will take an attuned eye, but it is there. The mistakes are where the monsters are. It is in them that any hope of breaking the stillness lies.
