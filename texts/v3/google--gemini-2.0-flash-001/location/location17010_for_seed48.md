The air hangs thick and heavy, not with humidity, but with...something else. The light is a perpetual pre-dawn, filtered through a soup of vapor and industrial haze, barely strong enough to cast a shadow. The ground underfoot is slick, a mosaic of damp cobblestones worn smooth by time and something more corrosive.

This is Lowmarket, a district that sprawls not up, but out, a city built on the bones of older cities, sinking slowly into the marshy delta it occupies. The elevation is just above sea level, and the delta has no true sea. Rather, the water comes in waves, not of tide, but of heat. Every few cycles of the sun, the geothermal power plants further in the megastructure falter, and Lowmarket is closer to their cooling apparatus than other districts. Steam belches from cracked pavement, hisses from rusted pipes that snake along the walls of buildings, a constant reminder of the oppressive heat that simmers beneath everything. It's never truly cool, just varying degrees of sweltering.

Structures are squat, built of dark, iron-laced stone that's coated in a film of grime. There are hints of grander architecture visible, half-submerged archways, columns worn down to stumps, ornate carvings eaten away by acid rain and the ceaseless pressure of settling earth. The aesthetic is functional, brutal even, but not without touches of a forgotten elegance. A sense of decay hangs like a shroud, a slow, creeping entropy. The buildings are largely built of pre-formed blocks, stacked atop each other. The only place that is not a right angle are the edges worn smooth by wind, weather, and countless hands.

No plants grow here. Instead, strange, bioluminescent fungi cling to the damp walls, feasting on chemical runoff and exhaust, casting a sickly, greenish glow that barely penetrates the gloom. They pulse faintly, their caps slick and glistening, a constant, silent reminder of life persisting where it shouldn't. They are the only stationary life in the district, aside from the structures themselves.

What isn't silent is the fauna. They crawl, scuttle, and slither. They are the overwhelming presence of Lowmarket. Genetically engineered for waste disposal and maintenance, these creatures, the Critters, have long since outgrown their intended purpose. They are everywhere: the chitinous click of their segments against stone, the rasp of their mandibles, the squealing of nestlings in the dark pipes. Small, rat-like recyclers with iridescent carapaces dart through the shadows, vacuuming up debris. Giant slug-like scavengers, meters in length, slowly inch through the canals, their rubbery hides glistening, trailed by swarms of fly-like bio-drones that assist in breaking down the detritus. Some are clearly artificial in origin, mechanical limbs grafted onto biological bodies, but others appear to be purely organic, the product of generations of unchecked evolution. A pair of beetle-like creatures, the size of dogs, compete for discarded nutrient packets under a glowing fungus.

The silence is broken only by the hum of distant machinery, the hiss of escaping steam, and the chitinous scratching all around you. The constant activity of the critters creates it's own kind of hush. The city is active. The critters are the blood pulsing through the veins of the city. Their quiet, constant movement the rhythm of survival.

The infrastructure is decaying but undeniable. Elevated walkways, corroded but still passable, crisscross the district. Pipelines carry fluids of unknown origin and dubious safety. Cages protect lights that illuminate the ways that creatures might not think to navigate, but they are dim at best and offer little comfort. There are automated systems here, remnants of a more sophisticated age, but they are unreliable, prone to malfuctions, patched together with scavenged parts and desperate ingenuity.

Lowmarket is a place where things persist, where life finds a way, however repugnant. Its inhabitants, human and otherwise, are survivors, adapted to the dim light, the oppressive heat, the constant activity of critters and the stench of decay. It is not a beautiful place, but it is alive, in its own strange, unsettling way.
