The engine thrums, a low, insistent drone against the backdrop of… well, of everything trying to become something. It's a wet heat, a suffocating blanket that weighs down the lungs and slicks the skin with condensation. The light, filtered through layers of steam and whatever particulate slurry hangs perpetually in the air, is a weak, jaundiced yellow. It barely penetrates the immensity of this… construction site? Womb? Hard to say.

Underfoot is not ground, but a slick of cooling slag. It’s mostly solid, formed into crude plates and girders, but vents hiss and bubble intermittently, releasing plumes of scalding vapor. The individual components are immense, dwarfing even the clumsy, hand-operated vehicles that crawl across the surface like beetles on a discarded plate. Primitive rigs, cobbled together from scavenged metal and powered by shuddering internal combustion engines that belch black smoke into the already oppressive atmosphere. It smells of sulfur, hot metal, and something faintly sweet, like overripe fruit on the verge of rot.

Scale is the first thing that hits you. Not just the size of the individual components, but the *space* between them. Vast distances separate these embryonic structures. You could drive for days across these slag fields and never reach what might become a wall, a tower, or some other unidentifiable element. It's a sparseness born not of natural desolation, but of colossal, unfinished ambition. It feels as though giants labored here once, or still labor, abandoning half-finished projects as their attention – or their resources - wane.

Turbulence is the second. It isn't constant, but episodic, like the planet itself is experiencing fits of creation and destruction. The ground shudders without warning. Vents belch not just steam, but pyroclastic jets of molten rock. Distant structures groan and shift, settling into new, unstable configurations. This isn’t erosion in the gentle sense; it’s punctuated violence, a series of birth pangs as something struggles to coalesce.

The *weirdness* hums along the edges of perception. There’s a non-Euclidean quality to the geometry, a subtle sense that angles aren't quite right, that perspectives shift unexpectedly. Sometimes, the very air seems to ripple, and you glimpse impossible vistas – impossible colors, impossible architectures – before the reality of slag and steam reasserts itself. It’s like a faulty memory, a dream bleeding through into waking life.

There is little sound beyond the thrum of engines and the hiss of vents. A low, resonant drone that permeates everything. Once in a while, the roar of fracturing rock or the splash of molten material disrupts the quiet, but these are fleeting moments. The overall impression is one of stifled potential, of a world holding its breath. A world waiting for something, or perhaps, waiting *to be* something.

The landscape is utterly devoid of anything resembling life. No plants, no animals, not even insects or bacteria. The environment is too harsh, too volatile. The only inhabitants are those who tend the engines, who haul the materials, who struggle to impose some kind of order on this chaos. They are a primitive lot, clad in soot-stained rags and crude protective gear made from scavenged metal. They communicate in a guttural language of grunts and gestures, their faces weathered and worn, their eyes hollow with fatigue and a kind of resigned desperation. They are the midwives, perhaps, to this stillborn god, or perhaps merely its parasites. Either way, they are integral. Integral in a system that is built, and building, and building still, but never done.

There are patterns in the turbulence, though. Hints. If you watch long enough, you might notice that the bursts of activity tend to cluster around certain structures, as if feeding them. You might see that the slag flows in predictable currents, guided by unseen forces. You might realize that the seemingly random arrangement of components is, in fact, a rudimentary framework, a skeleton waiting for flesh.

This is not a world, but a concept. An idea made manifest. And the idea, though vast and terrifying, is profoundly incomplete. It is the world's nature, and perhaps its purpose.
