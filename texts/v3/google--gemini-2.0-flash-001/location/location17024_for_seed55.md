The world is a single, vast, frigid ocean contained within a shell of ice kilometers-thick. Light penetrates to a bare few meters, and even that is mostly the sickly green phosphorescence of the strange, protist-like life that dominates the ecosystem here. The only sound is the deep groaning of the ice as it expands and contracts with temperature variations that cycle over centuries.

It is a totally built environment, but built is perhaps the wrong word. Grown. The ubiquitous protists secrete a calcium-rich compound, not unlike the shells of earthly diatoms. These collect over unimaginable aeons, forming a dense, layered substrate. New generations build upon the old, layer after layer, compressing those below into a solid stone that slowly, imperceptibly shifts under the pressure. The entire seafloor is composed of this organically-fabricated "shellstone."

The architecture is biogenic, driven by simple rules encoded in the protists' genetic material. As new layers are deposited, the protists respond to chemical gradients and subtle pressure differentials, creating intricate branching structures, vast archways, and spiraling channels within the shellstone. These aren't planned, in the human sense. They emerge, slowly, organically, from the interaction of countless tiny organisms following simple, local imperatives.

The upper layers, perpetually submerged in near-freezing darkness, are the living city. Here, the protists thrive, forming a near-solid suspension in the frigid water. They are not static. They build, dissolving old structures to create new ones in a perpetual cycle of creation and destruction. The architecture is therefore in constant flux, though the changes are so slow they are imperceptible to human timescales. The geometry is strange and unsettling, defying Euclidean intuition. Imagine a coral reef made of glass, but the glass itself is alive and slowly restructuring according to patterns beyond comprehension.

The "vegetation" here dominates; it is the only thing, forming the substance of the world and its atmosphere. It is a dense soup, a blizzard of constantly building and dissolving cellular structures. It is, for all intents and purposes, motionless, silent, and eternal.

What appears to be technology exists only as an embedded part of this process. Certain lineages of protists have evolved the ability to manipulate the shellstone, carving and shaping it with chemical secretions and brute physical force. These are not tools in the human sense, but rather organic extensions of the protists themselves, analogous to the beaks of birds or the spinnerets of spiders. Imagine a protist that secretes a dissolving agent that can etch patterns into the shellstone, creating intricate carvings or precisely calibrated channels for nutrient flow.

The system is closed, or nearly so. Energy is derived from trace amounts of geothermal activity and from the slow decay of organic matter trapped within the shellstone over millennia. It is a world of ultra-slow processes, where the dominant forces are pressure, chemistry, and the relentless, incremental accumulation of living matter. No animal life exists. There are only the protists, their shellstone architecture, and the cold, dark water.

Despite its strangeness, there is a profound sense of normality here. This is a world in equilibrium, settled into its final form. Erosion has done its work, smoothing away any rough edges and grinding everything down to its most basic components. There is nothing wasted, nothing out of place. It is a perfect, if alien, ecosystem, operating according to rules that are both simple and immensely complex.

It is a testament to the power of life to create order out of chaos, to build monuments to itself even in the most hostile of environments. It is a city built by the blind, each brick laid with infinitesimal care, a monument to patience, persistence, and the enduring power of the microscopic.
