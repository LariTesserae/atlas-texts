The air hangs heavy and still in the Factory Canyons. Not moisture as such, but a thicker, more viscous kind of damp, like breathing through oiled wool. It clings to the skin, blurring the lines of vision, softening the edges of the colossal machines that choke the valley floor.

This was once a river valley, or so the old charts claim. Now, the river is a memory, or perhaps a rumor told by rust. It persists only as a network of canals, choked with algae and oil, diverted and hammered into service by the sprawling manufactories. Water erupts in geysers of steam from corroded pipes and pools in the cracked concrete aprons around the factories, iridescent with chemical sheen.

Vegetation is a ghost here. Patches of tenacious moss cling to the dampest surfaces, a sickly green against the grey, and skeletal weeds push through cracks in the paving stones, their leaves coated in a fine, metallic dust. But life has largely surrendered to the grinding pulse of the machines.

The temperature is oppressive, a humid warmth that settles in the lungs. It radiates from the machinery itself, the endless churning of gears and the hiss of steam engines. It is the heat of constant labor, a forced and unnatural warmth.

The canyons rise on either side, sheer cliffs of eroded rock carved into unnatural geometries by quarrying and construction. They are riddled with the scars of industry – adits leading to abandoned mines, crumbling platforms for conveyors, the rusted skeletons of derricks. High up, where the warmth doesn't reach, the stone is stained black by acid rain, a testament to generations of unchecked emissions.

The scale is immense, but the architecture is strangely humbling. A town has grown into the machines, and a machine has grown into a town. Workers' dormitories cling to the cliff faces, built from scrap metal and scavenged parts, their windows like vacant eyes staring out at the industrial sprawl. Bridges arch across the canals, connecting workshops and hab-blocks, their surfaces slick with grime. The whole valley feels like a single, gigantic organism, sustained by the endless processing of raw materials.

The machines are powered by a network of cables and pipes that crawl across the landscape like veins. They feed factories that operate on a staggering scale, processing unknown materials into unidentifiable products. There is no sense of what is being made, only the constant, deafening roar of industry.

The sound dominates everything. It is a cacophony of grinding gears, the hiss of steam, the rhythmic clang of hammers, the mournful wail of sirens, and the deeper, subsonic rumble of immense engines working far below the surface. There is no silence, no reprieve. The vibrations resonate in the bones, a constant assault on the senses.

Fauna is almost entirely absent. The only creatures hardy enough to survive are rats, cockroaches, and a strange species of black, iridescent beetle that thrives on the oily residue that coats everything. They are the scavengers of this industrial ecosystem, feeding on the waste and decay, their lives inextricably linked to the machines.

There is a palpable strangeness to the place, a subtle sense of unreality. The sheer scale of the industry, the alien nature of the processes, the oppressive atmosphere – all contribute to a feeling of being trapped in a dream, or perhaps a nightmare. The air itself hums with an unspoken energy, as if the machines are generating something more than just material goods.

The changes here are slow and measured. Years might pass before a building entirely collapses, decades before the rust finally eats through a supporting beam. The valley has reached a certain equilibrium – a bleak and oppressive balance between industry and decay. The slow cycles of erosion and the barely-perceptible shift in the balance of the machine continues, and has happened for generations.
