The high peaks are forever white, but the snowline seems lower than it should be, smudging the grey scree of the upper slopes. This isn't a jagged, alpine landscape; the erosion has seen to that. These are old mountains, worn down by millennia of wind and ice, their rough edges softened into slopes that are still brutally steep but feel ancient, settled, final. It's an open landscape; you can see for miles, a series of valleys and ridges fading into a hazy blue distance where the curve of the world cuts it off. But the emptiness isn't a comfortable one. It's a sense of exposure.

Water is scarce, but present. It clings. Dew gathers every morning, heavy on the sparse vegetation, and lingers late into the day in the shadowed folds of the rock. Water trickles from unseen sources, weaving thin, icy threads down the mountainside, disappearing into the porous stone. There are no rushing rivers, no deep pools. But the stone itself is damp. Touch any surface and you'll feel the pervasive dampness. The prevailing wind carries a fine mist, more felt than seen, that beads persistently on exposed skin.

Vegetation is patchy: tough, low-growing things that cling to the mineral-rich soil where they can find a sheltered hollow or a crack in the rock. Lichens crust the stones in vibrant greens and oranges, as though trying to reclaim the stark grey. Wiry grasses, almost blue in color, sprout in clumps interspersed with hardy stubby shrubs. Twisted, ground-hugging trees, more bush than tree, offer scant shelter from the wind. Whatever can survive, does — but only barely.

Despite the cold and altitude, there's a profusion of life here. Not in its diversity, precisely — but in sheer numbers. The air is thick with the sound of it. Insects buzz and drone, filling the valleys with a constant hum that rises and falls with the shifting wind. Flocks of birds, small and quick, dart between the rocky outcrops, their cries echoing across the open spaces. Higher up, huge raptors circle on the wind currents, their piercing screeches adding to the din. Their prey consists of small rodents that scurry amongst the rocks, seemingly oblivious to the dangers above. Herds of shaggy mountain goats navigate the treacherous slopes with ease, their bells clanging like distant chimes.

The sound, though... the sound is the key. It's deafening. It's not just the wind, though that certainly plays a part, howling relentlessly across the exposed peaks. And it's not just the fauna, though they are ceaseless in their activity. There's something else, something deeper. The earth itself seems to hum, a low, resonant thrum that vibrates through the very stones beneath your feet. It's a pressure you feel more than hear, a constant, unwavering baseline to the cacophony of the living world above.

The light is stark and uncompromising. The thin atmosphere filters out little of the sun's harsh glare, bleaching the landscape to extremes of brightness and shadow. The sky is a pale, almost painful blue. At night, the stars blaze with an intensity rarely seen at lower altitudes.

There is no evidence of anything built, anything made. The remnants of a primitive people are not in structures but in subtle changes to the materials about. Rocks stacked to create windbreaks. Hollows scraped into the earth lined with skins. Paths worn faint over generations, barely discernible but there, if you know where to look. Tools of worked stone are the only indication of an intelligence shaping the landscape, a slow-motion dance with the harsh realities of this place.

And the strangest thing of all is the sense of… stasis. The mountains don't change. The seasons shift, but the overall pattern remains the same: cold, windy, and damp. The erosion continues, but at a glacial pace. The animal populations fluctuate, but within a narrow band. The people… perhaps they have changed over time. But their mark on the land is so light, so integrated, that it is nearly impossible to tell. It's as if the land holds them in its grasp, molding them to its own unyielding rhythm. The mountains have always been, and they will always be. And everything that lives here is merely a temporary ripple in the face of that eternal, unchanging reality.
