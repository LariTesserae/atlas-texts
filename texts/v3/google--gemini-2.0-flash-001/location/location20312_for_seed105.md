The world is a frigid ocean, black as pitch. No sun pierces the perpetual cloud cover, an endless, roiling expanse that presses down on the water with crushing weight. Rain falls in sheets – not gentle droplets, but thick, viscous curtains that sting the skin and obscure the already limited visibility. The temperature hovers just above freezing, the relentless cold seeping into everything, bone-deep and inescapable.

Yet, impossibly, life thrives.

Here and there the peaks of colossal, petrified trees break the surface. These are not the skeletal remains of a drowned forest; rather, they are stone, mineralized over eons into towering monuments. Their branches, once reaching for a sun that never shone, now resemble skeletal arms, encrusted with luminescent flora of impossible shades. Pale greens, sickly yellows, and bruised purples emit a ghostly light, illuminating the immediate vicinity in a spectral glow. 

This is the "Luminescence," a bio-geological phenomenon unique to this submerged world. Colonies of phosphorescent fungi, lichen, and algae have taken hold on the deadwood. An endless cycle of growth and decay pulses with a strange energy. The light they emit is also a form of sustenance. The water here is thick with a nutrient-rich soup derived from the slow breakdown of the petrified trees, feeding these unusual plants. The louder sounds are the constant rain and the groaning of ancient stone shifting beneath the water's surface. But in between these, there are creaking and rustling noises from immense plants as their branches rub against one another in the ceaseless current.

The depths between these monolithic trees are vast, empty and terrifying. The water pressure is immense, yet even here, strange processes are at work. Bioluminescent vents spew superheated, mineral-laden water into the frigid ocean, creating localized currents and underwater geysers. These vents also contribute to the strange, geological activity that reshapes the landscape over vast timescales. The erosion here is not the gentle wearing-away of wind and wave, but a brutal, relentless process of dissolution and reformation. The stone trees are slowly eaten away, their minerals incorporated into the water, only to be redeposited in bizarre, alien formations near the vents.

Scattered across the tips of the highest stone branches are the remnants of a civilization. The structures themselves are built of the same petrified wood as the trees, blended with a coarse, porous stone. These buildings are small, simple constructions – circular huts with steeply pitched roofs designed to shed the constant rain. The architecture speaks of a people adapted to the constant cold and wet, with a limited mastery of their environment. Weaving is present, and evidence of simple looms can be found in many structures. What little carvings exist have depictions of simple plants and geometric shapes. There isn’t much of an aesthetic. Form follows function.

These people are gone now. Their settlements have long been abandoned, their inhabitants lost to the crushing weight of the deep, or, perhaps, to some unknown factor. The only evidence of their presence is the occasional tool made of sharpened stone, a broken pot, or a piece of woven fabric, all coated in a thick layer of mineral deposits.

There is a palpable sense of activity here, despite the absence of fauna. The Luminescence pulses with a life of its own, expanding and contracting in rhythm with the underwater currents. The vents hiss and bubble, releasing plumes of superheated water that momentarily disrupt the perpetual darkness. And, above all, the rain continues to fall, a constant reminder of the world's hostility and its strange, persistent vitality. The sound blankets everything. The drumming of rain on hard stone echoes endlessly in the blackness.

The world is hostile, alien. But life has taken root here. Algae forms patterns on the stones. Lichen gives the stone color. Without animals present to alter the landscape, this location is the result of a battle between the plants that grow and the water that is the place.
