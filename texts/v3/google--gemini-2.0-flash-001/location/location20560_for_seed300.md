The rusted iron bridge sighs underfoot, each step a muted groan swallowed by the damp air. Not quite silence, but the kind of quiet that presses in on you, a low susurrus of moisture and decay. The light is filtered, overcast, enough to see but never enough to feel warm. A grey that leaches colour from everything, leaving only the oily sheen on puddles and the ochre stains of rust.

This isn't a place for big things. The scale is intimate, a hollow nestled into some forgotten fold of the earth. You could walk its perimeter in an hour, tracing the tangled edges where the bog cedes ground to the skeletal remains of industry.

Everything is wet. Not the clean, rinsing wet of rain, but a clinging, seeping damp. The kind that breeds in shadows and rust. Water gathers in scalloped tiers, and spills over the crumbling brickwork of what might have been a retaining wall. Further on, there is a stagnant pool, choked with lily pads and the iridescent slick of something unpleasant.

Vegetation is a rumour here. Patches of tough, wiry grass struggle to survive between the cracked concrete slabs. Moss thrives, a velvety green shroud clinging to every surface, softening the harsh angles of forgotten machinery. Reeds stand in stark, dark green lines between patches of open water. There are places where the bog seems to be actively reclaiming the land, sending out tendrils of sphagnum moss and swamp grass to envelop the metal and stone.

The air hangs heavy with the metallic tang of decay and the faint, acrid bite of some long-dissipated chemical. The low buildings loom like skeletal guardians, their windows dark and vacant. They are more than scattered, less than a village – the remnants of a small industrial outpost, perhaps a pump station or a filtration plant. One or two still stand, partially intact with rusted corrugated iron shells, but most exist only as foundations now, overtaken by the encroaching bog.

The technology here feels blunt, brutal. Thick pipes snake across the landscape, disappearing into the morass. The hulks of machinery sit half-submerged, their purpose obscured by rust and decay. The remains of pumps squat crookedly, their impellers frozen in place. Some of the machinery hints at an attempt to leech something from the ground, or from the water, but the site is completely quiet. The pumps are still, the electricity lines have long ago broken, and the few buildings open to the sky. Birds sometimes use the shells for high, sheltered nests.

It is in the stillness that you sense the turbulence. Not a visible chaos, but an undercurrent of entropy. The ceaseless drip of water, the slow crumble of brick, the patient advance of the bog. The place simmers with a low, constant churn. There is an *active* destruction, without any corresponding creation. It is something that takes and takes and takes.

You hear, sometimes, the clatter and groans and clicks of long-destroyed equipment. It might be the birds or the wind in the ruined shells, or it might be coming from somewhere else.

There are objects here, though it is hard to distinguish them from the detritus of the industrial remnants. A glint of broken glass or corroded steel sitting in the wet soil. A rubber boot filled with stagnant water. A ceramic object among the moss, like a gear or capacitor housing. Small pieces of the older life of this place, lost forever in the decay, and indistinguishable from it.

There are supposed to be *things* here. One or two local hunters, the ones desperate enough for the lean deer that drink from the tainted water of the bog, all tell stories. They see reflections in the dark pools in the night, or hear groaning, or find mechanical carcasses dragged deeper into the morass. The stories are told by the type of people who are easily dismissed, so the *things* are not widely known — but the hunters know to stay away from the ruins.

There are no paths to speak of, save for the tracks made by the occasional deer, and it is easy to get lost in the morass. It would be simple to find, one day, that you had walked past the same ruined building over and over.

This is a place where the past refuses to die, a place that is constantly shifting, settling, and falling apart.
