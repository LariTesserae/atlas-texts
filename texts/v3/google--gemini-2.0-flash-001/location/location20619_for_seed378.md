The sun is a brutal fact here. It bleeds the sky white and hammers down on the corrugated iron roofs of Pylon City, baking them to temperatures no sane creature would touch. The air itself shimmers with heat, a visible haze that swallows the horizon and makes the distant mesas look like melting candles.

Pylon City clings to the side of one of these mesas, a chaotic jumble of precariously stacked structures. It’s less a planned settlement and more like something that organically erupted from the rock face, a geode cracked open to reveal a mess of metal and concrete crystals. You can see the marks of industry everywhere – the skeletal remains of refineries clinging to the cliff face, rusted pipelines snaking across the landscape, and the ever-present pylons themselves, the city's namesake, thrusting skyward like gigantic, corroded needles. They hum with a low, barely perceptible vibration, a silent song of forgotten energy.

Water is the lifeblood and the paradox of this place. It's everywhere and nowhere. The air is thick with humidity, the kind that clings to your skin and turns your clothes into suffocating weights. Condensation drips constantly from the pylons, gathering in pools on the rooftops and running in rivulets down the rust-streaked walls. But there are no rivers, no lakes, no visible sources. The water is all atmospheric, wrung from the air like sweat from a stone. People collect it in cisterns and ingenious rooftop contraptions, recycling it with a frantic desperation that speaks to its preciousness.

Vegetation is, for all intents and purposes, absent. There are no trees, no grass, no hardy desert shrubs. Instead, the mesas are covered with shimmering black sheets of volcanic glass, remnants of some ancient and terrible eruption. In the heat they give off a dizzying array of distorted images, like looking through broken mirrors. There *are* things that live here though.

The fauna of Pylon City is bizarre and abundant. The most noticeable are the Glasswings – large, insectoid creatures with iridescent wings that catch the sunlight and scatter it in dazzling patterns. They flit between the pylons, feeding on the condensation and the strange, metallic fungi that grow on the rusted surfaces. Then there are the Scuttlers, crab-like scavengers that skitter through the city's shadows, feeding on discarded scraps and the occasional unlucky insect. Their shells are made of a strange, heat-resistant ceramic, scavenged from broken industrial components. And then there are the Ferals, packs of half-wild, genetically modified dogs that roam the city's outskirts, their eyes burning with a predatory intelligence.

The strangest creatures, though, are the ones that live *inside* the pylons themselves. You can sometimes hear them – a faint scratching and clicking from within the corroded metal. Nobody knows exactly what they are, or whether they are a natural inhabitant or another consequence of the region's industrial past. Some say they are the ghosts of the workers who built the pylons, trapped within the metal for eternity. Others say they are something far more alien, drawn to the residual energy that still pulses through the structures.

There is a pervasive sense of decay in Pylon City, a feeling that everything is slowly falling apart. But there is also a strange kind of equilibrium. The city has existed in this state for decades, perhaps centuries. The people who live here have adapted, carved out a life from the harsh environment. They are scavengers, engineers, and survivors, bound together by their shared dependence on the pylons and the strange, vital water that drips from them. They maintain their ramshackle existence with jury-rigged machinery, constantly repairing and repurposing discarded components.

The silence is deceiving. At first, it seems absolute, a stark contrast to the oppressive heat and the constant shimmering of the air. But if you listen closely, you can hear the subtle sounds of survival – the rasping of Scuttlers on metal, the faint hum of the pylons, the creak of rusted hinges, the distant yelping of Ferals. It is the silence of a place that has learned to endure. And in that silence, there is a strange kind of beauty. A beauty born of hardship, resilience, and the enduring human spirit, clinging to life in a world that seems determined to crush it.
