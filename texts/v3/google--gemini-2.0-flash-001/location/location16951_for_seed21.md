Wet heat rises off the violet slates. They are set at angles so steep that a fall would be deadly, but the violet stone is itself porous, perpetually weeping. It's warm to the touch, almost skin-temperature, and faintly slick. Looking closely, you see that each slate is faceted with an intricate, fractal geometry, like the cells of some impossible plant, tilted greedily toward the sun.

This is a high place, a cusp where a slope gives way to a scarp. The violet slates form a narrow terrace, only a few meters deep, from which the land drops abruptly. Below, a mist gathers in the hotter hours, obscuring the valley; above, the slope rises another hundred meters to a jagged, broken ridge. It's an entirely different geology up there – black, fractured basalt that rings like a struck bell when dislodged.

The "lush" vegetation is not what you might expect. It's not dense or overgrown, but rather a precise, almost architectural arrangement of specialized forms. Heat-loving epiphyytes cling to the sweating violet stone – strange, fleshy orchids with metallic blue leaves, and clusters of what look like glass sponges, their intricate internal structure visible in the clear mountain light. Further back, rooted directly in the sparse soil between the slates, are towering, slender stalks of a silver-white material, like spun moonlight. These "light-trees," as the locals call them (though whether that refers to light weight or luminous emanation is unclear), hum softly in the breeze, a high-pitched thrum that borders on inaudible.

The silence is punctuated, occasionally, by the splash of water. Small, perfectly spherical droplets condense on the undersides of the light-trees and fall onto the stones below. The slates are carved with shallow basins that collect this water, miniature pools of shimmering, almost viscous liquid.

These pools are the key to understanding this place. They are, in effect, micro-habitats, miniature, self-contained ecosystems. Within each, a single specimen of some unique, alien lifeform exists. Some are microscopic, visible only as swirling colors in the water; others are larger, gelatinous polyps with iridescent tentacles that extend to catch the falling droplets. The "rare" fauna boils down to this hyper-specialization – each pool is a world to itself, a tiny pocket of evolution isolated from all the others.

The marks of technology are subtle but pervasive. The violet slates themselves are clearly artificial, though their purpose is obscure. Perhaps a means of water collection, or a resonant surface for some unknown energy? The fractal geometry encourages the condensation, and the angle captures maximum sun. The light-trees, too, have a machined quality to them, though they are indisputably alive. It's tempting to see this place as an abandoned experiment, a terraforming project gone awry, or a garden for the gods overgrown, and then utterly slowed.

There is a structure here, a single building, almost invisible against the violet stone. It's a low, rectangular form, built from the same slates, and covered with a dense mat of moss and lichen. There are no windows, only a single, narrow opening, a dark slit that seems to swallow the light. A low, rhythmic pulse emanates from within, a subsonic thrum that vibrates the ground beneath your feet. The only sound above that threshold is the incessant dripping, and the high-pitched hum of the light-trees.

The landscape has shifted very slowly over vast time. There are signs of active geological processes — a delicate, constant settling. It will be different in another thousand years.

The weirdest thing is perhaps the sense of… attention. A feeling that you are being observed, not by any single creature, but by the place itself. The wet violet stone drinks the air, the sparse fauna drinks from it, the thin air stirs the trees, the wind circles round to do it again. The pools reflect the sky, and the sky reflects back. The hum of the light-trees vibrates in your bones. It is a quiet, watchful place. A place that remembers.
