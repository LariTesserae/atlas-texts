The air hangs thick and still, a warm, wet blanket. No sun penetrates the perpetual gloom, but neither is it a complete, suffocating black. A weak, phosphorescent glow emanates from the slick rock faces and the strange, fleshy fungi that sprout from every crevice, providing the only illumination.

This is the Sky-Garden Grotto, a vast, enclosed cavern high in the peaks of the Whispering Teeth mountains. Not vast in the manner of a natural cavern, carved and scraped by wind and water. This place is *grown*. The rock itself seems to sweat and ooze, forming bizarre, organic shapes. Water does indeed weep from every surface, collecting in shallow pools that mirror the faint, bioluminescent world. The pools are dense with life of a sort – single-celled organisms, colonies that pulse with a slow, rhythmic light. They are the base of this strange ecosystem.

Vegetation is sparse in the traditional sense. There are no trees, no grasses, no broad leaves reaching for a sun that never shines. Instead, the walls are carpeted in layers of clinging organisms – strange, pulsing molds, crystalline slimes, and rubbery, polyp-like growths that sway gently in the still air. They are not green, but rather shades of violet, grey, and a disturbing, bruised purple. Their density is overwhelming; it's suffocating. Every inch of exposed surface is colonized.

The elevation doesn’t feel as it should. One could climb steadily, rising through levels carved through the damp layers of earth, but one would never reach a thin, sharp peak. Even the highest points have a spongy softness underfoot, as though the very peaks can flex and bend.

The “erosion” here is not destruction, but genesis. The rock is not worn down, but built up, layer upon layer, by the strange biological processes that dominate the grotto. It's a place of constant, agonizingly slow expansion, an architecture constructed over eons by forces beyond comprehension.

The human scale is dwarfed. The grotto, as a whole, is not a hidden ecosystem, it's an integrated and essential organ of a wider creature. Stepping inside creates the unnerving sense of entering *something*. The scattered ruins reinforce the oddity. A few crumbling archways, a single, moss-covered altar, and fragments of pottery are all that remain of the Sky-Gardeners, the primitive proto-humans who once revered this place. Their technology was simple: clay, bone, woven fiber, and the secrets of coaxing the grotto's natural growth. Their tools are long gone, reclaimed by the all-consuming flora, though the ruins stand as stark reminders of their presence, and the absence of their presence now.

Fauna is rare and unsettling. Giant, blind cave salamanders, their skin translucent and glistening, lurk in the deeper pools. Strange, eyeless insects with chitinous shells crawl through the undergrowth of fungi. There are brief, unhearable movements in the furthest layers of dripping soil and rock. Life exists, but feels like an intrusion.

The air is almost silent. A low, omnipresent hum permeates everything – the combined susurrus of countless billions of microorganisms working in concert. Occasionally, a drip of water breaks the stillness, creating a resonant *plunk* that echoes faintly through the grotto.

Most unsettling of all is the stasis. The Sky-Garden Grotto exists outside the flow of time. Seasons have no meaning here, nor does the changing of the tides, or the phases of the moon, as if the mountain ranges are not subject to the laws of existence – merely an idea given shape. The processes are not fast. Slow and steady, constant application of minute influence. All the elements are in perfect, terrifying equilibrium. Generations of Sky-Gardeners lived and died within its embrace, and nothing changed. The grotto existed before them, and it will exist long after they are gone, an unchanging monument to the patient, relentless power of a hidden, wet world.
