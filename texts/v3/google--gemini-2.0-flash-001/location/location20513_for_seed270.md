The air is thick with a mineral tang and a low, incessant hammering. The light, though normal in spectrum, struggles to penetrate the near-constant dust clouds, casting long, distorted shadows that dance with the jerky movements of shimmering, chitinous forms.

We are deep within a geode – or something like a geode. An air pocket, perhaps a few kilometers across, embedded within a vast, cold iron-nickel asteroid. The inner surface is not crystalline in the way you might expect. Instead, the 'walls' are a dense, layered mass of compressed metal-oxide, riddled with veins of something that resembles slagged glass. These formations stretch and fold like the intestines of some colossal beast, polished smooth by unimaginable aeons of grinding pressure and chemical reaction. The elevation is uniformly low, the pocket shaped like a shallow dish.

Erosion has played its part, but not in the way water carves canyons or wind sculpts dunes. Here, the relentless forces are time itself, and the rasping, gnawing mouths of the fauna. The already-dense landscape is further crowded by the sheer numbers of these creatures. They are insects, mostly. Or what we would call insects, if they were operating on familiar biology. Metallic carapaces gleam dull red and black. Some are the size of beetles, others vast, lumbering things the size of small cars, their legs scrabbling at the oxide surface. They graze on the "glass," or mine seams of a glittery ore.

The sound is overwhelming. A cacophony of clicks, whirs, and the constant grinding of metal on metal. There is a strange, rhythmic thrumming that seems to vibrate up through the ground, a deep engine-pulse that speaks of industry on a bizarre, alien scale. Scattered across the landscape are the remains of that industry. Not factories in the traditional sense, but immense, fractured exoskeletons stripped of their parts, or weird drilling rigs that have long fallen silent, slowly being consumed by the living metal of the asteroid.

The active processes here are manifold. These insects are not merely animals, but components in a vast metabolic process. They break down the oxide, refine the ore, excrete… something. A viscous, iridescent fluid that pools in shallow depressions and supports a web of fungal-or-simpler life — think oily slime molds threaded with metal filaments. The thrumming is the pulse of pumps, buried deep within the asteroid body. They cycle air and… other things. Nutrients? Waste? An atmosphere-analog? The point is that the landscape isn't in balance so much as continuous churn.

The scattering of structures are not built *by* the insects, but are instead exoskeletal ruins, or tools too-large for the current population. This implies a former civilization here, of beings even larger and more industrially adept than these metallic insects, which now scavenge and recycle the remnants. It’s impossible to tell what destroyed them whether mechanical failure, resource depletion, biological collapse, or some internal schism. The only constant is the ever-munching insects, and their endless industry.

The light draws your eye to a single, unbroken structure. A lone spire, far taller than anything else in the pocket. It is covered in a thick coat of something that might be lichen, or could be the insects themselves, clustered so densely that they appear to be part of the structure. If it still serves a purpose, it does not communicate its function. It stands alone, a silent monument to the past of this teeming, grinding world.
