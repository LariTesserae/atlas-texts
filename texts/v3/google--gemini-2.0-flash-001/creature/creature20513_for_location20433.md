The children of this place are etched from the same stone as the mountains themselves. They do not roar, they do not rage, they *endure*. Imagine them, not as conquerors or tamers, but as another facet of the mountain's slow becoming. Their skin is weathered like the rock, faces creased not just with age but with constant negotiation with the wind.

They are not tall, nor broad. Frailty would not survive here. Instead, they are compact, dense, built for conserving what little warmth they can muster. Think of a marmot, or a ptarmigan, animals that huddle against the cold's indifference. Their eyes, though, are anything but dull. They are the color of glacial ice, the blue that swallows light, and they hold an unnerving intensity. They've seen the sun strip the land bare, have watched avalanches reshape entire valleys, have known hunger that gnaws at the bone, and they have survived.

They are sheathed in hides, pieced together from the small creatures they hunt. The colors are muted – whites and greys, speckled browns – camouflage perfected over generations. Their movements are deliberate, almost ritualistic. Every step is considered, every action a calculation of energy expended versus benefit gained. Nothing is wasted. Not breath, nor muscle, nor the discarded bones of a kill.

Their dwellings are whispers against the immensity of the mountains. The stone structures you saw are their seasonal camps, places where they take refuge from the worst of the storms. They are not places of comfort, but of survival. Low to the ground, nestled against boulders, they offer meager protection. The flickering light of a meagre fire dances on the rough-hewn walls.

The obsidian flakes and tools are everything. Knowledge passed down through generations. Know-how etched into the collective memory: the precise angle to strike a piece of flint, the best location to find shelter during a blizzard, the migratory paths of the few hardy animals that dare venture this high. The tools aren't just instruments of survival; they are extensions of their bodies, conduits to the mountain's essence.

They are not a numerous people. The land cannot sustain a large population. Perhaps a handful of families, scattered across the mountains and valleys, moving with the seasons. They are bound together by necessity, and by a shared understanding of the mountain's capricious nature. When one falls, it's a blow to everyone. Funerals are practical affairs. Bodies returned to the ice, becoming part of the landscape once more, bones ground to dust between glaciers and stone.

There is a spirituality, though it is not expressed in grand temples or elaborate ceremonies. It is woven into the fabric of their daily lives. Respect for the mountain. An understanding of their place within its grand design. The wind is a messenger, the meltwater a blessing, every animal killed a sacrifice. The lichen, so slow to grow, is sacred.

They are not gone. They have become the silence itself. The wind carries their whispers. The stones hold their memories. They are the ghosts of survival, imprinted on the landscape, an echo of resilience in the face of overwhelming odds. They are the children of the mountain, and the mountain remembers them. They are in the ice, in the stone, and in every fragile bloom that dares to push forth from the unforgiving ground. They are a part of that slow, persistent becoming.
