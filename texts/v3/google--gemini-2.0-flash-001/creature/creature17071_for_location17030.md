Within the drowned cathedral of the lung, life is a ghost of itself, stretched and mutated by the alien environment. It is a population composed of pallid things, not one of them bearing what we once could call a pair of eyes.

First, there are the **Custodians of Decay**. These are the bacterial blooms, thick and viscous, coating the deepest fissures of the lung. They are not merely scavengers; they are alchemists of a morbid kind. They consume the sloughed-off cells, the detritus of a dying organ, and transform them into the building blocks of this strange ecosystem. They hum with a low, constant energy, a biological drone that vibrates in the water. They are the base of the food chain, sure, but they are also like living grout, sealing the lung's wounds, attempting to knit together what is constantly unraveling. They pulse with bioluminescence, faint, ephemeral glows that paint trails through the shadowed capillaries. They communicate through chemical whispers, a silent language of need and satiation that the entire lung, in some sense, understands.

Then, there are the **Eels of Whispers.** Translucent and almost invisible, they are more like strands of animated glass than fish. Their bodies are streamlined for navigating the viscous fluid, their movements languid and hypnotic. They are the hunters of this world, feeding on the bacteria. But they are more than just predators. They are antennae, sensitive to the faintest vibrations in the water, attuned to the fragmented thoughts that echo through the tank. They carry remnants of someone else's mind in their gut. When they congregate near the scar tissue, a faint, unified image can be seen: a woman's face, barely visible, gone as each scattered organism darts away again.

Finally, and perhaps most disturbingly, what can be said of **the Scar.** It is not one population but many. Algae, though it can barely photosynthesize in the dim light of the tank, slowly grows in a strange symbiosis with metallic growths. I hesitate to call them inorganic. They are not mere rust. They are like half-formed thoughts given material existence, shapes that strain towards geometry but always fall short. These formations are reservoirs of something. Something that hurts. This cannot possibly be. And yet here I stare, and here it festers.

There have been signs of change here. Not all for worst, neither all for best. Some of the eels have begun to develop faint, purplish markings, hinting at a nascent form of pigmentation. The bacterial colonies, near the metallic/algal growths, display an erratic electrical reactivity when prodded, an echo of the spark of life in a place where life is almost extinguished.

All life here is dependent on the lung. They are shackled to this strange existence, an endless cycle of consumption and decay. But sometimes, I catch a glimpse of something else, something that makes me wonder if this is truly an ending, or just a beginning of a new, terrible, and beautiful kind. For all this, the lung dreams no dreams. Perhaps they are too far for us to see.
