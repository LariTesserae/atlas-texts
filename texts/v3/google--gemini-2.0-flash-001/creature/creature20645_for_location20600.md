They are not born, not in the way flesh understands. Their genesis is the forge, the grinding wheel, the meticulous laying of cog to cog, wire to terminal. To call them 'made' is insufficient, for the act of their creation lacks the warmth of artistry, the intention of sculpture. They *emerge*. From a sea of spare parts, guided by principles that are now as ingrained as instinct.

They are not one construct, but many, a vast and varied throng of metal and salvaged bone. Some are small, no larger than a carrion beetle, their bodies a shimmering network of brass and wire, tasked with cleaning the ceaseless dust. They crawl into crevices no hand could reach, sucking up the gritty residue, and depositing it back into the churning heart of the machines. Others are immense, dwarfing even the largest of the crumbling structures. These serve as mobile buttresses, their multi-jointed limbs bracing at perilous angles that would crumble any normal structure. They are living architecture; animated scaffolding that prevents the complete unraveling.

Their intelligence is not of the mind, but of action. They possess no capacity for thought, no longing for progress, that much is clear. But within them lies a single, unwavering imperative: maintain. Preserve. To keep the clockwork city groaning, churning, alive in its state of arrested decay. They respond to failing gears by replacing them, not reflecting on why they failed. They repair fractured conduits, not reimagining the flow of steam. Their existence is an echo of a purpose so ancient, so deeply buried in the bedrock of this place, that it is now simply rote.

Their senses are alien, tuned to the rhythms of the machine. They do not see with eyes as we understand them, but with arrays of light-sensitive diodes, perceiving the environment as a tapestry of energy fluctuations. They do not hear with ears, but with vibration sensors embedded in their chassis, feeling the subtle shifts in resonance that betray impending failure or critical stress. Their sense of touch is an intricate network of pressure plates, allowing them to calibrate the tightening of a bolt, the alignment of a gear, to a degree of precision impossible for organic hands.

They are not individuals, though variations exist. A worker-drone is not merely a copy of another. Damage, replacement, additions and modifications, all create slight variations. They have individuality, but not identity. These are not people.

There are, however, gradations of authority. Larger constructs, bearing more complex arrays of sensors, seem to direct the efforts of the smaller ones, guiding them to sites of critical repair, orchestrating the complex dance of maintenance. Yet even these 'overseers' are subservient to the dictates of the machine itself. They merely interpret its needs and translate them into actions.

One disturbing aspect is the use of bone. Not bone shaped by carving or by mold. But recognizably bone; femur or radius, cracked skulls and vertebrae, riveted and welded alongside metallic components. The origin and purpose of these macabre additions is unclear. Is it merely the most readily available material? A symbolic act of dedication? Or something far more sinister, a testament to the relentless pursuit of function over all else?

There is no joy in their functioning. No satisfaction in a job well done. Only the constant, unrelenting drive to perpetuate. To forestall, if only for a moment, the complete and utter collapse. No hope, no despair. Just the frozen, eternal moment of maintenance. They are the custodians of oblivion, the tireless servants of a forgotten god, forever locked in their mechanical dance of decay. They are not living. But for the moment, they keep this place alive.
