The people of the Shallows mirror their environment. They are etched with the same relentless grime that coats the factories and the same quiet resilience as the weeds pushing through the cracks. Their faces, often obscured by scarves or makeshift masks to ward off the worst of the fumes, are a palette of muted tones – ash-grey skin, eyes the color of rusted iron, hair like strands of tarnished copper.

They move with a sort of fluid economy, their bodies adapted to the uneven terrain and the low-slung dangers of the factories. Years of navigating overflowing streams of runoff and squeezing through shadowed alleyways have honed their movements, lending them a distinctive way of being in the world. Their clothes are layers of salvaged fabric and repurposed industrial materials, stitched and patched together with an almost artistic disregard for fashion. Function is paramount; pockets bulge with tools, scraps of useful metal, and scavenged edibles.

The children are perhaps the most striking. Despite the bleak surroundings, a spark of defiant energy flickers in their eyes. They are nimble and ingenious, transforming the city's detritus into toys and playgrounds. They possess an almost preternatural understanding of the Shallows' hidden pathways and dangers, navigating the treacherous landscape with the casual confidence of seasoned explorers. They speak their own language, a slang interwoven with technical jargon and the cadences of the factories, a code that keeps them communicating and connected.

The adults wear a more worn version of this vitality. Their bodies are marked by the Shallows, etched with scars from industrial accidents and darkened by sun. They carry themselves with a stoicism born of necessity, a quiet acceptance of the harsh realities that define their existence. They are mechanics, scavengers, recyclers, and re-purposers, masters of adapting and surviving in a world where nothing is wasted. There is a deep practical wisdom etched onto their faces, and a certain pride in their ability to coax life and value out of the discarded remains of industry.

Family units are fluid and adaptable. A child might have a dozen "aunts" and "uncles" – fellow workers who have taken them under their wing, older residents who offer guidance and support. This collective spirit is a crucial survival mechanism, a bulwark against the constant challenges of life in the Shallows.

There is an unusual connection that these people share, a quiet understanding that transcends words. They trade glances, nods, and small acts of kindness which keep them together in a way no rules are needed for. The families of the Shallows have come to recognize change in the city as a sign of more rot, and are thusly resistant to it. This keeps them close-knit, resilient, and skeptical of outsiders. They know they are the only ones who can care for themselves, and they greet the future without a single doubt that they will.
