They are the color of wet earth, of roots slick with rain, of the shadowed spaces beneath the vast fungal blooms. They are the children of the green, though children is too simple a word for beings so entwined with the life around them. Picture something akin to primates, perhaps, but whose skin is less skin than living bark, constantly shedding tiny spores that drift on the perpetually damp air, adding to the ever-present haze.

Their eyes are large and luminous, reflecting the soft blue glow of the fungi in the dark undergrowth. They do not need fire because they are already illuminated from within, strands of bioluminescence woven into their blood, visible as faint tracery beneath their bark-like skin. They communicate through subtle shifts in these patterns, a language of light and shadow that ripples across their bodies, understood only by those born into the green.

They move with a quiet grace, their limbs long and flexible, perfect for navigating the tangled vines and overgrown structures. Their feet are wide and padded, giving them purchase on the slick, moss-covered surfaces. They leave no trails, disturbing the undergrowth so lightly that it springs back into place behind them.

They are the tenders of the garden, though not in any active sense. They do not plant or prune, but rather observe and maintain the delicate balance of the ecosystem. They know where the rare and vital fungi grow, which vines must be redirected to support a collapsing bank, which creatures are falling out of harmony and need subtle guidance.

Their diet is simple: fruits and nuts that fall from the trees, fungi cultivated in hidden groves, the occasional insect or amphibian caught with surprising speed. They carry no tools, for the forest provides everything they need. They drink the dew that collects on the leaves, and shelter in the hollow trunks of the ancient trees.

They do not grieve for the dead, nor celebrate births, for they see life and death as two sides of the same coin. When one of them dies, their body is simply returned to the earth, where it quickly decomposes and becomes part of the life cycle anew. Their bones are riddled with pores and fungal networks; in death, the individual becomes a new host and anchor point for the endless green.

They have no leaders, no hierarchy, no concept of ownership. Decisions are made through consensus, a weaving together of individual understandings into a collective wisdom. They listen to the forest, feel its rhythms, and act accordingly.

The overgrown stone structures are not relics of their past, but remnants of a civilization they never knew, a story they only half understand. They treat them with a reverence born not of understanding, but of respect for all forms of life, even those that no longer exist. They have co-opted these remains as nesting sites, fungal farms, and nurseries.

They are custodians, not owners. They are the silent watchers, the gentle guides, the living embodiment of the ever-growing green. They are the whisper in the leaves, the soft glow in the darkness, the heart of the continent-spanning garden. They and it are one.
