They are the inheritors of pressure, the children of slow erosion. Picture them not as individuals in the way that surface dwellers conceive of individuality, but as nodes in a vast, distributed network. Each a cell in the colossal body of this deep-sea matrix.

Their forms are mutable, dictated by the currents and the chemical soup they inhabit. Some are elongated, worm-like, designed for burrowing into the hummocks, leaving behind trails of digested minerals and excreted… something. These are the miners, the terraformers, unknowingly reshaping the landscape with each slow, deliberate movement. Their "skin" is less skin and more a porous membrane, drawing sustenance directly from the surrounding water. They are pale, almost translucent, their internal organs visible as faint, pulsating glows. They have no eyes, no need for them in this world of perpetual darkness. Instead, they navigate by sensing the subtle vibrations in the water, the chemical gradients that mark the paths of others, a symphony of pressure and flow that is their language.

Others are more sedentary, filter feeders that resemble living sponges; vast colonies of single-celled organisms woven together into intricate, branching structures that cling to the hummocks. They are masters of surface area, maximizing their exposure to the nutrient-rich water. Their bodies are encrusted with a layer of the black mold-vegetation, a symbiotic relationship that provides them with a source of energy. They pulse rhythmically, drawing water in through microscopic pores and expelling the filtered waste in a slow, deliberate exhalation. They are the anchors of this ecosystem, the bedrock upon which all other life depends.

And then there are the floaters, the jellyfish-like creatures that drift through the water column like living balloons. Their bodies are composed of the same semi-permeable membrane that is used to create the breathing apparatus of their tool-using cousins. They are filled with a lighter-than-water fluid, allowing them to maintain their position in the water column with minimal effort. They feed on the microscopic organisms that drift by, their tentacles trailing behind them like living nets. They are blind and deaf, but they are exquisitely sensitive to changes in pressure, allowing them to detect the approach of predators or the movement of currents.

And there are others even stranger, forming symbiotic relationships that blur the lines between individual and environment. Barnacle-like organisms affixed to the miner’s underbelly, their exoskeletons grown together to form shields; a single, slow creature that filters nutrients as the miner plods through the deep. A vine-like organism creeping along a floater’s tentacles, reaching almost prehensile tips toward passing detritus; a living trap that also moves.

Finally, there are the tool-users, the apex "predators" of this strange ecosystem; though predation, in the conventional sense, is almost nonexistent. These are the creatures who are capable of modifying their environment, of creating tools from the raw materials around them. They are taller than most of the other inhabitants, standing perhaps a meter or more in height. They are bipedal, though their legs are more like elongated fins, suited for navigating the viscous water. Their hands are long and slender, with delicate fingers that are capable of manipulating objects with surprising dexterity. The tools are crude, made from scavenged shells and woven strands of kelp-like vegetation, but they are enough to allow these creatures to carve out a niche in this unforgiving world. They scrape the black mold from the hummocks, processing it with shell-scrapers to create a paste that provides them with sustenance. The paste is then stored in bladders made from the bodies of the floater-jellies for transport.

Like all their brethren, the tool-users are blind and deaf, but they have developed a sophisticated sense of touch, allowing them to map their environment and communicate with each other through a language of subtle vibrations.

But mostly, it's the *potential* for life here that is unique. These creatures have found ways to thrive, but new forms can emerge from the primordial soup. Sometimes, too much biomass coagulates, and other symbiotic creatures make use of it; or just as easily, it is reabsorbed. Such is the flow here, where the living can be the dead, and the dead reborn, an ancient, churning nursery that holds an indescribable potential. These are beings of the slow burn; the deep time dwellers who might not even notice the passage of centuries, their lives interwoven with the very fabric of the deep.
