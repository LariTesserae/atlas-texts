The people of the high plateau are carved from the same granite as their homes. They are not tall – the wind would have snapped the tall ones long ago – but they are broad, with dense bones and thick hands, each finger a blunt tool shaped by constant, patient labor. Their skin is the color of wet ash, permanently etched with fine lines that map the relentless passage of wind and cold.

Their eyes are their most striking feature. Deep-set and pale, almost silver, they hold a disconcerting stillness, as if they have seen too much or perhaps seen nothing at all for very long. You won’t catch them flitting, scanning, or betraying any of the restless anxieties that plague dwellers of the lowlands. They see what is necessary, and nothing more. They look outward rarely, and inward often.

They dress in layers of woven grasses and cured hides, dyed with lichen and minerals to blend seamlessly with the landscape. The clothes are not beautiful, and they are made more for function than form, but they are durable and tell a story of patient labor. Each stitch represents a moment wrested from the grinding demands of survival. They walk, too, with a peculiar gait, a kind of shuffling lope that conserves energy and minimises the impact on the rough terrain. It’s a gait that seems to hum with the same low frequency as the wind.

They speak little. Their language is not one of elaborate poetry and soaring rhetoric. It consists of clipped, guttural sounds, barely louder than the wind, used only when absolutely necessary. A grunt, a sigh, a hiss – each sound is laden with meaning, honed by centuries of scarcity. Silence is their truest language. A silence that understands the weight of a mountain, the patience of stone, the futility of complaint.

Their homes are more than just shelters; they are extensions of themselves. Every stone laid precisely, every corner angled to deflect the wind, every crack meticulously sealed to keep out the damp. And they show a love for practicality that is not quite utilitarian. Each home is imbued with its own history and a deep sense of the individual lives that inhabit them.

Children are born infrequently, and their survival is a miracle in itself. Those that endure are brought up swiftly. There is no time for frivolity or idle games. The young learn by doing, mirroring the movements of their elders, absorbing the knowledge embedded in every task. They learn to read the sky for signs of change, to coax sustenance from the barren land, to listen to the wind for secrets.

They are not inventors, not in the typical sense. They have no grand ambitions for technological progress. Their innovations are incremental, subtle refinements to ancient techniques, born of necessity and informed by generations of accumulated wisdom. A slightly sharper edge on a stone blade, a more efficient arrangement of stones in a water channel, a new weave that sheds the dampness more effectively – these are the achievements that matter.

They have rituals, of course, but their rituals are not marked by elaborate displays or ecstatic pronouncements. They are quiet, internal affairs, conducted in the deep silence of their homes or amongst the ancient stones of the plateau. They are acts of reverence to the land, to the wind, to the enduring spirit of their ancestors.

Most of their beliefs are centered around maintaining balance, not only with the environment, but also with themselves. They believe in the power of equilibrium, the importance of resisting change, the wisdom of acceptance. Their gods are not beings of fire and fury, but forces of nature, indifferent and eternal.

There is a sense of profound weariness that permeates their existence, a deep-seated understanding of the futility of striving against the inevitable. The very landscape, the weather, their bodies themselves, and the long history of this plateau has taught them to accept and endure. And yet, there is also a quiet strength, a resilience born of necessity, a determination to survive against all odds. They are the children of the stone, the whisperers of the wind, the keepers of a silent, enduring world. They are the embodiment of the plateau: weathered, patient, and profoundly, achingly, *there*.
