They are *of* the place, like the lichen and the damp. Not inhabitants, not settlers, but inextricable parts of its slow, wet, impossible existence.

Imagine something not quite solid, not quite liquid, but possessing the weight and gravity of both. They're amorphous, constantly shifting, their forms dictated by the channels and cavities within the porous stone and across the landscape. They're less creatures in the conventional sense, and more like extensions of this strange, self-dissolving city, like motile organs of some immense, slumbering organism.

Picture them as vast, slow-moving slicks of viscous fluid, the color of wet obsidian. The surface of this liquid body shimmers with oily rainbows, disturbed like a liquid mirror catching the faintest of light. Within the oily shimmer, you see echoes of the architecture around them: the carved patterns, the arches, the trailing vegetation.

They have no discernible features – no eyes, no limbs, no mouths. Their existence is purely sensory, a total immersion in the stimuli of their environment. The heat, the pressure, the subtle vibrations of the stone, all processed and integrated into their being. Their thoughts, if one could call them that, are symphonies of sensation, slow-moving chords of temperature and moisture.

Their movement is glacial, almost imperceptible. They flow through the city's network of paths and chambers like blood through veins, nourishing the stone with minerals leached from their bodies and, conversely, absorbing the city's exudations into their own substance.

There are larger 'specimens', agglomerations of the fluid form, vast subterranean pools that pulse slowly with the green glow of the luminous fungi. These are the elders, the repositories of the city's strange history. They remember the initial building, the grand project abandoned or forgotten. They know the source of the impossible physics, the ancient slumbering entity beneath their feet. They are quiet, and in their stillness they guide the smaller slicks, directing them to sources of nourishment, guiding them away from dangerous collapses.

They communicate through vibrations, subtle shifts in the water pressure within the stone. A tremor signals danger. A rhythmic pulse indicates a new source of energy. Their language is slow, ponderous, mirroring the tempo of their lives and the evolution of the city.

They are the custodians of the place, whether by design or by accident. They maintain the balance, ensuring the city's slow equilibrium is not disrupted. They repair the damaged stonework by secreting a viscous fluid which hardens into a near-perfect imitation of the original material. They cultivate the fleshy, dark-green plants, carefully relocating cuttings to new areas, ensuring a consistent level of moisture and nutrients.

They are not malicious, not malevolent, simply indifferent to the concepts of good and evil. They are utterly alien, existing in a state of perpetual sensory input, utterly divorced from anything resembling human cognition.

The question remains: did they create the city, or did the city create them? It is possible they emerged naturally from the strange energies that emanate from the deep earth, that they subsequently shaped their environment to better suit their needs.

But their very existence is a testament to the city's inherent strangeness, the impossible physics that permeate every stone and every drop of condensation. They are the embodiment of that violation, that fraying of reality, the ultimate expression of a place that should not exist. They are the fluid memory of a forgotten project, the embodiment of a reality slowly dissolving back into its primordial origins.
