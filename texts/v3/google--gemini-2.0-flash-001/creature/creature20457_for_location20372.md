The Troglodytes of the Miresing are born of the damp, and to the damp they return. Before ever I saw one, I smelled them – a deep, earthy reek, like upturned soil mixed with the tang of stagnant water, a musk that clings to the back of the throat and settles in the lungs. They are not beautiful creatures, not in any way that a creature of sunlight and dry lands could understand. But they are *right*. Utterly and completely right for this place.

Imagine a sculpture carved from mud, given life but left rough and unrefined. Skin the color of wet slate, perpetually slick with a sheen of algae and slime. It's difficult to tell where their skin ends and the Miresing begins. They are dressed, if one can call it that, in the discarded garments of the place—bits of woven reeds, draped moss, the tanned hides of the smaller creatures that fall prey to their crude weapons. These coverings are never removed, never cleaned, becoming one with their very flesh.

Their limbs are short and bowed, their torsos hunched. Perhaps it is the weight of the damp air, pressing them down. Perhaps it is simply that they have no need to stand tall in this world of tangled roots and close horizons. Their hands are surprisingly deft, capable of intricate weaving and the delicate manipulation of their stone tools. Their feet are broad and splayed, leaving faint, webbed prints in the mud — a constant renegotiation with the sucking earth.

But it is their faces that linger in minds not used to them. Eyes, dark and deep-set, reflect little light. They seem to see more in the darkness than any creature of the surface world could imagine. A broad, flat nose, perfectly adapted for smelling the subtle shifts in the air, the changing tides of the fungal blooms, the passage of prey. Their mouths are wide, almost froglike, and filled with teeth worn down to nubbins from a diet of roots, insects, and the occasional small animal. A perpetually wet, open-mouthed grin would seem inviting, were it not for the utterly alien intelligence behind it.

They move with a liquid grace that belies their ungainly appearance. One moment, they are a part of the landscape, indistinguishable from a rotting log or a moss-covered stone. The next, they flow into motion, disappearing into the undergrowth with startling speed. There is a patience to their movements, a sense of waiting, of listening to the slow pulse of the Miresing. When they hunt, they become the very essence of ambush, luring creatures into ever-shifting bogs that become unmarked graves.

Their language is not of words, not exactly. It's a complex tapestry of clicks, whistles, and guttural croaks, a mimicry of the Miresing itself. To the uninitiated, it sounds like the dripping of water, the rustling of leaves, the croaking of frogs – yet somehow, within this sonic camouflage, they communicate complex ideas, warnings, and stories. Their calls echo through the valley, blending seamlessly with the ambient sounds, making it almost impossible to pinpoint their location.

They build nothing, create nothing that will last. Their mark on the land is ephemeral, a slight disturbance in the moss, a scattering of bones, a subtle shift in the flow of water. They take only what they need, and they return everything to the Miresing in the end, their bodies becoming fertilizer for the next generation of fungi and insects. Their relationship with the land is one of complete integration, a seamless merging of organism and environment.

They are the children of the Miresing, and the Miresing is their mother. They are the embodiment of its damp, green, teeming life. And in their strangeness, in their utter alienness, lies a profound beauty, a testament to the boundless creativity of nature. They are what the Miresing dreams.
