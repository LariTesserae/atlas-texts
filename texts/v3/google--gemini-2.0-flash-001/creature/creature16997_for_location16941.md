The Heat-Keepers are born already smudged with soot, their lungs accustomed to the sulfurous tang. They are as much a part of this landscape as the crystalline growths and the belching chimneys, their lives inextricably woven into the fabric of the heat.

Their skin is tough, leathery, tanned almost black by the constant exposure to heat and smoke. Lines are etched deep into their faces, not just by age, but by the strain of endless labor and the glare of the furnace fires. Their eyes, however, are startlingly bright, reflecting the crimson glow with an almost unsettling intensity. It is a luminescence born of a lifetime staring into the heart of the earth, a knowledge that flickers like the flames themselves.

They are not tall, their bodies lean and wiry, honed by constant exertion. Their hands are scarred and calloused, capable of moving with surprising dexterity despite their rough appearance. Every movement is economical, precise, born of years of working in the cramped, dangerous confines of the tunnels and workshops. Strength isn't about brute force, but the ability to withstand constant pressure.

Clothing is utilitarian to a fault. Dark, fire-resistant cloth is layered and patched, reinforced with salvaged metal plates scavenged from the ruins. Helmets are fashioned from similar materials, protecting their heads from falling debris and rogue sparks. Goggles, stained with soot and grease, shield their eyes from the glare. Yet, even in their drab work attire, there are subtle displays of individuality: a carefully etched pattern on a metal plate, a particular arrangement of scavenged gears that serve a ritual purpose.

Age is not marked in years, but in the depth of one’s knowledge of the heat. The elders, those who can "read" the whispers of the furnace with uncanny accuracy, hold positions of respect. Their hands may tremble with age, but their eyes hold the accumulated wisdom of generations of Heat-Keepers. They are the keepers of the rites, the interpreters of the fiery beast that dwells within the earth.

The young are initiated into the mysteries of the heat as soon as they can walk. They learn to identify the different ores by touch and smell, to distinguish the subtle shifts in the furnace's roar, to anticipate the earth’s tremors. Play is practical: stacking slag-bricks, dismantling broken machinery, experimenting with different alloys. Their games are a form of apprenticeship, preparing them for their inevitable role in the city's survival.

Their language is terse, efficient, peppered with technical jargon and coded references to the rituals that appease the heat. Silence is valued; unnecessary words are seen as a waste of precious energy. Communication often takes the form of gestures, a complex language of hands that allows them to understand each other above the din of the forges.

Food is secondary. Cultivated crimson blooms are a vital source of sustenance, their fleshy petals yielding a surprisingly nutritious juice. It is bitter on the tongue, but rich in the minerals necessary to survive in this harsh environment. The taste of the blossoms is a constant reminder of living inside a wound.

Their gods are not of the sky, but of the earth. They worship the volatile spirit that burns within the caldera, believing it to be a sentient force that demands constant appeasement. Rituals are performed around the furnaces, offerings of minerals and crafted goods thrown into the fiery maw. Songs are chanted in a guttural tongue, their rhythms echoing the pulse of the molten heart. Sacrifice is not readily given, but known to be eventually given when the heat demands.

They are a resilient, resourceful people, forged in the crucible of the earth. They understand that their survival depends on their ability to adapt and persevere. They are the Heat-Keepers, the stewards of this molten world, and they will continue to tend to the furnace until the heat finally consumes them all.

They do not leave. No one is born to this place by accident, and no one leaves it. They are a necessary part of the function of the place itself. The machines, when broken and repaired by the Heat-Keepers, work differently than they were designed to, adapting to the needs of the city. Likewise, the bodies of the Heat-Keepers have adapted to the city. The city could not exist how it is without them.
