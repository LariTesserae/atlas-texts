They are children of the stone and the sighing wind. Imagine faces etched with the same grit that clings to the mountain face, weathered by a sun that scorches and a wind that chills to the very marrow. They are not beautiful in any conventional sense; beauty is too fragile a thing for Orok-Sul. Instead, they possess a raw, resilient kind of comeliness, a strength in their jawlines, a keenness in their grey eyes that mirrors the sharp edges of their home.

Their bodies are lean and wiry, conditioned by endless climbing and the constant struggle against the elements. Scars are common, a roadmap of survival etched onto their skin – the jagged white lines of rockfalls, the circular burns from misplaced iron, the intricate patterns left by the bite of chitinous insects. They move with a quiet, almost feline grace, placing their feet with a surefootedness that defies the treacherous terrain. They know the feel of the stone beneath their boots better than they know their own names. The constant presence of wind and scree has honed an almost supernatural ability to balance.

Their hands are their most valuable tools – calloused and strong, capable of coaxing life from the unyielding rock. They are builders, first and foremost. They understand the language of stone, know how to coax it, shape it, and bind it to their will. These hands can wield a hammer with brutal efficiency, and also weave the delicate silk of giant spiders into surprisingly strong fabrics. The weaving and the building are so intertwined that one person might handle both tasks on any given day.

They dress in layers of roughspun wool, dyed in muted earth tones that blend seamlessly with the surrounding rock. Spider silk is woven into the fabric for added strength and insulation. Furs from the mountain sheep, or the bizarre indigenous fauna, provide an extra layer of protection against the biting cold. They are utilitarian, their clothes built to last, to withstand the constant abuse of the mountain. Many garments are held together with ingenious combinations of knots and toggles, displaying an inventiveness born of necessity.

Their faces are often masked against the perpetual wind and the stinging scree. These masks are not elaborate or decorative, but simple affairs crafted from leather or woven spider silk, offering a modicum of protection and anonymity in the crowded city. The ability to disappear into the flow of Orok-Sul is a valuable skill, allowing them to observe, to listen, to remain unseen when necessary.

Their eyes hold a depth of knowledge that belies their primitive technology. They understand the subtle shifts in weather patterns, the warning signs of impending rockfalls, the migratory routes of the mountain creatures. They possess an intuitive understanding of the mountain's rhythms. They are attuned to the deep, resonant hum that vibrates through the city, interpreting it not as a mere geological phenomenon, but as a language, a warning, or a blessing from the mountain itself. It is often felt in the bones before it is actually heard, and the elders are particularly keen in discerning its meaning.

They speak in a dialect that grinds and cracks like the very stone of Orok-Sul. It is a language of necessity, concise and direct, stripped of ornamentation. Gestures are equally important, a silent language of hands and eyes that allows them to communicate in the deafening wind or across the echoing distances of the city. Many words reference the textures and formations of the mountain itself.

They are not a joyous people. Life in Orok-Sul is too harsh, too precarious, to allow for excessive merriment. But they are not without a quiet dignity, a stoic acceptance of their fate. They find solace in the rituals that bind them to the mountain, in the shared struggles that forge a deep sense of community. They are not conquerors or explorers. They are simply survivors, clinging to their existence on the back of a sleeping god. And within that simple goal is a profound and humbling strength.

Their eyes are always scanning the horizon. Always evaluating for changes. Always watching the stone.

There is a kind of fatalism built into their culture, a quiet acceptance that the mountain will eventually reclaim all that they have built. This thought pervades every aspect of their existence, from the way they construct their homes to the offerings they make to the mountain. It is a background hum in the song of Orok-Sul. But, until that day comes, they will continue to build, to adapt, to endure.
