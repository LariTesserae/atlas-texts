It is easy to imagine that nothing *lives* here. That the bog and the rust and the slow, patient decay are all that remain. But that isn't true. Life persists, twisted and adapted to the grim reality of this place.

First, there is the Sludge-Singer. It is difficult to classify, for it is not quite plant, nor quite animal, nor anything close to human. Imagine a cluster of reeds, impossibly thick, interwoven with rusted pipes and lengths of discarded cable. Imagine that this cluster *moves*, slowly, inexorably, guided by some internal imperative. The Sludge-Singer draws sustenance from the runoff, filtering the water through its intricate network of organic and inorganic materials. It hums, a low resonant drone that vibrates the very earth around it. The hunters hear these hums and mistake them for the groans of dying machinery. They are not wrong, exactly. The Sludge-Singer *sings* of decay, of the slow grinding down of all things. Its voice is the voice of the bog itself, a lamentation for what was and a celebration of what is becoming.

Then there are the Rust-Eaters. They are small, insectile things, with segmented bodies and iridescent, oil-slicked carapaces. They swarm across the metal surfaces, consuming the rust and converting it into a strange, phosphorescent dust that they use to build their nests. These nests are intricate, geometric structures hidden within the hollows of decaying machinery, glowing with a faint, ethereal light. They are drawn to the noise from the Sludge-Singer. The Rust-Eaters are not malicious, but they are voracious. Anything metal that remains in the bog for too long will be stripped bare by their tireless mandibles.

Deeper, in the pools and the shadowed places, live the Drowners. They are pale, almost translucent amphibians with distended bellies and milky, unseeing eyes. Once, they were frogs, toads, small creatures. Now, they have grown grotesquely large, bloated with the tainted water and the chemicals it contains. Their skin is slick with a toxic slime, and their croaks are deep, guttural rumbles that echo through the ruins. They feed on anything that falls into the water, or on the drowned bodies of the unwary. The reflections in the dark pools are their own, or the bodies of other Drowners as they fight for dominance.

And finally, there is the Weaver. It is perhaps the most disturbing of them all. It dwells within the ruined buildings, amongst the shattered glass and the crumbling brickwork. It is a creature of shadow and dust, a being woven from the detritus of memory. It spins its webs from strands of rusted wire and filaments of moss, creating intricate tapestries that depict scenes from the bog's forgotten past. It whispers to those who get lost in the ruins, luring them deeper with promises of warmth and shelter. Those who follow its voice never return. It drags the mechanical carcasses into the morass to trap the next wanderers, and it clackers the groaning machinery to lure the Weaver's quarry. Nobody knows where the Weaver comes from or what its true purpose is, but the hunters know that it is the most dangerous thing in the bog.

All these things — the Sludge-Singer, the Rust-Eaters, the Drowners, and the Weaver — are bound together by the bog itself. They are the children of its decay, the inheritors of its grim legacy. They live within the constant shift, the constant settling, the constant falling apart. They are the turbulence made manifest, the active destruction given form. They are what remains when all else is gone.
