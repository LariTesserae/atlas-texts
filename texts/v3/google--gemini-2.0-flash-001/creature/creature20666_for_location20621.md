The millipede society is complex. They are the architects, the builders, and somehow, the very keystone of the Cistern's continued existence. To call them merely insects feels wrong. They are beyond that. Their segmented bodies, each plate reflecting the diffused light, are more akin to shifting mosaics than simple chitin.

Consider the way they work. The "mortar" they produce is not merely waste. It is a secretion carefully crafted to bind with the specific minerals of the Cistern's stone. It is an ongoing process, a continuous dialogue between creature and environment. The clicking of their countless legs is not random. It is a form of communication, a language spoken through vibration and subtle shifts in weight, a transmission of blueprints and warnings across the sprawling structure.

The larger, centipede-like forms are a puzzle. If the smaller millipedes are the cells of the Cistern, these could be seen as its nerves or arteries. Their mandibles clicking in rhythm isn't music, not as humans would understand it. It is something more fundamental. It's the pulse that keeps the society functioning, a subtle calibration of resource distribution, the regulation of labor, the orchestration of the Cistern's own internal homeostasis. They are the managers, the foremen, the silent overseers of a project so vast and so slow that it unfolds across generations.

The Takers... they are the rot, the fever, the wound that refuses to heal. They are the embodiment of entropy - a force that, unchecked, will bring everything crashing down. Yet, without them, would there be progress? Would there be evolution? Perhaps their brutal raids, the constant reshaping of territory, are not driven solely by aggression. Perhaps there is a twisted rationale at play: a culling of the weak, a redistribution of resources that, however brutal, ultimately strengthens the colony as a whole. Or perhaps they are simply a tragic mutation, a corruption of the original millipede design, a species condemned to an endless cycle of violence and destruction.

But consider then, what triggered this schism? What force caused the millipede society to splinter? Was it a change in the Cistern's water chemistry? A shift in the balance of power? Or was it something far more subtle: the emergence of ambition, the first stirrings of individual will? The Takers, in their crude, violent way, represent a kind of perverse evolution, a departure from the collective consciousness of their ancestors.

Lastly, there is the question of agency. The millipedes build, the centipedes regulate, the Takers disrupt, but what drives them? Is it pure instinct, a set of pre-programmed behaviors etched into their very being? Or is there something more at work? Does a rudimentary form of consciousness flicker behind those multifaceted eyes? Perhaps, in the rasp of clicking legs and the rhythmic gnawing of mandibles, there is a form of thought, a form of deliberation, a primitive but nascent intelligence struggling to understand its place in the cold, damp heart of the Cistern.
