They are creatures sculpted by necessity, figures carved from desperation and the refuse of a fallen world. The Slag Flats have pruned them, stripping away all that is superfluous, leaving only the barest essence of survival. They are *recipients*, the human consequence of a deliberate environmental catastrophe.

Their bodies are their most immediate tools. Decades, perhaps generations, of exposure have subtly altered them. Their skin, where visible under layers of patched-up gear, is sallow, tinged with green or orange depending on the specific cocktail of toxins they've been exposed to. Their eyes, however, are remarkably sharp, pupils dilated to suck in every photon of the dim, polluted light. Some develop a disturbing nictitating membrane, a thin inner eyelid that protects them from the corrosive air and the spray of the Ponds.

Their lungs wheeze, permanently scarred by metallic dust and chemical fumes. A constant, hacking cough punctuates their words, which are few and measured. Their speech is a clipped, economical dialect, peppered with technical jargon and grim euphemisms. "The Feed" refers to the flow of effluent from the Ponds, "The Bleed" is the sickness claiming their bodies, and the ominous clicking from the Ponds is known only as "The Ticker," an ever-present warning.

They are clad in layers of repurposed clothing: scavenged hazmat suits, patched-up work overalls, and scraps of animal hide treated with what they can to protect them from chemical splatter. Metal plates, bolted or riveted to their limbs and torsos, offer meager protection against accidents, explosions or collapsing structures. Every garment is stained with the oily sheen of the Ponds, the unmistakable mark of the Flats.

Their faces are gaunt, hollowed by malnutrition and the slow creep of poison. Many sport crude tattoos, symbols of resistance, protection, or simple acts of rebellion against the uniformity of their grim existence. Scars crisscross their skin, mementos of accidents, fights, and the inherent dangers of the Flats. Some are branded with corporate logos, remnants of a time when the Ponds yielded a meager compensation for their labor; now merely reminders of abandonment.

Their hands are their true identifiers. They are calloused, scarred, and stained to the core. Some are missing fingers, lost to acid burns, grinding gears, or desperate gambles: these are the hands of artisans, of workers, of desperate survivalists. Each hand tells a story of labor, risk, and the endless struggle to extract value from the toxic wasteland. Many are fitted with crude prosthetics scavenged from dismantled machinery, or crafted from hardened resins and scavenged components.

Beyond the immediate pragmatism of survival, there is a strange, austere beauty to their existence. They are masters of jury-rigging, transforming discarded junk into vital tools. Their leech farms, despite their precariousness, are testaments to human ingenuity and resilience. They seem to possess some latent technical skill, passed down through generations.

They possess an almost shamanistic understanding of the Ponds. They can read the shifting colors of the sludge, predicting dangerous reactions, identifying pockets of valuable elements, and navigating the treacherous terrain with an uncanny instinct. They have learned to listen to the Ponds, to feel their pulse, to understand their volatile moods.

Survival relies on shared information. Although they don't seem to socialize much, they observe one another carefully, trading small efficiencies and improvements to optimize their individual leech farms. They communicate through the hoot of makeshift horns, the clatter of tools and equipment, barely-audible whispers, and a shared system of hand signals.

There is evidence of something more than simple survival. Small, carefully tended gardens sprout in sheltered alcoves, growing strange mutated vegetables that are both sustenance and a symbol of hope. Hand-carved trinkets, made from solidified slag or polished metal, are kept as keepsakes, objects of beauty in a landscape devoid of it.

They are, in the end, the children of the Slag Flats, bearing its mark on their bodies, in their souls, and in the strange, resilient culture they have forged in the shadow of the Ponds. They are a testament to humanity's capacity to endure, adapt, and even find a strange, twisted beauty in the face of utter desolation.
