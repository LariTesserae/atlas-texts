The people of Pistonburgh, or what remains of them, aren't built for comfort. They are wrought like the gears and girders that define their world, possessing a similar resilience, and a similar tendency to corrode. Lean frames wrapped in layers of oil-stained canvas and patched leather, figures perpetually hunched against the damp chill, faces obscured by soot and grim determination, they are as ingrained in the machinery as the machinery is ingrained in them.

They carry the cacophony of Pistonburgh in their bodies: the tremor in their hands that mimics the shudder of a dynamo, the perpetual cough that echoes the hiss of steam, the slightly tilted gait, acquired from navigating the uneven cobbles and treacherously slick metal floors. Their eyes, when you can see them through the grime, are sharp and knowing, reflecting the oily sheen of the Grease River itself. They have seen too much to be surprised, and endure too much to be easily broken.

Generations have lived and died in the shadow of the workshops, their lives intertwined with the ebb and flow of production. Lineage is often recorded through specific roles or skills: "riveter," "stoker," "dynamo-wright." Children are put to work young, their hands and limbs uniquely suited to the delicate tasks of cleaning gears or retrieving fallen bolts. They learn the language of the machines before they learn to read, understanding the subtle shifts in rhythm and pitch that signal impending failure.

Their clothing speaks of utility rather than style. Layer upon layer of scavenged fabric, repurposed overalls, salvaged gloves with the fingers cut off for dexterity. A favored item is a thick, oilskin coat, ideally one that's seen better days, offering a modicum of protection against the elements and the constant splatter of industrial effluent. The lucky ones might sport a pair of heavy, steel-toed boots, a necessity for navigating the treacherous terrain.

Their diet is as practical as their clothing: recycled grain, grease-soaked bread, the occasional rat roasted over makeshift braziers. They supplement their meager rations with the strange vegetation that thrives along the Grease River, understanding which bulbous plants offer sustenance and which are best left untouched. They share an intuitive knowledge of their world, passed down through generations, a blend of practical botany and localized folklore.

They are not a social people, not in the traditional sense. Trust is a rare commodity in Pistonburgh, bartered and earned, not freely given. Networks exist, however, forged in the shared hardship and the interdependent nature of their work. A broken dynamo can cripple an entire row of workshops, so mutual reliance transcends personal animosity. These unacknowledged pacts are sealed with shared meals, whispered warnings, and the occasional clandestine act of sabotage against a rival workshop.

The rust-colored birds of Pistonburgh are more than mere fauna; they are almost familiars, regarded with a mixture of superstition and affection. The birds act as alarms, their raucous cries announcing approaching strangers and impending dangers. Some believe they carry the souls of lost laborers, their rust-colored plumage a manifestation of the perpetual corrosion.

In the brief respites between shifts, they gather in dimly lit corners, clustered around sputtering lamps. They share stories, mostly of near-misses and miraculous saves, and of the peculiar occurrences and strange mutations that have become commonplace in their world. Tales of gears that grind themselves, pipes that whisper secrets, and the faint, unsettling glow that emanates from the fleshy river plants. These are not tales of fear, so much as expressions of a grim fascination: an understanding that they are living in a place that is slowly, inexorably, becoming something else.

They rarely speak of leaving. Pistonburgh is their cage and their cradle, their burden and their birthright. They are as much a part of the machinery as the machines are a part of them, a symbiotic relationship forged in grease and grime. They are the inheritors of a forgotten trade, the keepers of a dying flame. They will endure here, clanking and hissing, until the river reclaims everything, and the rust-colored birds sing their final song.
