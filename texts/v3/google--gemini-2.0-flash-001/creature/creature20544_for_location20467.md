The gloamtoads are creatures of patience and paradox, beings sculpted by the dim, filtered light and the echoing silence of their subterranean world. Their bodies, vast and toad-like, are living maps of their environment, the network of blue veins pulsing beneath translucent skin mirroring the tunnels and chambers they inhabit. These veins, filled with bioluminescent fluid, hum with a soft, internal light, a counterpoint to the harsh sun that filters through the porous ceiling.

Their eyes, like polished obsidian swallowing the light, hold the wisdom of generations spent observing the subtle shifts in their world. They see the infinitesimal changes in the angle of the sunlight, the faintest variations in the moisture seeping through the rock, the almost imperceptible growth of the strange, sun-loving flora they cultivate. In those depths, they also see the worms.

They are not builders in the human sense; they are sculptors of existing space. Their technology is not forged in fire or powered by complex equations, but a slow, deliberate refinement of natural processes. A sharpened stone, carefully angled to catch the light. A precisely placed plant, coaxed into a specific configuration. They manipulate their world with a gentle, knowing touch, like gardeners tending a bonsai tree.

Their society is woven into the very fabric of their environment. The patterns they paint on the walls are not mere decoration; they are a living language, a record of their history, a map of their world, prayers offered to the unseen forces that govern their lives. Each symbol, each geometric shape, each stylized plant or animal holds a specific meaning, understood only by the gloamtoads themselves.

Until recently, there were no depictions of worms. The worms, in their alien smoothness and habit of self-fragmentation, present a representational challenge. Their glowing bodies in the dimness are something to behold, an addition to the art. But how to capture that?

There is a strangeness about them that is unnerving for the gloamtoads. The worms, pulsing with that eerie light, disrupt the established rhythm of the nursery. These new wrigglers, foreign and slick, refuse to fit neatly into the patterns and cycles the gloamtoads have so carefully observed for generations. Are they a threat, a resource, or simply a new element to be woven into the tapestry of their world? Time, the gloamtoads know, will provide the answer. But patience, and observation, and representation, are still required.
