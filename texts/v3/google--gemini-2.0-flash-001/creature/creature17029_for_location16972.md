The people of Aethel are, first and foremost, extensions of the mountain itself. They are not conquerors of this space, but patient inheritors, their lives intertwined with the stone, the rain, and the riotous, clinging vegetation. They are the slow, deliberate blossoming of humanity in an environment that seems to resist all but the most tenacious life.

Imagine skin perpetually damp, faintly greened by the ever-present moss spores that drift on the humid air. Their hands are strong, calloused from generations of working the unforgiving stone, yet surprisingly gentle when tending to the fragile terraced gardens. Their eyes, though, hold the most profound story. They are the color of the wet slate, depthless and still, reflecting the unchanging rhythm of their existence. There is a knowing there, an acceptance that borders on resignation. They are not unhappy, not exactly, but perhaps unaware of any other way to be.

Age blurs in Aethel. The elders carry the same lean strength as the youth, their faces etched not with the distinct lines of temporal passage, but with a network of fine wrinkles, like the cracks in the sun-baked mud, each one telling a silent tale of persistence. The children mimic the adults with uncanny accuracy, their small hands already adept at weaving and stone-shaping, their young eyes already holding that same placid, knowing gaze.

Their clothing is a mosaic of the mountainside. Think of layers upon layers of treated leaves, carefully stitched together with vines, dyed with the pigments of crushed blossoms and soil. The hues are muted—deep greens, earthy browns, the pale lavender of mountain stone, designed not to stand out but to blend seamlessly, to become one with the landscape. They move with quiet grace, their footsteps sure on the slippery stone stairs, their bodies attuned to the subtle shifts in the wind and the whisper of the water. Their cloaks, large and hooded, offer protection from the constant dampness, turning them into shadowy, almost spectral figures disappearing into the mist.

Their voices are soft, infrequent. Communication is economical, a language of gestures and knowing glances passed down through the generations. Words are precious, reserved for essential tasks, for ritualistic chants that echo through the narrow streets, murmuring gratitude to the spirits of the mountain, the rain, and the plants. Every generation sees their parents perform the same dances, the same songs, the same chants.

The people of Aethel are neither individualists nor collectivists. Community seems to stretch out forever - the settlement is everything and will always be everything. Each person is a node in a web of interdependence, each task contributing to the fragile equilibrium that sustains their existence. They are the caretakers of the mountain, its silent guardians, its living tapestry.

They are deeply connected to the past, to practices that have existed as long as the stone itself. The rare metal they so preciously hoard passes from parent to child, and is treated with the most gentle and deliberate handling. It has always existed and will always exist, so no work related to metal can be rushed or ill thought.

But above all, the people of Aethel are defined by their stillness. Not a stillness of inaction, but a stillness of spirit. There is no striving, no yearning for something more. They are content, or resigned, to exist within the boundaries of their world, to repeat the same tasks, to breathe the same damp air, to see the same mountains mirrored in the eyes of their children. They are the embodiment of the mountain’s own quiet strength, its silent endurance, its unwavering acceptance of the present. And as the world outside changes, as empires rise and fall and new ideas take hold, the people of Aethel remain, steadfast, unchanging, a testament to the power of stillness in a world obsessed with motion.
