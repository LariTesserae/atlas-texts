The people of the Sump, as they are sometimes called by those who dwell in sunnier, drier districts, are carved from the same black stone as their homes. Their skin, perpetually damp and lacking sunlight, is pale, almost translucent in places, revealing a tracery of veins beneath the surface. Their eyes, however, are large and dark, pools that reflect the perpetual twilight of their world, quick to dart and assess, always watching for the next drip, the next shift in the water level, the next opportunity or lurking danger.

Generations of living in the Sump have molded them. They are short and wiry, built for navigating the narrow, uneven streets and squeezing into cramped workshops. Their hands are strong and calloused, stained permanently with the dyes and chemicals of their trade. These are hands that know how to wrestle a stubborn hide, how to mend a leaking roof with deft precision, how to coax a flame from damp wood.

Their clothing is layered and practical. Dark, roughspun fabrics, often patched and reused, provide some protection against the constant damp. Woven reed mats serve as makeshift cloaks in heavy rain. They favor close-fitting hats or scarves to keep their hair – often lank and dull – from clinging to their faces. Bare feet or wooden clogs are the norm; leather is too valuable to waste on footwear.

The faces of the Sump-dwellers are etched with a quiet resignation. Laughter is rare, but not absent. It surfaces in brief, sharp bursts, usually in the camaraderie of shared labor or the relief of a successful trade. Their expressions are guarded, a lifetime of bartering and haggling etched into the lines around their eyes. They are a people who have learned to trust few and rely only upon their own ingenuity and the unspoken bonds of their community.

The smell of the Sump clings to them, a mixture of damp stone, woodsmoke, and the acrid tang of tanning solutions. It is a scent that repels outsiders, but to the Sump-dwellers, it is the smell of home, the smell of survival. They carry this aroma with them even when they venture into other parts of the city, a subtle reminder of their origins.

Children in the Sump learn early. They are put to work as soon as they are able, fetching water, sorting hides, and running errands through the labyrinthine streets. Schooling is a luxury few can afford; knowledge is passed down through apprenticeship, whispered secrets of the tannery, the learned art of coaxing life (or something like it) from dead flesh. They are quiet, watchful children, old beyond their years, with a deep understanding of the precariousness of their existence.

The elders of the Sump are revered, not for their wisdom – though they possess it in abundance – but for their sheer endurance. They are living testaments to the Sump's ability to break the body and spirit, and their survival is seen as a sign of favor from the unseen powers that govern their watery realm. They are living libraries of practical knowledge, repositories of folklore, and the keepers of the rituals and superstitions that bind the community together. Their faces are deeply lined, their hands gnarled and twisted by years of hard labor, their eyes holding a lifetime of stories, both seen and unseen. They are the anchors of the Sump, the keepers of its past, and the guides for its future.

They believe the water has a will of its own, a capricious and sometimes cruel spirit that must be appeased. They leave small offerings – scraps of leather, woven reeds, even drops of precious oil – at the mouths of the drainage channels, hoping to ward off floods and ensure a steady supply of water for their craft. They whisper prayers to the unseen source, begging for mercy and guidance.

Their skills are both practical and arcane. They understand the properties of different hides, the effects of various dyes and fixatives, the subtle nuances of the tanning process. But they also possess a deeper, more intuitive understanding of the materials they work with. They claim to be able to feel the "memories" trapped within the hides, the echoes of the creatures they once belonged to. Some whisper of the power to imbue the finished leather with properties beyond the natural, to create garments that offer protection, enhance strength, or even grant glimpses into the future. These are closely guarded secrets, passed down through generations, and only shared with those deemed worthy.

Though the Sump is a hard place to live, its people are not without hope. They dream of a better future for their children, a future where they can escape the constant damp and the grinding poverty. They cling to the hope that one day, they will find a way to appease the water spirits and bring prosperity to their district. And in the meantime, they continue to work, to trade, to survive, bound together by the shared struggle against the elements and the unwavering belief in the power of community.
