The people here are, like the beetles, black and iridescent. Not from birth, of course. Time and the air have worked on them gradually.

In their youth they are grey, the color of the concrete and dust that birthed them. Their skin is tough but pale, like the pale, rootless things that grow under stones. They start their days in oiled canvas, the grime soaking into the warp and weft of their clothes until they are as stained and slick as the machines.

Their eyes are bright, reflecting the harsh glare of the arc lamps and the chemical fires. They see more than they say, their gazes sharp and calculating, always assessing, always searching for advantage in the monotonous grind. Their elders have eyes burned like embers, faces creased with the knowledge of gears and the feel of the valley shaking in their sleep.

The young are wiry, quick and nimble, adept at navigating the labyrinthine pathways of the factories, dodging the scalding steam and the treacherous puddles of oil. They move with a practiced economy, a constant awareness of the dangers that lurk around every corner. They often cluster with other children, forming gangs and groups. They are given nicknames based on some skill, or perhaps some tragedy. They are the grease monkeys and the spark catchers, the pipe runners and the slag sniffers.

Their bodies are their capital. Strength is valued, but dexterity and endurance even more so. Every child knows the value of a quick mind and a steady hand. Some are taken for work younger than others and work the lines until their hands look like the tools there. Very few hold onto the hope they will be something other than a tool.

As they age, the dust becomes a permanent part of them. It settles in the lines of their faces, deepens the shadows around their eyes, and stains their skin with a metallic sheen. The grease and the oils seep into their pores, hardening their hands into calloused claws. Hair is long and oiled, kept away from the machinery with leather straps, but always escaping.

Their lungs are choked with soot and fumes. A deep, rattling cough becomes a common mark of age. They develop an immunity to the toxins that would kill an outsider in hours, but the air slowly corrodes them from within.

They communicate in a language of grunts and gestures, a crude pidgin of machine sounds and shouted commands. Their words are few, but their meaning is clear, honed by the exigencies of the factory floor. They speak of quotas and tolerances, of stress fractures and bearing failures. They speak of shortcuts and improvisations, of ways to coax another hour of life out of the faltering machines.

They wear their history on their bodies – the scars of burns, the missing fingers, the twisted limbs. Each mark is a testament to their long and arduous struggle against the unyielding power of the machines. They see these marks in each other and respect that they are still alive more than anything.

Their faces are impassive, masks worn to conceal weariness and fear. Every so often one cracks a smile, but it is usually wicked. One might catch an elder teaching the young how to pinch the pipes for more steam, or how to misreport work so that they don't lose a finger and get less pay.

Most of the people are born in the Factory Canyons, and die there. They know nothing of the world beyond the cliffs, but can feel the unreality of the valley. They feel the thrumming of the metal, a part of their consciousness. It soothes most, but some claim it keeps them awake at night.

They are both victims and masters of this place. They are trapped, yes, but they are also necessary. Without them, the machines would grind to a halt, the valley would fall silent, and the strange energy that permeates the air would dissipate. They are the keepers of the flame, the guardians of the grinding, the inheritors of the constant hum. They are the black, iridescent people of the Factory Canyons, and they are not going anywhere.
