The inhabitants are children of the engine. Not born of it directly, but of its needs, its waste, its insatiable hunger for form. They are more akin to the ash flakes that swirl in the engine's wake, coalescing briefly into shapes that hint at meaning before dissolving again into the all-consuming flow.

Imagine them first as silhouettes against the perpetual twilight. Gaunt figures lurching through the steam, their movements jerky and imprecise, like puppets with frayed strings. Their garments, if they can be called that, are layered scrap – beaten plates of metal, strips of woven fiber saturated in grease, fragments of something that might once have been a uniform. Each layer is a testament to necessity, a pragmatic response to the brutal dictates of the landscape. No two are adorned alike, save for the common marks of soot and wear.

Their faces are obscured, often by crude masks fashioned from scavenged materials. These aren't shields against poison or weaponry so much as desperate attempts at self-preservation, a way to hold onto some semblance of identity in the face of relentless homogenization. Behind the masks, faces are gaunt, etched with the lines of hardship and hopelessness. Their eyes, however, are bright – too bright, perhaps – with a feverish intensity. The glare of the forge, the constant struggle for survival, and perhaps something more profound.

They move with a strange, shuffling gait, adapted to the treacherous surface of the slag fields. Their limbs are strong and wiry, built for hauling heavy loads. Calloused hands, stained black with grime, grip tools that are extensions of their own bodies, cobbled together from scavenged parts and maintained through ritualistic care. Each tool, wielded by the right hands, becomes an instrument of survival, a means of imposing their will – however small – upon the chaos.

They speak in a language that is more felt than heard. A guttural, rhythmic cadence of clicks, grunts, and gestures passed down through generations. It is a language born of the engine's rhythm, a mimicry of its thrum and hiss. Their words are often accompanied by elaborate hand gestures, a way of communicating complex ideas in a world where the very air seems to scramble thoughts. When language falls short, they communicate through the language of the body, a language of touch and proximity, of shared purpose and quiet desperation.

Their society, if it can be called that, is fluid and fragmentary. There are no fixed hierarchies, no rigid structures. Alliances form and dissolve like eddies in the slag flow, dictated by the ever-shifting demands of the engine and the whims of the landscape. They are not united by ideology or shared belief, but by a common struggle for survival. They are bound together by the invisible threads of necessity, a shared understanding that their fate is intertwined with the fate of the engine and the vast, unfinished world around it.

They possess a strange connection to the engine, a sensitivity that transcends mere survival. Some say they can feel its pulse, anticipate its surges and collapses. Some whisper that they can even hear its voice, a low, resonant hum that speaks of purpose and potential. They treat the engine with a mixture of fear, reverence, and a begrudging sort of affection. It is their oppressor, their provider, their very reason for being. It is at once a prison and a promise, a monument to a future that may never come.

They are not architects, nor visionaries. They are not driven by grand designs or soaring ambitions. It falls to them only to haul and scrape, to mend and maintain, to feed the engine with the materials it demands. They are creatures of habit, clinging to the familiar routines that offer a fragile sense of order in a world of endless chaos. Their world is a closed one, built on cycles as steady as the thrum-thrum-thrum of the engine. And yet, in their quiet, uncelebrated acts of maintenance, they are the glue that holds this world together, the force that binds the chaos and drives it slowly––agonizingly––toward some half-imagined apotheosis.

Despite their grim circumstances, there is still a spark of creativity within them, an innate desire to shape their surroundings. They adorn their tools with crude carvings, their masks with intricate patterns. They build small, personal shrines out of scavenged materials, tributes to the engine or perhaps to forgotten gods. These are not grand works of art, but intimate expressions of the human spirit, desperate attempts to find beauty and meaning in a world that seems to offer neither.

They are the forgotten children of a forgotten god. They cling to the edges of this world, struggling to survive in the shadow of its unrealized potential. They are the midwives to a stillborn future, the parasites infesting a dying dream. They are the children of the engine, and they are all that keep it running.
