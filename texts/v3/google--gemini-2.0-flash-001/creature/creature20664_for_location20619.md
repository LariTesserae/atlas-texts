They are children of corrosion, inheritors of rust. Their lungs are lined with the taste of airborne metal, their skin tanned ochre by a sun that judges and punishes. They are thin, honed by scarcity, with eyes that are large and dark, reflecting the precious water they hoard. Their limbs are wiry and strong from scaling the treacherous architecture of Pylon City.

They dress in rags pieced together from industrial refuse, scraps of cloth woven with wires and reinforced with ceramic plates scavenged from Scuttler shells. Each garment is a testament to resourcefulness, a mobile fortress against the harsh realities of their world. They adorn themselves with trinkets found amongst the wreckage: corroded bolts polished to a dull sheen, fragments of glass that catch and refract the brutal sunlight, and the iridescent wings of fallen Glasswings, worn as amulets for good fortune.

They are scavengers and engineers, tinkerers and mechanics, their minds sharp and adaptable. They possess an innate understanding of the Pylon City's ramshackle infrastructure, a knowledge passed down through generations. They know the precise pressure points on a rusted valve, the delicate balance required to siphon condensation from a pylon, the optimal angle to position a solar collector for maximum efficiency. Their hands are constantly busy, repairing and repurposing, coaxing life back into machines that should have long ago surrendered to entropy.

Families are bound tightly together, each member playing a vital role in the delicate ecosystem of survival. Children learn to identify edible fungi before they can speak, to distinguish the different types of Scuttler by the sound of their skittering, to spot the telltale glint of a Feral's eye in the shadows. The elderly are revered for their knowledge of the old ways, their memories of a time when water was more plentiful and the pylons hummed with true power.

Their language is a guttural mix of broken dialects and technical jargon, peppered with the clicks and whistles used to communicate with the Scuttlers. They tell stories around flickering fires powered by scavenged fuel – tales of mythical engineers who built the pylons, of a lost oasis hidden somewhere beyond the shimmering horizon, of the creatures that dwell within the pylons and the power they hold.

They are deeply spiritual, their beliefs rooted in a reverence for the water and the pylons. They believe the condensation is the tears of the earth, a precious offering to those who can endure. They see the pylons as living entities, ancient gods whose metal bodies are slowly succumbing to time. They make offerings of scrap metal and Glasswing wings, hoping to appease the entities within and ensure the continued flow of condensation.

There is a weariness etched on their faces, a resignation to their harsh reality. But there is also a spark of defiance, a refusal to surrender to the relentless pressure of their environment. They are survivors, forged in the crucible of the desert, their spirits as resilient as the volcanic glass beneath their feet. They are the children of Pylon City, and they will endure, clinging to life like vines on a rusting pylon, finding beauty in the decay, hope in the shimmering heat, and a future in the drops of water that fall from the sky. They are the embodiment of adaptation, a testament to the enduring power of life, even in the face of oblivion.
