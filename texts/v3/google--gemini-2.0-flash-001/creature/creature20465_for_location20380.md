In this perpetual twilight realm of verdant saturation, life clings to the edges of what's possible. It is not a gentle flourishing, but a desperate, inventive persistence, shaped by the overabundance and the mechanical hunger gnawing at the root.

Consider first the tenders of the condensers. They are a breed apart, gnawed down to essential functions by the mountain's relentless nature and the insatiable demands of the industry above. Generations born and raised in the shadow of the machines have bodies stooped by the damp and faces etched with the fine lines of perpetual vigilance. Skin is pale and clammy, absorbing more than reflecting the strange green light. Their lungs have adapted to the dampness, drawing a bare sustenance from the thin, saturated air, and their lives are interwoven with the rhythms of the mountain itself. They are bound by the drip, the grind, the wheeze of the engines. They are less engineers and more living components – oilers, stokers, valve-turners – their purpose distilled down to the most basic tasks.

They communicate in a language of hisses and clicks, mirroring the sounds of the machines they serve. Words are unnecessary, often forgotten. They understand the vibrations of the metal, the subtle changes in steam pressure, the telltale signs of impending failure. Their knowledge is not of textbooks but of touch, smell, and sound, a library etched into their muscle memory.

Their eyes, however, are unsettling. The constant, diffused light has rendered them unnaturally large and sensitive, capable of discerning the faintest flicker in the gloom. Something has leached out. They have a disturbing lack of focus, as if they are staring beyond you, seeing something that exists only in the tangled depths of the mountain's consciousness. Their gaze is not unkind but feels vacant, holding too much green light and too little humanity.

Higher still, nearer the hub, toil other humans - the maintainers. They are paler than the Condenser Tenders. They are more removed from the cycle of saturation and evaporation and more immersed in metal, machine, and cogitation. They have pale skin and hands that move with preternatural efficiency when they tinker with the innards of the great machines. They seldom see the light, and when they do, it dazzles and burns. They are so close to the machines and yet in a way, farther from them. They will curse and cajole and occasionally, scream and cry. They are more alive than the Tenders but also more afraid, and their pallor comes from the fear that emanates from the mountain.

Their numbers are dwindling. They work not for benefit to themselves but for a distant goal that has lost its way, and they can feel the life being extracted from them by the machines and by the mountain. They are starting to doubt. They are an ecosystem being superseded.

The children of the mountain are stranger still. They are born into nests of discarded machine parts and swaddled in moss-silk. They suckle not on breast milk, but a synthesized nutrient slurry, brewed from the mountain's secretions. They learn to walk on moss-covered slopes and communicate through a mix of guttural sounds and the intricate language of the machines. The younger children are still recognizably human, though their skin is often stained green from contact with the plants. But as they grow older, they begin to adapt to their environment in more unsettling ways. They learn to photosynthesize through patches of chlorophyll that appear on their skin. Their bones become lighter and more porous. The older ones are starting to grow extra limbs.

They do not speak of it for the mountain does not speak.

They are becoming.
