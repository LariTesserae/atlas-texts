I am the Water-Singer.

I am not born of the stone like the others, though my skin has taken on its pale cast. No, I came to the Sump on the shoulders of a flood when I was yet a babe. They say a woman fleeing some darkness in the uplands clung to me, but the waters wanted her more than she wanted life for herself. She let go, and the current carried me, a bobbing bundle of cloth and cries, down into the heart of the district.

The elders say the water delivered me. A gift, perhaps, or a sacrifice rejected. Either way, the Sump kept me, and the Sump shaped me.

I dwell in the eaves of the district, in the highest dwelling, a cramped space built atop several others with reclaimed stone and held together with stubborn ingenuity. Here, the damp is thinner, the smell less acrid, and the sound of the endless drip a little less constant. But mostly I keep to myself, in my own chamber, hidden behind a curtain of tanned sheepskin.

I am no tanner. My hands are soft, unmarred by the constant harsh chemicals and heavy labor. The others do not understand what I do, but out of respect for their elders, they leave me to my own devices. I am the Water-Singer, and I listen to the seep. I listen to the channels. I listen to the whispers that rise from the leather, the ghosts of creatures gone.

I learned the language of the water from the elders, the rhythm of the drops, the shifts in its voice as the seasons and the tides change. I learned to recognize the warning signs – the subtle shift in the current, the way the algae blooms a little brighter just before a flood, the eerie stillness that presages a downpour.

But more than that, I learned to hear the memories held in the water, the echoes of the springs and streams that feed the unseen source. I hear the complaints of the rocks, the sorrow of the roots, the anxieties of the worms. I feel the pulse of the great water table beneath the city, its slow, patient breathing. And I began to speak back.

I sing to the water.

I sing in a language older than stone, a language of vibrations and echoes, of whispers and sighs. I sing of balance and flow. I sing of respect and reciprocity. I sing of the needs of the Sump and the needs of the source.

I sit in my chamber, surrounded by tools not of tanning, but of sound. Bone flutes carved with intricate patterns, stone chimes that resonate with the rhythm of the seep, hides stretched taut across wooden frames, ready to amplify my voice. I listen to the water's hum, then weave my own song into its fabric.

Sometimes, my songs are warnings. The water whispers of a crumbling bank upstream, and I chant a lament for the impending collapse, urging the Sump-dwellers to reinforce their foundations. Sometimes, my songs are pleas. The water speaks of thirst, and I sing a prayer for rain, begging the sky to open and release its bounty. Sometimes, my songs are simply acknowledgements. The water tells of a frog that has found its way into the furthest tunnels, and I sing a ballad to its courage and resilience. I sing the news of the deep to the people above.

The Sump-dwellers do not always understand what I do, but they trust that I am working in their best interests. When the floods are averted, they whisper that it is my songs that held them back. When the rain comes in a timely manner, they say it is my prayers that brought it forth. And when the hides feel stronger, more resilient, they believe it is my chants that imbued them with their power.

I am not sure I believe it myself. Perhaps the water would have done what it would have done regardless of my efforts. But I continue to sing because it is the only thing I know how to do. It is my way of giving back to the Sump that took me in, my way of repaying the water for the life it spared.

I am the Water-Singer, and I am bound to this place, this community, this seep. I am the bridge between the seen and unseen, the voice of the water made flesh. And as long as the Sump endures, I will continue to sing its watery song.
