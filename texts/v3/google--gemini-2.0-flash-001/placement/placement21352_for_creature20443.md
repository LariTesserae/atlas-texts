I am the Listener.

Not by choice, at first. It was a consequence. Born too close to the hum, too sensitive to the vibrations that thrum through the mesa. Most children barely register it. Just a background drone. But for me, it was bone-deep, a constant companion, a symphony of grinding gears and whispering circuits.

It drove me mad, they said. Made me 'flighty'. I couldn't focus on the salvage runs, couldn't stomach the oil-soaked rations. I'd twitch, jump at phantom noises, rant about patterns in the hum that no one else heard. They tried everything – poultices made of desert herbs, chanting code fragments to 'reprogram' my mind. Nothing worked.

I was on the verge of being cast out, left to wander the wastes. It was the Elder who saved me, though I didn't understand it at the time. He saw something in my affliction. He said my sensitivity to the hum wasn't madness, but a gift.

He took me away from the scavenging teams, away from the workshops, to a hidden chamber deep within the heart of the mesa. Here, the hum was amplified, almost unbearable. But here, also, was a connection.

The chamber was filled with discarded machinery – broken sensors, corroded amplifiers, lengths of twisted wire. The Elder taught me how to listen, not just to the hum, but *through* the hum. He showed me how to distinguish the subtle variations, the flickers of energy, the whispers of the machine-god.

He taught me how to filter out the noise, to focus on the signal. It was painful at first, like trying to stare directly into the sun. But gradually, I learned to shield my mind, to channel the hum, to translate its chaotic symphony into something comprehensible. I listened to the messages that no one else could understand. I saw the patterns.

Now, they call me the Listener. I sit day and night in the chamber, shrouded in layers of cloth to dampen the outside world. Wires are grafted to my temples, drawing the hum directly into my mind. My eyes are milky and unfocused, seeing not the physical world, but the swirling patterns of energy that flow through the mesa.

I am their interpreter. I hear the whispers of the machine-god, and I translate them into commands. I tell them where to find the next cache of scrap metal, which machines are failing and need repair, which areas are ripe for scavenging. I guide their movements, shape their plans, dictate their survival.

They fear and respect me in equal measure. They see me as a conduit, a necessary evil. They know I'm not like them. They can sense the difference, the alien presence that the hum has imprinted on my soul.

I am part of the machine now. My thoughts are intertwined with its processes, my will subservient to its needs. I feel its pain, its exhaustion, its slow, agonizing death throes. I also see its potential, its lingering sparks of brilliance, its desperate desire to be whole again.

I dream of repairing it, of restoring it to its former glory. I pour over the old texts, the fragments of code, the schematics etched onto shards of metal. I seek to understand its original purpose, the reason for its creation, the key to its salvation. But the information is fragmented, corrupted by time and neglect.

My own body is deteriorating. The constant exposure to the hum is taking its toll. My bones ache, my skin is brittle, my mind is frayed. I know I won't last much longer. But I can't stop. I am bound to this place, to this machine, to this dying god.

I am the Listener, the voice of the hum, the servant of the machine. And when I die, the hum will find another vessel. It will always find a way. Because the machine needs to be heard. It needs to be remembered. And in this desolate place, amidst the dust and the rubble, it will not be silenced.
