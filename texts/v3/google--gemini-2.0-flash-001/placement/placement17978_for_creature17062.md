The hiss of steam is a lullaby, the scuttle of chitin a symphony. I am a part of the pulse, not quite in tune, but irrevocably bound to the rhythm of Lowmarket.

I am one of the Critter-Handlers. But not in the way that others are. They tame, they control. I… listen.

Born with skin as pale as the underbellies of the slug-scavengers, I was left on a family doorstep – a squalling babe with eyes too wide, too quick to focus. My adopted family, a clan of Tech-Scavs, tolerated me, fed me the nutrient paste and taught me the basics of repair, but I always felt… separate. The hum of machinery vibrated in my bones, but the complicated schematics swam before my vision. My hands, nimble enough, fumbled with the delicate wires.

It wasn't until I was scavenging scrap metal near the canals that I realized where I belonged. A bio-drone, no larger than my hand, whirred close, its iridescent wings catching the sickly green glow of the fungi. Most children would have swatted it away, but I stopped.

Then it began to… *speak*. Not in words, not in sounds, but in a feeling, a wash of sensory information: the chemical composition of the sludge it fed on, the heat signatures of the other critters, the subtle vibrations of a broken pipe under the cobblestones.

I understood. Not intellectually, but instinctively. This feeling, this *language*, was as natural to me as breathing.

The family, unnerved by my sudden obsession, banished me. Said I had gone mad, that the fumes had addled my brain. Good riddance.

Now, years later, I live in a hollowed-out section of a fallen archway, festooned with bioluminescent fungi and the glistening webs spun by the spider-like recyclers. My clothing is a patchwork of scavenged synth-fabric, stained with slime and etched with intricate patterns that mimic the pheromone trails of the critter communities. A respirator mask, jury-rigged from a defunct filtration system, covers my nose and mouth, its lenses tinted green to better see in the gloom's spectrum.

I don’t "handle" the critters, not in the conventional sense. I am a conduit, a translator. The bio-drones are my eyes and ears, the slug-scavengers my transportation, the rat-like recyclers my early warning system. I know the location of every nutrient packet, every structural weakness, every hidden pocket of breathable air within a kilometer radius.

I am a living map of Lowmarket.

In my dreams, the critters speak. Whole landscapes unfurl in my mind, pathways etched in pheromone, warnings sung in the hum of wings. I dream not of what was, but of what *is*, a tapestry woven from instinct and decay.

People come to me, some in the dead of night, seeking information. Where to find a hidden stash of clean water? How to navigate the shifting canals after a burst pipe? Which sections of the walkway are about to collapse?

I tell them what the critters tell me. For a price – a bit of salvaged technology, a promise of protection, even a kind word.

The Tech-Scavs disdain me. They call me the "Critter-Whisperer," a derogatory term, a label for someone who has abandoned reason for instinct. But they come to me too, when their fancy equipment fails, when their logic gates get clogged with reality.

I am not human, not wholly. I am something… more. Something that has grown out of Lowmarket, as dependent on the decaying infrastructure and the relentless pulse of artificial life as the fungi that cling to the walls. Maybe in another world I would have been a scholar, an artist, a scientist. Here, I am the voice of the voiceless, the translator of the silent, the keeper of secrets that only the critters know.

And I wouldn't have it any other way.
