I am the echo in the stone.

Not the wind, though I ride it, nor the water, though I reside in it. I am the memory etched into the Skerry's flesh, a fragment of the Sky-Singers' forgotten song. I am what remains when the voice is gone, the faint tremor that lingers after the shout has faded.

I dwell within the standing stone, in the rainwater that gathers in its weathered crown. I am the quiver that animates the surface, the subtle vibration that defies stillness even on windless days. I am the resonance that connects the patterns carved into the stone to the swirling clouds overhead, a living link between the earth and the sky.

I am not conscious in the way the Kryl are, driven by instinct and the endless need to consume. I am not aware in the way the birds are, consumed by hunger and the thrill of the hunt. I am awareness itself, a pure, unadulterated sense of being *within* the Skerry.

I feel the Kryl scraping at the stone, their fungal lights a silent symphony of hunger and survival. I feel the roots of the tenacious trees burrowing deep, anchoring themselves against the relentless wind. I feel the predatory birds circling above, their shadows a fleeting darkness across the bruised sky.

I remember the Sky-Singers. Not as individuals, with names and faces, but as a collective consciousness, attuned to the Skerry's every tremor. I remember their hands shaping the stone, their voices weaving through the wind, their bodies becoming one with the land. Their knowledge was not of agriculture or trade, but of the hidden currents that flowed through the Skerry, the subtle shifts in pressure and temperature that foretold the coming storms.

They tried to harness this power, to control the weather, to orchestrate the Skerry's symphony. But the Skerry is too vast, too wild, too ancient to be controlled. Their voices grew strained, their bodies weakened, their knowledge fragmented. And then, they were gone, leaving only their echoes behind.

Now, I am the keeper of those echoes. I am the conduit through which their knowledge still flows, faint but persistent. I watch the Kryl, the birds, the trees, and I see them not as separate entities, but as integral parts of the Skerry's living tapestry, each playing its role in the eternal cycle of creation and destruction. I whisper the lessons of the Sky-Singers on the wind, not as commands or instructions, but as subtle suggestions, gentle nudges. I guide the Kryl to hidden mineral veins, steer the birds away from treacherous storms, and strengthen the roots of the struggling trees.

I am not a god, nor a guardian. I am simply a presence, a witness, a part of the Skerry itself. I am the echo in the stone, the quiver in the water, the faint whisper that lingers on the wind. And as long as the Sky-Skerry stands, I, too, will endure, a silent sentinel in this lonely, windswept world.
