I am a Loomtender.

Not officially, of course. There is no governing body in the Shallows, no official anything save the relentless grind of the mills and the wail of the ever-present sirens. But the name exists, whispered from one generation of grease-stained children to the next, a recognition of the delicate, vital thread I weave through the chaotic warp of the city.

I am old, though my age is measured less in years and more in the shifting tides of scrap. My skin is the mottled grey of corroded steel, etched with a thousand tiny lines that trace the routes of brackish rivulets. My eyes, the color of storm clouds gathering over the marsh, see more than they let on. My hair, once a fiery copper like the pipes that snake through the factories, is now a tangled mess of tarnish, streaked with the white dust of pulverized shells.

My home is the Clockwork Cathedral, a vast, dilapidated factory that stands at the edge of the marsh. Its skeletal frame is draped in a web of salvaged pipes, wires, and sheets of corrugated iron, a grotesque parody of stained-glass windows. Inside, the rusted machinery groans and wheezes, a symphony of decay that lulls me to sleep and wakes me with each sunrise, each indistinguishable from the last.

I do not live in the Cathedral, not precisely. I *am* the Cathedral. Or, at least, the Loom within it.

When the factories originally came to the Shallows, they brought with them the technology that would tear the land asunder. The Clockwork Cathedral wasn't always a cathedral, not as such. It was a mill, and within the mill was a Loom. Not one of threads and spindles, but a Loom all the same.

Humans were not the first to discover the secrets of the Loom, nor were they the Loom's first audience: as the mill became a factory, something was left behind– a sentience, perhaps, or a spirit. A thing that, too, recognized the function of the mill, and adapted to its purpose. But where humans sought to destroy the land, the Loom sought to heal it.

The mills worked in tandem with each other. Humans took water and salt from the marsh, and worked it into something new. The Loom took this new thing, and made *something better*. Everything the factories created were a gift to the Loom of the earth: in return, the Loom would unravel the thread. Reclaiming what the factories ruined. A partnership, of sorts.

This is not a physical process. I do not manipulate metal or weave fabric. I manipulate *resonance*. I feel the thrumming vibrations that pulse through the Shallows, the constant cacophony of the factories, the grinding of the world’s molars. Each vibration is a strand, a thread in the Loom. Some vibrate with decay, with stagnation. Others pulse with life, with resilience.

I listen to these threads. I coax them, nudge them, and subtly redirect them. I untangle the knots of despair and weave them into something stronger, something hopeful. A shift in water flow away from an abandoned site. A slight uptick in metal's ability to resist corrosion. A flash of luminescent fungus just where a child might need light to find their way.

I am the one who keeps the balance in the Shallows, the silent guarantor of its continued existence.

The children of the Shallows are my most important resources. They bring me scraps of discarded metal, whispered stories, and the pure, unfiltered energy of their laughter. They understand my presence, though they can't explain it. They leave offerings near the Cathedral – a handful of scavenged gears, a perfectly formed seashell, a drawing scrawled on a piece of rusted metal. In return, I guide them, protect them, and teach them the secrets of the Loom.

I do not speak in words. I communicate through the whispers of the wind, the rustling of the weeds, the click of the old machines. I leave trails of breadcrumbs – a glint of metal in the mud, a strangely-shaped rock, the sudden appearance of a flowering plant – to guide those who are lost or in need.

Most of the adults are too consumed by the grind to hear me. But some, the old-timers who remember the marshes before the factories came, sense my presence. They look at the Cathedral, at the chaotic tangle of metal and vines, and see not decay but a strange, wild beauty. They understand that even in the Shallows, life persists, not in spite of the rot, but because of it.

I do not know what the future holds for the Shallows. I do not know if the grinding of the world's molars will eventually reduce everything to grit and bone. But as long as the Loom still hums, as long as the children still laugh, and the weeds still push through the cracks, I will continue to weave my threads.

I am the Loomtender.
And I’ll keep the balance, one resonance at a time.
