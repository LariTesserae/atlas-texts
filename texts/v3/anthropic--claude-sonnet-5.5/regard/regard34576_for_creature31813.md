# The One Who Sat

A benevolent power that looked down into the Sump Quarter would see at once how easily it could ruin the place. It could shore up the doorways and stop the slow fist of the streets closing. It could fill the tenements with light, or with food that wasn't taro. It could tell the Keepers why the numbers were first written down. Each of these would be a kindness in the way that pulling a seedling out to show it the sun is a kindness.

So it does none of them. It has seen that the Keepers have already solved the hardest problem, which is how to live well inside something that is ending. Its first act is to recognize this, and its second is to decide it has nothing to fix. That leaves only whether it has anything to *give*, and that is a smaller and stranger question.

## The descent

It comes by the iron stair, one landing at a time, and lets the air get heavy on it. It has not made itself small in any showy way. It has simply put on a shape that fits the doorways, since it wants to be able to use them. Its feet are bare. It learns the root-ways by falling off them once.

At the market arcade it stands under the vine and lets the warm water fill its palm. It understands what has been done for it: a stranger's hand held out, months in advance, by someone who will not come down. It does not look up at the window wound with fern. It drinks, sits on the wet step, and lets the drone find its sternum.

Then it waits through a full cycle of the pump. The canal rises a hand's breadth and the duckweed lifts and settles. Nothing else happens. It is the hardest part, because a power is not used to being unnoticed, and it finds, a little to its surprise, that it does not mind.

In the pipe beside it: three shorts, a pause, a long.

It does not know the reply. It has the whole of the language in its mind and cannot use any of it, because using it would be a kind of lie. It takes the small brass key that someone has left on the step, raps a clumsy rhythm on the iron, and gets the knock wrong. Somewhere above, it is fairly sure, a woman laughs.

## The count

It asks, in the only way that is honest, which is badly, for a turn in the niche.

They give it a night. They give it the worn hollow by the great valve, shaped for a body that is not its body, and they hand over the count in a whispered exchange of numbers. It sits and counts the breaths of steam. It miscounts once and does not know it. Later a child, passing with a bowl of violet taro, says the number aloud in passing, as though to herself. She is off by one from the ledger, and so is the power. It understands that she is correcting it, and that being corrected is being admitted.

Here it learns the one true thing about the place, which it will not say aloud. Whatever the machines do or don't do, the House is *held together by the attention*. The pump would run without them, or fail without them, and the counting would make no difference to it. But sixty people who count the breath, oil the governor, and rake the sluice-mouths are sixty people bound into a single act of care. The old belief, that what is watched keeps running, is false of iron and true of them. The power decides that this is a better sort of truth than the one the Keepers think they hold, and that there is no reason to take it from them.

## The woman in the turbine hall

The eldest waves it in.

She is stone-deaf, small, and dusted grey-green at the shoulders. She does not speak, because there is no need. She takes the power's hand, lays it flat against the pump-housing, then lays her own over it, and they stand like that. The power feels what she feels: the pitch, the fatigue in a bearing, a slowness in one of the valves, a thousand small things arriving as pressure in the bones. She has been listening this way for decades. Nobody has ever listened with her.

It could restore her hearing. It knows exactly how, and it asks her, by touch, in a sequence of taps against her wrist.

She thinks about it. Her answer is a slow shake of the head, and then she puts its palm against her own sternum, and it understands. The drone is not noise to her. It is the shape of her whole life, felt in the body, and she would not trade it for a sound.

Then she asks for something else, and it is very small. She points to the pump, and to the low bench in the corner, and closes her eyes with her palm against her cheek.

For the first time in longer than anyone can say, the eldest sleeps. The power stays with its hand on the housing and *listens*, and keeps the hall through a whole cycle. It is not a rescue. She would have been fine, or she would not, and the House would have gone on. But she is old, and someone sat up with the pump so that she could rest, and that is the entire gift.

## The ledger

They let it read the oldest columns, kept dry in the lead-lined box. It reads them through a full watch, and it recognizes, in the first hand, the handwriting of a person it once knew, or of someone like her. The reason the numbers began is in the margins, a few faded words about a flood, a fear, a promise made to a place. The power could say what those words mean. It closes the book and says nothing, because the Keepers' reason is better than the original one: they have made the numbers *holy*, and that took generations of work that no one could have done for them.

At the end of the last column it writes one line in the same waxed hand. It is a pressure reading, nothing more, marked with a small unfamiliar sign. That is its only mark on the House.

## What it asks

Late in the season, or the cycle, or whatever the House counts it as, the power raps a question on the pipe, awkwardly, in the grammar it has slowly learned. The Keepers understand it, and the reply comes back through the iron from all sixty at once, in a soft metallic murmur under the drone.

The question was this: *When the last of you folds into the wall, may I sit the niche?*

They take a long time to answer. The pipes carry a slow argument, then a gap, then a single phrase repeated by every hand in the quarter. It means, roughly, *keep us company*.

That is the whole of the power's promise, and it is one it can keep. It will not hold the House still. It will not stop the fig roots, the closing doors, or the stair that is already a slope. But when the last Keeper lies down in a root-hollow with moss over her mouth, someone will still be counting the breaths of the valve, and knocking on the plaster before passing. The House will never once go unwatched. The Keepers wanted nothing more than that, and had not known they were allowed to ask.

## Leaving

It goes up the stair at the end, landing by landing, and the air lightens and cools on its skin. Behind it, in a niche worn to the shape of somebody else, a child is already counting.

A few cycles later there is a new phrase in the pipes. It is not on any list the Keepers keep, and no one remembers who coined it. It is three shorts, a long, two shorts, and it means *the one who came and sat*. Children use it in their games, and the old ones use it when the valve breathes at an odd hour and something in the quarter feels briefly less alone.

On the market arcade's step, where the vine drops its bright line of warm water, someone has left a small brass key on a cord, worn bright, freshly polished, and set down with the hollow side turned up so that it catches the drip.
