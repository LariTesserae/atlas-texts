# What Came to the Bowl

## The Attention

It noticed the bowl the way you notice a held note in a crowded room: not because it was loud, though it was, but because it had been sustained so long without anyone's effort. Something had been in balance for centuries, with roots opening the seams while warmth and moisture closed them, and with no operator, no purpose, and no one in distress. The power looked at it for what would have been a long time for a person and was hardly a breath for itself.

It was benevolent, and it was wise, and it knew that these two things sometimes disagree. Benevolence wants to help. Wisdom asks what the help would cost.

## What It Considered

It considered telling them who built the wheel. It knew. Knowing was not difficult. But the old man calls the wheel a clock no one wound, the young woman calls it a heart, and a fourth says it is only a wheel. The question is not an open wound but a well they drink from. If it answered, the answer would be true, and everything else would become smaller around it. It let the question stay.

It considered securing the balance. It could see the thousand-year arithmetic, the tree ferns splitting brick against the engine's slow wet breath, and it could see where the curve eventually bends. But the people had said, with a palm on a plate, that the place does not need them and they do not need to be needed. A steward who fixes a garden that runs itself has only taught the garden to wait for a steward.

It considered giving them speech: a way to hear each other through the roar, or a fortieth node, or a hundred more. It saw at once that this would dissolve the thing that makes their sentences finished and their invitations meaningful. Their quiet is scarce, and that scarcity is what they use to love each other. To make it plentiful would be a kind of theft, however kindly done.

It considered watching them, all three hundred, all the time, to catch any harm before it happened. It found this the most tempting and the most corrosive. A place that is fully seen is no longer quite a place that is lived in. It decided its first act would be to look away.

So it withdrew nearly all of its attention, the way a person leaves a room and pulls the door almost shut. What remained was small enough to fit into a body: one pair of ears, one pair of shins, a limited and slightly nervous sort of presence. It made the body plain. Then it went up the stair.

## The Climb

The steps rang and it could not hear them. It felt them in its shins. It had known, in the abstract, what a body's shins were for, and it was oddly moved to discover the abstraction had been so incomplete.

At the parapet the sound stopped being a sound and became the world. It stood there longer than it needed to. This was the first thing the place did to it: it made it slow. Nothing in the bowl was fast, and the body's impatience, a borrowed thing, began quietly to leave.

It took the plugs out of its pocket. It had made them as it walked, from beeswax and moss felt and goat hair, because it had read that this was how arriving was done, and it did not want to arrive as something that had skipped the practice. They were slightly wrong. It put them in anyway.

## The Goat

On the upper terrace a small dark goat lifted its head. It had not heard a footstep, since no one could. It looked at the shadow.

The power had not intended to have a shadow. The body had one anyway, the way a body does, and the goat took a long moment over it. Then it looked at the power itself, with the flat gold, unimpressed eye of an animal that has stayed somewhere by choice.

The power had brought no salt. It felt the lack sharply, more sharply than it had felt the lack of anything in a very long time. It put a palm on the animal's neck anyway. The goat allowed it for the length of one breath, then walked off up the slope with no ceremony at all.

The power understood it had been assessed and had been found neither dangerous nor interesting. It was, it thought, exactly the right verdict.

## The Old Woman's Quiet

It walked between nodes as the visitors do, stopping in each, feeling the door close on the roar and open again. It did not intrude on any household. It went, in the end, where the whole story pointed, to a small node on the eastern terraces, above the pit's lip, where an old woman sat with her plugs in her lap.

It stopped at the edge and waited. It did not step in. She looked at it for a long time, and it let her, and did not arrange its face.

"Come in," she said at last. Her voice was low and dry and sounded like it had been kept somewhere cool.

It sat. It took out the plugs and felt the shoulders drop, a gesture it had practiced and that turned out to be real.

She asked it, gently, whether it had found its own quiet yet, and whether it was big enough.

It had not planned to be honest at this depth. It had planned only to be a visitor. But the question was a real question, and she had earned a real answer, and the power had never in its existence been asked it.

"No," it said, after a pause long enough to be worthy of the place. "I don't have one. I hear everything at once. I always have. There is nowhere in me that is not listening."

She considered this without alarm. She nodded slowly, as at a diagnosis she had half expected.

"Then you have been very crowded," she said.

"Yes."

"Sit longer, then. It is my quiet, and it is not large. But you may have some of it."

It sat. This was, in the end, the gift the place had for it, and it had not seen it coming: to be given a small silence by someone who understood exactly what a silence costs, and who did not hold back.

She traced something on the palm of its borrowed hand, a small spiral, tight, with an upward flick at the end. It did not ask what it meant. She said only, "So I can find you."

## The Hour in the Roar

Later that morning a young woman came across the terrace with her plugs already at her neck, and stood at the edge of the node. She was the old woman's granddaughter. Neither of them looked at the visitor. The old woman lifted her hand, and the meaning was clear: there was something to be said that no neighbor should hear.

The power stood up.

It knew what it was being offered, which was the most intimate courtesy the people have. It stepped out of the quiet, fitted the plugs, turned its back, and stood in the roar.

The sound closed around it like water. It had always heard the bowl as data: pitch, decay, the interference pattern of the nodes. Now it stood in it as a body, and the engine's boom went into the sternum on its eleven-second cycle, and the wheel's long groan slid up and fell, and the valve-stacks swelled into a chord. It found that the old visitors' phrase was correct: silence with a great deal in it.

Above the central void the swifts spiraled and tightened together on the boom. It knew, without effort, whether this was coincidence or something they had learned. It kept the knowledge to itself, and did not think of it as withholding. The people find the question mildly funny, and the answer would only end a good joke.

The hour was long. At some point it noticed that it was not waiting. It was only standing, being a wall in the noise so that two people could be alone. This was what love is made of here, and it did not take a mind of its size to understand it. It took a back and an hour.

## What It Left

When the granddaughter touched its shoulder, it turned and saw that both women's faces had changed. It did not ask how. It took the plugs out at the edge of the quiet, and the old woman looked at it and, for the first time, smiled with her whole face, briefly, like a door in a wall.

"You overstayed a little," she said.

"Yes."

"It was allowed."

It did not leave a boon. It did not touch the wheel, seal a seam, or add a node. If you asked the people what had changed, they would say nothing had, and in the ways that can be counted that would be right. The frogs still flash their pale blue signals across the terraces. The moss still thickens on the oldest brass. The rust still runs down the plates in violet streaks.

But three things were different, and none of them showed.

The first was that a woman who had cut many spirals, and who would soon need someone else to cut hers, now knew that the place had a kind of witness that would not interfere. She did not have to guard it. She could let it go to the iron in whatever order it went.

The second was that on its way down the stair, at the very last step, where the treads are worn into shallow dishes, the power stopped and laid something on the rail. It was not a gift but a habit borrowed from the place. It was a single fern frond, taken from the nearest tree fern that morning, left to wilt and be carried off by the steam. No one told it to. It felt the need and did it.

The third was inside the power itself. In the vast field of its attention, which has never in its existence been silent, there is now a small region, lens-shaped, a few meters across, where it does not listen. It was given, and it is kept. It does not move unless the wind shifts. When it does, the power does not mind. It says to itself, in the old woman's dry, cool voice, that the quiet is a guest that sometimes changes its chair.

And far below, at the lip of the pit, among the hundreds of small brass plates worn nearly smooth, no one has yet cut a spiral tight, with an upward flick at the end. The old woman knows this. She knows also that if one appears some morning, unasked, the moss will find it in its own time, and she will not scrape it away.
