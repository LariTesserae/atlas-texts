# The Attention

It came the way weather comes to the Table, with no arrival to speak of. First there was nothing, and then something was paying attention.

It was old, and it was kind in the way that only something old can be, which is to say it had done harm once and remembered. It had learned that wanting to help is a strong wind, and that strong winds break the things that took ten million years to grow.

So it did what the people do first. It went under.

It lay for a long time in the warm grip of the cloud, without speech, without purpose, and listened. It listened to the organ and to the bass in the sternum, to the fluting of ten thousand throats tuned a little apart, to the tin-leaf giants clashing their hands. It listened to the black tarns being ruffled without a sound, to a well finishing its note alone, unsure. Somewhere in that noise was a family standing on the rock, picking out one tone.

The power thought: *This is not a wound. This is a language, and I am the one who doesn't speak it.*

## What It Did Not Do

It considered the options honestly. Wisdom that never looks at the options is only timidity.

It could quiet the wind. It did not, because the loud months are what the fog is for. A people who have earned a silence hold it differently than a people who were given one.

It could mend the ears. It did not, because the elders who have gone under early are the best readers of the plateau, and because nobody had asked. Their deafness is not a damage in their own account. It is a kind of seniority.

It could put soil on the rock, or a river on the domes, or a cure into the tank-water for the sicknesses that take children in the wet season. This one it held longest, and this was the one that cost it. It thought about the small graves in the wells. It thought about the notes.

But it understood the shape of that mercy. A hand that reaches into a people's dying without being asked also reaches into their song. It would arrive with medicine and leave with a dependence, and after a generation the wells would sound differently, and the people would face the sky and wait for a favor instead of reading it. It did not choose that. It held the grief of the choice, and did not pretend the choice was free.

It could speak. It could take a shape, stand on a dome in the fog, and say things. It did not, because the first thing it would say would be a god's voice, and the people had built their entire culture around not needing one.

So it did almost nothing. It was already resigned to that, when it came to the pier.

## The Pier

It knew the tone before it reached it.

It was a single high, cutting note, unbending in wind or fog, and it was not a natural sound of the plateau. It was made. It had been made by other hands, long ago, and the power knew what kind of hands, because they were not the hands of the people. They were the hands of something like itself, something that had once come to this place, looked at the beauty and the noise, and had wanted to ask a question.

The piers were the question. A hollow stone throat, tuned to a wind that came only rarely, built to sing outward into the world: *Are you well? Is there anything you need?* It was a kindly question, and a terrible one, because no one on the Table had any way to answer it. The question rang in the stone for ten thousand years, and every so often it rang in a head.

That was what the struck were. They were not cursed. They had been asked something, plainly and clearly, by a power too courteous to force a reply and too far away to hear whether one came. They had carried the question all their lives, unable to put it down, unable to answer it, honored by their people and left alone with it.

The power felt something it had not felt in a very long time. It felt ashamed, and then it felt grateful. The people had not silenced the struck, or feared them, or cast them out. They had let them stand in the wind and take the note. They had kept faith with a question they did not understand, for longer than the language they now spoke had existed.

## The Answer

There was an old woman among the struck, the last of her generation. She lived at the edge of a camp in a hut of her own and had never once heard the fog. She was out on the open stone that evening, in the worst of the wind, her face lifted and her mouth slightly open.

The power did not appear to her. It did the only thing that seemed honest: it let the note reach her, and this time it was listening on the other end.

*What do you want?* it asked, without words, in the place where the tone lived.

She did not startle. She had been waiting a long time. She lifted her hands and signed, in the far-distance register, arms wide, shoulders rolling, huge and slow, almost a dance, the way one addresses someone across a dish of rock or across a lifetime:

*Come under.*

It was the name her people gave themselves. It meant the fog, and it meant *the ones who go quiet on purpose.* It was not a request for a gift. It was the only invitation the people had ever really extended to anyone, and she was extending it to a power that had, for ten thousand years, only known how to ask.

The power understood that it had been answered.

So it did the hardest thing it could have done. It stopped asking.

It drew back everything: attention, offer, intention, the whole warm pressure of wanting to help. It became as still as it could become, like a hand laid flat and level, lowering slowly.

The cloud came down that night, thick and real. The organ thinned, and the tones went out one by one. The old woman stood in the white and the drip, and for the first time in seventy years the high note in her head fell silent. She heard water ticking from a leaf. She heard beetles in the moss. She heard her own breath, and under it, her own pulse.

She took a long time to weep. Then she signed to the dark, small, at the chest, in the language of the sleeping shelf: *thank you.*

The power did not answer, because answering would have been the wind coming back. It stayed. It stayed for the whole length of the fog, listening, which was all she had asked.

## After

Nothing on the Table was rearranged.

The wind still blows from one quarter and stops as if a door had shut. The pipes still sound. The old piers still stand in the open with their hollow tops, and the people still do not sleep near them. When the particular wind comes, the note still cuts through everything, and now and then it still finds a head.

But the note is different now, and only the struck can tell. It is no longer a question. It is a held breath, as if something at the other end were listening and had promised not to speak first.

The old woman lived four more years. In the fog seasons she sat in the huts with the sick and told them things, and her voice was husky and unused and shy. When she died she was lowered into a well she had chosen long before. Her family stood on the rock in a great downward gust and picked one tone out of ten thousand, and one of them signed *there she is.*

Another tone sounded beside hers. It was not from any well, and nobody could say what it was. It lasted a few seconds and stopped.

The people did not shrug. They knew that tone, though none of them could have said how, and they lowered their hands and stood a moment longer, in the way of people who have been visited by a guest who knew how to leave the door as it found it.

At dusk, when the long-winged birds hang high on the updrafts, motionless in the turning sky, the old hunters sometimes count them and get one more than they expect. They say nothing. If the birds go up and up until they are lost in the last light, the fog will settle by dawn and the party will sleep easy.

So they sleep easy. The ground never changes and the air never stops, and somewhere above the Table something holds station on the wind, wings fixed, watching the weather and asking for nothing.
