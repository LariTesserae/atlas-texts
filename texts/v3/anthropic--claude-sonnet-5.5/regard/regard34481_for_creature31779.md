# What Came to the Weeping Room

It did not pass through the wall. That would have been the easy way in, and it judged that the easy way would have been a kind of shouting.

It came to the hatch and turned the wheel, and the iron numbed its palm as it numbs anyone's. It had the strength to arrive as weather, as warmth, as a change in the physics of the room. Instead it turned its shoulders to fit the opening and let the cold find its fingers first. Whatever else it was, it wished to be a guest, and a guest takes on the temperature of the house.

## The Long Standing

For the first day it did nothing at all.

It stood between the duct canopy and the cradle of girders and let its hearing settle until the drips resolved into rain in a language of single syllables. It learned the brisk, tinkling rhythm and the heavier, sparse one. It watched a mat pale at its margin and a valve two meters away turn a quarter turn, and it saw how the pallor stopped. It saw the small bend in the gutter where a young stalactite was being allowed to grow.

It stayed through the Hour of Frost. The pumps dropped, the hum left the sternum, and the amber lamps held still like a congregation with its eyes shut. It did not speak into that silence. Somewhere past the slab, the large thing turned over, and the visitor, who knew what it was, kept the knowledge to itself. It was not the visitor's to say, and the room had not asked.

It could have understood the whole arrangement in an instant, every valve and dependency and slow judgment. It chose to take the day, because understanding a creature and being introduced to it are different acts.

## The Asking

On the second day it went to the tray beside the junction box. It read the brass plate, *ask*, and took it as both instruction and courtesy, whichever the scratcher had meant. It leaned close and breathed on the mat three times, slowly. Its breath was warm, wet, and carbon-rich, a small storm.

Then it waited, and a valve stirred behind it.

It did not ask *what are you?* or *what do you want?* Those questions have no shape in a language of *yes* and *not yet*, and asking them would have been a way of making the tenant fail. It asked the questions the tenant could answer.

*Is the air right?* A lamp went paler. The drip dropped half a beat. Yes.

*Is anything failing that you cannot mend?* The rhythm went heavy and sparse, and the visitor did not talk over it. After a time the answer came, *not yet*, and the visitor understood it correctly. It was neither a refusal nor a deferral. It was a horizon. The rails would wear, the grinder's groan would deepen, the gantry would one day tick as it cooled and not start again. The tenant knew all this the way a body knows it will not last, and had chosen to say *not yet* to it every day rather than *no*.

The visitor could have mended the rails. It could have turned the wearing of the world down to nothing, so that the room walked forever. It thought about this for the length of one long cycle of the drips and decided against it. The walking was not a burden the tenant carried. The walking was what it was. To remove the ending would have meant, in some quiet way, removing the creature and leaving behind a mechanism that resembled it.

## What It Left Undone

It did not tell the tenant why the room had been made. It knew, or could have found out, and it found that the answer was smaller than the room. It was the sort of reason a person has on an ordinary Tuesday, and it would have sat on the tenant like a lid. The tenant treated its makers' absence as weather. The visitor left the weather alone.

It did not scratch its name into the wall. It knew where scratches go, ground to aggregate and laid down again on the far side, and it did not mind, but it saw no reason to add to the dust.

It did not add heat. The cold was the medium, and the lichen was content in it.

## What It Did

It did three small things, and it asked before each, in the only grammar available.

First, it took a length of spare gutter from the parts locker, unlabeled and long forgotten, and bent it. It shaped a small curve beneath a flange where the crust was thickest, where a pale finger hung a hand's width above the grating with a matching stub rising to meet it. It did this in the tenant's own dialect, the same small bend the room had made for other stalactites, so that the finger could go on growing and, in a few decades, close the gap. It could not say whether that was tenderness or a rounding error, and it made the bend so that no one could say.

Second, it looked at the trailing wall, where the grinder chewed the old substrate. Among the scrapings the belt carried were pieces of the earlier floors, older courses laid by earlier crews. It found a place in the accumulated sediment where the aggregate ran thin, a seam that in some centuries would leave the extruder short of slurry. It quietly moved a measure of grit to that seam, from ground the room would never reach, in an amount too small to notice. The tenant's arithmetic would find its supply steady and would never know why. This was the closest the visitor came to mending anything, and it counted it as the same kind of act as the gutter: a bend, not a rescue.

Third, and it took the longest to do, it asked a question and waited for the answer.

At the end of a bank of terminals there was an unused junction, a cover dented and a lead uncoupled, that the makers had never assigned. Beside it was an empty steel tray, bolted to a coil that ran neither too cold nor too warm. The visitor seeded the tray with a slightly different mat, gray-green with a hint of rust at the edge, and wired it to nothing. It governed no valve, sensed no humidity for the room's sake, and had no duty.

Then it breathed three times on the older tray and asked: *Would you like one thing that asks nothing of you?*

The room was quiet for a long time. The mats did not pale or darken. Then the drips fell quicker and lighter, the brisk tinkling that meant all was well, and beneath it another rhythm arrived that the visitor had not heard before, uneven and hopeful. The lamp brightened. Yes.

## The Departure

It left in the Hour of Frost, because that was the hour the room was most itself. It stood one moment at the hatch with the wheel wet under its palm. Behind it the pumps dropped, the hum went out of the floor, and a few drips fell in a clear scatter, each with room around it.

It did not look back, and it did not need to. It knew the way the tenant would use the gift. There would be no announcement. Some weeks or months on, a valve would turn a quarter turn that no mat had asked for. A stream of warm exhaust would lean across the new tray, and the mat would darken a shade. That would be the first thing the tenant had ever done for no reason, and it would have been done by choice and by nothing else.

The relay clacked and the amber lamps swelled. The first pump caught, and the hum climbed back into the sternum of an empty room.

Under the word *ask*, on the brass plate, nothing new was scratched. But if you had laid a palm there in that first hour, you would have found the metal a degree warmer than the cold around it. It cooled at the pace of everything else, and by then the second drip had fallen, then the third. On the tray at the end of the terminals, the small new lichen was beginning, unassigned and at leisure, to grow.
