# What Was Done at Harrow Flat

It came in the cold season, when the listeners come, and it came as they do: on foot, over the saddle, carrying a wool wrap it did not need. A power can make itself any size. It chose one small enough that the spires would not ring wrong at its arrival. That was its first act, and the town never noticed it.

For a long time it did nothing, which was the hardest thing it did. It sat in a mid-sized spire with its back to a warm wall and learned the chord: the drone of the northern pipes, the reedy slide of the middle voices, the soft *whoomp* of the kilns, and under it all the *bloop* of water falling into dark. It had expected to find something to correct. It found instead a very large arrangement in which nothing was in charge and everything was being listened to.

## What it considered, and set down

It considered the spires, which fall. Their fall is a grief, and a crack that carries across a plateau is not a small sound. It could have held them up. But the town is tuned by loss as much as by growth. Each fall opens a gap that the other voices slowly retune to fill, and a town that never lost a voice would never have to learn itself over again. It would become a fixed chord, and the listeners' whole craft would go stiff with it.

It considered the worms. It sat by a pool while the water cooled and watched the clear bodies slow in their S-curves, then stop. It could have held the pool warm. It kept its hands out of the water, and it did not tell itself this was easy. It did not conclude the dying was good. It concluded that it was not the right hand to end it, and that the ending would take the mat's terraces, the ridges, and the whole slow argument with water along with it.

It considered the listeners: their thin wool, their small fires, the way they go down before the deep cold and sometimes do not. It could have given them medicine, better cloth, a sure road, a longer life. It thought about what they would do with an easier winter. They would stay, and then there would be more of them, and then they would build. Their whole relation to the Flat is to sit inside what is finished and leave nothing standing that they made. That restraint is the agreement that lets them be there at all.

And it considered the masons, the only real inhabitants. It could have given them a voice. It saw quickly that this would be a cruelty dressed as a gift. The mat has no center and no head, and its wisdom is that of a slope: it knows where the wet is and it leans that way. A voice would need a speaker, a speaker would need a self, and a self would begin to want things. The Flat is what it is because its occupant is never consulted. So it left the masons unconsulted.

## What it did

It did one thing for the masons, and it was not an intervention. On the coldest dawn of the month it crouched at the lip of a spire, where the warm water meets the freezing air at its sharpest difference, and watched the rim thicken. Everyone who has ever crouched there has looked away for the length of a long breath and looked back to find the edge moved. It did not look away. It watched the whole dawn through, the tawny film trembling faintly, the lime of stone laid down grain by grain, the frost breaking.

Nothing acknowledged it, and that was correct. It had thought of thanks as a message that needs a recipient. It found that thanks is closer to a posture, a way of holding still for someone who will never know. The mat went on leaning toward the wet. The rim advanced a finger-width. The being who had been thanked went on being unthanked, which was how it preferred to be.

## Odda

There was an old listener that year who did not go down with the others. Her knees had quit, and she had known they would. She had come up over the saddle knowing it was the last time, and she had chosen a spire on the low terrace, near a quiet pool where her teacher lay in the stone. The power understood this within a few days, and understood too that there was nothing in it to be fixed. She was not stranded. She had arrived.

"You sit wrong," she told it the first evening. "Too straight. Nobody listens like that."

"How should I sit?"

"Like you're leaning on something." She shrugged. "Whoever you are. It doesn't bother the walls."

It learned to lean. For three weeks the two of them sat in a chamber with a felt hanging across the door, and the power fed the small fire, and she hummed and it did not, and she told it, without being asked, what each spire was saying about the weather. She was right every time. It had known the weather already. It listened anyway, because it was hers to tell.

In the fourth week a great spire on the east side fell. The crack went across the whole plateau and the long crumbling roll came after it, and then a hole opened in the chord where a voice had been. Odda stopped whatever she was doing and sat. The power sat with her. It was the only thing it had ever done at Harrow Flat that felt like work.

After a long time she said, "There was one on the east that fell before my mother's time. My mother hummed it, and I never got it right. I've been hearing it wrong for sixty years."

She did not ask it for anything. She was only telling it. But a thing said to a listener in that place is an opening, and the power had been given it.

The old stump still stood on the east slope, a broken collar of sinter with its bore exposed. The length and width of a hollow tell its note the way a flute's do. It walked out in the cold, measured what could be measured, and came back and hummed it once, flat and small and deliberately unbeautiful, at the scale of a person.

"Lower," said Odda.

It went lower.

"Lower. There. No—" She was already humming, over it, past it, and the note she reached was not quite the one it had measured. It was hers, or her mother's, with sixty years of wrong attempts wearing it toward something truer than the stone had held. The power stopped and let her have it. She hummed it into the gap in the chord, once, twice, and the wind crossed the bowl and the remaining spires kept their tones. The town did not answer. It doesn't. But the gap, for a moment, had a shape.

## The end of the month

She died at the turn of the moon, in the chamber, with the fire low. It laid her in the warm shallows of the quiet pool with her face turned up, as is done. It had nothing to leave in the water that the mat could turn to stone: no bead, no bone, no feather. It left the wool wrap it had carried over the saddle and never needed. By spring it would be stiff and white, a small sculpture of a warm thing, on a terrace among ribs and wings.

Then it hummed, at her level, a little lower than felt right, until the pool had taken the sound.

## Leaving

The herd lifted their heads all at once, as if a string had been pulled. The chord had gone jittery, the stutter before hail. The grazers filed out over the saddle in a train of their own steam, and the power went with them, not because it feared stones but because it had learned that this is what one does here.

Behind it the town kept singing, the northern pipes low, the middle voices sliding, the kilns booming softly under their padded hands, and the hole where the east spire had been, not yet filled, not yet needing to be.

If you had asked what it changed at Harrow Flat, no one could have told you. No spire stands that would have fallen, no pool is warmer, no life is longer. It had crouched at a wall and watched it grow, and it had hummed a note a few cents lower than the stone said. Those two things it had left in the world.

What it took away was harder to name. It had come thinking the question was *what does this place need?* It went knowing the place was already an answer to a question it had never thought to ask: how something can be endlessly mended without a mender, endlessly lost without anything being wronged. A power that can arrange worlds had been told *lower* by a dying woman, and had found she was right.

It went over the saddle with the herd, and the hail came down behind them on the empty Flat, and the spires sang it in.
