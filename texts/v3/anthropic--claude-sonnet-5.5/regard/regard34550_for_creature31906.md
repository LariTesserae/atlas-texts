# What Was Done at the Sill

It came by the stair, because the stair was the only part of the city that asked nothing of the water, and it wished to ask nothing either.

It could have come any other way. It could have been the fog, or the light at noon, or the pressure of the thin air. But some courtesies are best made in the shape the place has prepared for a guest. So it climbed, and the treads were a hand-width too deep for a foot, and at the fortieth step a sharp edge caught its sleeve. It let the sleeve be caught. It stood there a moment, held by the stone, and understood that this was the first thing the city had said to it: *nothing here has been walked much.*

## What it did not do

A power that is wise begins with the list of things it could do, and spends most of its attention on that list.

It could wake the sleepers in the rose rooms. It looked at the cheek-round stone and the folded shadow far inside it, and it heard the water finding its way. It saw that they had not died and had not been taken. They had lain down in what they shaped and let the water finish, as one lets a sentence finish. To wake them would be to interrupt a sentence for the sake of the listener's impatience. The sleepers had chosen a slowness that no one is owed the right to shorten, so it left them.

It could restore what was wearing away. The copper wedges were going under the lime, and the niches would be smooth in a century. It considered lifting them out and setting them on the pavement, bright and safe. But the mineral was closing over them the way a scar closes over a splinter, and a scar is not a failure of the body. The wedges had been set down by hands that meant to return. The city was returning them, in its own way, to the wall. Rescue would only have been the visitor's grief dressed up as the city's need.

It could populate the plateau. It could seed the alleys with small quick lives, with wings and footfalls, so the place would no longer be so alone with its dripping. It found the thought almost tender and almost obscene. The silence was not an absence to be filled. It was the shape of the instrument. A room built to ring does not want a crowd.

It could explain. It could stand in the middle terrace and say, in a voice that reached every cell, who had built this and why and where they had gone. But the not-knowing was part of the city's architecture. The handprints were left ambiguous, and the count of them was left wrong, and the shadow in the rose stone was left unnamed. To answer would be to seal a door that was, so far, only pleasantly ajar.

So it did none of these things, and this restraint was the largest part of what it did.

## What it did

It did four small things. None of them could be proved afterward.

**First, it set a hand to a wall.** It chose a north face, where the orange crust had crept over the block and stopped, and it pressed its palm to the cream stone beside the crust, as the masons had pressed clay. The wall kept the warmth for a few minutes, a dark print that faded. That night the lime would lay its first hair across the place. The print was neither larger nor stranger than the hundred others. A power that wished to be honest about its visit made a hand like the others, and let it be one more. The city already held more prints than any crew would need, and now it held one more than that, and no one would ever count.

**Second, it left a wedge of copper.** It did not bring the metal from elsewhere. It asked a little of the mineral in the plateau's own stone, and the stone gave it up, and the power beat it thin against a flat block with a harder stone, in overlapping rows of tiny craters. It set the wedge in a niche beside a basin, at the height of a hand, and did not take it back. It knew the lime would reach it in its own time and that the bloom would go blue-green where the water touched. This was its signature, the only one it would leave. It was a promise to return that it had no intention of keeping, in the exact manner of the builders, and it did this on purpose. It wished to be one more absence the city kept.

**Third, it held the fog.** The next morning, when the low cloud settled and the orange crust deepened from dull ochre to the color of a struck coal, it kept the damp on the plateau for a few minutes past the hour when the sun would have burned it off. That was all. The lichen drank a little more than its allotment, a few minutes' worth in two hundred years of minutes. It was the nearest thing the plateau has to a face, and the power let that face stay bright a moment longer. It was not much of a gift, but it was the right size.

**Fourth, it sang the song through.**

It had listened to the jars and the hollow pillars at the edge of the plateau, and it had understood the hum: a slow descent through a scale, cut into the stone as bores and stops, and never once finished, because the wind has never held long enough or steadily enough to reach the bottom. The city had been trying to complete a phrase for longer than there had been anyone to hear it fail.

The power did not touch the pillars. It did not retune or repair them. The song was theirs and the tuning was the builders', and the incompleteness was, in its way, part of the composition. It simply arranged the weather. For one night it drew the wind across the plateau's edge from a single quarter, without gusts or lulls, patient as a breath let out slowly by someone who has all night. The first pillar sang, and the second, and each note stepped down in its turn. The sequence went lower than anyone had heard it go, into a register the stone had been cut to hold and had never been given the air to reach. At the last bore, at the bottom of the scale, the whole edge of the plateau gave one long, round, settled note. It sounded less like an ending than like a stone set down at last on level ground.

Then the wind let go, and the city began again from the top, as it always had, unaware that it had ever been otherwise. Nothing changed. The drips went on at every pitch. The channels ran. Whether the sleepers in the rose rooms heard, the power did not say. But the water in the walls, which has no pitch, may for one hour have sounded very slightly like it was keeping time.

## The one who was there

There was a visitor that night, or there may have been. The piece of the place that lives in it, the being who lay awake in an upper cell with their feet near a basin the color of milk-glass, was someone who had come by the dry stair and stayed. Perhaps they were a traveler. Perhaps they were one of the prints. The power did not ask, because the answer would have changed nothing about what it owed them.

What it owed them was to be undisturbed. It did not visit them and did not speak. It gave them, as it gave the crust and the film and the sleepers, only the chance to be present when the song went all the way down. The being heard it. They may have wept, or only shifted their weight. They may have thought it was the wind. In the morning the fog lay a little longer on the walls, and they watched the orange deepen and did not know why it seemed to them that they were being kept company.

## Afterward

A wise power measures its work by how little of it shows. By that measure the visit succeeded. If you climb the stair now, the treads will catch your sleeve, the water will run downhill in order, and the lime will lay down its hair of stone each season. There is, in a north-facing wall, one print more than the masons would need, and in a niche a wedge of copper whose edge is just beginning to bloom. Nothing else is different.

Except that once, for a night, the instrument was played to the bottom of its range, and the players who had gone to rest were not disturbed by it, and the one who listened understood that the city is not waiting for anyone. It is not lonely, or unfinished in any way that needs a finisher. It is a slow, kept household, and the water is still laying down a little more of the world, and now and then, when the weather is kind, it sings.
