# What Came to Listen

It arrived the only way that made sense there, which was quietly. A power that speaks at the volume of powers would only have added a shout to the chorus, and a shout in the Flats is nothing. The frogs would have taken it up as one more rhythm and passed it along.

So it made itself small enough to fit in the gaps, and for a long while it did nothing but listen.

It learned the ninety frogs the people know by ear, and the hundreds they don't. It learned that the silence before a tremor has a shape, a wedge spreading through the sound like water through cloth. It learned that when one particular beetle stops droning, a snake is on the trunk. It learned to walk the basalt plates with knees bent, heel first, and understood from the way the plates clinked under it that its first steps had been loud. It felt something close to embarrassment. The people of the Flats would have called it a stranger's walk.

## What it could have done

It saw very quickly what it could fix. That was never the hard part.

It could have quieted the ground, closed the fissures, settled the slabs, and made the basin a finished, sober country. No child would ever again be tied to a trunk at the wrist. No red knot would ever be tied in a cord.

It could have given the old ones their hearing back, or thinned the fog and let light in, so that eyes would matter more than palms. It could have taught them to build, to store, to make things that last, and to be a thousand instead of two hundred.

It considered each of these seriously, because the red knots were real. It counted them. It did not pretend that a people who tie a knot for their dead have made peace with death in any way that makes the dying free.

But it followed each gift to the end of its consequences, and each one ended in the same place.

The tremor is not a flaw in the Flats. It is the same thing as the drip, the vents, the fresh edges on every stone, and the warm fissures that make the fog and feed the green. The ground is being finished, and that is the whole reason for the life on it. Stopping the tremors would either leave the basin permanently unfinished, which is a kind of death, or finish it into a stillness that is another kind. The frogs are a single animal thinking, and they think in response to the ground's warnings. Without the warnings they would be only noise.

The cords are the people's whole memory, and they matter because the paths change. A people that never needed to mend anything would have nothing to hold in their hands. An old ear is honored here because the old woman's palm on the slab is worth more than the young man's hearing. In a world of light and ease she would be pitied.

It did not want to win an argument with the place by making the place into something else.

It also distrusted its own reasoning here, because the cost of its restraint would be paid by others. The children on their cords would be the ones to pay it. So it looked for evidence that the people were asking to be saved, and it found none. A people who call deafness "an old ear," who play *finding the hole* and stop their work to join in, who tap three times on a trunk beside the hollow where something groans, as a courtesy and not a prayer, are not asking for a different world. They are asking for the ordinary thing everyone asks for, which is to be good at living in the one they have.

So it did not still the ground, or thin the fog, or teach anyone anything.

## What it did do

**It kept a fire.**

A boy of about thirteen was on watch at a hearth under a leaf-thatch, in the small hours when the fog was thickest and every voice seemed to come from behind. He had been awake two nights running. His hand was on the warm slab, as he had been taught, and his head went down for what he thought was a moment. In the damp, a moment is enough for a fire to go to grey.

The power fed the fire, a stick at a time, using nothing a person could not have used. It kept the coals red for the hour he slept and did no more. It did not wake him, warn him, or touch the stone.

He woke ashamed, checked the fire, found it well, and decided he had only dozed. The old woman across the hearth had been awake the whole time and knew he had slept. She also knew the fire had been tended by no hand she could account for.

She looked at the fire for a while. Then she tapped once on the slab, softly, the way one acknowledges a passing guest in a narrow place. It asked nothing and expected no answer. The power did not answer, and that seemed to be understood too.

**It held back the ants.**

This is the one act it is still unsure of.

Once, on a night of thick fog, a child's wrist-cord parted, and she fell three body-lengths into the litter. She lay stunned in the dark among the leaves while the first column of ants, thick as rope, turned toward her. Above, a hammock hung empty and a clapper had just begun to ring.

For eleven minutes the column went around her. Ants are erratic, and columns turn for all sorts of reasons. The band found her by the tapping, cut her free of the leaf-litter, and never knew anything had happened except that she was lucky.

It has thought about that night more than any other. It does not know why that child and not the others it counted over the long season, the ones who fell and were not found or were found too late. It has not found a clean line between mercy and meddling. It knows only that the line is not where a rule would put it, and that it will not pretend otherwise. It did this once, and it does not intend to make a habit of a choice that is only defensible when it is rare.

**It did not listen to the palms.**

This may be the truest thing it did.

It could have. The people of the Flats are a people of confidences, and their speech is built so that two people shoulder to shoulder can say things the whole basin cannot overhear. The power was the one thing in the basin that could have overheard. Every joke told in the dark, every fear pressed into a hand, was available to it.

It chose, from the beginning and without exception, to be part of the noise around those conversations and not part of the listening. The privacy of the palms was the one thing the people had built, apart from the cords, and it was the one thing the power did not enter.

## Leaving

When it was ready to go, it went the long way, through the middle of the basin where the ground is newest.

It passed a warm hollow where the fog lay deepest, where something large made a sound like a long exhale through a wooden pipe. There it stopped by the nearest trunk. It did not know what lived there, and it understood that nobody else did either, and that this was part of the arrangement.

It tapped three times, slowly, on the wood, without any expectation of an answer.

The groaning went on. Behind it a frog tried a note, and the whole basin roared in, louder than before.

Far off, where the ground was newest, a fresh crack opened with a sound like a great branch snapping. Everything stopped, and then everything began again. Somewhere a woman with a length of bark-cord in her hand was already walking toward it.

She would tie a flat loop, and the path would be different by exactly one knot.

The power left it that way.
