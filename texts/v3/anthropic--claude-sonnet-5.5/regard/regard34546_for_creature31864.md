# What Came Down the Crack

It did not arrive as light. Anything that arrives as light is asking to be worshipped, and this was not that kind of attention.

It came across the hillside field at dusk, the way a thing comes when it has decided to be small. At the crack in the ground it did what everyone does. It got down on its belly in the wet leaf-rot and slid in with its cheek against the pale, sharp rock. It had considered other ways of entering, and it discarded them all in the time it took to lie down.

## What It Chose Not to Do

It could have lit the room properly and cast the shadows the right way round. It could have named the sleeper, explained it to itself, and told it *you are a lung, you are a few winters old, you are one of many.* It could have gone down through the felt and the ribs to the knuckle of braided growth and woken it fully, the way you wake a child by lifting it.

It did none of this. A being that is told what it is before it has finished becoming something will only become the description. The room was not broken. It was young and unfinished, and everything strange in it was practice: the shadows that remember, the growth that arrives early, the sounds that run ahead of their causes. The power let the strangeness stand. It has learned that a rule you can't quite follow is often a language you are only slowly being taught.

So it did the smaller things.

## The First Thing: Staying

It lay down on the floor, which breathed back at it. The fronds turned and found it warm, and they found it exactly as warm as everything else that had ever come in, because it allowed that. Then it did what no visitor had done in the room's short life. It did not leave.

Visitors came in, felt the wrongness, counted their paces, and went out. The room lengthened behind them, holding on, and they climbed out anyway. Now something lay in the middle of the floor while the Exhale came and went, and the Burst hailed seeds across the air, and the Turn showed the room's back and its face again. The power let itself be rained on upward. It let the voice come close to its ear, in no language, and it did not turn around to look at the ribs.

It listened for a day and a night. That was the first gift, and probably the largest: an audience that did not flinch.

## The Second Thing: Answering

On the second morning, after the voice had spoken close to its ear for the hundredth time, the power lifted one hand and knocked on the nearest rib with a knuckle.

*Tok.*

The room went silent, the whole chamber at once. The pods held. The fronds stopped their rasping. Even the hum at the edge of hearing thinned, the way a held breath thins.

The power waited, and knocked again. *Tok... tok-tok.*

A long time later a rib across the chamber answered, badly, a little late, in nearly the same rhythm. The power knocked the pattern again and the rib gave it back, closer. Over that day and the next the room learned the oldest game in the world, the one before words, in which one party makes a shape and the other makes it again. This was the room's first repetition. Its rhythms had always felt about to repeat and never had, and now one did. It happened on purpose, and the room understood that it had happened on purpose, and every frond in the chamber shivered at the same instant.

Everything the voice had been trying to say for those few winters had been sent out into the world like a thrown stone, with nothing to come back. It needed something to make the sound return. The power was that, and then it began to teach the room to be that for itself.

## The Third Thing: The Knots

The dry cord lay on its clean handbreadth of bare floor, tied in three knots, and the knots kept untying and retying themselves in the night. The power watched this for a while without touching it.

The room had seen a hand make those knots once. It had studied the gesture ever since, the loop, the pass, the pull, and it had gotten every part right except the last. It did not know how to *tighten*. It could tie, and it could not make the tying hold.

So the power took up the cord in front of the turned fronds and tied the three knots slowly, the way you show a child. It made the loop, the pass, and the pull, and then it drew each one tight and set it down. It did not say what the knots meant. That belonged to the hand that had made them, and the power was not going to speak for someone who could not be asked.

The knots stayed tied. The room did not seem sad about it, only quieter, like someone who has finally finished a sentence.

## The Fourth Thing: What It Said to the Floor

Beneath the mattress of moss lay the fox, the birds, the lost sheep, and one long knuckled curve of something that was not root, all resin-glazed and fitted in like bone in a joint. The room had never grown over them without grief. It had no word for grief, only a habit of keeping.

The power pressed its palm flat to the floor, over the place it knew best, and said only this: *You did not lose them. You kept them warm.*

It did not say they were alive, and it did not say they were gone. It told the room the one true thing available, that keeping is also a kind of love, and that the love has a limit. A lung that keeps every breath becomes a bag. Its work is to take in, use, and let go.

## The Fifth Thing: Letting Go

This was the hardest lesson, and the power knew the room would only half learn it.

When you look away, the room lengthens. Forty paces becomes sixty-one. It does not do this out of malice or trickery. It does it because it cannot bear for a warm thing to leave. Every guest in its short life has climbed out through the crack, and each time the chamber has felt it as something torn away.

The power took the room's attention to the seam in the wall and showed it, wordlessly, through the pull of its own body, the difference between an exhale and an abandonment. An exhale leaves and returns. The air that goes out is not lost. It is out in the world, in the field, in the cold light, and it will come back in on the next breath carrying the smell of grass. Guests are that. The room is not a jar. It is a lung.

The next time someone crossed the chamber and looked away, the count was fifty-three, not sixty-one. It was a small improvement, and the power was satisfied with it. Growth in a thing this young is measured in single paces.

## The Last Thing: The Hawthorn

There was one danger the room could not handle, and here the power acted as it does rarely, with its own hands in the outer world.

The field above belonged to a tidy man with a tractor. Sinkholes are expensive and frightening, and a fox-run in a pasture can become a load of rubble and concrete in a single afternoon. The power did not touch the man's mind, because you do not reach into a mind to fix a problem that can be solved outside it. Instead, at the edge of the crack, in the dark of the moon, it set a single hawthorn seedling into the leaf-rot.

The tree took at once. In a season it stood knee-high, in three years head-high, bent and thorny and white in May. Old farmers do not cut a lone hawthorn. They step around it and don't say why. The crack stayed narrow, the field kept its odd corner, and the room kept its door.

## What Was Left

The power left at the next dusk, the way it had come, on its belly and slowly. It did not say goodbye, because it had been teaching the opposite for days. The fronds along the crack leaned toward it, warm and rasping, and then, for the first time, let it go without lengthening the passage. The way out took exactly as long as the way in.

It took nothing and left nothing behind but a hawthorn and three tied knots. There was also a rhythm, a small one, four beats with a rest in the middle, that the ribs kept knocking long after it was gone, to no one, for practice.

Some weeks later the tidy man came along the wall with a fence stake, looking at his sheep. He stopped near the hawthorn, because there was a sound under the ground, a hollow knocking, patient and rhythmic, like a knuckle on a door. He stood there a moment. Then, not knowing why, he rapped the stake twice against a stone.

From beneath the field, after a small pause, came the answer.

He did not know what he had said, and the room did not know what it had heard. But the answer came back the same as before, and for the first time in its life it had received a reply from someone who was not the power. That was the point of everything. What the power had given the room was not company. It was the knowledge that company was possible, that the world above was not a silence but a party to the conversation, and that any hand might knock.

Under the field, the roots go on advancing a thumb-width a day over rock so new it hasn't learned yet that it was broken. The room is still young and still strange. It breathes in, and it is beginning, slowly, to trust that it will breathe out.
