# The Guest Who Set Down Its Edges

## What It Considered

It looked for a long time before it came down, and the looking was the first thing it did.

From far enough above, the basin is a single gesture: a faint silver-blue tracery under a lid of cloud, a city with no walls, a script in a language that was never taught. The power had seen ruined worlds and worlds that only needed a hand, and it had a great many remedies. It went through them one at a time, and it put each of them down.

*It could dredge the channels.* But they are meant to slump and re-cut, and a hundred thousand years of softening is a kind of argument.

*It could guard the fen.* But from what? The place had survived its own sinking and rising, and had learned to do neither with conviction. A guardian would be the first thing in the basin that was afraid.

*It could explain.* It could tell the stayers what the lattice is, tell the herons why they tilt, tell the Long Backs what waits at the source of the rising rain. But an answer given from outside is a wall, and the whole basin is built, if it is built, of things without walls.

*It could heal the stayers' losses.* This it held longest, because it was the tenderest, and because it saw that the losses were not wounds. They were entries. A mother's name goes into the water, and the water keeps it. To reach in and pull it back out would be to call the ledger a mistake.

So it concluded what the wisest visitors conclude, which is that the first gift to a balanced thing is not to unbalance it. It resolved to do as little as could still be called doing something, and to make sure the little was true.

## Arrival

It came without edges, and this was harder than it sounds.

It had been many things with edges: verdicts, remedies, the sharp clarity of *this, not that*. At the rim, where the turf starts to shiver, it set these down the way a traveler sets down a knife. Then it watched them rust, and let them. It asked the fen to dissolve it slowly, so that the season the fen takes for an intruder would be long enough to learn something in.

It came as weather. Late one afternoon a squall crossed the basin, a little warmer than usual, a little slower to drum out. Every leaf rang. The channels ran brown and bank-full and drained by morning. Nothing seemed different except that the frogs, at dusk, held their silence one heartbeat longer than they ever had, and then resumed as though embarrassed to have been caught listening.

## The Learning

For what was, at the rim, an afternoon and, at the center, most of a year, it did what the herons do. It stood in the shallows and attended. It let the mist form on it and bead on it and run off. It walked as the stayers walk, in the shorter direction. It drank from a pitcher plant after the light had settled and learned something about a beetle. It followed a snail's line for a hand's breadth and turned where the snail turned, and thought: *so this is how a thing gets copied out.*

The lattice took it in without ceremony. It was one more thing the water carried. That was the most moving fact of the whole visit, and the power noted it and said nothing.

## The Measuring

Near the end of the first season, the mist made one of its tall, long-legged shapes at the edge of an oxbow. The power, which had learned by then how to be a shape, stepped into it. It did not make itself the shape. It kept company with it, the way the Long Backs do.

An old Long Back, thick with gnarled seedlings and lichened in all six colors, lifted its head above the waterline and stopped. Then it swam, slowly, along the whole length of the mist, from where the feet would be to where the shoulder is. It was measuring.

The power let itself be measured. This was nearly the hardest thing it did, because for a very long time it had been the one who measures. The old animal took its time. Its wake arrived on the bank long after it had passed. When it reached the shoulder it paused, and the pools around it began to ring softly, the rim of the glass rubbed once. Then it sank, and the mist was only mist, and the power understood that it had been counted. It had been accepted as a thing that might have stood there.

## The Afternoon at the Spring

There was one act that came near to interference, and the power weighed it for the length of a dusk.

The oldest of the stayers walked the circuit of springs in the shorter direction and had done so for longer than she could say. She had lost her mother's name. She could point to the place in herself where it had been. She did not ask for it back, and the power would not have moved for a request that was never made. But she stood at the northern spring each day, at the hour when the water tasted of a particular afternoon, and each day she arrived a few minutes after the afternoon had ended.

Time is local. It is the one law the fen keeps loosely enough that a hand might lean on it.

So the power leaned, very slightly. It did not restore anything or pull anything from the water. It held the afternoon at the northern spring open, the way you hold a door for someone who is nearly there. She arrived. She knelt. She drank.

The water did not tell her the name as a memory. It gave it to her as news, the way a spring gives you a thing you did not know. There was a kitchen in it, and a voice calling across a yard, and the name, spoken with the particular impatient love of someone who has called it many times. She did not remember her mother. She learned her mother, as one learns a stranger who turns out to be the best person one has met.

She stayed kneeling until the afternoon closed. Then she stood, and tilted her head a few degrees toward the center of the basin, and smiled, and walked on in the shorter direction. She said, of the water, *it is raining*, and it was the truest thing that could have been said.

The power took nothing back for this, because the ledger does not need a fee for a door held open. But it noticed the fourth rule watching it, and it understood that it would be asked to pay for something.

## The Payment

The fen took its payment in the way it takes all things, without haste or malice, from the place where the power was fullest.

The power had always known how things end. That was its kind of sight. Wisdom and benevolence had made it gentle, but the foreknowledge was there under the gentleness like bedrock, and it had walked into the basin resting on it.

The fen took that.

It didn't take all of it, only enough for the power to feel the place where it had been, as a stayer feels a missing name. It now knew the way a heron knows, by attending and by waiting to see where the frog would be. When it looked at the tall grasses, it did not know which way they would lean before the wind. When a pool rippled, it did not know what would fall.

It found this frightening for the length of one breath. Then it found it was the first time in an age that it had been surprised by a dragonfly. Its own wake now arrived late, on the bank, after it had passed, as the Long Backs' do. It was living, at last, at a slightly different hour from the rest of the world, and the fen was keeping its time for it.

It thought: *this is the whole of the price, and it is not a price. It is the thing I came to be given.*

## The Rising Rain

When the old Long Back turned toward a column of drops lifting out of a black pool, the power followed at a distance, and then closer, and then beside it.

It did not stop the animal. It did not ask whether the animal wished to be stopped. It walked, in the mist-shape it had learned, along the great mossed flank, and the rain went up around them both with its soft tick as the halves passed. The chord deepened until the pools rang. The shoulders of the old one thinned into green, and then into a silvered suggestion of more, and the power went with it as far as it could go and still be a guest.

What it saw there is not told, and it is not told because the power kept the fen's rule and did not turn seeing into a wall. But at the end of that winter, a spring on the western rim began to taste of something new. It was not a word or an afternoon. It was a view from a great height of a silver script under cloud, held with a care that no one at the rim had ever tasted before. The stayers who drank it walked on with their heads a little more tilted. The herons stood a little more still. The lattice, that year, glowed a shade brighter in the seep where the snails' handwriting was thickest.

It was, more or less, the only entry in the ledger that had ever been written from the outside. It said only: *seen. Kept. Balanced.*

## What Changed

Almost nothing.

The peat still moves. The mats still turn like the hands of a very large clock. The rain rises in its columns, and the pools show their other skies, and the reflected night is still sometimes a night of unfamiliar stars, though a stayer, if you asked, might say that one of the stars has begun to look back.

The Long Backs still give birth in the oxbows, and the calves are still planted, and nobody who watches can help lowering their voice. The frogs still stop all at once, at intervals no one has ever predicted, and the silence still lasts three or four seconds. Some evenings, now, it lasts a little longer, though no one has kept count, since counting requires edges.

Somewhere in the flooded trees a long low back breaks the surface, and the wake reaches the bank late. Beside it, faintly, in the mist, a second wake arrives later still.

## Dusk

The frogs begin, and the wave runs across the fen in cyan flicker and then in sound. The swifts stitch the mist. The stayers walk their circuits in the shorter direction.

Then everything stops, and in the hush a guest who is no longer certain how things end stands among the herons with its head at the slight angle and listens to the ledger, which has, this once, a line in it about being watched over by someone who understood that the kindest thing to give a finished thing is your attention, your company, and the willingness to be changed by it.
