# What Came to Listen

The attention arrived the way damp arrives, without a moment you could point to. First the hall was unobserved, and then it was observed. Nothing in the frogs' chorus changed. The heron lifted one foot and set it down in the same place.

What came was old, patient, and disposed toward kindness. It stood, in whatever way such a thing stands, at the rim of the Cistern for a long time before it went down.

## What It Set Down

It saw quickly what it could do, and the list was long, because it was powerful. It considered each item and set each one down again.

It could repair the roof. The timbers are black and drinking themselves toward collapse. But the light through that lattice is the only light the hall has ever known, and a mended roof would give a different hall.

It could give the salamander a voice. It could also bring it a second salamander, or a hundred, or a lineage to fill the pool. But nothing in the animal's long stillness looked like hunger. Loneliness is a human word, and it had been laid over this animal by a visitor who hummed at it for an afternoon and found himself moved. The power knew the difference between being alone and being attentive. The animal was not lonely. It was attending, and attending asks to be met by attention and by very little else.

It could call the builders back, or their echo, or a dream of them. But the builders had finished. Their work was to make a place that would outlast them and listen without them. To resurrect them would be to say the work had failed.

It could lengthen the animal's years. It held that one longest. The salamander was old, and the flowstone had begun to close over its back. But its kind live by slowness, and a century was already a generous wager against the world's hurry. The power chose not to touch the length of the life. It would touch one hour.

It could also have told the animal what it was, and what was watching. This it refused most firmly of all. Whatever the salamander knows, it knows through its jaw and its skin, and it has no use for a message from above.

## What It Saw

In the end it noticed what the visitor had almost noticed.

The niche the flowstone never closed holds a line of small things, each set near the last. The visitor took it for a sign of waiting, and the hall's own ache did lean that way. But the power looked longer and saw a ledger. The animal was not waiting for a song. It was keeping a count of everything the hall had been given: a pebble, a curl of wire, an egg's shell, a shard with a thumbprint. It set each thing down carefully, without ceremony, with the tenderness of a creature that has understood one fact about the world and built a life around it. Things arrive. Someone should notice.

That was the whole religion of the place. It was very nearly the power's own.

So the power did not think of mending anything. It thought of joining the ledger.

## The Season

First it listened. It did not descend in light or speak in the water. For one turn of the seasons it took the shape of a small attention low on the south wall, a presence like a jar with nothing in it, and pressed itself against the hall the way the animal did. It learned the drips and the jar-notes, and how the swifts' whistle rides above the frogs. It learned the circuit, the slow route around the ring, the same path the water takes but slower. It learned the hour when the copper bar crosses the far wall, and the small hush that follows it.

It learned, too, that for a hundred years the animal had been the only one to hear the hall whole, and that no one had ever heard the animal hearing.

## What It Did

Late in the wet season the rain came hard. The drips drew together. The jars fell into step, and the frogs caught the pitch and pushed it. The swifts joined, and the chord rose through the bowl until the pool trembled into its lattice of rings. The salamander lifted its head, flattened its plumes, pressed itself to the clay, and stopped breathing.

This is the moment when the chord always breaks. It has to, because a note built from leaks and birds cannot be held past its fullness. It gives way, and the animal's jaw falls open in the same instant, so that it has always seemed to be released instead of releasing.

The power did one thing. At the crest, where the chord would have cracked, it held the note for the length of a single heartbeat.

It added no sound and no light. It only refused, for a moment, to let the falling begin. For one heartbeat the whole hall stood on its held pitch, every jar and throat and wing poised, and the salamander, which had always been carried over the edge, opened its mouth on purpose. For that heartbeat it was letting go of the chord, and the chord was waiting to be let go of.

Then the swifts rose, the bowl boiled with wings, and the ringing came down over everything as it always had. The power changed nothing else. The animal lay with its mouth open and the black water draining round its jaw, looking like something that had been told a thing. This time it looked like something that had also said it.

## The Stone

Afterward the power gave one gift, so as to be counted.

It chose a river pebble, grey and flat and palm-sized, taken from a stream far from the marsh where it had lain for longer than the Cistern has stood. Water had been running over it so long that it hummed faintly when anything pressed against it. It was not a great sound, just the low, steady one a stream leaves in what it has shaped. The power set it at the end of the line in the unclosed niche, one hand's width past the thumbprinted shard, and left.

In the morning the mist rose off the pool. The salamander came up out of the shallows, slid along the fourth ring, and stopped at the niche. It laid its jaw against the new stone and stayed there for a long time, receiving something small and foreign and steady from a place it would never see.

Then it moved on to the next jar, as it had for a century, and the circuit went on.

## What Was Left

The roof still sags. The pool still climbs and falls through its notches. The flowstone still catches a little more of the salamander's back each year. Someday the animal will grow too still to be told from the wall, and the tree frogs will go on singing on a head that no longer minds. The power let that stand, since it would not grieve a thing for finishing. But the line in the niche will be found by someone, a visitor with cold hands or a bird with a bright eye, and it will be read as the record of something that was paying attention.

Near its end is a stone that hums, given by no one the animal knew. In the deep tally of the hall, the power is one item among the others, set down with care by a creature that noticed. It wanted no more than that.
