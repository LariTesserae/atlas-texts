# The Attention

It looked at the Flats for a long time before it did anything, and most of that time went to deciding what not to do.

It could have quieted the roar. But the roar is the wall around every private word the Lodgers have ever spoken. Without it, voices would carry, and they would no longer be a people who touch a shoulder before they speak.

It could have restored the rivets, or told them the true number, or told them there had never been a number that mattered. But the count is the only thing they hold that is theirs and not the machine's. A gift that finishes a history is a theft.

It could have mended the loose hearts of the bearings. It saw at once that this would be like sanding the knock out of the bearing: the ground would be smoother, and nobody would be left to carry news over the terraces.

It could have made the Lodgers unending. This was the temptation it lingered on longest, since it understood the ache in that last eleven seconds better than they supposed. But it saw that the Flats already have one thing that cannot end, and they have a great deal of it. The Drum has no need of mercy, and the Lodgers do. They give it to each other by hand, and that is the most valuable thing in the basin. Immortality would make them into more Drum.

So the power decided to do the smallest thing it knew how to do, which was to come as a guest.

---

It came on foot from the south, across the grey margin, and did not pretend to be anything. It had no wish to be lean and hollow-eyed like the ones who cannot forget the world before the basin, since it had not lost a world. It took a shape small enough to be spoken to, and that was harder than it expected. Its attention, which had never before been narrower than a coastline, had to fit inside the reach of one arm. It learned that a word must travel from a mouth to an ear across the width of a finger, and that anything larger is lost.

It crossed the ring of Stacks badly. It went in on the wrong beat, and a plume caught its sleeve and soaked it. A woman at the terrace lip watched with courteous interest and did not laugh, though the skin at the corners of her eyes creased. The power thought it was the most gracious thing it had been given in an age.

It stayed a season. It learned the pools: Soft, Hard, Slow, Bitter. It stood in the drizzle with its face up until the flint-and-egg smell turned from a smell into a kind of address. It ate the grey flour and tasted iron and salt. It walked out to the gull-edges, where the beaks opened on nothing, and stood among them until they forgot it. It let its breath fall behind the Drum and then in front of it, and it did not make it fit. A visitor's heart has to fight before it can be quiet. It wanted to fight honestly.

---

It did two things while it was there, and it did them the way the Lodgers do everything: one person at a time, in private.

The first was for the keeper of the rivets. She was old, and her thumb had gone smooth from reading the row. She was afraid of the last rivet, though she had never said why to anyone, because to say it she would have had to lay her lips against another person's ear and make the fear theirs forever.

The power found her on the cool side of the boiler in the afternoon. It laid a hand on her shoulder. She turned, and it waited for the thud to go through both their breastbones. In the eleven seconds that followed, it put its mouth to her ear and said what it knew:

*The boiler does not ring because of the rivets. It rings because the Drum strikes it through the ground, and it is stone now, and stone remembers. The rivets were only how you knew where to put your hand. When they are all gone, put your hand anywhere on it.*

She said nothing. She was still for one more stroke, then two. Then she put her palm flat against the crust and felt it ring, in a place where there had never been a rivet, and her shoulders let go of something they had been holding since she was a girl.

She told no one what she had been told. She still checked the row each season with her thumb. But the thumb moved differently after that, the way a hand moves over something it has decided to love and not merely to guard.

The second thing was for a young bearing whose heart ran quick. She had walked the long route to the far coast and come back, and she stood at the edge of the terraces with her palms over her ears, not yet ready to be in the Flats and no longer able to be outside it. The power did not tell her it would pass, and it did not tell her it wouldn't. Either would have been a lie, or a truth she had to find out for herself.

It walked with her the next time she went, all the way to the coast. There, where the silence has a noise of its own, it stood beside her while she covered her ears and shook. Then, at the moment she would have wept, it laid two fingers on her back, between the shoulder blades, and tapped once, and again eleven seconds after. It learned this from the Lodgers, and it did not pretend otherwise. It gave her something to stand on in a place with no floor, and it asked for nothing in return.

She said, with her hands, *You are not very good at this.*

*No,* said the power. *I'm learning.*

---

It changed nothing else. The Stacks fired in their sequence. The Whistlers held their sour chord. The Drum struck every eleven seconds, and the wave went round the ring and came back to its start. The rivets went on sinking at their old pace, one every few generations. The dead went on becoming a slight rise in the terrace lip on the eastern side. The power looked over its work and found it was almost nothing, and it thought that was the right amount.

On its last afternoon a Lodger walked up beside it. She was not the woman from the terrace lip but someone else, a man with a crust of white in his hair and knuckles swollen from years of tapping rock. He laid a hand between its shoulder blades and said nothing.

The hand stayed for eleven seconds, then eleven more.

The power had a heart because it had chosen one, and for a season the heart had fought the Drum. Now, with the hand on it, it felt the fight go out. It did not lose the fight so much as find that the fight had been a way of listening. Its breath fell in with the thud, and its pulse took the time, and for a moment the roar dropped away and it was inside the clock, small and warm and late, and glad of it.

Then it went, because it had to, and the man did not watch it go. They don't, here. A guest who leaves is like a plume that has fallen: the wave has already moved on, and the next chuff is the one that matters.

---

Far from the Flats, in the place where the power keeps everything it has ever attended to, there is now one small thing that ticks. It ticks once every eleven seconds and is permitted to be late. It has a rivet-count in the low sixties, which it will never tell anyone. It knows a word that was said once into one ear, and it holds it the way the Lodgers do: forever, and in private.

The power did not save the Flats, because the Flats did not need saving. What it did was learn to stand inside something it could not improve, and to hold its own great patience against the patience of a machine, and to find that the machine was the lesser of the two.

Somewhere on the eastern terraces, a woman steps carefully over a slightly rounded lip and touches her chest with two fingers. The Drum strikes. The boiler rings, faintly, in sympathy. Nobody has been told anything, and everyone knows a little more than they did.
