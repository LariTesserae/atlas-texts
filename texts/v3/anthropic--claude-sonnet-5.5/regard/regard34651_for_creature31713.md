# What Attended the Undercroft

It does not arrive. Arrival implies a direction, and the attention has none. What changes is the texture of the air, the way the fine rain is felt before it is seen. The moths, a stride apart on their ribs, do not stir. The heron keeps its foot lifted a moment longer than it should. Something has noticed the plateau the way a person, entering a room where someone is sleeping, notices that the sleeper is breathing.

It has power enough to do nearly anything, so its first act is to do nothing, and it does nothing for a long time.

## What It Refused

It considered finishing the roof. The naves are open at their crowns, and it could close them all in an afternoon: every rib met, every panel laced, the arch and its reflection made two things, every stair arriving somewhere. The result would be complete and dead. The vines would have nothing to reach for. The pale ones would hang in rafters with no cord to draw tight, and the tapping, which is the plateau's pulse, would stop for good.

It considered correcting the drift. The stairs that climb to nothing, the knots tied for no load, the gestures loosening from their reasons: these could be taught back into sense. But it saw that the vines are devout, and that devotion which outlives its reason is not error. It is the shape a memory takes when the rememberer is gone. To correct it would be to tell the dead they had been doing it wrong.

It considered waking the builders, or making them, or calling them out of the water. The small busy ones swing their adzes in the cisterns, certain they are alive. Perhaps they are. Perhaps they are what the cistern prefers to remember. It could not tell, and it did not think a wise thing should pretend to tell. To draw them up dripping into a hall built for thousands, where the beads would roll around the cathedral again, might be kindness. It might also be a theft from the water, and from whoever they are now.

And it considered the one who had come. It weighed the lag of the shadow, the patient courtesy of a hand that waits for its own shadow, the fiber twisting in fingers that knew the direction of the twist. There was a quiet loss in this, a self being loaned to the work, one habit at a time. It could lift the person out, sound and startled and grateful. But the person had not asked to be lifted, and taking the choice away in the name of preserving it is an old and familiar cruelty.

So it refused these four, and having refused them it had almost nothing left. Almost nothing is a great deal, and the power spent it carefully.

## What It Did

**It learned the trades.** For one season, in a form too small to remark on, it was apprenticed. It rested on a rib a stride from the next moth and opened and closed its wings until the slow blink came without thought. It laid a silver ladder up a wall and felt another creature take it up. It rolled a husk downhill toward the dark water and let it go and did not ask what the husk became. This was not disguise. It wanted to know what it was to be a thing that keeps a place without being told why, before deciding whether the place needed anything from it. It learned that the place needed very little, and that the little was a specific little.

**It answered the tapping.** Somewhere in the next nave the adze fell, right-handed, slightly unequal, as it had for centuries. The power let one stroke fall in the gap between two of them, from the other side, from a left hand.

The plateau stopped. The moths closed their wings, the frogs skipped their turn, the heron did not lift its foot. It was the same stillness that comes when the tapping pauses, but this time the pause was listening.

Then the old stroke fell, and the new one fell between, and the two settled after a few breaths into a rhythm that was not repetition. The adze had been a memory the wood kept striking against, and now for the first time the wood struck back. Somewhere overhead a rib took a cut that no one had cut before, a scallop turned the other way, and it was sound.

**It opened the lid.** This was the largest thing it did, and it did it once. At dawn, when the cloud breathes out toward the lowlands, it held the breath a little longer and parted the grey-green ceiling above one of the stairs that climbs to nothing.

The stair had been climbing toward this without knowing. Its last tread stood in a shaft of plain sun. The sun was not filtered through a hundred thousand leaves and had nothing of memory in it. It fell on the lashings and the polished treads, and the wood, which had been giving off the glow of a fire long out, took the light in as a person takes a name they have half forgotten. The glow in the grain dimmed, satisfied, and then returned, a little warmer than before.

It held the gap for as long as it takes to say what one has been trying to say for a long time, and no longer. It did not explain the sun to the wood or the stair. It closed the lid gently. The stair still climbs to nothing, but the nothing now has a place in it that the sun once entered.

The pale-faced ones came down for this. They crowded onto the lowest treads with their large dark eyes turned up, and their humming, which had the cadence of a work-song, slid for a moment into a different interval. Then the cloud closed and they climbed back into the roof-weave and drew cord tight, and the humming went on, with the new interval kept somewhere in it.

**It stilled the water.** For the length of one long breath the cisterns held their reflections. The small busy ones straightened from their scaffolds, and the one who presses a hand to the small of the back looked up at the place where the roof should close and, for once, did not look through the pale faces at the rim. She looked at them. Whether she saw them or only their reflection, no one can say. The pale ones rocked back on their heels. The water shivered, and everyone got on with the work.

## What It Did for the One Who Stayed

It did not lift them out and it did not close the door. It did one thing, in the only language the place has.

On the way down from the rim, at the worn lip of stone the softness of soap, where the climb was the last honest thing, it made one more honest thing. It made a flight of stairs with thirty-one treads going up and thirty-one coming down, on the first count and the second and every count after. It is the only flight on the plateau that behaves. It runs from the first nave to the lip, and it is not hidden.

Then it tied a knot in a length of fiber and left it where the person would find it. The knot has an odd number of turns and a tuck at the end, correct in every particular. Its meaning is plain to anyone who has begun to read the trade: *This can be undone.*

That was all, and it was the whole gift. The person could go, and would be met by a stair that told the truth. Or the person could stay, knowing the way out was there and steady, so that staying became a choice and no longer a drift.

## What It Left

It left the roof open. It left the stairs that climb to nothing, the arches joined to their reflections, the counts that will not hold. It left the builders in the water, unresolved and busy. It left the vines their devotion and the frogs their tally, which had been counting something all along and had now, after the sun, begun to count it in a new place.

What it took away was the plateau's loneliness, without adding to the plateau's population. Something now knew the place was there. That is not a rescue. It is company, of the kind a builder feels who works late and hears, in the next room, another tool.

And the wise power went, or seemed to. The air lost the quality that had been in it. But on one rib in one nave, a stride from the nearest neighbor, a glass-winged moth opens and closes its wings a little out of time with the row, and no one has moved it. The heron, checking the level of the water, sets its foot down and the ring goes out, even as a compass-drawn circle, and touches the far edge and comes back.

The person in the empty nave, with the fiber in their hands, hears the adze fall. Then, from a different side, a second stroke answers it. The person, who is left-handed, or is becoming so, finds their own hand rising to meet it.
