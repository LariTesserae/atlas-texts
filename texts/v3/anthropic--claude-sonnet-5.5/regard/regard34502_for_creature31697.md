# The Third Book

It did not descend. Nothing that wise would arrive from above onto a mountain that is still deciding which way is up.

The first anyone knew of it was in the ledger-room, where a drift-naturalist named Ondrey Vasse, sixty-one and half deaf, noticed that the scavenger birds had changed their circling. They had always circled *over* empty places. Now they circled a place that was not empty, the flat roof of the Ninefold signal-house, and they did it with the same dull patience, but facing inward, the way people sit around a table.

He logged it. He poured tea. He did not know that this was the whole of the arrival.

## What it considered

It looked at the mountain for a long time, and it took the looking seriously. It was a wide attention that could hold a rising range and one moth's extra wings in the same regard, and it did not hurry, because hurrying is how good powers become bad ones.

It saw first the temptation, which was obvious and enormous. It could finish the mountain. The rules could be set in an afternoon: distances agreed upon, echoes obedient, salamanders sorted into their one or two kinds. The plumb-lines would hang true forever, and the second locks would become a quaint habit and then a forgotten one. It would be a kind of mercy, and it would be a kind of erasure. Whatever was testing shapes in the deep soft country would be told, all at once, *this one, you're done*, and it had not been asked.

It saw the opposite temptation too, which was to leave altogether and call the leaving reverence. But there were people here who fell out of the books. That was a cost, and a wise thing does not hide behind non-interference when a hand is slipping.

So it settled on a rule for itself, and the rule was small: *do only what can be refused, and only what can be undone, and do it in the open.*

## The tea at both chimneys

It went first to the Widow at Half-Saddle, because she was the one being in the range who lived in two places and admitted to one.

It did not knock on either door. It sat on the ridge between her two chimneys, where the cloud came up thick and silver, and it waited until she came out to hang a cloth on the line. She came out of both doors at once. She looked at the ridge, and at whatever was sitting on it, with the calm of a woman who has long since stopped being surprised by company.

"You'll want tea," she said, from the east door.

"You'll want tea," she said, from the west.

It accepted both cups, which took some doing.

"I'm told," it said, "that you've only ever been in one place."

"That's what I say."

"Is it true?"

She was quiet on both sides of the ridge. The kettle ticked. "I don't know," she said at last, and it was the first time in eleven years she had said it aloud. "I say it because the other way's too lonely to think about. If I'm in two places, which of me gets to be sure?"

The attention had come with many things it might say, and it set them all aside. "Neither has to be sure," it said. "You are not two half-women. You are one woman who is not finished being placed. That's a different thing from being wrong. The mountain isn't wrong either."

It did not fix her. She had not asked, and she had not been unhappy in the way that asks. It only stayed for the length of both cups. When it left, both chimneys drew a little better, and nobody could say why, and it did not claim the credit.

## The third book

Then it went to the clerks.

This was the true work, and it did it in the ward of Sable Saddle, where the light was worst and the people said only *us*. There, in the registry, two clerks kept two books and did not compare them. It was an old precaution, and it had a cost that no one liked to name. Sometimes a person who had walked out of the loose ground, dry and calm and carrying nothing, became slowly less definite, and one spring the entries no longer agreed, and then a name was in only one book, and then in neither.

It did not seize the books, and it did not tell the clerks how to do their work. It asked to be allowed to keep a third.

The senior clerk, a hard-faced woman named Ilse Marrow, wanted to know what a third book would *do.*

"Nothing," it said. "That's the point. It won't correct the other two. It'll only remember. When the entries disagree, someone can come and read what the third one says, and it will say what was true on the day, without settling which of the others is right. If a person is going faint, the third book will still have them written plain. That doesn't hold them here. It only means they're not alone in the ledger."

Ilse thought about it for the length of one whistle and its echo. "And if they don't want to be in it?"

"Then the page stays blank, and I'll keep the blank page too."

She gave it a desk. It was a cheap desk, warped by damp, in the corner under the window with the bad glass. The third book began that evening, and its first line was in a hand that no one could later describe, only that it was legible and kind.

## What it did not do

It did not touch the *fix.* It tasted a little of it in the pipes and understood at once what it was. It was a borrowed settledness, drawn out of the lantern-stone and spread over the seams like a held breath. It was a good tool, and a decent one, and it held, mostly, because the mountain half wanted to be held. But it was also a quiet *no* said to everything in the soft country that was still trying. The attention wanted to tell the fixers this, and it saw at once that telling would be a small violence of its own, because it would make them ashamed of work that had kept their children's arithmetic intact. So it did not tell them. It only left, in the work-song of one crew that heard it hum on the trestles, an extra verse:

*Two and two and two make six,*
*leave a seam for what's still fixed*
*in its own good time to be.*

Nobody remembered learning it. The crew sang it, frowned, and kept singing.

It did not stop the mountain rising. It did not stop the moths or their wrong wings. It did not speak to the tenants, though it went to the edge of the soft country one night and stood there. It felt, from a long way in, an interest as wide as its own and much younger, tentative, curious, not hostile. It understood that the tenants were not a danger to be managed but a conversation not yet ready to begin. So it did the one thing it could think of that was neither a command nor a silence.

## The whistle

At dusk, on the outermost ledge above Sable Saddle, the fifty wards sent up their whistles and the echoes fell over one another across the moss, and the people went quiet and turned their good ears toward the dark.

From far in, the thing whistled back. It was never quite in tune, and it was never quite late.

The attention answered too. It did not answer in tune. It had learned, by then, that the tuning was part of the meaning, and that to answer perfectly would have been to say *be like us.* So it answered a little flat, a little unfinished, in a note that leaned toward the wrong one and stayed there. It was an offering of company rather than correction: *I also am here, and I'm not finished either.*

Something out in the dark whistled again, differently. It could have been coincidence.

Then the people on the ledge said, as they always did, "If it holds."

And the wise thing, which had never once in its long life been asked to promise, opened its mouth to say *it will*, and did not. It was not sure. It had looked at the whole mountain and could not honestly say. It said instead, quietly, in the voice of the one who answers:

"I'll be here."

They heard it, all along the ledge. A few of them frowned, because it was not the right answer. Then a boy of nine, who had been taught to correct the rising note, tilted his head and said, "That's better, actually."

Nobody corrected him.

## The ledger

Ondrey Vasse, at his desk that night, opened the sixty-first volume to the page where he kept the scavenger birds, the settled ones, the ones with no drift. He had been writing *none observed* in that column for thirty-four years.

He looked through his brass field-glasses at the roof of the Ninefold signal-house, where the largest of them sat facing inward at nothing, and saw for the first time in his life that its beak, from where he stood, had two shapes.

He set down the glass. He was, he found, not alarmed. He wrote, in his tidiest hand, *drift observed, one bird, first instance*, and under it, after some thought, *does not appear to mind.*

Then he went to put the kettle on, since it seemed like the kind of night for it, and since something odd had happened, and it would be the ground.
