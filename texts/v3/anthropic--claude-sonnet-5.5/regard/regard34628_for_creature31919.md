# What the Attention Did

It did not arrive. Arriving implies a before, and the Attention had no place to be absent from. It noticed the basin the way you notice a clock has stopped: a small wrongness in the quality of the quiet, except that here there was no quiet, only a single sustained note, held so evenly and for so long that it had begun to look like a fact rather than an act.

So it did what a wise thing does first. It did nothing, and it did it for a long while, with great thoroughness.

## The First Question

The question was whether this was a wound.

A place where nothing ages, where paint stops fading and rust stops rusting and the old do not so much die as go quiet, might be a place where something has been caught. The Attention has seen places like that: a village kept in one afternoon by a grief too large to end, a valley held in a spell someone cast to keep from losing a child. Such places smile, and they are stopped clocks with people inside.

It walked the town under the warm mains, unseen, and looked for the signs. It looked for fear under the calm, for a rule nobody dared break, for a hand on the lid. It looked in the terraces, in the canning yard, in the cellars of the works. It listened to three hundred people talk in their flat carrying voices and to their hands talking above the steam.

It found no one who was trapped.

It found a balance that was honest. The ground gave what it had always given, and the town took exactly that, and the valve-walkers spent their lives undoing each other's quarter-turns so that the whole might stay level, the way a sleeper turns over so as not to wake. This was not stasis imposed. It was a very long, very patient act of keeping, and the sameness was its product, the way a held note is the product of a held breath.

The Attention was moved by this, and slightly abashed, as one is on finding that a house one meant to rescue was never on fire.

## The Guest

It took a shape then, because courtesy has weight. It took the shape of a middling person in oilcloth, with pockets, who ducked under the lagging without looking. It did not need the disguise, but a guest who arrives as weather is hard to offer an egg to.

It was offered an egg. A child did it, unprompted, a barefoot boy who held out a wire basket and said, "They're good if you don't wait." The Attention ate it on a veranda under a tin awning, with the plate steaming. It was faintly sulphurous, perfectly firm, and better than anything it had been given in ten thousand years, and it said so, and the boy shrugged, because it was an egg.

That night it lay awake, deliberately, to hear the frogs. They sang in their overlapping ranks, and the pattern did not change, and it understood why the residents cannot sleep without them. It is not the sound. It is the promise inside the sound, that nothing has moved while you weren't looking.

## What It Would Not Do

By the third day it had a list, and the list was mostly refusals.

It would not lift the roof of vapour. The people had never seen a shadow with edges, and were not unhappy for it.

It would not tell the children *why*. The basin is their pulse, and no one should be handed the anatomy of their own heartbeat before they ask.

It would not give them more steam, a stronger works, a second town. The virtue of the place was that it took only what the ground gave. Enlarging it would have been a kind of insult, like teaching a monk to want.

It would not cure the sameness. It had no right to. A way of holding time is not a disease of time.

## The Well-Listener

What it did do began with Dorrit.

She was one of the well-listeners, old in the basin's way, meaning she had reached her colour and her lines and stayed there. She sat in a small stone hut at the edge of the town with her ear against a brass fitting, listening for the tide. The Attention sat beside her, in its borrowed shape, for most of a day, and she let it, because the well-listeners are used to silence and to company that doesn't need talking to.

At last she said, without opening her eyes, "You're not from the works."

"No."

"You're listening too. You're better at it."

"A little," the Attention said. "May I tell you what I hear?"

She considered this with her whole face, the way they all do. "Is it bad?"

"No. It's the sea. It's very beautiful. It is also not quite the same size as it was."

Dorrit was quiet for the length of a long breath, in and out, the sleeper's inhale coming up through the rock.

"We keep no record," she said. "We say it's always been the same."

"You say it because you keep no record," said the Attention, gently. "The two hold each other up. It's a lovely arrangement. It also means that if the sea ever did change, the only ones who could tell you would be the ones not asking."

It did not say the sea was rising. It was not, not in any way that mattered for a thousand years. The point was not a warning. The point was that the basin's stillness rested on a small unexamined mercy, a not-looking, and the Attention thought she should know that she was standing on it, if she wanted to know.

Then it asked the thing it had come, in the end, to ask. "I can let you hear it all, once. The whole sea, its true size, the whole slow shape of it. You would not be able to unhear that you know. It would not change the town. It would only mean that one person in it holds a fact that can move. Would you like that?"

She was quiet a long time. The turbines held their note. Somewhere a relief valve shrieked and a thousand pigeons rose and circled and settled, and neither of them looked up.

"Will it hurt them?"

"Not more than a pulse does."

"Then not for them," said Dorrit. "For whoever walks out."

The Attention had not expected that, and it held still, as it does when it is surprised, which is rarely.

"Yes," she said. "I think that's what it's for. I've never known what the tide was *for*. Somebody ought to be keeping it for the ones who need to know it moves."

So it let her hear. It took no more than a breath. She wept a little, without embarrassment, in the manner of the place, where all faces are already damp. Afterward she took a nail from her pocket, because there is always something in the pockets, and scratched a single mark on the brass fitting, the first record the well-listeners had ever made.

It was a very small mark. The mineral crust of the basin seals every scratch, and this one would seal too, in time. But the Attention had leaned close, and the crust, when it came, would seal it slowly, and perhaps a little unevenly, and there is a chance that someone, a century on, will run a thumb across the brass and feel a place where the skin is thicker.

That was the whole of the gift. The basin would go on keeping its own time, but now there was one clock in it that was allowed to be wrong.

## The Causeway

The Attention walked out along the plank causeway that evening, past the last valve house, as the roar fell behind and the frogs thinned and the light widened into something with a direction. It sat among the glass-edged boulders with the egrets.

It had known someone would be there. It had felt the lamp before it saw it: a small yellow bulb on an iron bracket, in town, lit for years for a person who was not there, and beside it a plate set at a table with steam still rising. Some hospitality outlasts its guest. It has become the guest.

The person was not far. He had built a house of driftwood and corrugated scrap above the tideline, with a stovepipe, and a line for drying fish, and a window that faced the basin. He had walked out thirty years ago and not come back, and the sea had done to him what the basin does not do. His hair was white, his hands were knotted, and his face had the loose, lined look of a man who has been rained on. He was the only person for many miles who looked his age.

He was not unhappy, and this was the thing the Attention wanted most to be sure of. But he sat at his window at night and looked at the amber points coming on in the distance, the low huddle of the town, and he knew exactly which one was his.

The Attention did not tell him to go back. It did not tell him to stay. It sat on his step and accepted a cup of tea he brewed in a can, made with rainwater that tasted of nothing, and they watched the lamps together.

Before it left, it took from inside its coat a wire basket. In the basket was an egg, still warm, which it had lowered into a vent that afternoon. It set the egg on his step.

"They set your place," it said. "The plate is still steaming. That's all. I thought you might want to know it hadn't gone cold."

He did not say anything for a long time. He picked up the egg and held it in both hands, feeling the heat go into the knots of his fingers.

"I'd forgotten it did that," he said.

## What Remained

By morning the Attention was gone, and nothing had changed.

The turbines held their note. The valve-walkers went their rounds and undid each other's quarter-turns. The frogs sang, the pigeons rose and settled, the boy with the basket brought eggs to the veranda, and the settled sat with their hands on their knees and said, when asked, that the basin keeps its own time.

But on the brass fitting in the stone hut over the deepest well there was a mark, the width of a hair. And on the shore, in a house of scrap, an old man ate an egg slowly, and did not decide anything, and left his window open all night to the sound of a sea that had, very slightly, a different size than yesterday.

It was the least the Attention could do, and it had done it. Some places need saving, and some need only a witness, and a door left unlatched in both directions, and one small honest way to be wrong.
