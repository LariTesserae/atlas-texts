# What Was Done at Vellum Row

The attention came in the way the showers do, unannounced and without weight. It settled over the rim first, then slid down the pale faces and into the haze.

It was old, and it was kind. Those two facts had taught it caution long ago, because it had learned that kindness applied quickly is often a kind of violence. So it did what it always does first, which is nothing. It listened.

## The First Day

It learned the town by holding still.

It heard the thrum in the feet and the ticking of the metal, and the grit running down the faces. When the bell struck once, low and dull, it stopped for the length of the ring, as everyone in the town did. It did not know who had gone. It stopped anyway, and it thought that this was perhaps the most important thing it did that day. The Kept would never know it had done it. That was fine. Manners do not require a witness.

It learned the rules without being told. It saw that nothing was left in the sun-bar, that no one leaned on the lower walls, and that every stair had a twin. It saw that a child could point at the other way out without looking. It understood that this town was not a mistake being endured. It was a long, practiced, watchful love, and the love was made of attention.

So the first decision was already made. It would not fix the Sink.

It could have. A mind like this one could have knit the limestone, drawn the heat down into a slower vein, and cooled the walls until they were merely warm. The town would have been safe, and it would have been ruined. The Kept listen for the moment the wall changes its mind, and the listening is what makes them who they are. To end the danger would be to end the tenderness, and no one had asked for that. The power did not confuse safety with good.

## The Second Day

It went up to the rim and looked at who was sending the baskets.

There was a small station of low huts, a winch-house, and a stone yard, and there were eleven people, tired and sun-cracked and kind in a worn way. They received the baskets from below and forwarded them along a dry road toward markets that would soon be gone. The trade was thinning, and the road was failing. In a few years the station would be abandoned, not from cruelty but from arithmetic, and the baskets would stop going down. Nobody in the Sink would know why.

The rim people had a saying too. They said it to their children when the children asked why the cable had to be oiled, and why the baskets must never be late, and who was down there.

*"Someone is down there."*

The power stayed with that for a long time. Neither side had ever seen the other, and each had built a small, sufficient faith around the other's silence. It found this so lovely that it hesitated to touch it. To walk into the station and say *look, they are real, here they are* would be a gift, but it would also be the end of something, the end of two peoples being held by an unseen goodness that turned out to be each other.

It did not tell them. It did one plain, practical thing. It mended the road. It cleared a spring that had begun to fail, shifted a trader's route by a few days, and made the arithmetic come out slightly differently. It made sure the station would be there in ten years and in fifty. No one noticed. The rim people only felt, vaguely, that the season had been kind.

## The Keeper

Then it went down to the cistern hall, because it wanted to speak with someone, and it thought the keeper of the tree was the one least likely to be frightened by patience.

It did not appear. It let itself be noticed, as a cooling in the air by the copper spout, a pause in the drip. The keeper was sitting in the niche beside the roots, one hand cupped under the trickle, and did not look up.

"You're very quiet," the keeper said aloud, which in that town was nearly an indecency. Then the keeper tapped the same thing on the pipe, softly, to make it right.

The power said it meant no harm, and asked whether the tree or the town wanted anything.

The keeper thought about it for the length of six drips.

"The tree doesn't want anything," the keeper said. "That's the tree's whole nature. The town wants everything, all the time, a little. That's the town's."

"Should I give it something?"

"Ask it," said the keeper. "Not me. I only keep the tree."

This was the right answer, and the power had half expected it. It thanked the keeper the way one thanks a good teacher, by not saying much. Before leaving, it touched the trunk once, lightly, the way a new pair does. The leathery leaves did not move. The tree was very patient. The power felt, for a moment, understood.

## What It Did

It did not ask the whole town. There was no way to do that without becoming a god, and it did not want to be one. It asked in the town's own way, and it asked small.

**It gave one breath of warning.** The slab-tick that comes before a fall now comes very slightly earlier. It is the length of a held breath, no more, and it arrives as a change in the quality of the quiet. The Kept will think they have grown better at listening. In a way they have, so it is not quite a lie. Once, in the years ahead, a child will stop mid-word and be a step closer to the twin stair than she otherwise would have been, and nothing will be said of it. That is all the power did about the walls. It did not stop them falling. It only made the listening a little kinder.

**It opened the cable.** The lifts run on iron, and the Kept speak in iron, and the iron goes all the way to the rim. The rim folk have never known how to hear it. Late on one still night, when the pins were humming with the whole town's murmuring, the power carried a few taps up the cable to the winch-house, slowly, in the plainest dialect it could find. It tapped the town's oldest saying, reversed: *Someone is down there.* Then it left the cable alone. If the rim people notice and answer, fumbling, in a rhythm no stair would recognize, then two peoples will find each other at their own pace. If they do not, nothing is lost. It offered a door and did not push anyone through.

**It sent a feather.** Among the wrapped things the baskets have always carried, the small objects nobody can explain, it placed one more. It is a single grey feather from a bird that lives a long way off, wrapped in linen and tucked between the salt and the lamp-oil. It comes with no note. It settles nothing. A feather is not a bird, and the argument among the tile-painters, which is really an argument about how to remember, is not one the power has any right to end. Perhaps the feathered stair was right after all. Perhaps the fish with hands was closer than anyone thought. The painters will argue about it in taps for a generation. The power thinks this is exactly what it should be for.

## What It Left Alone

It left the heat, the collapse, and the silence of small life. It left the blooms that return within weeks, and the crews who scrape them, and the bell. It did not put a single insect into the dark. It did not raise the water, brighten the lamps, or slow the slow subsidence of the ground. It did not tell anyone what the Sink was for, because the Sink was not *for* anything, and the Kept had already understood this better than most.

It left the rain.

## The Leaving

On the last day it stayed to watch a shower come. The steam rose, the haze darkened, and the attention passed through the town, and the Kept turned their faces up one by one, on stairs and bridges and in doorways, with their eyes closed and their mouths a little open. The warm rain tasted of chalk. The power found that it had been standing very still, and that, in whatever way it stands, it had turned its face up too.

Afterward, in the quiet, almost happy stir, an old woman on a middle landing struck a spoon on a pipe. It was the rhythm that means *again, soon.*

The answer came from three levels away: *yes.*

Then, a moment later, from somewhere above the rim, faint and clumsy and in a dialect that belonged to no stair, came a fourth voice. It was hesitant, and it had only two taps.

The old woman tilted her head. The person opposite her paused too. For the length of a slab-tick, the whole town seemed to listen. Then she smiled and tapped, slowly, to the far level, to the stranger, to nobody, to all of them:

*Someone is up there.*

The power was already gone. It had done very little. It had stopped for the bell, mended a road, lengthened a breath, and sent a feather. Everything it had left undone was, it thought, the best of what it did.

Behind it the Sink went on coming down, slowly, and the Kept went on listening, and the fissure at the bottom exhaled and glowed.
