# What Came to Ossary Height

It arrived the way the noon build does, so gradually that no one could say when it began.

It had no shape suited to the place, so at first it had none. It lay over the plateau the way heat does and looked, and what it saw pleased it. It had seen worlds of glass and worlds of fire and worlds so tidy that nothing in them could grieve. This one was sloppy, humid, and loud, and it was made entirely of its own dead. The power felt something like the pleasure of a teacher who opens a student's notebook and finds the margins already full.

Then it considered what it might do, which took a long time, because it was wise, and wisdom mostly consists of taking a long time over the things you will not do.

## What it did not do

It could have given the walkers their hearing back. It looked closely at the deafness and understood that it was not an injury. It was a trade the whole people had made with the noise, and the old did not grieve it. They had been moved by it, slowly, into the palm and the sole and the sternum, and to undo that would have been to tell them their understanding of their bodies was a mistake.

It could have brought water: a river, a spring in every camp. But water was the reason there was a morality at all. The first handful poured back into the moat, the basket slung low, the one unforgivable act. Those were not costs of scarcity to be repaired. They were the way the people had learned to love one another, and a river would have made them richer and left them less able to do it.

It could have spoken. It could have shaped itself into something tall and luminous, stood on the rope-grass, and answered every question the walkers had never asked aloud. But their stone stacks meant nothing agreed upon, and that was the point of the stacks. A people who each hold a different unanswerable meaning around the same cairn are held together by the unanswered part. The power knew that if it named the thing that boomed in the night, it would take from them the hour when they all stop and breathe together, and give them a fact in exchange.

So it did none of these things. It had already learned that the largest gifts are often thefts done politely.

## What it did

It took a body. The body was lean and long-legged, and the feet were the hard part, because they had to be broad and sensitive and willing to be taught. It came down out of the weather at the edge of a slump terrace, where the skirt-moss hangs, and it walked onto the mat as a child does, badly.

The ground fought it. The power had crossed oceans of nothing and been ferried on light, and it could not step on a breathing floor. It planted its foot as the mat drew in, and the mat pushed back, and its legs were spent in a morning. It laughed at that, silently, because sound was not going to carry anyway. It sat down on a moss-cushion the size of a house, felt the cushion sigh under it, and began again. It watched the coal-birds pour through the grass. It watched a stilt-beetle test a frond for a lie. By the second day it was stepping with the exhale, and the floor began carrying it, and it understood something about the place that it could not have learned from above: the ground was not resisting anyone. It was only keeping time.

On the fourth day it reached a spire-tree with a hanging hut, and no one was there.

It climbed the stair. The steps were worn into dishes, and it put its feet in the dishes, and it felt ten thousand feet ahead of it. In the hut it found what the custom required: dry fiber, a coil of cord, a few days of food, left for whoever arrived next, even a stranger. It sat on the woven floor with the insect flood below and understood that it was the stranger. The hut had been waiting for it for longer than it had known the plateau existed.

It took nothing, because it needed nothing. It only took the coil of cord, held it for a while, and put it back. Then it went down and did the other thing, the older thing. It knelt at the edge of the moss moat and did not drink. It waited, though there was no eldest to wait for and no band to see it, and lifted a handful of the water and let it run back into the moat, and said in no language, *I know whose this is.*

## The walkers

A band came in at the end of the following day, tired and dusty with fern-wax. They found a stranger at the moat, and they did not ask what it was, because that isn't how it's done. The eldest, a woman whose sign was a palm laid flat, looked at the stranger's feet and saw that they were learning. She nodded. She poured the first handful. They all drank.

The stranger stayed a full circuit. It carried a basket. It was taught the sign for the poisonous false lily, in the silent song, and it was careful to be a poor student, because it had promised itself not to be a good one. It learned to tap, badly, and the children laughed and drummed the correct rhythm on their thighs for it. It slept in the hut with the others, above the flood.

On the last night it sat awake with the old woman. They did not speak. At some point she laid her palm flat on the branch beneath them, then looked at the stranger, and there was a question in her face too old to be asked.

The power answered the only way it honestly could. It laid its own hand next to hers, and let her feel through the wood what it was, for as long as she could stand it, which was not long. It was not cruelty. She withdrew her hand and held it against her chest, and her face was not afraid. It was the face of someone who has had a suspicion confirmed and finds the confirmation is gentler than the suspicion.

She tapped one small rhythm on the branch. The power knew it was the rhythm the band used to say *someone has passed, and they were not unkind.*

## The booming

Before it left, it went out alone on a night when the low voices were up and the high ones had dropped away, and it walked toward the thing that booms.

It walked for hours. The distance did not change. This did not trouble the power, which had expected it. At a certain point it stopped and set its palm on the mat, and waited, and then the boom came, and it was not a voice. The power felt the size of it: a slowness bigger than any animal, older than the spire-trees, in no hurry, and it understood.

The thing wasn't calling to be answered. It was calling to be *held still for.* The walkers, in their thousand years of stopping and breathing with the floor when the sound comes, had been answering it correctly all along. The pause was the reply. The rest of the chorus goes on because the thing has been acknowledged.

What passed between the power and the booming is not told here. It would be a theft too. But when the power stood up from the mat afterward, it seemed to those who saw it from a distance to have grown a little quieter, the way you do after being told something you already knew, by someone who did not need to say it.

## What was left

It went back to the edge of the plateau, to the slump terrace where it had come in. Before it shed the body, it stopped at a root-knot and built a small stack of stones. There were five of them, balanced badly, and no one could have said what they meant. It meant nothing agreed upon, and that was the whole gift.

Then it lay down at the seam where the rope-grass meets the plate-fern, and let the body go.

By evening the beetles had come.

The band returned on the next circuit and found what the seam had made: a dense little cushion of a moss no one had seen before, deep silver-green, and at its center a cistern-lily unfolding where no lily had grown, with a gliding frog sitting in it like a second, darker heart. They sat with it. The old woman signed a name for it, quietly, and the band took the name up and used it. The sign was a palm passing across the back of a hand, but slow, as if asking the surface a question.

No one found this sad.

The heat pulsed, the cloud towers rose, and the grazing mounds moved across the green. Far out in the dark, something enormous boomed once and was held still for. Under everyone, a little, the mat kept growing.
