# What Was Done at Ashgrave Col

The power did not come down the shaft. The shaft bends, and it would not have fit. It came the way attention comes, without arriving anywhere, and for a long while it did nothing at all.

This was the first act, and the hardest. It had to slow down. The Kiln lives at the tempo of a table trembling under a seated crowd, and the Stacker lives slower still, one breath in several minutes, one stone in a season. To meet such a creature at its own pace, the power let its thoughts stretch until a beetle's huff felt like a sentence and a pulse of the fissures like a day. It did not read the rock. It let the rock pass through it, the drum of the Sextons, the thin shivers of the lamplice, the dry note of the chimneys, and it learned that the place was not noise but speech, a very large, very quiet speech that had no listener except itself.

## What It Considered

It looked at the power it had and set out the things it could do.

It could light the chamber. But the dark was load-bearing. The green ripples that carry rumor across the slopes are the Kiln's nervous system, and daylight would have made them a whisper in a flood. It could cool the fissures or bring water to the salts. But the tide of heat is the crowd's whole calendar, and a spring would drown the ones who have learned exactly where the damp is. It could widen the shaft and let the mountain's air and stars in, as if the Kiln had been waiting to be rescued from itself. It had not.

It considered the lamplice, who die without screaming, the Threadwalkers' single patient leg, the hooked needle, the Wall-Mimics' brief and terrible efficiency. It did not pretend this was nothing. It stayed with each death long enough to be sure of what it was: a small warm stopping, quickly closed over, in a creature that is mostly not an individual and is not grieved as one. The crowd is one thing, and the thing is not in agony. To take the hunters away would be to unmake the hot line, the ring of scoured stone, the whole layered order by which the young live at the rim and the black workers live at the fire. It would be cruelty presented as kindness, and it would fail even at kindness, because what it saved would no longer be the Kiln.

So the power left the Kiln as it was. It saw that its first duty was not to improve what it had come to admire.

## The One It Came For

Then it turned to the small pale thing in the ring.

It looked at the Stacker for a long time. It saw that the Stacker was old past reckoning, that the last of its people were long gone, and that it tended a wall whose purpose it might or might not remember. It saw the narrow hands and the sightless face and the great, almost geological slowness. It looked for suffering, since suffering would have changed the reasoning, and found instead something it had not expected: a settledness, the posture of someone finishing a sentence they began before the mountain cooled.

It thought about the questions it could answer. It knew, or could learn, what the ring was for. It could learn whether any of the Stacker's kin still lived on some other mountain. It could learn what the honeycomb was and why the crowd will not settle there. It weighed whether to tell.

And it found that it could not decide this for the Stacker. Knowledge of that size, given unasked to a creature that has spent centuries in patient unknowing, would not be a gift. It would be a verdict, either "you are the last" or "you are not," and either would arrive as the end of something. If the Stacker wanted to know, it would have to be able to say so. The power could at least build the door.

## What It Did

It made one sound.

It did not speak in any language of its own, which the Stacker would have felt as a blow. It spoke in stone. Through the cool ground of the ring, at the pace of the Stacker's own breath, it made the faint knock of one stone set gently against another, then waited an hour. It made the knock again, and after a third and a fourth, it added a small variation: a pause, a lifted note, the shape of a question, if a question can be made of stone.

The Stacker's breath did not change. But the long-fingered hands, which had been resting on the ground, closed slowly and opened again, the way a sleeper's hands do when they hear their name.

The power waited through the next pulse. The heat rolled up the terraces, the lamplice climbed the walls flashing green, the Sextons ran steaming to the cool ledges, and the crowd's flow bent around the ring as it always had. The Stacker sat in the center, wholly still.

When the ground was coolest, the Stacker rose, crossed the bare floor, and turned a stone. It was a stone in the inner wall, not the outer, and it turned it a quarter turn, no more. Then it went back to the center and sat.

The power understood. There had been no answer to the question, or the answer was *no*. The Stacker did not want to be told. It wanted the ring, the turning, the work. It had already been told what mattered, by its own hands, and had been telling itself for longer than the power had been paying attention. The power felt something then that had no name in the Kiln's language. It let the questions go.

## The Gift

It did one more thing, so small the crowd took it for weather.

The Kiln receives the world through the shaft: a moth, a seed, a feather, one more small dead thing for the Sextons to carry to the fire. The power chose to speak in that dialect. It let the mountain's bent throat drop one pebble, no larger than a Sexton could bear, worn smooth by a river the Kiln has never known. It was cool, and slightly damp inside, and it carried the memory of running water in its shape. The Kiln would not take it for a miracle. It would take it for what it was, one more leaving.

A Sexton line found it on the rim and, being Sextons, lifted it. The line carried it down through the terraces toward the fire, blind and faithful. At the lowest edge of the third terrace the line met the ring and parted around it as the tide parts around a rock. One small black body, whose jaws were tired, set the pebble down against the outer wall, in the place where the crowd's pressure had worn a hollow. Whether it meant to, no one can say. Then it went on.

The pebble sat there through two pulses. On the third night of the Kiln's unseen calendar, when the ground was cool, the Stacker rose and crossed the bare floor. It put out one hand and found the pebble. It held it for a long time, turning it, feeling its smoothness, the water-shape that no wind or heat had ever made.

What it thought, nobody knows. It took the pebble to the wall and, over the course of a season, set it in the ring: not on the outside, where the crowd's stones go, but in a low seam near the center, where the oldest stones are, in a gap that had perhaps been waiting.

## The Leaving

The power stayed until the pebble was set, though it did not need to. Then it withdrew the way it had come, without anywhere to withdraw from.

The Kiln registered one thing. On the night of the first knock, the beetles' breath skipped a beat, exactly as it does when a Threadwalker's leg comes down among them. Everyone felt it: the beetles, the lamplice, the walls. The rhythm faltered, and then it took itself up again, and went on. The crowd accounted for it as it accounts for everything, as a small local disturbance and nothing more.

The ring is still closing, or rising, or being rebuilt. The Stacker still rises in the cool and turns a stone. The moths still come down. The power did not change the mountain, or the crowd, or the heat, or the rule that nothing here is sharp and nothing is wasted. It made a knock, it asked a question, it accepted the answer, and it left one stone that was not there before.

That, it judged, was the right size of kindness for a place that was already whole: to let the last hand at Ashgrave Col know, for the length of one quiet pulse, that someone in the dark had noticed the work, and had found it good.
