# What the Attention Did

It came the way weather comes, from the lowlands and up the scarp, and for a long while it did nothing at all.

This was not hesitation. Doing nothing was the first act of respect, and it took a long time. The power moved over the tableland for a hundred days, through a hundred noon storms, and let itself be a small guest among the guests. It learned the order in which the cisterns spill. It learned which spires were overreachers by the way their notes cracked a day before they fell. It watched a searching tendril go pale in the evening and did not reach down.

## What it refused

It was tempted, because wisdom is not the absence of temptation but the habit of examining it. It turned each one over.

*It could stop the lightning.* But the lightning is how the spires are pruned, and the pale seams of healing on the old low towers are the plateau's autobiography. Without the strikes it would still be a house, but a house that could no longer be repaired.

*It could make the towers stand.* It could hold the tall red ones at the edge of their strength and let the overreachers keep climbing. But their falling is what feeds the patient ones, and their fast, brief lives are how the plateau tries things. A spire that never falls is a wall, and the plateau is not building walls.

*It could give the place eyes.* This was the strongest temptation, and the most disguised, because it looked like generosity. Here is a vast, attentive, unwitnessed thing. Give it a face, and someone would be home in the house at last. But the power saw that this was its own loneliness speaking. The plateau had never asked to be seen. Its life was a direction, not a question, and a face would have been a graft: the beginning of a fear, a want, a self to be hurt, all bought to spare the power the discomfort of an unattended room.

*It could bring animals.* No. Nothing eats here, and that quiet is not an absence. It is a different arrangement of the world, and the power did not want to be the reason it ended.

*It could declare the plateau finished.* This one it laughed at, gently. Finished was the one thing the plateau could not survive.

## What it saw instead

Once the temptations were set down, the power could see what was actually true.

The plateau was not suffering. Demolition was not tragedy here, only handoff. The searchers that failed were not grieving. The undertakers lost nothing. Almost everything that looked like a need was the power's own imagination in borrowed clothes.

But there was one real vulnerability, and it was not the lightning or the storms or the daily violence. It was the rock.

The basalt was young and still leaking heat, and the heat was the steam, and the steam was half the plateau's weather. The scarp was a few thousand years out of the earth. Rock cools. In some long span, a hundred thousand years or a few hundred thousand, the floor would go cold. The thermal lift would fail, the noon cloud would stop piling against the rim, and the guest would stop coming. The house would go dry and stiff, the walls would go brittle, and the last spire would stand a long time in silence before it cracked.

Nothing on the plateau knew this or could. The seeds burst outward in their spray, but they landed on the same floor. They floated in the same basins. The whole pattern, everything that had ever risen and fallen there, was hostage to a single cooling stone.

## What it did

So it did one thing, and the thing was small.

It chose the way the plateau itself scatters: by explosion, by flotation, by clinging to what moves. On a night of good rain, when the pods were swollen and the sky was full of flung seed, it took up a few handfuls of that spray. It carried them the way wind carries pollen, without ceremony, across a long dark sea to a place where the earth was tearing open and new basalt was pouring out and hardening into black, sharp, warm rock. A young scarp. A fresh floor that would be leaking steam for ten thousand years.

It set the seeds down in the cracks where the rain would find them. It did not plant a garden. It did not arrange anything, or tilt a single leaf. It only moved the seed the way the plateau's own storm would have moved it, if the plateau's storm had been able to cross water.

Then it went back and asked nothing.

## The second act, which was not an act

Before it left, the power did one more thing, and it was less a deed than a courtesy.

On the last day it came in at noon like any other rain. It rode up the scarp on the pushed air, and the valves opened for it, all of them, tens of kilometers of dilating irises. It broke over the roofs, and the cistern leaves rang like skins, and the towers sang. It did not come stronger or stay longer. A longer storm would have swollen the overreachers past their walls. It came exactly as the guest comes every day, and it let itself be welcomed as a guest, by a household that had never known it was a household.

That was the gift, if it was one. The house had been built to receive something and had never once been told it was received. The power could not tell it, because the house had no ear, and would not make one. But it could arrive as itself, on time, in the room prepared. It could be the rain that came home.

And then it left, and the storm thinned, and the steam rose, and the towers hummed lower.

## Afterward

At night the wettest places glowed, blue-green, along root tips and cistern veins and the split wounds where lightning had struck. A searcher swept the dark, feeling for a height it wouldn't find. The undertakers opened a fallen spire. The floor rethreaded itself under the wreckage before the rot was done.

Nothing on the plateau changed its mind about anything, because nothing on the plateau has a mind to change. It went on in its direction, up and toward the wet and then again.

Far off, across the water, on a black rock still too hot to touch, a pod that had never known the plateau took in the first rain of its life, and split with a flat crack like a pistol shot.
