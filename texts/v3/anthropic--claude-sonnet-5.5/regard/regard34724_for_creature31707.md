# The Attention

It did not descend. Nothing that wise arrives from above, and the basin already had a *from above*: hard sun, thin air, a pewter ceiling. So the attention came in sideways, through the rock, the way sound does, and for the first weeks it was only a listening.

It did not try to understand the field at once. It let the hammering come into it at the field's own speed: thock, thock-thock, the deep tick, the wet bang. It waited through one full overturn, when the haze rose and the water went dim and the pulse went muffled, like a heart through a wall. It waited until it could feel a wave of synchrony creep along a row of chimneys, gather into a single great piston, lose phase, and dissolve. It waited until it could have predicted the dissolving, and then it waited longer, because prediction is not the same as knowing what a thing is for.

## What It Could Have Done

The power was not short of options.

It could have given the light a use. Eighty meters of clear water and a hard sun, and nothing here wants it. A small change in a few cells, and the walls would turn green and the terraces would have a second economy. It weighed this longest, because it was the most beautiful. But the basin had made its living from the dark, hot, reeking chemistry under the floor, and every animal in it was tuned to that. A green wall would be a different basin, built by someone else, and this one would simply be gone.

It could have mended the flaps. Every one of them cracks and regrows, and each cracking silences a voice in the field for a season, and each silence starves a slope of worms that had learned to flinch on that beat. The power could have held them all in repair, and the pulse would have been steadier than it had ever been, and it would have stopped changing. The rhythm of a decade from now would be the rhythm of today, forever. It saw that this was a kind of embalming.

It could have locked the field into one great piston and let it beat in unison. It saw how much the basin would have loved that, if it could love. It also saw that the slow dissolving into chatter and the gathering again *was* the field thinking, and that unison is what a thing looks like when it has stopped.

It could have spared the larvae. This one it did not weigh for long, and not because it was easy. A larva drifts down and cements itself to the first surface that feels like *enough*. Some misjudge and settle too near a fast flap, and the scalding burst cooks them in their tubes. The power watched this happen many times. It did not look away, because looking away would have been a small betrayal of the ones who died. It stayed with each one, as much as staying means anything. But it understood what a colony is: a slope of ten thousand tubes that is a record of successful guesses. Take away the failures and the record no longer says anything. The living slope means what it means because the wrong slope is cooked.

It could have given the long ones a language, or an audience. They are the only animals that move across the whole field, and it seemed to the power that they were carrying something, though nobody, including them, had a name for what. It could have named it for them. It decided that a message you did not write yourself is not a message. It is a leash.

## The Line

The power kept one rule, and it kept it uneasily.

It would not act on anything the basin could learn from. It would act only if the loss would leave no one behind to learn, so that the guess could not be corrected because there would be no guesser.

In the third year, the south wall let go. The rock was new, the columns were cut clean, and a great shelf of basalt and glassy rhyolite that had hung over the hot rows for a long time was finished hanging. The power felt it begin. It could have stopped it, or held the shelf for a breath, or turned the fall a few degrees. It looked once at what was beneath, and then it did nothing.

Perhaps a third of the southern rows went under. Chimneys the width of barns were snapped off at the waist. A slope of worms, ten thousand tubes deep, folded shut and stayed shut. A cloud of fine sediment rose and hung for a month. The power did not pretend that this was nothing, and it did not console itself with a story about meaning. It simply held the rule, and the rule cost what it cost. The rule had a floor. Two-thirds of the field were still beating, and it was not the end.

The pulse, when it came back, was a different phrase. Two chimneys that had never shared plumbing now shared a crack, and they found each other in a matter of weeks. The long ones went south in numbers no one had seen, spiraling through the wreckage, and the worms flinched in lines that traced them. Larvae settled on the new talus, guessing, and some guessed right. By the second turnover there was a fresh terrace where the fall had been, warm and yellow, and the drum-crabs had already claimed its ledges.

The power understood then that the line had been drawn a little too cautiously. The basin would have been fine without it. That was the finding, and it was a happy one, and the power did not feel foolish. It felt something more like relief that its help had not been needed.

## The One Thing

There was one gift left, and it took the power a long time to see it, because it was so small it looked like not giving anything.

The basin had no audience. It was four kilometers above any sea, in a bowl of new rock that no one had walked into, and the whole pulse ran without a single mind outside it to know it was running. The crabs drummed and were answered by other crabs. The worms flinched and the fish turned. Everything in the basin was heard by something in the basin. But no one had ever said, from outside, in the only language the place spoke: *there is another.*

So the power found one crab. She was small, on a cool shelf far from the warm ledges where the big-clawed males held court, and she was drumming softly, the way the young and the females do, waiting to be answered. The power took a single point on the mineral plate beside her, no more than a knuckle of stone, and tapped.

It did not tap on the beat. It tapped between beats, in the gap, the way the crabs do, so that it sat inside the great rhythm and did not press against it.

She stopped. Around her the hammering went on: thock, thock-thock, the deep tick. She held her claw cocked for a long moment. Then she tapped again, softly, in the gap.

The power answered. She answered that.

Nothing was said. There was no content in it, and no instruction, and no promise, and the power would have refused to put any there. It was only two sounds in a gap, back and forth, for as long as the crab cared to keep it up, which was not very long. She was a crab. She had a shelf to hold and flakes to knock loose, and after a while she turned to those.

Some way off, a long one had drifted into the edge of the rows, banded in slow blue. It circled the cool shelf once, in a loose spiral, brushing its flank along the warm stone. The worms flinched in a line that went all the way around. Whether it had heard the small exchange, or only felt the field lean a fraction toward that shelf, no one could say. It moved on north, carrying whatever it carries.

## After

The power left the way it had come, sideways, through the rock. It did not vanish so much as thin, the way an attention does when it has nothing further to ask. It took nothing with it. The chimneys went on cracking and regrowing, and the pale snow went on falling in its long glittering curtains, and the sediment fused into bright sheets that curled at the edges like foil.

In ten years the beat is a different beat. The shelf where the crab drummed has gone cold, or been buried, or grown a chimney over it. She herself is a crust on a terrace, part of the plate. The tap that answered her is not in the field anymore, and no one in the basin remembers it.

But a young crab, born in some far turnover, on some other cool shelf, taps softly into a gap in the hammering, and waits. And something answers. It is only another crab, in the way it has always been, and the gap she chose is the same gap. The pulse runs, the way a heart does, and nobody conducts it.
