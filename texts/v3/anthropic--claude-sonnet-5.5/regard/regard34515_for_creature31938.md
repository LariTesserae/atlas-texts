# What Came to the Hollow

## The Attention

It came first as a kind of noticing, the way you become aware that a room has gone quiet. Something very old and very kind had been passing over the plain, over the sedge and black puddles and bent birches, and it stopped, because there was a place in the land that was *held.*

It stood at the rim, ten paces from the edge, for a long time. It could see the whole of the trouble from there and could not tell whether it was trouble.

Here was a town that never changed. Its people did not remember a birth or a burial. A cold had lain on it beyond all counting. A child crouched to warm her hands on a stone and was lifted away. A power less wise would have seen a cage and reached to break it. A power less patient would have seen a peace and left. The Attention saw both and knew it could not yet tell them apart. Most of its wisdom was in that admission.

So it did not descend as light or voice, and it did not descend as anything grand. It made itself small and pale, with large dark eyes and a hood of grey-green felt, and it went down the dished stairs one step at a time, each step holding its little puddle, and it made no sound at all. That was easy, since silence was the one language it did not need to learn.

## Clean Hands

They noticed at once, of course. Its hands were clean.

No one said so. A mosser on the third terrace glanced at the bare fingertips, then at the face beneath the hood. Without a word she took the visitor's right hand, turned it palm up, and laid a bone spatula in it. Then she pointed with her chin at twelve paces of wall that belonged to no one that morning.

The visitor understood that it had been given the whole of the town's welcome. There was no ceremony, and none was needed.

It mossed. It lifted the corner of a cushion and pressed it back and eased a fern from a lintel. For the first time in an age it did something slowly on purpose, because the work asked for it. The green crept into its fingertips to the first knuckle. It did not stop the stain, though it could have.

It learned what any of them could have told it, if telling were their way. The hush was not an absence. It was a courtesy, worn smooth by use, like the stair treads. The children's silent laughter was the fullest laughter it had ever seen. The palm on the pillar was not worship, and it was not quite a greeting. It was closer to the way you rest a hand on the shoulder of someone asleep, to be sure they are still breathing.

A child noticed it noticing. She looked at the visitor a long while, then reached up and ticked a small rhythm, three soft and one long, against the copper ring on her own finger. She held up the ring, pointed at the visitor, and ticked it again. It was a name. The Attention had been called many things in many tongues, and none had ever pleased it more.

## What It Heard

On some day that was indistinguishable from any other, the visitor went down the deepest lane, past the stones that were warm, and did what the child had done. It knelt and laid both palms flat and half closed its eyes. No hand came down on its shoulder. It stayed as long as it needed.

What lay beneath did not speak. It pressed, softly, like the tap of copper heard through moss. If the pressure had a grammar, it had only one sentence, said over and over so patiently that it had worn itself into the stone:

*Is it still needed?*

The visitor understood then, or understood as much as it was ever going to. Long ago, for reasons that no longer mattered and that the town had mercifully forgotten, something had needed keeping safe. So something had taken up the holding: the even light, the stopped seasons, the count that never had to lengthen. It had held so faithfully, for so long, that it could no longer tell whether the holding was still a kindness or only a habit. And it could not put the Hollow down to find out. If it was wrong, if it let go and it *was* needed, then it would be the one who let it drop.

Above, the people held the silence in their turn, passing it hand to hand, each afraid to be the one. They had never known the other end of the cupped hand was a hand. Below, the holder had never known the other end was a town, breathing slowly, with green on its fingers, holding back.

Two mercies, each keeping the other in place, each too gentle to stop.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not warm the air. Warmth would have melted the rime, and the rime was not its to melt.

It did not break the lid of cloud or send the sun to find its place. Light with a source would cast shadows, and shadows are a kind of weather. Weather comes with endings. It was not the visitor's right to bring endings into a place that had not asked.

It did not restore the memory of the grandmother who went missing from the terrace. It thought about this longest. Grief is what love costs, and the Hollow had somehow been spared the bill. But who was the visitor to say the sparing was a theft? Who was it to hand back a weight unasked?

It did not speak words to the town, and it did not teach them any. It did not tell them what it had heard, because a thing told is a thing decided. It wanted them to be the ones to decide.

## The Drop

It waited through as many days as it took. Then, one ordinary morning, it went to the Measure and took its turn on the ring of white stones as the sitter, with its eyes on the waterline. The sitter of the day nodded, unsurprised, and went home with the same look as the person who had come.

It sat, and in time a bead of mist gathered at the tip of a willow leaf above the pool, and hung, and trembled.

The town stopped, as it always did. The mossers' hands were still. The carriers stood with brimming scoops. Above the pin-menders' anvils the hammers rested. Even the pale moths seemed to fold their wings a little tighter.

Then the visitor did the only thing it did in all its time there. It let the drop fall with a sound.

The sound was very small. A single clear *tik* went into the Measure, and a ring spread across the dark water and touched each white stone in turn, and the waterline did not move at all. Nothing was lost, and no level rose or fell. There was only the sound and the widening ring, and then the pool lay still again, perfect and unchanged and, for the first time in memory, *answered.*

No one breathed out. The held moment stretched, and it was the visitor's whole gift that it did not fill that moment with anything. It left it entirely to them.

In the ivy above the third terrace, a wren opened its throat and sang three notes. It seemed almost embarrassed by them. It had kept them folded for so long that they came out a little rusty, a phrase held back since before the oldest house. Then it stopped, and the ivy was still. Nothing fell. Nothing broke.

Down in the deepest lane, a child laughed with sound. It was a single hiccup of a laugh, startled out of her. A mother's hand came down on her shoulder, as it always did. But this time the hand did not lift her away. The mother knelt beside her, laid her own palm on the warm stone, and left it there. Beneath them both, something very old received the message it had waited centuries to hear: *we are here, and we are holding too, and you may rest.*

## After

The Hollow did not transform. The visitor would have been dismayed if it had. The next morning the water was carried up from the dished steps without a spill. The moss was pressed back into its corners, and the combers lay on their bellies in the ferns and set a moth back where it had been. The rime stayed a finger long on the north faces, and the light had no source.

But the held breath had become a question, and the town now knew it. Some days, when the bead of mist gathered and hung, everyone stopped, and it fell in silence, and they breathed out together and went on. Other days it fell with a small clear *tik* into the Measure, and the ring crossed the pool, and someone somewhere might sing three notes or laugh aloud, or might not. Either was allowed. Neither was ever the one who dropped it.

The visitor left the way it came, down no stair and up one, hood back, its fingertips green to the first knuckle. That stain did not wash out, and it did not try to make it. At the rim it turned and looked once at the bowl of pale light, the town packed like a single organism, and the plain around it, sedge and puddles and bent birches, all as it had been. Then it went out into the wider weather.

Behind it, on the last terrace, the child took the copper ring from her finger and ticked three soft and one long against the lip of a stone bowl. She was not calling in earnest. She only wanted to hear how it sounded.

Far out on the plain, among the sedge, ten paces from an edge no one would know was there, the visitor turned.
