# The Guest at the Edge of the Light

It did not come down through the steam. That would have been a stage entrance, and the Lineholders have no use for stages, and no eyes turned upward, since there has never been anything up there but the lid.

It came the way crossers come, on foot, in the dark between two lamps, steering by the pitch of the tones. It kept its knees loose. It stayed out of the middle of the pools. When the breath went quiet it held still with its palms on the floor, and said nothing, though it could have said anything.

It knew from the first hour what it could do. It could lift the lid and let a sun fall on the plain. It could relight every pole in a night, quiet the pistons or mend them, and put ears in every child. It could tell them what the machine was for and why the water goes home. It did none of these things, and the reasons are most of what it did.

## What it left alone

**The sun.** A light that reached the whole floor would rub out the small orange rooms, and the rooms are the households. It would rub out the moss-diagrams too, because the Lineholders read the plain by its dark. Their whole art is a way of seeing in a place that gives little to see.

**The ears.** It looked at the deaf and saw no wound. The ear is a small organ in a place where sound is a condition, and they hear with the shin and the sternum and the palm. To give them ears would be to hand them a loss and call it a gift.

**The machine.** It understood the works, or enough of them. It found it did not need to say. Nobody had asked, and the Lineholders' silence on the question was an answer: *the water is always going home* is a whole philosophy, and it is theirs. If a wise power spoke the purpose aloud, the saying would shrink to a fact.

**The decay.** It could have held every piston and beam in place, and it considered this longest. But the figures in the moss are a listening, and a listening needs something that changes. The slow decay is what makes them gardeners rather than curators. To freeze the works would turn a living practice into a museum of one, and the Lineholders would become its attendants.

## What it learned first

It learned to be slow. This cost it nothing, because the Floor-Tongue makes hurry impossible. A phrase waits out the eleven seconds, a sentence is a few strokes, a story is an afternoon. It knelt at the edge of one lamp after another and rapped out its poor beginner's grammar, and was answered gently, as one answers a child.

It chose its lamp with care. It did not choose a strong one. It went to a pole that had begun to gutter, where a household of five was already speaking, in the way of such houses, of where they would go when it went out. It took a guest's seat at the light's edge. It did not do this to suffer. A guest's seat is the only honest place from which to ask a question.

## The asking

After three years of knuckles on concrete, it put its palm on the breastbone of the oldest one, the one who could recite the lamps in order, pole by pole across the plain. It waited for the stroke. It went through them both at once, and they had met again.

Then, in the slow tongue, it asked one thing: *What is hard?*

The old one did not speak of the roar, or the dark, or the lack of ears or sun. The old one said:

*The count.*

Each year the recitation is shorter. Each lamp that fails takes a household into someone else's guest seat, and a guest seat is a hard thing to be for long. The old one had recited the same tally so many times that the missing poles had become a second recitation, said silently after the first. Nobody else could bear to learn it.

The power understood that this was the only real request in the place, and that it was not a request to stop anything.

## What it did

**It learned the second recitation.** It went to the old one and let itself be taught the dark poles, the ones that had gone out, in order, with the households that had been theirs. It learned them all, and it keeps them. This was not so the Lineholders would forget. It was so that they could stop carrying the whole weight, knowing that someone did not forget. The old one, for the first time in memory, rapped the first recitation and stopped there.

**It taught one pair of hands to make a lamp.** It did not make lamps itself, and it did not hand out new ones like a god handing out fire. Handed-out fire teaches a people to wait for the hand. It chose one young woman, the one who had cut a ring closed as a child and been walked out to sit beside the ruined pool for a whole breath. She knew what a loss feels like when it cannot be undone, and she had stayed anyway. That was the temperament it wanted.

It took her down into the flooded chambers, where the works keep their old stores. It showed her the glass, the salts, and the seals, and how to tell which were spoiled. Then it made her do it herself, badly, for six years. It corrected only her hands and never her decisions. When her first lamp lit, it was dimmer than the old poles and slightly the wrong orange. It burned.

They set it up at the edge of a household that had been three winters in a guest's seat. The count went up by one, for the first time in living memory. The old one recited the new line slowly, and did not seem to know what to do with her hands.

It did not make a second. She would.

**It stood through the skips.** Twice while it lived among them a piston seized and a beat dropped from the chord. The whole household gathered where the beat used to arrive and stood through eleven seconds of nothing, and a few more. The power stood with them and did not try to fill it. Being a guest at a silence is a rare skill, and it had that.

## What it left undone

The lid is still there. The roar still fills the chest and then the teeth. Pistons will seize and stacks will let go, and most lamps that go out will stay out. One woman's slow lamps will not outrun the decay, and the power knew they would not when it taught her. She will make perhaps thirty in her life, and the plain has hundreds to lose.

It did not count this as failure. The Lineholders' saying is true: *the water is always going home.* Everything the machine spends is spent, and every household is temporary. But the going home can be done in company or alone, with the count kept or lost, with hands that know a craft or hands that only know a loss. That difference was all it had any right to change, and it was enough to spend a life on.

## The leaving

When it left, there was no ceremony. It went to the old one, who was very old by then, and laid a palm flat on the breastbone. The old one laid a palm on its own. They waited for the stroke, and it went through them both at once.

Then it walked out from lamp to lamp into the dark, steering by the tones, with its knees loose. Behind it a girl's new orange room burned, a little the wrong color.

At the next breath the valves seated and the plain fell quiet. Far out on the flat, one figure stopped walking, knelt, put its palms on the floor, and said nothing. It was not for the Lineholders' sake, because they could not see it. The silence was not for it either. It was only the machine drawing air before the next shout, and the guest, who could have said anything, held still through all of it.
