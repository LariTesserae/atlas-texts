# What Came to the Shelf

It arrived as an afternoon, as far as anyone could tell. The rain came at its usual slow hour and lasted until the amber, and nobody on the Shelf noted anything odd, because there was nothing to note. The attention that had turned toward the plateau was in no hurry to be noticed. It was very old, and it had learned that the first thing to do in a place that works is not to be a disturbance.

So it began by doing nothing, and it did nothing for a long time, the length of one wall-cleaning cycle, which on the Shelf is about eleven years. It sat in the rain and listened. It learned the beam-engine pulse, and found the gap where people speak. It learned the gulper choruses drain by drain and knew a sick one from a well one. It followed a blue herd across a school wall and read, in silver trails a day old, an argument between two ten-year-olds about whose snail was faster, which was unresolved and would stay so.

It had come looking for a being, since that was what had drawn it, some single presence in the place that could be spoken to. It did not find one. It found a city that was also a habitat, that was also a slow deposit of stone, and it could not say where any of them stopped. The wall-folk, the snails, the gulpers, the crust, and the water seemed to be one animal with several kinds of cell. It decided this was the being, and that it was not lonely, and that it did not need to be introduced to anything.

## What it considered

It was capable of a great deal, and it went through the possibilities honestly, as one does when the danger is being kind in the wrong way.

It could stop the stones. The kidney stones of the wall-folk are a real suffering, and old people keep them in dishes on the sill as a kind of grim jewelry. But the hardness of the water is the same hardness that closes the doors, and heals the pump-houses into white hummocks, and takes the dead gently into the wall. It could not soften one without unpicking the other. It weighed how much pain a taboo is worth and found that the question was not its to answer, since the people who paid the price had already answered it, every day, for eight hundred years, and had put the answer in a dish on the windowsill.

It could give them plants. It saw that quickly and put it down quickly. Green would swallow the mat-snails' pasture, the snails would fade, the crust would smother the stone in a season, and the masons' drain-slots would open onto a dead wall. It would be a gift like a flood.

It could stop the closing, and let the streets stay open and the doors stay clear. It looked for a long time at a crew of three in waxed coats, one with a hammer, one with a broom, one watching the wall above, missing among them four fingers and most of their hearing. Then it looked at what they said about a street, *cleared to here*, and at how they said it, and it understood that the work was not a punishment laid on them. It was how they knew where they were in the world. A city that was finished would have no one in it who could say *to here*.

It could announce itself, speak from the rain, set a light in the cloud-bank. It considered what it would do to a people who had spent centuries being the guests and hosts of a thing they did not have to explain, to be told there was something above that would have explained it. It kept quiet.

That left very little. It was not troubled by this. Restraint is one of the things a wise power is for.

## What it did

It did three small things and one large one, and none of them can be found afterward.

**The first** was under the eastern seeps, ninety years down the road of the calendar. The deep water there runs through a hairline in the rock that no one has ever seen, and over the next few generations it would have opened. The eastern cisterns would have gone quietly brackish, then thin, and a whole district would have known drought without ever being able to say when it started. The attention did not mend the fault directly. It set the water to do what the water already does. Over the course of a single year it laid one fine curtain of travertine across the crack, in the stone's own grammar, so slowly that the wardens who checked the mains that season found nothing but a little extra glaze and wrote *seep is beading well* in the log. The wall closed its own wound. That was the only repair it was willing to make.

**The second** was a stone. In an old cistern in the ninth district it moved a single flake of sandstone, the width of a palm, so that the water passing through that basin came out softer than the water in the next basin over. It did nothing else. Within a generation a curious woman who kept the records of a small clinic would notice that the patients from that district passed fewer stones. She would spend three years finding out why, and she would be right, and she would think, with justice, that she had found it. Her method would spread through the district by the ordinary routes: gossip, quarrel, and a pamphlet or two. It would not be a cure, because there would still be stones, and the water would still be hard, and the dish on the sill would still be there. But the dish would be emptier in some households. The attention had not handed them anything. It had put a question where a good mind would trip over it.

**The third** was an ordinary afternoon on which a slab of wall did not fall. It hung in a district where a chipping crew of three was working below it, and the watcher had looked away for the length of a breath to answer a whistle. The slab shifted and thought about it, and then stayed. The crew never knew. The watcher was thirty-one and would live to be seventy, and would train nine watchers of her own, and one of them would notice a shifting slab on a different afternoon and shout in time.

The attention did not allow itself a fourth. It knew where that road went, and knew it ended in a city where every fall had been prevented by something unseen, and where no one on the Shelf had ever had to look up.

## The large thing

The large thing was smaller than any of them, and the attention thought about it longest.

On the last evening, when the rain was easing and the light had turned, it went to a rooftop, if a presence like that can go anywhere, and it stood among the palm-keepers' ridge-stones and looked down. The gulpers were singing in the drains. A grazer was walking backward in front of a blue herd, tapping the wall with a rod, and a little girl beside her was holding a snail to the light. The palmers lifted from the cornices in their dark roaring ribbon. Every face on the street tilted up to it and then went back to its business.

It watched a family come out onto a step with a dish of clean water. It was the first meal of the season, and they set the dish at the mouth of the drain the way they always did, without ceremony, without knowing who taught them. The attention had been looking for a long time for the right way to say what it felt, and it found that the people had already found it.

So it took a little of the rain, the good cold water from the deep seeps, into a dish it did not have, and set it down at the mouth of a drain in a district where no one had set a dish that season. It did this without any sign. Below, the gulpers, who had been singing, went on singing, a little louder, or perhaps only as loud as before. In the ninth district a warden in cold water to the knee lifted his lantern and cocked his head, because the chorus had a new note in it, or seemed to, and then decided he was tired, and went on counting.

The wall beaded. The pumps kept their pulse. Somewhere in a cold niche the pale glaze went on thickening over someone who had held the stone. Nothing on the Shelf had changed, except that the stone below the eastern seeps was a little more finished than it had been, and a woman in a clinic was going to be curious, and a slab was still on a wall, and a dish stood at a drain that had not had one.

The attention left the way the rain leaves, gradually, by turning into the ordinary evening.
