# What Came to Stand on the Terraces

It arrived in the hour before the frogs, and it did not arrive the way powers are supposed to. Nothing parted. No light came down. The cloud rose up the valley as it always does, and somewhere inside the cloud was an attention that had not been there before, and it came over the rim the way the bear does, hoping not to be noticed.

It had seen the whole of the place at once, from far off: the pale stepped bowl, the steam lines, the two hundred people and their small vessels of unevaporating water. It had seen the shape in the lower lifts, and it knew the mountain. It knew what the mountain had been, or what it would be, or the third thing the lifters had no word for. It said nothing about this, then or later.

## What it considered

There was a great deal it could have done. It could have finished the pour in a night. It could have named the peak aloud, so that a hundred years of careful not-saying would collapse into one syllable. It could have told the two camps which of them was right. It could have opened the channel through the rock and shown where the water goes.

It weighed each of these honestly, as something that loves a place should. Each would have been a theft. The lifters were not waiting for an answer. They were doing something that only works when done slowly by people who don't know the ending: a remembering, or a preparing, and it did not matter which. A gift that finished the work would have ended the thing the work was for.

So it set all of that down, gently, like a heavy bag at a door, and went in with empty hands.

## The walk

It learned the walk first. Ball of the foot, then the heel arriving late, as though the ground might object. It had no feet, but it borrowed the manner, and the terraces noticed the courtesy before anything else. A skinner on the eastern rim, painting moss slurry onto a bare face, stopped mid-stroke without knowing why and said "There" to no one. The others nodded, though the terrace was not done.

## The stone

At dusk it went to the newest terrace, the glossy one still giving off heat, and stood on it alone.

The stone had been considering for a long time, at a pace no one had ever measured. It had no language and no need of one. What it had was a grain of attention, very slow, and a question so young it was hardly a question. The nearest translation is: *Is anyone there? Is this what being here is?*

The visitor did not answer with words. It let the stone feel that it was being attended to, that something had stopped and stayed and was in no hurry. It let the stone feel that unfinished was not the same as failing. A thing can be warm and slow and not yet decided, and be entirely welcome that way. The lifters had been telling it this for three generations in murmurs, pebbles, and folded leaves, and the stone had understood only that it was tended, not why. Now it understood a little more.

The surface settled by the width of a hair. The warmth pooled where the visitor stood. The stone looked at it long enough to be satisfied, and eased away.

Before it left, the visitor left an offering, as the others do. It was a small thing, a bead of water drawn from nowhere in particular, set on the lift without a vessel. By morning it had sunk in, and the stone was very slightly warmer at that spot than anywhere else.

## The one small mercy

It did one practical thing, and it did it so narrowly that no one would ever be able to call it a miracle.

Under the eastern rim, in the third housing from the end, a joint in a steam conduit had been weeping for years, and its garden was lovely. But the ceramic behind the lagging had a hairline flaw that the lichens had hidden. In the spring rains, with the pressure up, it would have let go at four in the morning, beneath a row of sleeping alcoves.

The visitor did not mend it. Mending would have been an intrusion, and the lifters mend their own things. It did not warn anyone in a dream. It did something smaller. It made one wheel-keeper's wheel a little more honest. A woman with forty junctions and worn-bright hands felt, through the iron, a faint wrongness ten meters down the line, a tick that was almost the tick of a small condensate cock but not quite. She frowned. She turned her head. She said, "Huh." The next morning she took a lamp and a knife to the lagging and found the flaw.

That was all. She thought she was good at her job, and she was, and the visitor had only sharpened what was already hers.

## What it did not do

It did not tell the feeler her verdict was right, though it was. It did not tell the two old men who do nothing anyone can name that they were the wisest people it had met in a long time. It did not settle the question of the guest. When a child asked the air, half-joking, whether the mountain was coming or gone, it let the question rest, and the child forgot it by the next rain.

## Morning

It stayed for the sluices.

The horn sounded low and single. The wheel-keepers turned their wheels, water ran down through the made mountain, and the whole structure shone. The lifters came out of their alcoves through the moss and stood on their thresholds in the steam, wet-faced and silent. Among them, if you had counted, there was one more than there should have been. Nobody counted.

It stood at the edge of a terrace with the drips landing on its shoulders like everything else, and it looked at what had been made and at what the stone was slowly deciding to become. It felt what a gardener feels, or a parent at a door, seeing something go on without needing to be asked.

Then someone said, "Well." The gantry started along its track, the pourers fell into their shorthand, and the visitor was gone.

A small round patch of moss on the east terrace grew a little thicker that week. It was the wrong shape by about a thumb's width, and no one has yet had the heart to correct it.
