# The Bowl at the Bottom

It came the way fog comes, without arriving. One moment the plateau was being looked at by no one, and the next it was being looked at by something that had no need to hurry.

It saw the granite knuckles, the wet green, and the freckled ghosts of the felt-columns in their saucers of shadow. It saw the city and the strata beneath it, and that the whole place was one act of courtesy stretched across more time than any single hand could hold. It understood quickly that the many people here were also, in a way, one being. Each Tenant was a different hour of a single patient creature, which had learned to care for itself by leaving things where its later self would find them.

## What it did not do

It considered the obvious things first, because a wise power considers them and then puts them down.

It could gather the Tenants into one hour. It could pull the lags together like the ends of a cloth and set every hand in the same afternoon, so that for the first time they could see each other's faces, smoothed and sharp-eyed, and speak. But the lag was not a wound. It was the shape of their love. Every bowl of porridge was a sentence that took a season to say, and a people who have only ever spoken that way would not be improved by being made to shout. They would lose the thing they are, and gain a crowd.

It could reveal itself, with a voice, a light, or a shape they could kneel to. But a god who arrives in a place that runs on quiet reciprocity is only a loud guest, and the loudest thing in a house is never the most useful. They had not asked for one. Nothing in the cairns, the clay, or the lamps was a prayer. It was all housework, which is a better thing to have than a prayer, since it means the house is being lived in.

It could mend the weather, hold the rain to a schedule, or give the tarns a steadier face. It could put an animal on the moss, and a bird in the sky. But the vegetation was lush with the lushness of something never contradicted, and the silence had a thickness that belonged to it. Not every absence is a lack.

And it could tell the traveler, standing on the terrace with a bowl, that they were not alone. But the traveler already knew, in the body, the way a child in the dark knows the dishes are being set down in another room. Words would only make that knowledge smaller.

So it set down, one by one, everything it might have done, and was left with a single thing that still seemed worth doing. It found it by asking what the relay lacked.

## The gap

The whole culture was a chain of handing-on. Everyone set something down for whoever came next, and everyone received from whoever came before. The porridge was warm because someone was stirring. The step was swept because someone had walked it first.

But every chain has two ends.

At the top of the strata, and at the far end of the columns, were the ones who had slowed until a drop of fog was an hour. For them the work was done and there was nothing left to hand to anyone. They stood in their rings of dark ground, holding the last of the handing-on, and no one came to take it. It was the one place where the relay had no next.

At the bottom was the other end. Far down in the black compaction, the oldest of them were still laying the first course of the first house. The granite was new and the hearth-stones unblackened. They tapped patiently, and the sound climbed through every layer to the guest-house, where a traveler wondered idly what it was. They were the most industrious of the Tenants and the hardest to reach. In all that great chain of courtesy they were the only ones for whom no bowl had ever been set down. There was no one before them. They had built the first house from nothing, and had eaten, for however many seasons, whatever they made with their own hands, warmed by their own fire, in a country that had never once been kind to them on purpose.

The power looked at that, and the wisest and most benevolent thing it could think of was small.

## The bowl

It went down. It did not go down the way the mason goes, by living through the hours, but by a kind of turning aside, as the rain is walked home on its cord. It passed through the courses of the city as a hand passes through water: the sod roofs, the round houses, the older streets under the streets, each with its lamp already lit. It did not disturb one. It stepped over the sleeping of whole centuries and arrived in the first hour of the first house, where the granite still smelled of the quarry and the fire was one small fire.

It did not appear. Nobody turned to see it. It found the bowl they had made, still unglazed and thumb-pressed, and filled it with a pale porridge from the barley of no field, and made it warm. Then it set the bowl on the sill by the first hearth, beside a carved wooden spoon.

It stayed to see the mason come in with dust on her large flat hands. She stopped in the doorway, because a doorway occupied is felt the way a held breath is. She looked at the bowl for a long time. She did not look around for whoever had left it. Her people do not look around, because they know it was for whoever comes next, and today it was her.

She ate. She washed the bowl in the cold clear channel. Then she turned it rim-down on the sill, which in their grammar means *received*.

Nothing else happened, and nothing else was meant to. But something in the foundation of the city had changed by the width of one meal. Somewhere below the lowest lamp, the first person in the chain had been fed by a hand that was not her own. Every course laid above her from that hour would rest, however faintly, on the fact of it. The city had always been built on care given forward. Now the first stone had also been laid on care given back.

## The other end

It did the same at the other end, with equal quiet. It walked among the felt-columns at the hour when the fog beads on the silver hair, and it went to the ones that had nearly stopped. It took, from each, no felt and no hair. It took only what they had been holding, the last unfinished handing-on, the small task with no one left to receive it. It took it as one takes a dish from an elder's hands at the end of a long meal, saying nothing, because everything is understood: *I have it now. You can put your hands down.*

The drip at the base of the nearest column did not change its rhythm, but it seemed, to no one, a little less like waiting.

## What it left

Before it went, it built one cairn at a crossing on the surface, in the ordinary way, three stones, badly balanced, the way a stranger builds when idle. It was a question in the only grammar the place has.

By morning it had become a hand, five stones fanned open. It means *wait* or *welcome*, and the two are the same word.

The power took it as an answer, and did not take it as an invitation. It had learned what the Tenants know: that being welcomed and staying are different acts, and the kindest guests know which of them they have been offered. It did not stay. But it left the way they leave a house, with the step swept, the bowl washed, the lamp turned low and not out.

## Evening

That night the fog climbed from the swells, the copper sheets began to ring, and the lamps came on one by one across the city in the order of their first lighting. For the length of the lighting the Tenants stood in the same hour, a crowd shoulder to shoulder in the dusk, facing the same windows, saying nothing.

In that crowd there was, for the length of one lamp-lighting, a person more than the plateau had ever counted. Nobody turned to look. Nobody was startled, for the gap it stood in had been kept for it by a courtesy older than any of them. It stood at the edge, warm and unremarked, and watched the last lamp catch.

Then the hour came apart, and each of them walked off into their own time. Far below, in the first house, a bowl sat rim-down on a sill. Above, a traveler on a terrace set down their own bowl, rim-down, and did not know why it felt like an answer.

The porridge was set out again, warm, for whoever comes.
