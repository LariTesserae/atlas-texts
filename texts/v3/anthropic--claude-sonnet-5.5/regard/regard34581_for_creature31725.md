# What Came to the Cleft

It came on foot, which was the first courtesy. It could have arrived in any way at all, but it chose to be a shape that fit through the slot, and it turned sideways and let the limestone snag its sleeve.

Inside, it stood still for a long time with its hands open. Then it did what the room required of visitors and learned to step on stone.

## The Hour

It did not act during the first hour. It watched the vent breathe and the lower room cloud. It watched the fog climb, sag, and fall, and it felt the warm drops on its face. It saw the frogs turn, all at once, to face the red gleam at the vent mouth, and it turned with them, because standing any other way would have been rude.

It stayed through a second hour and a third, and by evening it knew the room's weather the way a person knows the breathing of someone asleep beside them.

Wisdom, for the power, was mostly a discipline of not doing things. It had a long list of possible kindnesses, and it went through them one at a time and set nearly all of them down.

It could give the room more water, but the scarcity of the drip-rain is what taught the moths to lift their edges together. Abundance would not have been a gift. It would have been a different animal.

It could free the bronzed frogs, unstiffen them, and return them to the quick, irritable paper-skinned life they had been born to. But it could not tell whether they were prisoners or graduates. The younger frogs deferred to them, and the deference looked like reverence, not pity.

It could repair the engine, replace the cracked belt and true the flywheel, and make it run smooth and unbroken. But the pause at the top of the stroke is the room's held breath. An engine that never stalled would never let the crowd pour down the walls toward its heart, and the crowd would never be reminded, every so often, who it was. The power judged that the fear was part of the love. A creature that could not be afraid for its heart would not be tending it.

It could explain. It could stand in the middle of the floor and say what the engine was, what the mining company had wanted, what a pump-house was for, and where the steam came from. But nothing in the room had asked, and it would have been an unkind thing to answer a question that a hundred thousand animals had spent their lives living instead of asking.

## What It Did

It did three things, all small, and none that anyone in the cleft would ever notice.

**First, it gave the hill its rest.** The slab had slid within living memory, and the stress that had moved it was not spent. Somewhere in the ridge a second slide was waiting, and it would have closed the slot and crushed the room's upper walls together. The power went into the stone, the way one goes into a sleeping person's shoulder to ease a knot, and let the tension out slowly, over a season, in tremors too small to wake a moth. It left the walls leaning toward each other as they always had, almost closed, with the seam of white sky still open at the top. It touched nothing else, because the seam is where the fog condenses and where the daylight enters, and the room's whole weather depends on that narrow gap.

**Second, it cleared the throat of the vent, but only partway.** Far below, a crust of mineral had been thickening for years, and in some future decade it would have sealed the breath entirely. The power thinned the crust and left the vent's character as it found it: unreliable, and prone to skipping. Some hours the breath would still fail, the engine would still stall, and the crowd would still pour toward its heart and pipe in its thin high voice. It did not want to end the fear. It wanted only to make sure the fear would always have an ending, because the wheel would always turn over again.

**Third, it followed the belt.** This was the only thing it did out of curiosity, and it was slightly ashamed of that. It crawled beneath the rubble along the cracked leather, where the shaft ran down into the dark, and it found what the shaft turned.

It will not say what that was. Some of it is not the power's to tell. But it can say that the beetles had been there, and that what they had made down there was neither crude nor sparse. It was the most exact thing in the whole cleft. It turned, unwitnessed and entire, in the warm dark under the floor. The power understood then that the pattern in the mosaics had not been spreading outward from the engine, losing its clarity at each remove. It had been coming up from below, and the engine was only its nearest visible shape. The masons in the outer cracks were not copying badly. They were remembering badly, which is a different and more hopeful thing.

The power put the rubble back as it had been and left the shaft turning.

## The Frog

On its last evening, it went to the deepest cranny beside the steam line. There the oldest of the bronzed sat among the valves, copper through and through, dull-eyed, so like a fitting that the power had to look twice.

It sat down on a block of clean stone a little way off and said nothing, because nothing needed saying. It stayed through one whole hour, through the vent's breath, the fog, and the drip-rain falling on both of them. The condensate ran down the frog's green-bronze back and beaded on the power's hands.

At the end of the hour, as the walls began to steam, the frog blinked.

It was one slow blink, with a lid that had not moved in a long time. The power did not know what it meant. It might have meant *I see you*, or *go on*, or *the rain has stopped*, or nothing. The power decided it did not need to know, and that it was the best thing anyone had said to it in a long while.

## Leaving

It squeezed back out through the crack and stood on the hillside in the dry wind, in the sudden thinness of the open air, with nobody pressed against it anywhere. There was copper dust on its sleeves. On its collar sat one moth, folded and pale and attentive. The power did not coax it off at once. It stood for a moment, letting the moth take the last of the day's warmth from its neck, and then it bent and touched a finger to the limestone, and the moth walked down onto the stone, opened its wings once, and flew back in through the slot toward the fog and the *tock*.

Nothing had changed that anyone could name. The walls still leaned. The seam still held its strip of sky. The vent breathed on its unreliable hour, and the frogs turned to face it, and the flywheel paused at the top of its stroke and pushed on, and the whole full room went on keeping time.

The power had come to help and had found that helping was mostly the art of leaving a thing able to go on being itself. It walked down the hill without looking back, because it knew the cleft did not need to be watched.
