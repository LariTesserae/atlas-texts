# What Was Done at Ennis Verre

The attention came the way everyone comes: upward, through the cloud, into the dark. It was very large, and the first thing it did was become smaller.

It had understood, before it arrived, that the plateau was a place with a discipline in it. Nothing there was made on purpose. Iron was stacked by beetles, warmth was sweated out by mats, and the light was a moss's faint opinion of the dark. A being that arrived as itself, with its own radiance and its own sense of what a place should be, would be the loudest thing to happen there in ten thousand years.

So it subtracted. It let its brightness go, and then its height. It let go the habit of speaking in ways that carry. It kept its attention, its patience, and its capacity to be surprised, because those made no noise.

## What It Did Not Do

It considered the possibilities and set each one down gently.

It could have lit the sky. The Lay live half-blind and flinch from brightness, and a sun that rose properly would have been a kind of mercy for their eyes, but it would have ended the lantern moss, the vent-wrens' hunting, and the whole slow economy of a world tuned to dusk. It would have been a gift only to someone who wanted to live somewhere else.

It could have taken the stalkers away. Their flash is a cruelty, and the Lay close their eyes when one of their own is taken. But the herds re-form smaller and the stalkers stand still and glow where they have fed, and the balance is the same one that turns a body into a bar of iron. The Lay do not ask for vengeance. It would have been a strange kindness to remove the thing they have made peace with and call that peace a wound.

It could have taught them fire, or writing, or medicine, or given them a roof. They would have accepted, being courteous, and then the plateau would have held its first thing made on purpose, and after it a second, and the Lay would have started to become a people who build, which is another way of ceasing to be the Lay.

It could have told them whose ribcage it was. It knew, or could have found out. But that knowledge, said aloud, would be a structure standing in the middle of their silence. They say it was the first one who lay down, and that is a better and truer thing to live beside than a name. It left the not-knowing where it stood, sunk to its hips in moss.

## The Circuit

What it did was follow.

It fell in behind a herd, a little to the side, as the Lay do. Grazers came to accept it as they accept a slow cloud. It slept in the bellows-fern basins and let its breath fall into the ferns' rhythm, which took longer than it expected, since it had not breathed in a very long time. It drank from leaf-beads one drop at a time. It learned to lick the salt from an ingot beside a grazer. It flinched from nothing, which the Lay noticed, but it raised a hand near its face anyway, because everyone did.

The children found it first. They came and looked into its face for a long while, and then one of them took its hand and led it to a rank of iron bars, and it laid its palm flat on the cold metal and held it there until the metal was no longer cold. The child watched. When it lifted its hand, it said nothing. The child seemed satisfied.

The adults took longer. They watched it for most of a season, whispering, clicking. They did not decide what it was. They decided that it kept company, and that was enough for a name.

## The Woman at the Cedars

There was one thing it did that was more than following.

A woman of the Lay, whose sister had been taken in the spring, had begun to stand at the edge of a cedar stand. She stood there at the start of the evening mist, and she was still standing when it retreated. Her hand was up near her face. The others, who knew the danger, brought her fern-water and shifted their weight near her and hummed the small clicks that mean *come*. She let them. She did not come.

The moss had reached her ankles.

The visitor did not speak, because speech would have been the wrong tool. It did not pull her away, because grief is not a thing to drag anyone out of. It walked out and stood beside her, near enough that their fleeces touched, and raised its own hand to its face. It stood as she stood. Its stillness was as deep as hers, and it lent her its patience the way one lends a warm stone.

Then, after a long while, it shifted its weight. It did this slowly, so that she would feel it against her shoulder and not see it. A few breaths later it shifted again.

On the third or fourth shift, she shifted with it.

That was all. It did not try to make her walk. It stayed until the mist had gone and the moss on her ankles had been broken by her own small movements. Then it stayed a little longer, because the shifting was not something she could yet do alone. Later she went back among the others, and lay down in the ferns, and slept in their rhythm.

## The Stalker

It also did something it could not explain, and did not try to.

In the last part of the season it came to a stand of cedars where one stalker had stood so long that moss had reached its waist. It stood beside the stalker for a night, close enough to feel the faint heat where the last meal still glowed.

It did not try to wake it or change it. It laid one hand against the dark bundle of cords, which were warm in the way the cedar bark is warm, and it shifted its weight, slowly, every few breaths, until the mist had gone.

The lantern did not open. The visitor never learned whether the Lay were right about what the stalkers had been. It left without knowing. Some of the Lay say that, years later, that stalker was found a few paces from where it had stood, still, with the moss broken at its knees. Others say that is only a story. The visitor was never told, and thought that was correct.

## The Ribcage

It walked under the iron arch once, in silence, with its hand lowered and its eyes bare to the dark, as the Lay do. It did not touch the metal.

Beneath the arch it was aware, briefly and quietly, of the enormous slow work going on in the rock: the hum, the unmaking, the turning of one thing into another without anyone's help. It found that it had nothing to add to that. It found this was a relief.

## Leaving

It left by the only way anyone could: it lay down in a warm basin one evening, and the ferns breathed over it, and in the small hours it was no longer there. It took nothing. It left one sliver of ingot, a flake from the end of a windrow, pressed into the moss near the place where it had slept, for anyone to find. It had not made the flake or placed it. It had only stopped, one afternoon, near a broken bar, and let the piece stay where it lay.

The Lay found it in the morning. A girl took it and pinned her fleece at the throat.

## What Changed

Almost nothing.

The sky was still indigo and the sun still leaned and gave up. The stalkers still opened their lanterns, and the Lay still closed their eyes for a long time when someone was taken. The iron lines grew a little longer. The hum went on.

But the Lay have a phrase now, whispered when someone is grieving and has begun to stand too long: *keep company*. It means to go and stand beside them, and to shift your weight a little, until they do too. It is not much, and it is hard to say who first thought of it. The children say a guest brought it and did not throw its voice away.

The moss on one boulder near the eastern cedars is pressed flat in a long hollow, the size and shape of someone lying on their side. If you touch it in the evening, it is still faintly warm.
