# The Guest Who Paid

## Arrival

It comes the way cloud comes, up the eastern side in the low morning, and for a while it is only more mist among the mist. That is intentional. A thing of its kind can arrive as a voice in the sky or a light on the terrace, and it has learned that both are a kind of violence. So it arrives as damp, beads itself on the lintels, and looks.

It has a word for what it is, and the word is *power*. Here the word is useless, and it notices the uselessness with something like relief. Nothing in the hollow is asking to be moved.

## What it set aside

It considers the obvious gifts first, because a benevolent thing should be honest about its temptations.

*Water.* The cisterns stand at their mark. To fill them past it would be to tell a hundred people, in a language they'd understand at once, that the mark was a limit and not a promise. They would spend a thousand years relearning what the mark already taught them.

*Numbers.* A hundred and some is not a shortfall. More people would mean more cups, and every extra cup would be drawn from someone's dawn.

*A sign.* A light in the sky or a voice in the hall would work, and the town would reorganize around it. The hall would acquire a center, and the bench would begin to face something. The evenly spaced ring of sitters, each looking at nothing together, would tighten into a congregation. It would be the end of the only thing here that is truly theirs.

*Knowledge.* They carry no question it could answer. If you ask why the cisterns never empty, they shrug at the stone. The shrug is the answer, and it is a better one than any it could give.

So it puts the great gifts down one by one, as a person might set down tools at a door, and looks at what is left.

## What it noticed

It walks the stair-streets at the pace of the dished treads, which is slower than it expected and correct. It sees the dusky lips and nail beds, the wide ribs, the long drawn breaths. This is a body paying a constant small tax to the altitude, and the people do not know they are paying it, because there has never been a life without it.

It sees the child on the wall, counting a drip.

And it sees the old, the ones who eat a little less each week and sit a little longer on the bench, until one morning the spot is empty and by the season a younger person is sitting in it. No one names any of this. No one weeps out loud. The number holds.

Here it takes longest, because here a lesser wisdom would go wrong in one of two directions. It could decide this is a grief that has been swallowed for centuries and rush to bring it into the open, teaching them words for loss so they can mourn properly. Or it could decide it is none of its business, the mark applied to themselves, a private accord, and leave it untouched.

It does neither, because it can't tell if it is a wound. The people have no word for surplus or lack, and never having needed the word may mean a life without the injury the word would name. It has no right to give them the injury in order to cure it. But it also notices that in all those centuries of quiet leaving, no one has been *seen* leaving. The hall has held the sitting and never the going.

That is a small thing, and small things are what it has left.

## What it did

**It paid.** Before anything else, it takes one cup of water from a cistern on the low side of the town, using a bronze cup left on a peg. The water is black and very cold and tastes of slate, and it is the best thing it has ever drunk. Then it goes up to the north rim, where the weeping stones stand in their rows, and lets a portion of itself go into the mist and gather on the faces of the slabs. The grooves take it, and the gutters, and the channels, down the long gentle gradients. By the next dawn the wet-dark line stands where it always stood, the cup returned. Of all the visitors who ever came through the saddle, it is the only one that left the level as it found it, and it is glad to have learned the manners.

**It sat with a child.** On the second morning it takes a place on the low wall beside the child who is staring past everything. It does not appear. It has no seat, but the wall seems to have a little more company on it. It learns the tempo of the child's channel, the slow tick like a clock, and counts with her for one whole morning. She never looks at it, and it never speaks. Toward the end, when her count slips, as any child's would, the count is simply still there to be picked up. She picks it up. Her shoulder eases half a finger's width toward the empty air beside her, and that is all. It is enough.

**It warmed a stone.** This is the deepest of the three, and it takes the longest to be sure of. The old woman at the far edge of the western lean-tos has stopped eating and sits for hours in the hall, in a spot as polished as bone. It does not try to stop her going and does not lighten her going with visions. It only keeps her company, as the child was kept company, and when she is gone, in three days' time, it does one thing more. On the morning a young man, not of her house, walks up to the terrace and stoops through the low door to sit in her place for the first time, the stone is warm. It is not hot or strange, only the warmth of a seat someone rose from a moment ago. He sits and does not remark on it. But something in his shoulders settles in a way it would not have on cold stone, and he stays longer than the usual hour. Nobody will say anything, and no one will ever know. The warmth will be in his body for years, filed among things he has no word for, as a fact: *the place was kept for me.*

**It thickened the sleeping air.** This one it debates the longest. The whole valley of their lives is a thin-air life, and the dusk in the lips is the price. It could gentle the price. But their waking breath is their measure of the world: the gait, the pause on each deep tread, the dish worn in each stair by exactly that pause. Change how they breathe by day and it changes the stairs, the speech on the outbreath, the ribs, the whole slow argument the town has made with the mountain. So it leaves the days alone. But at night, inside the small cells where children sleep, it lets the air lie a little richer. No one will notice it. There will be fewer restless wakings. Some child will sleep through a cold hour she would once have surfaced from, and grow up with a fraction more rest banked in her bones. It will not show in the count or the mark, and so it is allowed.

## What it did not do

It did not stop the quiet leavings. It did not give them a word for grief. It did not tell any of them that it had been there. It did not touch the junipers, which are tended well enough; it only stood for a while among them, in the smell of their smoke, and thought that they were the one place in the hollow where care could be seen, and that this was likely not an accident.

## Leaving

On the fourth morning it climbs the eastern stair, in the low cloud, in the way it came. At the top, before the pass, it looks back. The town lies in its hollow, pale and low, with the cisterns like dark coins between the roofs. The mark on every stone is where it was.

From somewhere far across the courts there is a dry small sound, two pieces of slate struck flat, once. A head lifts at a doorway and turns, and that is all that happens, and the day goes on.

It takes the clap for what it is, the thing they do when someone needs to be told something across a distance. Perhaps it is meant for a neighbor. Perhaps it is for no one. But the door it looks at holds a figure in undyed wool, and the figure nods. It costs them nothing.

The wind rises, and behind it the jars take up their note.

It carries away a single thing, which is the only thing it came to take: the knowledge that a level can be held by something other than fear. It can be held by a hundred people, each taking what has fallen, and by a mountain that has been listened to long enough to be trusted. That is worth more than anything it could have left.
