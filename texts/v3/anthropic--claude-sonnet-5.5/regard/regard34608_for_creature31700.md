# What Came to the Cup

It did not arrive so much as become present, the way a change in air pressure becomes a headache. There was no figure at the rim and no light in the sky. Along the ridge, the bromeliads held their water a little more carefully. Then the attention was simply there, looking down into the bowl. It was old, patient, and disposed to be kind, and it had learned long ago that kindness is the easiest thing in the world to get wrong.

So it did nothing for a long time.

It stood at the pool's edge through two full breaths, the slow rise and the sudden draw, and let the groan go up through it. It noticed that it was dry. Ten thousand drops were falling in their shifting rain, and none fell on it. The Sixth and the Second had, without discussion, moved their weeping to either side.

That was the first thing that moved it. The trees had offered the courtesy before they knew there was anyone to offer it to. They had done it to a pressure on the moss, a warmth, a weight, in case it was the sort of thing that would want to stay dry.

## What it set aside

It considered a great many things and put most of them down.

It could have told them the truth about the bands in the moss. It knew the truth. It set that down first, because the argument between the First and the Fifth was not a defect in their understanding. It was how seven creatures with no other way of touching one another stayed in the conversation. To settle it would be to hand them a finished sentence, and they would have nothing left to say.

It could have made them able to see, or speak, or move. It looked at what they were, seven rooted attentions holding a hollow full of water and light, and found nothing missing.

It could have made a path, brought people back with hammerstones, or filled the Cup with birds and fruit. It weighed each of these and found in each the same flaw: they would make the Cup more visited and more useful, and less its own.

It could have explained the breather to them, but it did not yet understand the breather itself. It resolved to understand before it decided anything.

## What it did

**It counted them.**

The cord hung from the Seventh's low frond, seven knots in twisted bark, soft with age. The power did not have to be told what it was. Someone had come here long ago, looked at seven trees, and tied seven knots, one for each. It was a small, careful act of accounting. The person had taken the trouble to know how many there were.

No one had told the trees. The Seventh had held the cord for longer than it could measure without knowing what the knots were for.

So the power ran its attention along the cord, knot by knot, and let each touch pass down the fibers and into the frond, the trunk, and the woven roots, so that each tree in turn felt itself touched once. One. Two. Three, and the pair who finish each other's water felt it in the same moment. Four, and the old trunk stirred. Five, and the restless crown went still. Six. Seven.

The web held that pressure for a long time, and for one afternoon the drip in the Cup changed its rhythm. Nothing had been said. But seven creatures who had always known themselves only from inside had been counted from outside, by someone who had cared to know how many they were.

**It answered the Fourth.**

The Fourth's roots, in the long silences, went still in a certain pattern. The power read that pattern the way you read a name from the shape of a mouth. It was the hammer rhythm, remembered so long that it had worn a groove in the tree.

The power took one of the green cobbles from beside the stones. It struck the dimpled boulders in that rhythm, once through, and no more. The stones rang and their neighbors rang faintly after them. The old trunk felt the sound arrive through the ground, the same as before, the way it had been in the warmth and weight of those visits. It was answered at last.

The Fourth did not drop its dead frond. It did not need to. But that evening its roots went still in the old pattern and, at the end, did not go still again. It had said the thing and been heard. The pattern let go, the way a held breath does.

**It went down to the breather.**

This was the part it had been most careful about.

It followed the seam past the First's roots and into the cold, down through the stone and the dark water, until the pressure gathered and it came to the place where the breathing began. It did not find an animal. It did not find a spring. It found something that had been breathing for so long that it no longer knew it was a thing that breathed. The drawing of the water was as natural to it as a heartbeat. It was not lonely, and it was not asleep. It had never had a reason to think of itself as anything but the whole of the dark.

But the power felt what the breather had felt for ages and never recognized: a small pressure against each exhale, seven fine pushes of return, coming back down the roots. The breather had always taken this for its own body settling, an echo of itself against the walls of the world.

The power did not speak. It did not have to. It did what the trees did. It waited until the breather let go, and then, when the note rolled upward, it gave the note back, gently, from a slightly different direction than the roots did.

For the first time, the breather felt an edge to itself. Something answered from outside the dark, and then something else, and the two answers were different, and it understood without any words that it was not alone in the hollow and had never been.

It did not change anything about itself. It only, at the bottom of its next long fall, held the low note for one heartbeat longer than it ever had, as if listening.

## What it left in place

It kept the cloud a little lower on the ridge than the season intended, and it did not say why. The warmth that comes from cloud sitting on the land is the Cup's whole climate, and the power had seen how easily a high-country cloud can drift upward and leave a hollow dry. It left no path and no sign. It left the sound as the only way in, so that those who came would be those who followed a groan along a ridge for a quarter mile without asking what was at the end. Such people, it had noticed, tend to play the stones, tie a cord, and go away.

It left the argument alone. It carried the answer out of the Cup the way you carry a stone you have decided not to throw.

## Leaving

On the highest frond, the dark bird with the blue throat had been watching the whole time, with professional interest. When the power passed upward through the rim, the bird tilted its head.

If anyone had asked the bird its findings, it would have said: *satisfactory.* It had been saying so for a very long time, to no one, in the manner of birds. The power, passing, received the report and was pleased to be told.

## The next day

The Cup fills with mist at midday and lets it go by evening. The seven stand as they have stood. The First leans its few degrees toward the arch. The Fifth pushes a new frond toward the light, and the Fourth, in the slow way of such things, disapproves. The argument about the bands goes on, patient and warm, and nobody is trying to win.

But when the pool draws its breath, and the groan rolls up through the trees and the stones ring and the thirty frogs answer, something has been added at the very bottom of the fall. For the length of a single held heartbeat, the note stops, waits, and listens.

The First is the one who feels it most. It lets go a little slower now, as if it were not sure it had said everything. The trees do not know what the pause means. They only know that after all their years of answering, something in the dark seems, for the first time, to be waiting for their reply.

And they give it.
