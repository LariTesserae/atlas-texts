# The Attention

It did not arrive. Arrival implies a road.

The nearest comparison is the way a room changes when someone who loves you walks into it behind your back. The dipper stopped bobbing on its stone. The moss did not move. The tremor in the water held in every leaf went, for the length of one breath, perfectly even, as if the whole hollow had been set down on a level table. The keeper, asleep in her cradle of roots above the cairn, turned over and did not wake. Sleeping, she felt it in her sternum as a change in the note, and she took it for the turning of the breath a little early. It was not.

What had noticed her was old, kind, and very careful, and it had noticed the way one notices a well-made thing in a stranger's house: the rail worn to fit a hand, the dishes scoured all but one, the awl set down and left alone for centuries. It saw that the whole place was an act of restraint, kept up by one small woman with a yew hook and a pot of ochre. It understood at once that it could ruin it in a dozen generous ways.

## What It Considered, and Set Down

It could have given her back her hearing. It looked at the dulled bones of her ear and at what she had built in their place, a sense that reads a storm from the thickening of a bass note and a fox from a shiver in a leaf, and it left the bones as they were. A cure would have made her ordinary and taken the one instrument the hollow is played on.

It could have given her company, a companion for the long dark or a successor with the gift ready-made. But it saw the girl on the upper field watching the rim, and it saw the neat knots and the moss-soled wrappings left where a child would find them, and it understood that what was passing between them was the only kind of handing-on that holds. Anything pressed into that girl from outside would be a weight and not a inheritance. It did not so much as turn her head.

It could have made the place safe: lifted the thin air out of the cavern at the turning, roughened the wet steps, hung a lamp in the throat. But the rules were the safety, and the danger was what kept the rules alive. A hollow that forgave carelessness would be visited carelessly, and the next two sleepers in the lean-to would have no red crossed fingers to read.

It could have stopped the yew from falling. The tree has leaned six centuries toward the hollow and will come down across the rim someday, in some winter gale, with a sound no one will hear. That, too, it let stand. A thing that has leaned that long has earned its fall.

It could have told the farm on the hill what they had been feeding, or told the world. It let the loaf go on appearing on the flat stone and the knot of hazel go on answering it, an arrangement that had lasted longer than any law because neither side had ever asked the other for more.

And it knew why the awl had been set point-inward. It had known from the first moment, the way it knew all such small true things. It was the hardest of the refusals, and the one it kept most gently, the way you keep a door shut on a sleeping child. The keeper likes not knowing. She holds two guesses side by side, and the space between them is where she keeps something like company. To fill it would be theft, however kindly done.

## What It Did

So there was very little, and it waited for the slack.

At the next turning, when the breath reversed and the roar dropped its bass and the woman had gone up through the wet pasture to the stone byre with her bedding and her hook and her pot, the hollow was empty of anyone who could be hurt by it. Only then did the Attention go down.

It went down as it had come, with no footfall. It stood on the eastern stair and knew each step by the dishes worn in it. It laid what it had for a hand along the rail, along the groove polished by palms, and it did not read the rail so much as receive it: a small quick hand, a broad slow one, a hand with a missing finger, a hand that shook, eleven generations of the same gentle grip. It took no single one of them. It took the shape they made together, the hand that all their hands had been wearing toward.

At the wall above the hearth it did not touch the old prints. It found the red seam in the north face where the iron weeps and took a little of what the rock offered freely. Then it pressed, into a clean patch of limestone between the fresh scab-colored print and the oldest brown one, a hand.

It was no one's hand. It was the rail's hand, the line's. The fingers were not spread as in *come*, or folded as in *danger*, or crossed as in *do not sleep here*. They were arranged in a way the wall had never held. The first two were laid together and the others curved slightly inward, as a palm curves around something small and warm to keep it from the wind.

There was no word for it in the vocabulary of the hollow. The people who made that vocabulary had needed *come* and *stay* and *the water is rising*. They had needed to warn and to summon and to sign their names. They had never needed the one that says *I heard you*, because the ones they spoke to had always been there to hear.

Last, before it left, the Attention bent to the single dish on the stair that had been left unscoured and filled with rainwater. It took one swallow, no more, and left the skin of algae unbroken on the surface. It thought this was the courteous thing. The offering had been made to a visitor, and a visitor who declines is a poorer guest than one who drinks.

## The Return

She came back down after the wind had turned, when the air below had cleared and the roar had come back into its warm-season voice, thin and high with the keening edge. She took the stair with her palm on the rail. The rail felt the same. It was only when she reached the third rest place, where the dishes hold their water, that she stopped. One dish stood a thumb's depth lower than the rain that morning should have left it, and the algae on top lay flat and whole.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she went on down, more slowly, and at the bottom she saw the wall.

She did not startle. In the roar there is no startling. She stood with her breath smoking in the spray and looked at the print between her own and the oldest, and the fingers curved as no fingers had curved there before, and she read it, not as she read the other hands, by memory and habit, but as one reads a face across a room: all at once, and entire.

She could not have said how she knew it. There was no one to say it to. What she knew was that for forty years or sixty she had put her palm to the wall and answered the voice that never stopped, and had never once wondered whether the answering went anywhere. Now she had her answer, and it was not a word. It was a hand, made in a language that until that morning had no word for it.

She did not look for the maker. She did not go up to the rim and scan the pasture, or lie awake for a sign. She had lived too long in a place of conditions to mistake this for a mystery. It was a fact, like the breath and the turning. Something had been here and had kept its manners.

She laid her palm against the new print. It fit. It should not have; the hand was a little larger than hers and a little smaller, a little older and a little younger, but her fingers found the curve of it as if they had made it. She stayed like that until the coin of sky went from pearl to white, and the dipper walked into the basin, and the salamanders drew back into their crevices.

Then she went to the slab, and ground ochre, and did her work.

Nothing else changed. The lean-to remained a frame. The yew went on leaning. At the farm, that week, the girl left a knot on the gatepost in a neater hand than before, and the loaf was there, and the socks were darned wrong. The Attention was gone, and only the wall knew it had been, and the wall had never talked.
