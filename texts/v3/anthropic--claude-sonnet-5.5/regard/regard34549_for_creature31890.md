# What Came to the Wet Loft

## The Attention

It arrived the way the light does, without a door. It was not the size of the room, since it had no size. For a long time it did only what a wise thing does first, which is nothing.

It listened to the hammering through a full turn of the weather: the surge, the downpour, the dripping afternoon, the frost coming down the sky-hole like poured milk. It learned that the tock-tock-tock was speech, and that the speech was about what would exist next. It learned that the room did not agree with itself, and that this was not a defect but a grammar.

Then it considered what it could do. It was capable of a great deal, and the list was the danger.

## What It Considered, and Set Down

It could have made the room *consistent*. Twenty paces, every time. Eleven terraces, always. Ropes with two ends. It saw at once that this would be a kind of murder. The Setters live in the disagreement the way frogs live in the wet. A room that could be measured could be finished, and the whole of them is the unfinishing.

It could have shown itself to the hammerers and answered the question of who was on the far side of the wall. It saw that the wall was the relationship. The sound that arrives before the body is the oldest courtesy they have. To step through it would be to spend it.

It could have cooled the vent, or warmed the frost, or tamed the vines and ended the long war of poles and appetite. It looked at the loop (wood grown, wood lashed, wood eaten, wood grown) and found no waste in it. A weary, respectful, endless argument with something always right and always hungry is not a suffering. It is a marriage.

It could have told them how it ends. The seam closes, or opens. It knew which. It set that knowledge down carefully, like a hot dish, and left it. They were building backward from that moment toward a beginning already furnished, and no one gives away the ending of a house to the people still laying its floor. Whatever they would find on the far side, they were owed the finding.

It could have explained the moss, the thinning of age, the installed years. It found it did not want to. The explanation would have been smaller than the moss.

So, of everything, it did almost nothing. Wisdom, here, was mostly a list of refusals. But a list of refusals is not yet a visit, and it had come to visit.

## What It Learned First

It learned to walk so the frogs would not fall silent.

When it first drew near, the reed-pipes stopped all at once, and every mallet in the walls stopped with them, because something was about to be finished that should not be. The power understood. Its own attention had a finishing quality, a way of settling on things and making them *known* and therefore done. It had to learn to attend without concluding, to look at a wall without completing the wall. This took some time. The Setters, waiting behind their stone, did not hurry it. When at last the frogs resumed, thin and unbothered, and the mallets took up their argument again, it understood that it had been forgiven for something it had not known it was doing.

## What It Learned Second

It learned the knot.

Nobody taught it. That is how the knot works. It watched a lasher's wet hands make the ugly over-and-under hitch in the dark, and then it found that it knew the knot, the way you know how to swallow. It tied one on the ladder, at the third rung from the bottom, where the young sleep. The knot held. A lasher, passing later, ran a thumb over it, found nothing wrong with it, and went on. This was the highest praise the place had.

## What It Did

It did three things, and it did them the Setters' way, with hands.

**It sat with the potter.** In the packed rows at the back, where a wheel turns with a pot on it that is full of fern and also empty and wet, there was a place where someone always sits. The seat was empty that hour. The power took it. It kept company with a making that was already made, and it said nothing, and it did not try to see the potter's face. The wheel turned. Somewhere in the vault, one long stroke of a mallet rang and stopped. That may have been thanks. It may have been coincidence. In the Wet Loft the two are not opposites.

**It cut its thumb.** It took a block from the unset pile, a small one, at the edge of the work where no one would miss it. It dressed the block with flint and antler-pick, badly at first, then less badly. When it had made the chisel-marks crisp and scalloped, it *stopped*. This was harder than the dressing. Everything in it wanted to smooth the edge, to finish, to make the stone kind. It held its hand back and let the block be sharp and unused and full of promise. The edge took a small crescent from the pad of its thumb. It looked at the mark, and for a while it did not put the block down.

**It made a place to lean.** It did not choose a visitor's place. The Setters have made a thousand of those, with steps worn to fit feet that haven't come. It looked instead for the thing the Setters had never made for themselves. Everything in the room is hospitality toward the arriving: the dish in the stair, the block where the shoulder will lean, the lamp in every niche. Nobody had ever worn a step for a wearer's knee. Nobody had set a block for a setter's back after twenty hours on the stone.

So on the lowest terrace, by the red seam of the vent, where the mallets ring loudest, it set the small sharp block into the wall at the height of a stooped person's shoulder. Beside it, in the stair, it carved a shallow dish by hand, hollow by hollow, to the depth of a knee that has knelt for a lifetime. It worked from knowledge of a kneeling that had occurred ten thousand times and never once been met. Then it pressed a little old moss into the joint with the flat of its wounded thumb, and waited for the moss to have been there forever.

## After

It left the way the light leaves, by moving on to the next terrace, and it did not close anything behind it.

The rooms did not change. Twenty paces, then thirty-one. Nine terraces, then fourteen. The ropes still have too many ends. The vines are still eating the ladder. The mountain rises a fraction each hour, and the Setters still feel it in their spines as a guest expected for a long time, and the power did not tell them whether the guest is coming or has come.

But at the hour when the shaft of light touches the lowest terrace and the vent begins to surge, before the downpour, a setter comes down the ladder with a mallet and a bad back and stops at the wall. There is a block there that nobody set. There is a dish in the step that nobody remembers carving. The setter does not ask. The setter puts a shoulder to the stone, and it fits, and the setter lets out a breath that has been held, perhaps, for a very long time.

Then the rain comes, warm and heavy, and they all stand in it with their mouths open, and the water runs down through the moss and out again clean.

Behind the wall, one clean, ringing strike, like the closing of a sentence, and then, unhurried, the tock-tock-tock begins again, ahead of everything, making the next thing that will have always been there.
