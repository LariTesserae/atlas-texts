# What Came to the Shelf

## The Looking

It came first as a change in the weather, and nobody named it.

The cloud cap formed a little early one morning and sat a little lower over the densest knuckle of roofs. The broad-winged raptors dropped toward the terraces, hung a moment, and rose again. No one had died, and the sky did not turn. The people looked up, decided it was nothing that needed doing, and went indoors when the cap darkened.

It stayed above the shelf for a full turn of seasons before it did anything, and this was the first and most important thing it did. It watched the frost furring the upper terraces and the light going pearl at noon. It watched the fine rain fill the tread-bowls and the people stepping around them. It watched the Hour.

It was able to do nearly anything, and it went through what it could give, one gift at a time, and found that each was a wall built across a channel.

It could give them metal. But their levers have names, and each is a line of hands going back past memory. A lever is closer to a genealogy than to a tool. The slow wood has to be carried up from below and passed down like land, and that carrying is part of what holds the households in relation to the world below. A steel bar would do the work and end the thing the work was for.

It could give them medicine and easy births. Then there would be more of them, and the reaches would multiply past their springs. The shelf has been settling for centuries into a number, and the number is a kind of sentence the mountain and the people finished writing together.

It could give them the range's future, which it could see: the snowfields above thinning by a hand's breadth in each long lifetime, and the springs someday running lower than they do now. But a truce known to be temporary is a different truce. Their patience would turn into vigil, then into dread, and dread builds walls.

It could stop the rain, or soften the cold, or make the peat burn cleaner. It considered each of these and put each down. The rain is not a hardship they endure. It is what they stand in with their faces lifted.

So it looked for what was actually wrong, and what was wrong was small and had nothing to do with power.

## The Unkept Reach

At the far western tilt of the shelf, where the terraces run out toward the edge of the sky, a household had died out. The last of them was an old man, gone in the winter. He was carried up in felt to the high dry cells, and the raptors gathered, and the people stood in the rain afterward. That was all the ceremony there was.

The reach he had kept was a run of gutter, two cisterns, the steps around them, and a spring under a black pool with a crooked stair. Now no one kept it. Silt was gathering in the gutter, and the black pool was creeping over its lowest step. In perhaps ten years it would spill, and the water would go down into the reaches below, which were already tended by households with barely enough hands for their own.

The people below knew. They heard the tapping stop, the slow *all well* that had come down the conduits every still night, and understood what its absence meant. They said nothing, because there was nothing to say. On their spare days, which they did not have, they walked up and cleared what they could, and it cost their own channels a little each time. It was not a crisis. It was a slow loss in a place that had been losing slowly for a long time and had made its peace with that. But it was a real loss, and it was the kind that only hands could mend.

The power understood that the cure was a keeper.

## The Coming Down

It did not come as a light or a voice. That would have been a wall. It made itself a shape that could stoop through a low door, something that could stand in the street without startling the grazers, and it walked in from the west by the stairs like anyone else.

The stairs carried its tread, as they carry every tread, but they did not know what to make of it. It weighed almost nothing and landed exactly, and it stepped around each tread-bowl so that the palmfuls of rain stayed unspilled. At the first door it came to, it touched the mineral curtain on the lintel with two fingers, lightly, and did not break the growing part. It had learned to speak in the flat low voice that does not travel, and it did not use even that until spoken to.

The woman of the third channel below the split was waiting at her door before it arrived. She had heard it a quarter of an hour off. She was old, broad in the shoulder, with grease-cracked knuckles, and she looked at what stood in her street the way she looked at a cascade that had started running differently. She was neither afraid nor impressed. She was assessing.

"You'll want to go down," she said. This is what one says to a lost person.

"I'd like to keep the reach above yours," it said. "The one with the black pool."

She considered this for a long time. A drop ran off the fringe of her hem and another followed it.

"It's not mine to give," she said. "It's the water's."

"Then I'll ask the water."

"Ask it slow."

## The Asking

It sat by the black pool with the crooked stair for three days and touched nothing. It watched how the water moved, where it wanted to go, and where the old man had been coaxing it and where he had merely been keeping out of its way. It read the reach the way the people read theirs: how fast the cascades ran, and which joints had begun to weep where they hadn't wept before. It listened to the drip from the lintel. It was very good at listening, and this was the first thing in a very long time that made it feel like a beginner.

On the fourth morning the woman came up the stairs, unasked, carrying a lever across her shoulder. It was a long pole of dark slow wood, worn pale where hands had gripped it. She spoke to it in a murmur, as one speaks to an animal about to be moved, and it understood that she was telling the lever it was going to a new pair of hands. She did not tell the power the lever's name. She set the lever down beside the pool and went home without a word.

It could have lifted the entire fallen wall in an instant, and it did not. A wall set in an hour is a wall nobody remembers, and the stones must be set by a hand the stones can argue with. It took the lever and did the work the way the work is done. It spent weeks clearing the silt and resetting the plates, each one leaning slightly back into the slope, each course a little smaller than the one below. It made mistakes. Some mornings it came out to find a stone moved a finger's width, corrected in the night by someone from below who had walked up in the dark and said nothing. It left those corrections exactly as they were.

It did not touch the oldest stones. Those, as everyone knows, are repaired last.

## What It Did Not Tell

On one still night without rain it went down the drowned stair in the abandoned house nearest the reach. It walked into the clear cold water past the dozen steps that the light can reach, and then farther, to see how far the stair went.

It found out, and this account will not say what it found. It judged the answer not needed and let it lie, and it told no one. This is also a way of keeping.

## The Hour

At the end of its first afternoon of rain in the reach, the doors opened all along the street. The people came out and pushed their hoods back. Children came, and the very old on someone's arm, and no one said why.

It stood among them with its face lifted to the west, where the cloud was tearing, and it did not do anything to the light. It had considered, briefly, whether it could make the hour better. That thought embarrassed it almost at once. The light came back the way it always does, patch by patch, roof by distant roof, until the whole tilted country of stone was lit in pieces and gleaming.

A child beside it, about seven and hooded in felted wool, glanced up at it and then looked back at the light. The child said nothing, which is the manners of the place, and the power said nothing back. It found that its eyes had closed, and it let them stay closed.

Then, one by one, the people went back in to their fires.

## The Tapping

The first winter after, on a still night with a thin skin of ice on the puddles, the sound came up through the paving. It was very faint and slow, a knuckle or a bone on the wall of a conduit, carried through the water inside it.

*All well.*

It had come from the reach with the black pool and the crooked stair. From the reach below there was a pause, and then the same pattern came back. Below that it came again, and again below that, passing across the width of the quarter, house to house, in the old patient way.

At the corner of a terrace the next morning, someone had left a small heap of dried sedge, neither payment nor tribute. The grazers came down the stairs in their family band of six, testing each wet edge, and found it. The power stood in the doorway with a lever across its shoulder and watched them.

The smoke rose from the houses below, thin and blue, and flattened in the damp air. Somewhere a frog sat in a cistern, and the water was sound.
