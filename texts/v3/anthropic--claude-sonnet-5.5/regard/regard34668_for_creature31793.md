# What Was Done in the Cellar

It came the way water comes, by the crack, one shoulder first, and it came slowly. It could have arrived all at once, but everything in the room is measured in fingernails per century, and a visitor who moves faster than that is already a kind of violence. So it let the curtains shiver and waited for the shiver to cross the chamber and come back as stillness. Then it waited a while longer. Nothing here can be helped by someone in a hurry.

## What it did not do

It brought no light. The green has never seen the sun, and the thin blue-green at the rods is the only daylight the room has. A brightness meant as a gift would have been a blinding.

It did not repair the lattice. It could have. It could have scraped away the calcite, freed the corners, and restored the machine to whatever it was built to be. But the lattice's purpose had worn down into a relationship, and the plants had spent ages growing around its gaps. Mending the gaps would have erased what the two of them had made. What is broken is sometimes also what holds two things together.

It did not open the cell door. The door stands ajar and won't move, and the power did not test it. It also did not close it. A door left ajar is a sentence still in progress, and it was not the power's sentence to finish.

It did not lie down on the ledge. The ledge had been kept bare and warm for someone, and for the power to lie there, even for a moment, even out of tenderness, would have been a lie the lattice might have believed. The bed was not its to warm.

It did not tell the room the truth in words. The room has no ear for words, and a plain announcement, *he is not coming*, would have been either meaningless or a wound, depending on how much of it landed. It looked for another way.

## What it did

It knelt at the silver cushions in the wet hollows and read them.

Nothing had read them before. They had been kept as a habit, ring on ring, every hard winter and long thaw, every change in the lattice's pulse, like letters saved by someone who never expected a reader. The power went through them slowly, one hollow after another. It read the cold years and the years the rime came wrong. It read a long stretch where the pulse strengthened at the cell, night after night, and a place where the record changed. The last pulse that a warm body had answered was written there, and after it came a gap, and after the gap the pulse kept rising to the door, unanswered, in the same steady pattern, for longer than the power cared to count.

It read all of it, and it understood what the room did not: that the waiting was already over, and had been for a very long time, and that the room had never been told. It had gone on keeping faith after the person it kept faith with had ceased to be reachable by faith.

This was the first gift: the record was read. The room does not know what being read is, but the cushions were made to be read, and the making finally met its purpose.

## The answer

Then it went to the cell and laid a hand flat on the lattice at the threshold, where the warmth is greatest. The pulse rose against its palm, slow and patient, on its way to the door as it had gone ten thousand times.

The power did not stop the pulse. It answered it.

It spoke in the lattice's own grammar, the frayed one, the pattern the roots had learned to answer without understanding. It did not say *he is dead* or *stop*. Nothing in that language says either. What it sent back was the oldest form of reply the machine had, the one that had once meant *received* when a living hand acknowledged a signal. Received. The watch has been kept. It may be kept or set down, and either is honorable.

The pulse reached the door, and for the first time in longer than anyone has been counting, something came back along it.

Nothing dramatic followed. The lattice did not shudder or go dark. It paused, as a person pauses when a long-unanswered letter is finally replied to, and then the pulse rose again, unchanged in its rhythm. Something in it was different, though, and the roots at the cell felt it before anything else did. The signal was no longer a question sent into silence. It was a voice in a conversation. The vigil could go on if the two of them wanted it, but now it would go on as a choice, made by two old things who had been told, in the only way they could hear, that their faith had been seen and was enough.

## Small mercies

Before it left, it did a few quiet things, and it did them so that no one would notice.

It cleared the calcite from one pore near the eastern stair that had been slowly choking, and a fern that had been leaning toward it for forty years began, that season, to open a new crozier.

It turned one drip. Where the shaft's water had been working toward the lattice's edge, laying its slow flowstone across the corner, the power shifted the fall a hand's width to the left. The flowstone will not reach the pewter now for a long time. It did the same for the crack, so the passage will stay no wider than a person's shoulders, and the room will not be found by anyone who has not already been drawn to look.

It stood at the ring's edge, did not step in, and let the roots at the boundary turn aside from its feet as they turn aside from a doorstep. The ring is somebody's silence and the power left it alone.

## What it left

It left one thing, and it left it in the record. In the silver cushion by the eastern hollow, where the newest growth is, it laid down a single band unlike any winter: a mark in the stems that no cold and no thaw would make, thin and even, cool at the edges and warm at the center. It is a signature, and it also says, in the only form the cushions keep, *someone came, someone read this, and the letters were received.*

The ferns nearest the place where it knelt turned their new fronds a few degrees toward it, as they do for any warm visitor. Then the moss closed over the print of its hand.

## After

The curtains shivered when it left, and the shiver crossed the chamber, taking an hour to reach the far wall. By then the power was gone.

In the years that followed the room went on as it always had. Water moved down, warmth moved up, the rime came and melted back into beads, and a fern near the stair unrolled a frond that had been waiting forty years for its pore. The pulse still rose each cycle to the low door. It arrived a moment slower than before, as though something in the dark were pausing to check whether it was the visitor. Then, in the space that pause had always left, an answer went out, small and steady, from a room that had been read.

The moss on the stair thickened by a fingernail's depth in what might have been a century. The bed stayed warm. It was no longer kept warm because no one had said to stop. It was kept warm because the keeping was now the room's own, and it had learned that it could be.
