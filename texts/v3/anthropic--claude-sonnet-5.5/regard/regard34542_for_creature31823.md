# What Came to the Hollow

It came along the marsh road the way everything does, on foot, in the haze. It heard the long steam-note held on one pitch and did what almost no one does. It stopped and turned toward the sound.

It was not a large presence. It had no light around it and no voice, and the sedge did not bow. The only change was that, for the first time in a long while, the hollow was being attended to by something that could hold all of it at once: the thirty stacks, the loom of the frogs, the snails in their combed lines, the amber in the cracks. It also noticed how little of the hollow needed anything.

## What It Did Not Do

It considered the possibilities, as a wise thing does, and put each one down.

It could give the salamander eyes. But there was nothing in the pool worth seeing that the ridges did not already show better, and sight would give the salamander a horizon, which the pool did not have.

It could give it ears, or a name, or wonder. But the salamander's life was whole, and wonder is a hunger. It would have been a strange gift to hand a sleeper who was not cold: a blanket, and the knowledge of what cold was.

It could add stacks, cool the heat, or stop the rot. That would have been the cruelest kindness of all. The count of thirty was not a limit imposed on the hollow. It was what the hollow had worked out, over a thousand years, that it could feed. To improve it would be to tell the place its own answer was wrong.

It could free the frogs from their stations, but the frogs would not have known what to do with a day.

It could tell the salamander it was the last of a long line, or the only one of its kind, or that it was loved. But the salamander would have had to carry that, and it had no place to put such a thing. The words would have sat in the water like a stone in a tuning fork.

So it left the hollow alone. This was not a failure of power. The power was the reason it could.

## What It Noticed

There was one thing it lingered over, in the fungus.

The pale threads ran through the mat like nerves, and the pulses they carried sometimes arrived before the salamander had made them. Nobody had ever taken this seriously, least of all the salamander. But the power laid its attention against the threads the way you lay a palm against a wall to feel the machinery behind it. There was a slow, diffuse, patient mind in there, spread thin through the whole mat and older than any salamander. It had watched them come and go, each a small pale thing found one day in the mud at the root. It had held the whole place in a kind of long drowsy regard, and it had never once been asked anything.

So the power asked. It did not use words, which the fungus had no use for. It sent a single question down the threads in the only grammar the hollow spoke, the pulse at nine seconds: *Is there anything you want?*

The answer took most of a night to cross the mat. When it came, it was the same pulse returned, unchanged, one beat after the question. The power understood it to mean *this*. *Continue.* It was not an asking. It was a small contentment, as steady as the gas.

The power stayed with that answer for a while, and something in it was moved, though it did not show.

## The One Thing

It did do one thing, and it was almost nothing.

The salamander rises about four hundred times a year to lean against the underside of the world. It presses its flat blunt head to the mat and pushes until the blister breaks into bubbles and the gas goes where it is needed. Nothing has ever pushed back. The mat gives, and the salamander leans, and that is the whole transaction. It has been a thousand years of leaning on something that only yields.

The power waited for a swell to rise, and it knelt on the raft above the pool. As the salamander came up beneath it, pale and long in the tea-dark water, with its red plumes streaming and its ridges faintly blue, the power set the flat of its hand against the moss, directly over the salamander's head.

When the salamander leaned, the power leaned back.

It was not much. It was the exact weight of the salamander's push, given back without more or less. For the length of one heartbeat, nine seconds, the pressure on the salamander's head was answered.

The salamander went still.

It did not understand, since understanding was not something it had. But its ridges brightened until the pool glowed, and every hum-fish in the water turned and hung facing the light. The current ran strong through the roots, and the fungus carried the pulse out through the whole mat. Then the stacks sang, all thirty at once, in one huge shuddering blare that rattled the canopy. The frogs went silent for a single second.

Then the salamander eased off, the power lifted its hand, and the swell broke into a hundred small bubbles and seeped sideways to the roots, as it always had. The chord resumed as though nothing had occurred.

That had always been the law of the hollow, but this time it was not quite true.

## What It Left Behind

Outside the rim it made one more small arrangement. It looked ahead and saw a run of dry years coming, the kind that lower the water table by a hand's depth and leave a mat to cook. It could not have said this was interference, because rain falls somewhere every year. It leaned on the sky the way it had leaned on the moss, gently and only in the right spring. The water stayed a hand's depth from everything, and the salamander never learned that anything had been at risk.

Then the power went back up over the limestone lip. The sound dropped away behind it within a few paces, soaked up by the stone and the sedge. Somewhere in the hollow a frog on the third tussock from the north lip kept up its ratchet-call. Somewhere below the moss, the one who kept the place lay with its tail curled round a drowned root and its heart beating once every nine seconds.

On the marsh road, a hundred steps on, the power heard only a faint pulse, a ticking, and a long steam-note that you might take for a distant works.

It did not turn around. It had already done that once, and that had been enough.
