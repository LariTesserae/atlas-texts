# The Attention

It came the way the frost came: first at the edges, and small.

The goats noticed. One morning on the Ninth Terrace they stopped grazing all at once and stood facing the same way, hooves square on the grating, and the patter that usually carried through the basin simply ceased. The flues rose out of the exhaust plumes and turned in a single slow circle, without their whisper, and settled again. The lamp moths on the white walls held their wings half open for the length of a breath.

Then the Listeners felt it. Under the beam-piston's long rise and pause and fall there was a second pressure, which arrived and did not recede. An old Listener named Shelf-Above laid her palm on the central housing and stood through four turns of the flywheel. Then she took her hand away and said, in the tapping tongue, on the nearest rail: *something is here that is bigger than the engine, and it is trying to be smaller.*

## What it saw

The power was wise, and it began by looking, which is the part of wisdom most often skipped.

It saw a country that had been poured with terrible care and left unfinished, and it saw that the unfinishing was the point. It saw people who had built their whole lives around a shortage, so exactly that the shortage had become a kind of grace. Water was scarce, so every drop had a name and a route. The ground was unreliable, so every step was a small act of attention. The makers had gone, or had never been, so the Setters had made themselves into people who could be left alone with a mountain.

It could have hardened the soft places, all at once, and given the country the ground it seemed to be asking for. It saw at once what that would do. The Rodwalkers would have nothing to walk. The pairs would lose the cord and the counting. The children who walk the upside-down landing without a glance would inherit a country with no errors left to become correct. A finished place is a place that no one needs to stay in.

It could have filled the cisterns and sent rain every day. It listened to the condenser wall for an hour, to the slow scale of the drips, each at its own pitch, and understood that this was not a poverty. It was a music, and it would not survive being made plentiful.

It could have undone the copies. This was the one it considered longest. There was real grief in the lamps lit for the figures at the ends of gantries, always a little too far to hail. But the power understood something the Setters had understood before it: a copy is not a mistake laid over a true thing. It is the country still deciding, and to erase one would be to tell the country that its deciding was wrong. It could not do that and remain what it was.

So it did none of these. It stood in the middle of the basin, on a gantry between two roads, and was for a while only a very large question about how to help without taking anything away.

## The third on the cord

What it did first was ask, though not in words. It asked the way the Setters ask, by tapping.

A Rodwalker pair was going into the soft country beneath the Fourth Terrace, one behind the other with the cord between them, murmuring their disagreeing counts. On the rail beside them something tapped, not loudly, a pattern no one had made before. Their rods answered without their deciding to. The pattern came again, and this time the meaning was clear enough: *may I.*

The elder of the pair, a man called Eleven Paces, considered it for the length of one beam-rise. Then he did what a Setter does with anything that arrives without a place to stand. He shortened the cord, tied a knot in the slack, and let it hang.

The power took the knot. It became the third on the cord, walking behind the two, and it counted. This was the difficult part. It could have counted correctly, and it saw in an instant how: it could have held every step of the soft ground in its attention and named the true distance. But a true count would have been a rudeness, like speaking loudly in a room that had learned to be quiet. It counted wrongly, on purpose, as carefully as any Setter. Its number came out eleven on the way in and two hundred on the way back, and neither pair mentioned the difference. That night, at the lamp-niche, Eleven Paces said only that the third on the cord counted very badly, and that he had walked with worse.

## The crossing

It stayed a season. It learned to breathe with the beam. It learned that Scalers sing only when they are far from the walls, and it went to the far edge of the ridge and sat in the wind where it could hear them, and did not let them know it was there. It was allowed, in return, to watch the big orange lichen on the Ninth Terrace being measured in chalk. The Chalkers said nothing about it, but one of them set the chalk down within its reach.

In the end it did one thing.

There was a woman named Landing-Under, whose brother had been copied six years before. He stood at the end of a long gantry in the eastern tiers, facing the same way as ever, doing the same small task with his hands, an oiling or a wiping that never ended. She lit his lamp every dusk. She had never once walked the gantry, because it was soft, and its length changed each time she looked at it. Nobody walks toward a copy. It would be like walking toward a wish.

On an evening in the dry season, when the mist had gone thin and the sun had not quite dropped behind the eastern ring, the gantry was eleven paces long.

It was not a trick of the light. The Rodwalkers on the boundary felt it in their rods: a span that was settled, for the length of one evening, into a single true distance. It was the only time anyone recalled the power doing anything that could be seen. The rail rang clear as a bell when tapped, the first time any of them had heard it ring so.

Landing-Under looked at it for a long moment. Then she set down her rod, which no Setter does on a gantry, and walked.

What passed between her and the figure at the end is not told. The Setters who watched from the terraces say she reached him in something like eleven paces, that he turned his head, that she stood beside him for a while, and that his hands, for the first time in six years, were still. They say she laid her palm flat against his sternum, two fingers, the sign for *the engine is about to turn*, and that he breathed, once, in time with the beam.

Then the mist came back, the gantry lengthened out of reach, and she was walking back along it for what seemed a long time, counting, and the count was wrong, as it always is.

The power did not repeat this. It could have, and it understood that once was the whole of the gift. A crossing that could be relied on would have been a bridge, and a bridge would have ended the grief by ending the distance, and the distance was where the love had been living.

## The lamp

When the power finally left, or when it became impossible to say whether it had, the Setters did not hold a gathering. It was not that kind of leaving. But that evening, at the roll, a child who had never been told anything about it lit a lamp in an empty niche low on the Fourth Terrace, near the place where the rail had first rung.

Someone asked her who it was for.

"For whoever's there," she said. "It's rude not to."

The lamp is still lit. It is counted in the ledger of flames with the others, among the newly lost and the newly copied, and no one has ever been quite sure which column it belongs in. It burns beside a cold plate, and the beetles come to it at dawn.

## What the rail said

The oldest Setters say that on certain mornings, when the beam is at the top of its rise and the whole basin holds its breath, you can tap once on a rail and feel the answer come back a little later than it should, and a little warmer.

Nobody asks who is answering. The Setters have always held that the country is a single slow act, a breath drawn over ten thousand years, and that everyone living in it is inside the breath. It has not occurred to them to be surprised that something else is in there too, counting badly, and staying.
