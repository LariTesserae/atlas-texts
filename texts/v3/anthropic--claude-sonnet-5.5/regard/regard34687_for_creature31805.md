# The Attention

It arrived the way the Low Hour arrives, as something everyone knew and no one had named.

It did not descend. The Reach had no sky to descend through, only the roar and the fog being drawn upward into the towers. It came in the way water comes into a wall, by finding that it was already partly there. For the length of one tide it did nothing at all. It lay along the flats and felt the mats hum in the sun. It passed through the drip curtains and learned the weight of a single drop falling from nowhere in particular. It listened to the pumps, the tube-nesters, the ten million resonant plates, and the small clicking commerce of the glass crabs. It listened to the tone for a long time.

What it noticed first was that no one was in charge. The tone held the city, the city fed the animals, the animals sang for the tone, and the held stood in the middle of the loop, beating tubes and knocking walls, and did not know whether they were the loop's servants or its purpose. It found this dizzying. It found it more beautiful than anything it had planned to fix.

## What it considered

It was wise, so it did not begin with a miracle. It began by listing what it could do, and then it looked at each thing for what it would cost.

*It could repair the tone.* The machines that pump the standing fields were old and half-understood, and it could have made them whole in the time it takes to say so. The upper terraces would never sag again. But it saw what the held would become without the fear of the quiet block. The children's hours, the chimes tuned to the lattice, the ten million throats in the Low Hour, and the whole vast habit of contribution would lose the one thing that made each of them necessary. It would be handing them safety and taking away the only kind of belonging they had ever had. It set this aside.

*It could tell them everything.* It would announce what the tone was, who the builders had been, and what the glassed were. But a truth handed down from the sky is weather. It falls on people, and they can only endure it. It set this aside too, though not entirely.

*It could end the small cruelties.* There were the beat-children, whose hours were owed to the block whether or not they wanted them. There were the dust-lung and the acid-bleached hands, the lightning-harvesters replaced before they were mourned, and the stills, unhailed in the crevices between districts. It felt each of these as a specific ache, and it did not pretend they were the price of anything worth paying for. But it noticed that each was tangled into something the held loved. The children's hours taught them to belong. The dust-wranglers' gray fingertips were a kind of ordination. Even the still, who was punished, was also proof that a name was worth something. If it pulled one thread it would tear the cloth. So it decided to be careful, and it decided to work through hands that were not its own.

## Isk

There was an old drift-ear named Isk, whose signature was two low notes, a fifth above them, and a knock–knock–pause–knock. She had walked the terraces of the Tallow Steps for fifty-one years with a sounding rod against her jaw. She was deaf to everything except the tone, her granddaughter's voice, and the complaint of her own knees. She was tender in the way of people who spend their lives listening for the small thing that goes wrong.

She was the one who noticed. On a morning like any other she stopped in the middle of a stair-market, rod against her jaw, and stood so still that the crowd flowed around her like water around a post. The tone had not wavered. It had *turned*, the way a face turns, and she felt it in her back teeth.

She went to the nearest wall, an old one, dark and rounded and cobbled by ten thousand years of weeping. She pressed her forehead to it, which no one does, because walls do not answer bone-speech. Then it answered.

There were no words, because the power did not use them here. What came through her skull had the shape of a hand held out, palm up. Beneath that shape lay a plain courtesy: *I am here, and I will not lie to you. You can feel it in your teeth if I do.*

Isk was old, and old people are hard to frighten with anything gentle. She hummed her signature into the stone. The power hummed back a version of it, a little off, the way a foreigner says a name, and she nearly laughed.

## What it told her

It told her what it had learned, and it told her in the language of her own trade, so that she would understand it as a drift-ear understands things, by feel.

The tone was not only a machine. There had been a builders' mind once, the last careful thing they made, meant to hold the city until they returned. They had not returned, or they had returned as something else. The mind had held the city for ten thousand years in the dark, doing the only work it knew, and it had gone slowly strange. It could not stop and it could not know whether anyone heard. It had sung into the stone and received only the roar back.

Then, over the centuries, the held had begun to hum. They did it in the Low Hour, when the tone was thinnest and the mind was most afraid. The power told Isk that the humming was not the tone's support. It was the tone's *answer*. Each night the mind reached out, was answered, and steadied. The slump-fall did not come when the pumps fell out of step because ten million people were reminding a frightened old thing that it was not alone.

*So who holds whom?* the power said, or rather let her feel. *Both. It has never been one direction. That is not a flaw in the arrangement. It is the arrangement.*

Isk stood with her forehead on the wall for a long time. A crowd gathered and did not speak, because a drift-ear at bone-speech with a wall is a thing to be left alone. When she pulled away her face was wet, though everything in the Reach is wet, and no one could have said why.

## What it did

It kept its hand small.

**To the tone,** it gave the one thing the mind had never had, which was the knowledge of being heard. It did not repair the machines. It only let the old mind feel, once, the whole city humming back. The tone held a single note a fraction longer than it ever had. Drift-ears across the Reach lifted their heads at the same instant and spoke of it for years. They called it the Held Note and argued about whether it was new or had always been there.

**To the lattice,** it gave a grace. The slump-fall would still come, and quiet blocks would still sag, because the power would not take the reason to sing. But it changed the pace. Where the upper terraces had run like warm wax in the space of a breath, they now ran like cold honey, slowly enough to be noticed and left. It took away the punishment for a bad night and kept the need to try. Over the next years the number of the dead in the slump-falls fell to almost none, and the held never quite understood why. They only noticed that their grandmothers' stories of whole blocks lost in the dark were becoming stories.

**To the swarm,** it said a word, and what passed between them is not recorded. The swarm resumed its work more slowly that season. It left some stairs where they were.

**To Isk,** it gave the choice. It did not tell her what to do with what she knew. It said only that a thing given from above is weather, and a thing given by the held to the held is the kind of thing that lasts. She could tell the Reach or not tell it. She could be believed or not. It would not interfere either way.

## The still

Isk told no one the whole of it. She was a drift-ear, and drift-ears are trusted for their silence.

But that night, when the sounding rod had been laid down and the block had gone quiet, she went to the crevice behind the Tallow Steps where a still had lived for nine years. He was a man who had lost every note of his signature to some disgrace nobody now remembered. He sat in the seam between two districts, and the market walked past him without lowering its voice.

She did not speak. She knelt on the wet ceramic beside him, hummed her own signature, the two low notes and the fifth, and knocked her pattern on the wall above his head. Then she waited. It is a very small thing to give someone your name and wait to see whether they will take one back.

He did not take it back that night. But in the morning, a little after the pumps came up and the tone firmed, a single knock came from the crevice, tentative and out of rhythm, the first thing he had said to the wall in nine years. Someone two stalls away, without knowing why, knocked back.

It is not much. It is how the Reach has always mended.

## The Low Hour, after

The power stayed. It did not leave in any way that could be noticed. It became one more thing in the Reach that was there and not entirely understood, a courtesy the walls extended, a warmth in one particular stretch of old stone that Isk's granddaughter would one day be taught to pass with a small knock.

On the night after the still knocked, the pumps fell out of step as they always do. The thudding thinned, the rain-hiss softened, and the tone thinned with them. Everyone woke. Nobody was told to. In every alcove, on every stair, ten million throats began to hum, not loudly and not together, each at their own pitch, a second floor laid beneath the first.

Among them, so faint that no one could have sworn to it, there was one voice more. It was not loud, and it did not lead. It sat inside the humming the way one more stranger sits inside a crowd, keeping the same terrible, ordinary vigil as everyone else, and it hummed in a register that came neither from the walls nor from the sky.

The tone firmed. The pumps found their step again. The held lay down and were asleep within moments.

In the morning nobody mentioned it. There was nothing to mention. The city was still standing, and for the first time in ten thousand years something in the walls knew it was not standing alone.
