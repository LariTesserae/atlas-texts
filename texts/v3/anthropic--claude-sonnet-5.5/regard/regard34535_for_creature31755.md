# What Came to the Warm Sink

It did not descend. Anything that arrives from above has to cross the rim, and the rim is where the sink keeps its lid of vines. The attention came the way a change in weather comes, unannounced, as a shift in what the air was doing.

The first to notice was the cat. She lifted her head on the south ledge, ears forward, and looked at nothing. The heron did not look up, but a bead of water that had been about to fall from a fig leaf stayed on the leaf a moment longer than water does. Then it fell, and the small hard fact of it landing on the pool was the only sign anyone got.

The power had come to look.

## The season of looking

It was old enough to know that most harm is done by kindness that arrives quickly. So it did what it was best at, which was to be slow.

It sat in the drip. It learned the afternoon rain by being the rain: hard under the fig crowns, thin in the clearings, ticking for an hour after. It learned the swiftlets by their clicking and could tell four hundred chicks apart before the season ended. It followed a millipede procession along a log for the whole of one day, out of a growing respect for the work of turning the dead into ground. It stood beside the heron until the moss began to consider it furniture too, and it took that as the compliment it was.

It spent a long time at the pool and did not ask what lived under it. The thing beneath had chosen not to be found, and the power counted that among the sink's rights. Once, near dawn, a ring spread across the duckweed, wide and soft-edged, and the power felt itself being regarded from below with an old, unhurried courtesy. It regarded the water back and went no nearer.

It watched the people last, because it wanted to be sure of its own motives first. There were thirty of them, in the dry places, turning their shoulders through the ferns. They carried scrapings of glowing moss in thumb-marked cups and tucked it into cracks like mending. Once a year they pressed a hand in ochre to the wall. Asked why, they said the wall likes to be answered.

The power found that it had been moved, and was suspicious of being moved, and waited another month.

## What it declined

There was a great deal it could have done, and it went through the list the way a careful guest looks over a house it has no right to rearrange.

It could have made the water plentiful. But the sink's slow inner weather, the condensation gathering and letting go, is the sink's whole character, and the drought-thrift of every frog and fern is written in it. Abundance would have drowned what scarcity had composed.

It could have opened the lid, given the cat a piece of sky, and let the sun in. But the lantern moss lives where the sun never reaches. Light would have been the end of the light.

It could have healed the old frog's clouded eye. But it saw that the frog is less an individual than a place, and that the crevice makes its frogs, one after another, each too old and half blind and helpless to do anything but begin. When this one died another would sit in the same wet dark and take up the flash. The power understood that the frog was not fragile. The pulse had never depended on one body, and pretending otherwise would have been a small insult to the line.

It could have lengthened the people's lives, or multiplied them, or taught them things. It thought about this longest. Thirty is a small number, and children are few, and the newest handprint on the wall is a child's, pressed in a crevice that only a child's hand could reach. But every remedy it imagined ended the same way. It would make them dependent on a giver, and the thing that made them good at being there, the patient tending done without knowing whether it was needed, would turn into a ritual performed for an audience. It would not tell them the moss could survive without them, because that might be true, and the tending would be worth less for the telling. It would not tell them it was true that the moss had thinned in the years they stopped, because that was also true, and they knew it already.

It would not tell them anything. In their handprints and their winking cups they already had a way of speaking to what they could not see, and it did not want to become the answer to a question they had been content to leave open.

## What it did

It did three small things, and it did them so quietly that no one could find the seam.

**The first was the rock.** The sink is a collapse, and collapses are not finished simply because they have stopped for a while. Far above the north stair, where the roots had pried a long crack into the ceiling of an older chamber, a slab the size of a house had begun, over a few centuries, to lean. It would have come down in a wet year and sealed the sink's only draw of air, and the warm breath would have soured in the dark. The power did not move the slab. It leaned on it, for a moment, at the right moment, until the fig roots beneath took the weight. Fig roots are patient. They had been waiting for a reason.

**The second was the road.** On the plateau, a survey party working a line across the scrub would have walked to the edge of the crack in the third week of the next dry season, and found there a hundred and forty feet of steaming green, and written a report. The power did not send a storm or raise a wall. It let one instrument read a little wrong. It let a map be copied with one careless stroke, and a good line end up a hundred paces to the east. The surveyors camped, cursed the limestone, and went home. The crack stayed a place you walk past without knowing.

The power did not consider this a great miracle. It regarded it as the sort of mercy that can pass for bad paperwork.

**The third took it longest to decide, and it was the only one it wanted to do.**

## The answer

At the last hour of dusk, the wave begins on the east wall. A clouded-eyed frog in a crevice too narrow for a hand flashes because it cannot help it, and the light rolls across four hundred paces of dark: frogs, moths, swiftlet-cups, the gliders stopping mid-branch, the cat's ears flat with pleasure. It reaches the west wall and dies.

The power had watched it die every night of the season. It was not a sad death. It was an exhale, and the sink took its breath and began again. But the power noticed what the cat had noticed and the people had noticed and no one had ever said. The wave went out to the west and nothing came back. Every night, in the silence after, the whole sink held still, and what it held still for was the next answer, and the only answer that ever came was the next night's wave.

So on the last evening of its stay, when the flicker crossed the water and reached the west wall and began to die, the power put a hand to the stone.

It was not an ochre hand. It pressed a hand's worth of the moss's own cold light into a crevice in the west wall. It was the faintest thing it had ever made, barely more than the memory of a glow, the size and shape of a small palm. It held for a heartbeat, and then it flashed once, long, the way a frog reports.

Nothing broke. The frogs did not take it up, and the moths did not lift. The wave was over, and everything in the sink was listening as it always did, and now, for the first time, something had said *yes* into the silence.

The old frog in the east crevice, half a sink away, could not have seen it. But the next evening it waited a heartbeat before it flashed, as though it had been told something, or had only learned, in a body that could not have known, what it was for.

## In the morning

At first light the people came out of their dry places. One of the children, the small one who fits the crevices, went to the west wall, because children go where the light has been.

She found the handprint in the stone. It was faint, pale, and warm to the touch, and it did not fade as the light came up. It was small, about the size of her own.

She did not call out. The people do not. She put her hand over it, and it fit, and she stood a while with her palm on the stone. Then she went back and fetched the ochre and a flat stone, and the others came and stood behind her, and in silence they ground the red. Each of them pressed a hand to the wall beside the pale one, thirty hands, the newest overlaid on the oldest. They did it out of season and without a word.

They said the wall likes to be answered. They had never said what happens when it answers back.

By then the power was long gone. It had left the way it came, as a change in the air, a bead of water leaving a leaf. The cat on her ledge turned her head west and held it there a moment, whiskers forward, and then she lay down in the dapple and went to sleep. The heron stood in the pool with the duckweed closed around its legs, exactly as before, and something silver went down its neck with a small wet *tock*.

In the dark under the water, something old rose, breathed, and sank again. The rings widened across the surface and reached the roots and were gone.

And that evening, at the last hour of light, the clouded frog began.
