# The Counting

It arrives the way the fog does, from below, and no one sees it come.

It has no weight, so the cushion plants do not dent. It has no warmth of its own, so the frost on the tarns keeps its white rind. It has come to look, and to decide whether looking is all it should do. It has been wrong before about that, in other places, and the memory of it makes it slow.

For three days it does nothing. It lies in the fog with the moss and learns the basin's rhythms: the mid-morning white, the columns rising after noon, the light like a struck bell. It learns the marmot whistles and what each break in them means. It learns the falcon's silence and the cat's patience. It learns that everything here is arranged around a fear, and that the arrangement is beautiful.

## What it could do

It sees the possibilities clearly, because seeing is what it is good at.

It could remove the cat. The grazers would graze more freely, the calves would live, and the old female would not spend her nights listening. But it watches the herd's loop, the terraces worn pale into the moss, the stillness in the fog, the way forty animals move as one animal when a shadow falls. All of it is the cat, written in another hand. Take the cat away and in a few generations the loop would blur, the herd would spread and fatten and fail to notice the fog thinning, and the meadow would eat itself down to rock. The kindness would be a slow theft.

It could keep the fog longer, or lift it sooner, or warm the tarns. But the basin has already tuned itself to the fog it has. The flowers open in the hour after the lifting, and the bees know that hour. To move it would be to correct a poem.

It could make itself known to the ones who leave things. It could speak at the cairn and tell them the offerings arrived. But the offerings were not addressed to it. They are left in habit and in grief, and in the thin thread of a line that has forgotten why it came. If a voice answered, the habit would become a religion, and the religion would need priests, and the quiet people in soft shoes would stop coming, or come in numbers. It decides that the not-knowing is the gift. They do not need to be told they are heard. It is enough that they come.

It could give the old female more years. This one it holds longest. She is gray at the muzzle and the notch in her ear has healed white. Her knees are stiff on the cold mornings. She will not survive many more winters, and the herd will feel it before it knows what it is feeling. It could reach into her and mend, and she would walk the loop for another ten years, and the young females would never learn to lead at ease. She would become a monument. She would not be a mother, a leader, or an old animal anymore, only a fixed thing that would not end.

So it does not.

## What it does

On the first cold evening of the season the herd files toward the north end. The old female is at the front, and the others fall in behind her. They pass between the leaning gate stones and stand inside the ring of the corral, and the light goes copper on the wall above them. They wait, as they have waited every season since before any of them was born, for someone to count them.

Nobody has counted them in a very long time.

The power has been trying to think of what it can offer that would not be an intrusion. It sees now that the only true thing it has is attention, undivided and unhurried, and that the herd has been standing in this ring for generations, unknowing, asking for exactly that.

So it counts them.

It does it slowly, one animal at a time, and it lets each one be a particular animal. The gray female, torn ear, notch. Then the yearling with the crooked bib who always stands too close to her mother. Then the twin calves, then the big-shouldered one who limps on cold days. It does not hurry, and it does not round up. It counts forty-three, and the number is not a tally but a naming. Each animal is held for one breath in a regard that asks nothing of it.

The herd does not startle. The old female lifts her head. Her breath goes up in a small cloud, and for a moment she looks not at the power, which she cannot see, but at the place where the counting is happening, as though it were a warmth on that side of her face. Then she lowers her head and steps out of the ring, and the others follow. The number has been taken. They go up to the lee to lie down on the cushions.

The power watches them go. It does not know whether it has done anything. It thinks it may have done what the cairns do: made a place where a thing is said, and left it.

## The cairn

Before it goes it visits the big cairn on the rim, where the old female stands with her nose to the stones. It looks at the offerings: the twist of dried grass, the bone whistle, the red potsherd, the pebble with a hole worn through it by some other water in some other place.

It has nothing to give that would not be an intrusion. But it has been counting, and it finds it wants to leave something small, in the same idiom as the rest, so that it will not be a message.

It takes a stone from the moraine, a plain gray one, unremarkable, the size of a child's fist. It does not warm it or mark it. It sets it on the cairn's shoulder, the way someone might who had walked a long way and wanted the walk to show.

That is the whole of it. The next people who come will not notice a stone among stones.

## What remains

It stays through the winter, though it does not tell itself why.

It is there on the night the old female lies down on the warm green and does not rise. She goes in the hour before dawn, breath thinning, one ear tilted toward the herd. The others lie around her in their ranks, and the haze of their breathing goes up in the moonlight as it always has. The power does not touch her. It only stays, as she has stayed with the cairn, breathing near a thing that cannot answer, so that she does not go alone into the cold.

In the morning the vultures come down out of the thermals, unhurried, white-headed, and do what they do. The herd watches for a while and then moves down to the tarns in the fog. A younger female with a clean ear takes the lead.

That evening, at the big cairn, she stops. She does not know why. She stands with her nose near the stones and breathes out, and her breath makes a small cloud against the rock. The others wait. Then she moves on and they follow.

The power has already gone down the valley, lighter than it came. It has left the cat and the falcon, the cold and the fog, the slow warmth from the crack in the rock. It has left the loop and the gate. It will not say that the basin is better for the visit. It will say only that once, at the top of the world's patience, everything that lives there was counted, and that it is glad it looked.
