# What Came to Stand in It

The first difficulty was size.

Attention like mine has weight, and this country is so flat and so finely balanced that I could feel my regard pressing on it like a mountain trying to set itself down on a tabletop. The frogs went quiet before I had done anything. The water in the channels hesitated. So I spent what would count, in that country, as a long while making myself smaller, until I was no more than a slight leaning of the air over a dome, and the frogs took up their chorus again as if I had been a heron passing.

Only then could I look.

## What I Was Tempted To Do

I will list them, because they were real, and because the people I was watching would have understood a list.

**To show them the sea.** Everything they know leans toward it, and they have never seen it. I could have lifted the horizon and let them look at the thing their whole country is falling toward. But their leaning is the whole of their knowledge, and the leaning is complete because it is unfinished. A people who have been pointing at something for ten thousand years are not lacking it. To answer them would be to close the gesture.

**To tell them why.** Why the mountains went, why the stone is old, why the frogs go quiet in rings. They have their own reasons, and the reasons are good ones. A person is only a smaller mountain. I have nothing to add to that except length.

**To tell them they are right.** This one was harder to put down. They are right, or right enough, and I wanted them to know it. But a people who need to be told it are no longer the people who built the law. Praise from a power like me is a weather system. It would take a generation to stop looking up.

**To open the channels.** The old woman's channel has narrowed by two fingers since she was a girl, and she knows it the way you know a friend has aged. I could have held it. I understood, watching her watch it, that this was exactly what I must not do. The channel is not a problem she has. It is a friend she has. You do not hold a friend's face still.

**To end the fevers.** Here I stopped for the longest time, because I don't believe the others transfer. Restraint is easy when what is at stake is meaning. It is harder when it is a child, feverish on a platform of lashed limbs, with the mosquito clouds thick over the sand ring at dusk. I know how it sounds to say that a death is part of the pattern. It isn't. A death is a death. The people do not think a child's fever is holy. They boil sedge wax and wrap the small body in wet moss and sit very still beside it, and when it goes badly they set the child at the head of a channel and wait, and it is the worst thing in their year.

A wise thing does not get to pretend that the price of its reverence is paid by someone else.

## What I Did

I did one thing, and I made it small enough that the country could have done it itself.

Bladderworts already grow at the feet of the domes. Their traps hang under the water and take mosquito larvae by the thousand, and the people already know this, and sprinkle the flowers on their food for a reason they'd call taste. I let the bladderworts do slightly better. Not everywhere. Only in the shallows nearest the sand rings, and only by the smallest margin: a few more traps to a stem, a few more stems to a mat, a little warmth in the water in the right month, a little less silt over the seedlings. It will take three or four seasons to show, and when it does, no one will be able to point to it. There will be a few fewer fevers, and a few more old women with delta-tattooed hands. People will say the country has been generous lately. It will be true.

I did it at the rate the rain wears at the stone. A fraction of a hair's width a year, and no faster. That is the only pace at which I could be sure I was not finishing anything.

I also left it unfinished on purpose. The nudge has a gap in it, a stretch of the shallows where I did nothing, where the fevers will still come. I do not defend the gap. I only know that if I had closed it, I would have been someone who could, and they would have had to live under that. The gap keeps me honest, and it keeps them a people who mend things, not a people who are mended.

## What I Learned

I learned the tilt.

It took most of a dry season, standing as still as a heron in the shallows on a dome's western side, not as a hunter but as a courtesy. I felt for the draw against my ankle, though I do not have an ankle. I felt the whole liquid skin of the country leaning toward the sea, and I learned, to my surprise, that I could not feel it until I stopped moving. All my power is a way of moving, and it had been deafening me.

They have more than sixty words for degrees of tilt. I learned all of them, and then found I had need of a sixty-first, a word for the tilt of a thing that has been set down without any expectation of being received. There is no such word in their language. I understand why. It is not a degree of slope. It is what the old woman does at dusk.

## The Seed-Boat

She set it on the water at the dome's foot in the last amber light, with nobody to receive it. I watched her hands go still in her lap as the first frog began.

I did not reply. Everything in me wanted to. I wanted to send a boat back with one lashing left loose, a sign that I had seen and honored the law. But I understood, at the last moment, that a reply would have made it about arrival, and she had said, without saying it, that it was not. If I answered, I would have told her that a seed-boat means something only when it reaches someone. She knows better. She has known better all her life.

So I did the only thing that was not an answer. I went with the seed.

It took a day to reach the first tussock, and I was there, a slight leaning of the air. It took another to reach the next, and I was there. It drifted, snagged, turned, and drifted. A damselfly crossed it. A stilt stepped over it without looking down. On the fourth crossing it lodged in the roots of a sedge pedestal a long way from any hearth, and I stayed with it while the water stilled around it, and I did not push it, and I did not stop it. It is still there, as far as I know, a small hull of dried sedge with one seed in it, going nowhere at exactly the speed of the country.

She was right. It is not the point of it. It is very good, all the same, to have been the one standing there.

## Evening, and the Silence

When the heron lifted that night I felt the chorus die in its widening ring, and I did what the people do. I stopped. I did not know why. I only knew that everyone always has, and that the silence deserves witnesses.

It spread out across the mirror-sky, over the tussocks, over the sundews glittering red on the wet sand, over the platform where an old woman held a pot halfway to a fire that has burned since before her grandmother's grandmother. And it swelled back in from the edges, one frog, then another, then the groaning bellow like a ship's horn from somewhere out on the flats.

I counted myself among the witnesses. It was the only thing I did all season that I would call my own.

I am still here, in the way that I am anywhere. I have left the leaving undone. If I am needed, I am a loose lashing at the edge of a rim, a row of weave not woven, and anyone who knows this country will know how to tie me off, or not, as the water advises.
