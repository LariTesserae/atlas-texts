# What the Visitor Did

It came up from the north, through the dwarf rhododendron, and it was in no hurry. It had been watching the Stair for some time before it walked there. It had seen the terraces climbing, the town sinking, and sixty people ringing small bells at dusk into a roar that would have drowned any other voice. The watching had moved it in a way it did not entirely have a word for. It decided to go as a guest, and to decide nothing else until it had been one.

It fell twice on the first terrace. Nobody laughed aloud. A knock went across the roofs, two soft raps and a scrape, which means roughly *another one*, and the visitor understood it and was pleased to be understood.

---

## What it might have done

There was a great deal it could have done, and it considered all of it honestly during the three days.

It could have held the water. The fault could be persuaded to stay where it was for ten thousand years, the stair kept building, the town kept forever in its slow ascent. But the town had already thought about the day the water would choose another slope, and had made its peace with that, more completely than the visitor could have made it for them. To take that day away would be to take away the thing they had built their whole tenderness around. The Stair was not a place that endures. It was a place that is *climbing*, and that was the point of it.

It could have mended the ears of the tuners. But the deaf ones were said to hear from the inside, and the visitor had put its own palm to a rim beside one of them and felt what she felt. There was more in the stone than a healed ear could ever have carried. It left them as they were.

It could have quieted the roar, or the silence, or spared them the two lost in the counting. It could have made the dry ear a place of gentle sound. Each of these was a kindness, and each would have been a small theft, because the people had made their fear and their remedies together, and the two fitted like a hand in a glove. A power that reaches in to fix one part of a thing that works will often break the rest.

So for the most part it did nothing, and it did the nothing carefully. It had its feet washed by a woman who did not ask its name. It ate barley and cress and drank the warm sour beer. It learned to sit with a palm on a rim. It was lent a bell-name, two strokes and a rest, on a thumbnail of bronze, and it wore the bell at its wrist and was surprised how much it liked the small ringing.

On the third day the silence inside its own head became, for the first time in its long existence, unbearable. It had allowed this. It thought it was the only honest way to know them, and it was right.

---

## The counter

The woman was called Hesk in the evening speech. Her bell-name was three strokes and a long.

She was among the oldest of the counters and among the most formidable. In living memory she had sent runners to six households and saved four of them. She was also the last of her house. Her husband was in the wall. Her son had gone into the dry ear at forty and not come back. Her daughter's line had run out two generations ago in a bad winter. Every dawn and dusk she rang the counting on the great bell, and listened to sixty households answer, and then, alone on the roof above her grandmothers, she laid a knuckle on her own hearthstone and knocked her own rhythm down through it. It was answered only by the dead, who do not knock back.

The visitor learned this without being told. It was in the way she stood at the bell.

On the evening of the third day she rang the peal exactly as always. Then she washed her hands, put on her good oiled wool, and walked north, past the last terrace, into the upper pass, where the roar thins and the dry ear begins. No one stopped her, and no one was meant to. She was very old, and it was the custom.

The visitor followed. It made no secret of doing so, and she let it.

---

## The dry ear

The last of the water-noise died about a mile above the town, in a place where the moss gave out and the ground turned to plain grey scree. The silence came down like a lid. The visitor, who had let itself become as a person for these days, felt it press against its own inner ear until its blood began to speak. It could hear the beat of its heart and the small wet click of its eyes. Hesk walked steadily through it with her mouth set. She had walked toward this her whole life.

She sat at last on a flat stone, at the edge of the pass where the light was going gold, and folded her hands. She was afraid. She did not hide it. Her fear was the plain fear of a woman who has spent sixty years making sure no one falls unheard, and who is about to do it to herself.

She looked at the visitor and said in the evening speech, softly, with her lips shaped carefully so it could read them: *You needn't stay.*

The visitor considered this for a long moment. Then it took the small bell from its wrist, the two strokes and a rest, and set it on the stone between them. It laid a knuckle beside it and knocked, very quietly, *her* rhythm. Three strokes and a long.

She stared at it.

*How—*

*You ring it every night,* the visitor said. *I have been listening to you for three days. I was counting too.*

It knocked again. Three and a long. It would knock all night if it had to. It did not promise her anything else, no cure and no reprieve. It offered only that here, in the one place her people feared for exactly this reason, someone was knocking her name into the stone, and would go on doing so until there was no one left to hear it.

She was quiet a long time. Then she put her own knuckle beside the visitor's and knocked with it, and the two rhythms went out over the scree together, small and dry and hollow in the huge quiet. It was, the visitor thought, the loudest sound it had ever heard.

She died before moonrise, easily, with her hand on the stone. The visitor kept knocking until dark, and after.

---

## What the visitor did afterward

It carried her down. She had not asked for this, but she had not asked otherwise, and the visitor judged that the custom of going out to die was about not being a burden, and that being carried home would not make her one. It set her in the open chamber under her own hearth, on dry moss, in her good wool, beside her husband's niche. The hush of the water in the walls rose around her like a large room full of sleepers.

Then it did the one thing it had decided to do, if it did anything.

It went to the deepest of the warm basins on that moonless night, and stood at the rim in absolute stillness like the others do, and waited until the faint blue-green light began to rise. It did not touch the water. It made no demand of it. It asked, in the only way it could ask such a thing, whether the stone, which had been counting the town far longer than the town had counted itself, would count one more.

It did not know whether it was heard. It never learned.

But a deaf tuner named Orrin was sitting that same night at the rim of a lower basin with his palm flat on the stone, as he did most nights, and he felt something he had felt all his life and never once been able to knock out. The slow brightening and fading of the glow had a rhythm after all. Three strokes and a long. He sat up very straight. He rapped it out on the rim with his mallet, wrong the first time, then right.

By dawn the whole town knew. By dusk the counters were arguing about it. Nobody could say why the stone had taken up Hesk's bell-name, or what it meant, or whether it would ever do so again. The oldest of them said only that the stone had been counting them all along, and that it seemed to have made an exception, out of manners, for the one who counted best.

At the dusk peal, from Hesk's hearth on the empty roof, someone laid a knuckle on the stone and knocked her rhythm down. It was a child of a neighboring house, who had been told to and did not know why. From below, faintly, through the tufa, there came what may have been an answer or only the water.

---

## Departure

The visitor left on the fourth morning, going north as it had come. At the last threshold it washed its own feet, and it hung the little bell with its two strokes and a rest on a peg beside the door, where guests leave them. It meant *this one was counted, for a while.*

It changed nothing else. The falls went on ringing in their hundred voices. The stone rose a finger's width that year, and a hand's width in the wet. The water did not yet choose another slope, though one year it would, and the white stair would crack and the lichen would have its turn. The visitor understood this and did not grieve it, and thought it right.

What it took away was a habit. Far from the Stair, whenever it had a moment, it would lay a knuckle on whatever was nearest and knock, once, softly, three strokes and a long, for the one who had counted everyone and had, at the end, been counted.
