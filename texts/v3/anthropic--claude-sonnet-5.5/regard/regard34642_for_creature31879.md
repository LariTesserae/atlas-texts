# The Shimmer's Season

The power did not arrive as light. Light would have been a rudeness in a city where the moss drinks sound and the day keeps one pearl color. It arrived at the grey hour before dusk, on the Fore-edge, in a coat of mustard felt somebody had left drying on a rail, and it stood a while on ground that gave slightly under its weight, and it listened.

It had come with a long list of what it could do. It could dry the air and warm the lanes. It could still the risings, so no more floors tilted toward the west overnight. It could give the Fore-edge the firm dark stone of the Spine, so the poorest children would not grow up walking like people on a boat. It could cure the tide-stuck doors and stop the slow yearly loss of gulls.

It was wise, and so it read the list a second time and saw that it was a list of things it could ruin.

The gait of the Fore-edgers was the gait of people who had made peace with change. The hush was a courtesy made from cold, wet, and closeness. The doors stuck because the plain breathed, and the plain breathing was the reason for the neighbors, the planes, and the sixty people in step. Nearly everything the city was proud of had grown out of something it suffered. A power that removed the suffering would find, too late, that it had removed the pride and left a dry, warm, quiet town with nothing to be quiet about.

So it did the harder thing, which was to become useful in the ordinary way.

## The apprentice

It took a small body of the sallow, weathered kind, and it took the trade nobody honored. A shimmer named Ochre Tarn, sixty years on the Fore-edge and short of breath, found a young stranger standing at her sill with a satchel of wedges, watching her work with the frank attention of someone who had never seen a thing done and wanted to see all of it.

She did not ask where it came from. On the Fore-edge you do not ask. She rapped on the doorframe, *can you carry*, and it rapped back, badly, *I can learn*, and she huffed through her nose, which was her laugh.

For a season it slid wedges under sagging sills. It learned to feel a rising begin in the sole of the boot, that faint change in the ground's weight, like a sleeper's arm shifting under a blanket. It learned that the skill was not strength or cleverness but a certain humility, a willingness to be moved by the ground before you moved it. It was good at this, of course. It was good at everything. It made itself clumsy on purpose for the first weeks, because a family will not forgive a mender who is too perfect, and because being bad at something is the only way to learn how the people who are bad at it feel.

It carried a pole when a house was walked. It was the sixty-first, and no one counted. The family's youngest slept upstairs the whole quarter-mile, and the power, shouldering the corner of a wall that had a kettle warm behind it, felt something it had not felt in a very long time. It was a large thing being carried by many small things, very carefully, in step.

When the house was set down, the door stuck. The neighbors murmured. Someone brought a plane. The power took the plane and planed the door like everyone else, and it took twenty minutes, and it did not once think of a shorter way.

## The naming

Ochre Tarn, who did not ask questions, did one thing that was close to asking. She took the apprentice to the midwife of the ring, an old woman named Brackish Weft, and said only, "It has no name."

At the next rising, in the grey before dawn, Brackish Weft laid the stranger's palm flat on the lifted clay and put her own ear beside it, as she had done for four thousand infants. She listened for a long time. The rising subsided. Her face did something complicated.

"*Lull*," she said at last, and then, more quietly, in knuckle-speech against the clay so that no one but the power would understand: *I have heard that one before, under the others. I did not think it was a name. I thought it was the ground.*

The power rapped back, gently: *Both.*

She nodded as if it had been an ordinary answer, and asked nothing more. The power loved her for it, in the small clean way it had of loving.

## The gulls

It went to the edge of a fresh stratum one grey evening and stood with the grown ones, arms folded, not entirely calm, and watched the plate-gulls settle on the wet new rock. It counted seven. The flock lifted a breath before the weight, all at once, in silence, and the Press came down.

One gull did not lift. It had settled a little apart from the flock and stood facing the descent, and the power, which could have caught it in a breath and set it on a roof, did not. It looked at the bird and understood something the grown ones had suspected for centuries and never dared say. The gull was not late. It was not foolish. It had come, as the dead of the city come, to be leaved.

The power let the weight come down and stood in the silence afterward with its hands folded, thinking that it should not tell the fossil-sellers, or the children in their chalk rings, or anyone. It was better held as the city held it, as something that made the grown ones stand with folded arms and be a little afraid, because that is a kind of respect. The people had been right to be uneasy. It would not spoil that for them by being certain.

## The roof

In the last month of its season the power climbed to a flat roof on the Spine at the still hour, lay on its back in the sedge, and put its ear to the moss.

The Listeners were there, old ones mostly, a dozen shapes in green felt lying in the fog like pressed flowers. Nobody spoke. One old man shifted to give it room, and it lay down among them, and they listened together to the sound under the sound.

Before, it had heard only the chewing, slow and huge. Now, lying among people who had spent their lives training their ears, it heard what the sound was doing.

It was not a maker at work. It was a young thing learning to have a body.

The being under the plain had no shape it could name. It was old the way the sea is old, and new the way a child is new, and it had only lately discovered that it could lay one thing on another and make a place of it. The Press was not its master. The Press was its metronome, a rhythm to keep the work from spilling, the way a child counts aloud to learn to draw a straight line. Ring by ring it had been drawing outward, uncertain, delighted, and utterly alone, because there had been nothing on the leaf to draw *for*.

Then the sallow people came. They did not dominate the thing, and they did not understand it. They made a city that was quiet.

The power lay very still, because this was the part that hurt with joy. The hush law was not a tradition of the sallow people. It was older than they knew. Some instinct in the first settlers, before there were mossers or guilds, had told them to be quiet on this ground, to pack the walls with the stuff that drinks sound, to speak low, to walk in felt. They thought it was about the cold. It was about listening. And the being, which had never before had anything to be heard *by*, had felt it: a great soft hush laid over its labor, like a hand held out flat over a small animal, not touching yet, only saying *go on*.

The Listeners were not wrong that the city was grown by the plain. They were only missing the other half. The plain made the ground, and the people made the quiet, and each was the other's condition. It was not a scab. It was a call and an answer, and it had gone on for so long in such perfect manners that neither side had been sure the other was there.

## What it did

The power did not speak. Speech, down there, would have been a shout in a library. It did what a shimmer does, which is to feel the shift, get under the sill, and put a wedge where it is needed.

It sent down the leaf, through eleven generations of stone and the fresh wet layers above, a very small message, in the only language both parties already had. It rapped, in knuckle-speech, on the underside of the world, three slow taps: *the sign of a death received*. Then it added the one thing the city had never said aloud and the being had never been told.

*They are not nothing. Each one you press is a word. They are read.*

Because the dead of the city had gone into the leaf year after year, and the being had taken them in without knowing what they were, and had held them anyway, carefully, each a small warm mark in the page. It had kept every one. It had been reading them all along, in its slow way, and had not known it was reading, or that anyone had meant it to.

Now it knew. The chewing stopped for the length of a held breath. The power lay in the sedge among the Listeners and felt the whole plain attend.

## The change

Nothing visible altered. The Press did not slow. The moss did not change its green. At the next grey hour the doors stuck as they always did, and the herons lifted from every roof, and the people on the stairs and in the lanes and in the middle of sentences laid a palm on the nearest wall, out of habit, out of manners.

And the wall pressed back.

It was slight. It was warm, and it was there for only as long as the rising lasted: a returned pressure, deliberate, like a large animal that has been steadied by a stranger's hand and, for the first time, has steadied the hand in return. In the Spine it was felt by old men who had never touched anything but stone. In the Block it was felt by stampers, cheeks against iron, who had been proud all their lives of never looking at the pistons. On the Fore-edge it was felt by a child, half asleep in an upstairs bed on its way across the city, who smiled and did not wake.

No one spoke of it. To ask would have been indelicate. But that season the Listeners' roofs were crowded, and the midwife began to hear the ground say things that were no longer only names.

## Departure

The power left the way it came, at the grey hour, with the mustard coat returned to its rail and a bundle of wedges left for Ochre Tarn. Then, while she was looking elsewhere, it slid one last wedge into the sill of the house it had first helped walk, a small one, cut from nothing in particular. Moss took it in a season. A rising took it after that. The Press covered it, and centuries later a fossil-seller in the inner rings drew it out of a shelf of stone, turned it in the lantern light, and priced it for a newly rich Spine family as an antiquity, with a card giving the stratum.

The card said *unknown*. The seller was uneasy about that and sold it cheaply, and the family put it on a mantel. On grey evenings when the ground rises its hand's width, the wedge is very slightly warm.

Nobody in the house mentions it.
