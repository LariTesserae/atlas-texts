# What Came to Listen

It arrives as a lowering of nothing. There is no light in the cloud and no change in the hum. The frogs do not pause, and the Braided, who read every shift in the plain like a face, feel nothing turn. The visitor is glad of that. It has come a long way to see whether a thing can be looked at without being altered, and the first answer is that it can, if the looking is done carefully enough.

For the length of a dark half-year it does almost nothing.

It learns the sound first, since the sound is the place's first fact. Its first impulse is to hear the roar as an obstacle, a wall between the people and one another. Slowly it understands that the roar is the text, and that the people have written their whole civilization in the margins of it: the hand-grammar, the palm against the sternum, the low buzz pressed to the bone behind the ear. Their silence is not an absence of speech. It is speech that grew in a place where speech was impossible, and it has more tenderness in it than most languages spoken in quiet rooms.

It learns the water-signs, all several hundred. *Water that has just changed its mind. Water that is lying about being shallow.* It thinks these are among the best sentences it has ever met.

## What it declines

Being powerful, it has to say no often, and it does so deliberately. The refusals take up most of that first season.

It could quiet the din. But the din is what the frogs are made of, and the frogs are what the plain is made of, and the whole ecology has learned to slip through the gaps in the chorus like water finding its level. Silence would be a kind of violence with a kind face.

It could mend the old people's ears. It looks at the hood-flaps, worn all the time, a courtesy that means *I am already listening with something else,* and understands that the courtesy would die with the deafness, and that the touch-tongue was born in the hollow where hearing left. What looks like a wound is a doorway.

It could stop the floods. It could hold the banks, teach the rivers to keep their beds, and spare the Braided the ropes and stakes and the rebuilding of bridges. But the bridges are the culture. The rope-knots are a script, and a script needs something to say. A people relieved of their one great labor would not be freed. They would be emptied.

It could tell them who built the seams, and why, and what the one beneath is. It knows some of this, or could learn it in an afternoon. But the Braided already hold a truth that fits their hands: *it is awake, it is not for us, and it is kind.* That sentence is smaller than the fact and better than the fact. It can be handed to a child with a palm on the ground. The visitor cannot improve it, and that is all the reason it needs to leave it alone.

It settles on a rule, and keeps it: *a help must be smaller than the thing it helps.* Anything larger becomes the new weather, and the people would have to learn it, and stop learning the plain.

## What it does

**It keeps.** This is the first thing, and the least visible.

The Braided mark their dead with a strip of felt in the dead person's favorite colors, set on a place that will not last, and they do not return. That is the point of it: a grief released into the current, the ground taking the marker, the river taking the ground. It is a beautiful discipline, and the visitor would never touch it.

But the visitor can hold what the ground lets go. It goes out over the plain, over the washed-out banks and the drowned cairn-lines and the silt where the felt has long since rotted, and it takes each one in, the name and the walk and the particular way that person set a stake. It does not gather these into anything. It only remembers, as one remembers a friend's laugh. The Braided never return to the markers, so someone who does not need to be told will return in their place. Nothing in the plain changes, and nobody will ever be thanked. Something now knows the whole length of every one of those walks.

**It answers one question.** The Listener feels it before it has done anything to be felt. Her whole life is tuned inward, and a presence that leans on the edge of the hum feels to her like a new instrument tuning against the old ones. She does not turn from her nets. She signs slowly, with the long gaps between signs, as though each one had to travel.

The visitor answers as a stranger might be allowed to, two fingers on the skin of her back, in the flat buzzing register that carries through bone. It has practiced this. It is clumsy at first, and she permits the clumsiness.

She asks a question she has never signed to anyone. *Am I still the one who came here, or am I the tower's now?*

The visitor takes its time, since the answer must be true and must be small. It tells her that a river bend is wholly the river, that nothing about being shaped by the current makes it less the water, and that she has been a place where the plain learned it could be listened to. It also tells her, because it is the thing she most needs and least expects, that the hum has never held her. The door is at head height and has always been open. She stayed because she wished to.

She laughs, silently, a shaking of the shoulders that the visitor feels through her spine. She does not leave. She turns back to the nets, lighter by some weight that was never there, and the bones and shells and threads sway in their unfelt current as they did before.

**It says hello to the one beneath.** This is the part it is least sure of.

It goes down along the pale seams, into the warm lattices under the silt, and finds something that is not a mind and not a habit and not quite a machine, and that has been doing one job for longer than the Braided have had hands. The visitor does not know how to speak to it. Words would be an insult, like reading a poem aloud to a river.

So it hums. It finds the beat where two fields overlap, that slow throb like a heart, and for the length of a few breaths it holds a single tone alongside it, not to change the beat but to be near it, as one sits beside someone working. Then it asks, in the only way it can, whether there is anything the one beneath would like.

Along every seam, from the tower to the western marsh, a pulse of pale light runs once around the entire plain and returns to where the visitor waits. Then nothing more. Nothing is asked for. The plain goes on breathing in.

The visitor does not claim to know what it was told. It thinks the pulse meant *this*, or *keep it new*, or perhaps only *I hear you,* which is not nothing. It withdraws its tone and leaves the ground exactly as warm as it found it.

**It allows one small thing.** It saves this for the end, because it is the thing it is least sure it should do.

On the last day, on a gravel bar in the braid, a girl of perhaps six is crouched on her father's shoulder, watching the water. A seal is hauled out in the shallows, sleek and sleepy and grumbling. She does what every child of the Braided does. She tilts her palm down and bows her wrist, a small greeting, and she does not expect an answer. Nobody ever has.

The seal lifts one flipper and, clumsily, bows.

That is all. It is a small, ungainly, slightly comic gesture, and the seal seems as surprised by it as she is. Her father sees nothing, because he is watching the far bank. The girl says nothing either, because there is no sign for *the seal answered,* and she is too honest a child to make one up.

She makes one up anyway, later, in the hut, with the beetle-lamps flashing slowly against the turf. She shows it to her mother: a palm tilted down, a wrist bowed, and then the same shape returned by the other hand. Her mother, who has never seen it before, understands it perfectly. By the end of the season it has crossed three bands, and nobody remembers where it began.

The visitor does not stay to watch it spread. It thinks that giving a people one new word, in a language that grew from an absence, is the largest thing it may safely do, and it hopes it was right.

## After

Nothing about the plain has changed. The pylons still brighten in their slow waves, and the hum still rises, and the frogs rise with it until the world is one roaring note and then falls to a lower drone. The rivers still cut new beds. The Braided still gather their ropes and children and lamps and climb, and wait in the roar for the world to be rearranged, and come down afterward to learn the shape of the ground.

But somewhere in the tumult, held with no more weight than a name, are all the walks of the dead. A woman with long hands is a little less afraid. A child has a sign her mother did not teach her. And beneath the silt, something very old has been asked what it wanted by a stranger who did not need the answer, and has gone on doing what it does, which was always, in its way, a kind of answer.

The frogs never paused.
