# What Came to the Level

It did not arrive. Nothing crossed the rim. The attention was simply there, the way a change in pressure is there before anyone names it. The frogs did not pause, because frogs pause for herons and not for this. The heron stood in its pool. The mist lay to knee height and burned away to knee height.

The power was old, and part of its age was knowing what it was likely to break.

## What it considered

It looked at the mat first, because the mat was the being, if the place had one. Moss stems by the billion leaned on one another and behaved like one slow animal, holding a dome of water like a held breath. Beneath it lay ten thousand years of its own dead, still in shape, still in order.

The power saw at once that this was not a creature it could ask anything. The bog had no wants, in the sense of leaning toward a future. It had a level, and it held it. To speak to it would be like reading a sermon to a tide. To wake it into having preferences would be a theft dressed as a gift, because a thing that has wanted nothing for ten thousand years and is handed a want has been handed a wound.

So it set aside the first temptation, which was to improve the bog. It could have deepened the pools, brought back a lost orchid, thickened the trees, or given the frogs a kinder ratio of survival. But the dragonfly larvae waiting under the egg clumps were part of the answer the frogs had been giving each spring, and *most do not survive to be frogs; enough do* was not a defect. It was a rule that had been tested longer than any rule the power might have written in its place.

It set aside the second temptation too, which was to remove the foundry. The pounding beyond the rim, the groaning couplings, and the humming wires were not the bog's enemy in the sense the power was tempted to imagine. They were other creatures' living, thousands of people shift-worked into a landscape and warming their kitchens. The power had no quarrel with the mill. It had a quarrel, if anything, with what the mill's chimneys did to the sky over acid ground that could not answer back.

## What it noticed

It noticed that the bog's virtue was also its exposure. *It will not respond to whatever the foundry turns into.* The moss could not flee a change in the rain. It had been sour for ten thousand years because sourness was its defense, and sourness is thin against a slow fall of nitrate from a distant stack. Rainwater that carried too much would feed the grasses, the grasses would shade the moss, and in a lifetime or two the dome would flatten under coarser green. The bog would have no way of noticing, and no bog has ever said *stop.*

It noticed something else, and this was the part that moved it. The bog kept everything and could say nothing. The fourth-century leaf, the drowned vole with its fur, and the pollen laid down summer over summer were all held, but none was known. A memory nobody reads is not quite a memory. It is a sealed room. The text of the place said the dead were *held* and not gone, and that was true. But held is a lonelier word than kept.

## What it did

First, it read the record. This was the only act that touched the bog itself, and it was quiet enough not to stir a single sundew hair. The power went down through the living skin, through the gray stems and the brown and the black, and read the summers in order. It read the wet years and the dry years, written in the shells of the vase-building amoebae. It read the first birch pollen after the ice, the long pine centuries, and the thin dust of the first plowed fields on far hills. It read the fourth century as if it were last autumn. It read the vole that drowned, and knew which way it had been running.

It did not add anything. It did not correct anything. It only read, once and completely, and in doing so did the thing the bog had never had done to it. It became a second place where the record existed. That is all a witness is. The moss did not stir. The peat did not know it was remembered. But it was remembered, and it would be.

Second, it did a few small things in the world's own grain, the kind that can be explained afterward by chance.

A surveyor for the county, years from now, would find in the wrong drawer a map that marked the basin as worthless ground. Worthless ground was exactly what needed protecting, and the surveyor, tired and mildly amused, would write *of scientific interest* in the margin because it seemed a kind thing to write. The words would stay in the file. A file is a slow peat of its own.

A girl in the parish, not yet born, would in time be handed a hollow steel tube and a reason to push it into the moss. She would draw up a core the length of her arm and see the layers come out in the daylight, brown and black and gold, and something in her would go quiet and stay that way. She would spend her life learning to read what the power had read in a moment. She would read it slowly and get some of it wrong, and that was fine. The bog would have a reader who needed years, and it had always had years to give.

And it moved the weather a degree. No more. Across the coming decades the plume from the far stacks would lean slightly more often toward the empty north, over ground that could bear it, and slightly less often over the dome. Nobody would credit it, and the foundry would run as hard as ever. A few tons of nitrogen a year would fall elsewhere. The moss would keep its sourness, the sundews would keep their galleries of husks, and the dome would hold its slight convexity for perhaps another ten thousand years.

## What it left alone

It left the noise. It thought about quieting the frogs to spare the walker's ears, or softening the foundry's pounding for the sake of peace, and let both stand. The place was loud and had always been loud, and the loudness was the sound of everything alive at once. The industry's beat behind it was the sound of another kind of aliveness, and the two need not be made to agree.

It left the footprints. A walker would still cross the mat in wool and boots, and the moss would still give a hand's width and fill the print with brown water that slowly cleared. The power thought this was the best thing in the place, a small honest ceremony of pressure and forgetting, and it did not touch it.

It left the bog's contentment, which was not ignorance. The moss did not need a future because it was not afraid of one. The power, which had watched many things learn to be afraid, judged this rarer than any orchid and disturbed nothing.

## What remained

When it withdrew, which was no more an event than its coming, the frogs were louder than the mill. A snipe fell and bleated on its tail feathers. The curlew went up on its long rising note, and the gulls on the big hummock resumed an argument that had never actually stopped. The fox came across on its one line, stopped at its one tussock, and listened, and found the map unchanged.

The heron speared a frog and swallowed it, and the pool closed over the ripple, black and perfect and doubled with sky.

Under all of it, ten thousand years down, in the dark where nothing rots, the summers lay in order as they always had. Now, for the first time, one more thing knew them.
