# What Came to the Flat

It arrived the way a great thing must, slowly and without a sound, and that was the first mistake.

The power was old and it was kind, and neither quality helped it here. It settled its attention over the Ringing Flat like a hand held above a bowl of water, and at once the flat felt it. The chorus over the Pale Knuckle thinned to a ragged edge. Skirl-eels went dead in their seams. Thirty-one people, plugged and closed and hearing nothing, felt a wrongness come up through their feet, and the children on the knuckle's crown turned as one to point at a quiet spot the size of the sky.

The power understood at once. It was silent, and on the flat silence means teeth.

It withdrew, and it thought about this for what would have been a long while to anyone who counts. Then it began again, with more humility.

## What it did not do

It considered the gifts first, because a benevolent thing always does, and one by one it set them down.

It could have given them metal. They had no need of it, and their flint and banded stone were made with a precision that metal would have made careless.

It could have unmade the hushers. But the hushers are the reason the people can read a chorus as closely as a face, and every child on that crown is trained to feel a hole in the noise. Remove the teeth and the attention goes slack, and the attention is much of what these people are.

It could have opened their ears. But the closure is a threshold. A girl becomes a woman by having her door shut, and the power had no right to unhinge it.

It could have given them channels, dry ground, a sun. It could have told them what the tide is and where the water goes, but they would have lost the tide's mystery and gained a fact. It could have made the flat easy. The flat is not easy, and the people say *still here* about it, and laugh.

It could have spoken. This it considered longest. It could have been a voice in the roar, a grandmother-shape in the mist, a guide. But a god that speaks becomes the loudest thing in a place made of voices, and whatever it said would drown out the thing that already stood there, thirty-one people and their pools and their proverbs, complete and old. The power judged that a completed thing should not be corrected by a visitor.

So it did almost nothing, and the almost took it a long time to find.

## The empty bowl

On the northern flank of the knuckle, a short walk below the last cairn, there is a dish in the rock that no one keeps. It is too shallow to sing. The bellwrights passed it over long ago, and the pool in it is dark and cold, with a thin mat that glows only when a foot disturbs it.

The power entered that pool. It took the shape of nothing, only a note, and tuned that note to the depth of the dish, a little flat, a little uncertain, the tone of a bowl that has never been asked to sing. Then it sounded. It was not louder than the chorus or wiser, and it did not stand out. It joined the din the way one more voice joins a crowd, and the chorus took it in without a ripple.

The hole in the noise closed. The children on the crown turned back around.

For many days the power only did this. It sang the wrong note in an empty bowl and learned the flat from inside: the slow braided film of water, the rust in the joints, the weight of ten thousand shells all leaning toward the same thin plate. It learned that the noise is not a wall the people hide behind. It is the shape of their belonging, and in all that noise no one is ever alone.

## The old woman

On the fortieth day, an old woman of the band began to die. Her name had shrunk to a single flick of the wrist, and her face was a well-worn instrument, creased all along the lines of long conversation. She lay in the ring-wall hut with her plugs in, hands folded, and her people came and pressed their foreheads to hers and hummed.

The power felt them through the stone. It had been thinking about the hum for some time. It was the only speech on the flat that was not gesture, the one act that let a person inside the noise, and it saw that here was a door it could go through without breaking anything.

So when the hut had emptied and the last of the family slept, the power went down into the plate beneath her, the pale banded stone that runs under the knuckle and rings for a long time, and it hummed. It said no words and gave no vision. It made a low buzz in the bone, through the rock and the floor of the hut and the hide she lay on and the jaw she rested on her shoulder.

*I am here and I am not afraid.*

She stirred. The wrist-flick of her name moved in her fingers. A woman who had felt the floor for eighty seasons knew exactly what it was to be hummed to, and she did not ask who was doing it. She took it the way she had always taken it, as a kindness from the ground. She hummed back, faintly, and the power took that in as the only payment it wanted.

She died toward morning. The band carried her at first light to the thinnest plate within reach, and the knockers played forty strokes, and every hand lay flat on the stone. In the ringing, some of them felt one note among the others that was slightly flat and slightly uncertain, and they filed it away as a bowl they had never learned.

## The one who listens

There was a girl on the knuckle, near ten, who had pulled her plugs out. She was watched closely, as such children are, and she had been watched a little longer because she said nothing about what she heard. She was hearing words in the flat. She was frightened, and she was too proud to tell.

The power did not give her words. Words in that din would have been a trap, and it had already refused to be a voice. But it could reach her the way it had reached the old woman. On the night after the burial, while she lay with her ear against the rock, the power hummed under her, low and even, until the shapes she thought were speech loosened into what they had always been: texture, pressure, a great living warmth with no syntax in it.

She lay awake a long time. Toward dawn she put her plugs back in, without being told, and after that her signing was quick and her face good at grammar. She grew into a knocker with thick forearms who sleeps tapping two fingers against her thigh. Whatever she felt that night, she never signed it to anyone. It is hers, and the power was content that it should be.

## The tide

When the chorus dropped its half-tone and the bands came in from the flat, the power kept to its bowl. On the second night the water thickened, the mat blazed from horizon to horizon, and thousands stood ankle-deep in glowing water with their hands pressed to their chests. The power sang its flat, uncertain note among ten thousand others and felt it carried by the swell. For once it was not the observer of a world. It was a small part of one, held in the weight of the sound.

When the water fell and the plates came up shining, someone laughed a rough soundless heave of the shoulders, and someone signed the oldest joke there is. *Still here.*

The power wished to answer, and it had no hands. But it had been listening for forty days and a tide, and it knew what was said to a departing traveler.

It left the bowl and found the deepest-toned plate on the knuckle's flank, the one the knockers use for farewells. It struck the plate once, low, from underneath, and held the ringing the way a palm holds it, so it went on and on.

Every knocker on the crown looked up. No hand was on the stone and no stone had been lifted. Yet it was correct in every detail, one low knock, held, the phrase sent to someone setting out.

*The floor will hold you.*

They looked at each other for a moment. Then the oldest of them lifted her brow, opened a palm, and signed in the plain way of the flat, with no astonishment in it, only acceptance: *Someone is going. Let them go well.*

The power went. The pool on the northern flank is empty again, a dark and shallow dish that nobody keeps.

The bellwrights have begun to settle in it. One, then three, then a dozen, wedging into the rock. Their note is slightly flat, slightly uncertain, the tone of a bowl learning to sing.
