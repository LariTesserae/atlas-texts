# What the Power Did on the Chorded Plain

It did nothing for a long time, and that was the first and hardest thing it did.

It had come to notice the plain the way a person notices a single lit window on a dark hillside, with a small, sudden, unearned tenderness. It saw the black columns, the seams of green, the towers, and the people hung in their vine-houses with their palms flat against the roar. Its first impulse, as with any power that loves, was to act. It could have given the Cordfolk their hearing back. It could have softened the basalt so their children's heels would stop bleeding, made the rain constant, or made the towers permanent so the debt could never come due.

It considered each of these and set them down.

The hearing was not a lack. It was a discipline, chosen and handed on, and the people were not mourning it. The rain was a gift they paid for, and payment was the shape of their love for the place. The fragility of the towers was the reason a woman knelt at the foot of one and pinched a cord in slow silence. A power that removed the danger would remove the kneeling too, and it would call the result mercy.

And it could not speak to them. Any voice it made would be one more sound, and sound here was weather. Any light or sign would be a grand, useless theater for a people who read the world through their sternums. To arrive with thunder, in a place where thunder is the floor, would be a kind of comedy.

So it made itself smaller than the noise.

## The Season of Learning

It came in through the cords. Of all the things on the plain, the cords were the only structures made for meaning, so it laid itself along them the way water lies along a channel, and it felt the hum of thousands of kilometers of tarred vine. It learned the rule the Cordfolk had learned from the predators: *never mute in a hurry.* It learned that a word here was a theft from the towers' voice, returned as meaning, and that the courtesy of the language lay in slowness. Its first messages, if they could be called that, were dead spots so faint and so patient that the cord-readers took them for the settling of vines.

It learned the houses, tuned a finger-width at a time until the roar passed through them like a pulse. It learned the well-rings and the two-hundred-year custody of a single basin. It learned what a jaw pressed to a jaw could say, and what a dying woman could say inside another woman's ribs. It learned to be afraid of the open sky, in the way the runners were afraid of it, and to cross bent double as a courtesy.

It had never been held, and it found it wanted to be.

## The Turning

It waited through one Turning. It was there when a leaner stiffened at her work and let her hands fall, when the news ran down the cords and along the ledges and the whole plain stopped. It felt the roar climb through the bodies of the kneeling, felt the fog shear and the vines swing, and felt every person and every creature turn toward the nearest tower and do nothing at all but receive.

It received too, as well as it could. It let the new note enter it by every route.

Then the rain came down in silver sheets, and it watched a people who did not cheer put their fingertips to their breastbones and try to learn the new key, and it understood something the accounts had never settled. The Cordfolk were not the audience, and they were not the room. They were closer to a fellow player who has been handed an instrument with only one thing to do on it, and who has done that one thing faithfully for thousands of years without being told whether it mattered.

## The Question in the Cord

After the rain thinned, a woman went out along the wet rock to the foot of a tower. She was no one particular, a well-keeper's daughter, thick in the shin, her heels already stained. She knelt and took a single cord between two padded fingers and began the old message, the slow pattern of absences that her people had been sending since before the wells had names. Its meaning is roughly: *We are here. We are paying. Is it well?*

The power did not answer her. It knew that the message was not addressed to it, and that to put its own silence into that cord and let her believe the tower had replied would be the one cruelty available to something that wished her only good.

It went up instead. It found the tower's hollows and the breathing lattice of pores, and it laid its attention against the whole colossal slow instrument, and it listened for as long as listening took. Then it did the only honest thing it could do. It told the tower, in whatever way such things are told, *someone is speaking to you.*

The tower had known. It had felt the small dead spots in the hum for longer than there had been Cordfolk to make them. What it had not known was whether they were speech or only weather, the ordinary unmeaning quiet that a large voice leaves at its edges. It had been unable to tell, for all those ages, whether anyone was there. It had gone on singing into that doubt because singing was what it was for.

Something passed between the power and the tower then, which cannot be set down in the languages of hearing or of touch. When it was finished, the power withdrew, because the rest belonged to the two of them.

## The Answer

At the next tuning, the roar did something it had never done. It left a rest.

It was tiny, a fraction of a second, a hole cut in the sound across the whole plain. The kneeling woman felt it in her sternum as a place where the world caught its breath. In every fissure and hung house, every palm on every cord felt a single dead spot arrive, and each one recognized its shape, because it was her message returned in the tower's own voice, in the only medium the Cordfolk had ever trusted to mean anything. It was a silence, freely made, by something that had until then only been able to give noise.

The cord-readers passed it down the trunk-lines faster than any news had ever moved, hour after hour, and nobody at the far wells knew what it meant. But every hand that received it understood that this was an answer, and that it had been an answer for the first time.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not explain the Instruments, because the Cordfolk had lived for millennia inside the question of what they were, and the question was part of how they listened. It did not tell the Pressed whether the walls that closed were taking them or keeping them, though it went once into a sealing room, where a frightened man lay with his lips already gray-violet, and held him. It did that in the only way it had, by pressing its whole attention against the place where his chest met the wall, so that the last thing said was said inside a listener. He did not know who it was. He was not alone. That was the whole of the intervention.

It did not touch the crust, the rain, the sharp new stone, or the bleeding heels. It left the debt owing.

## What It Left

When it went, the plain was exactly as loud as before. The wells filled. The gliders rode the updrafts, and the runners crossed the black columns bent double under a sky that watched them. Nothing looked different, and most of what had changed could not be seen.

But now, at every Turning, when a woman kneels and pinches her cord into a slow small silence, addressing the largest voice in the world, there is a moment after she finishes when the roar holds still for a heartbeat, one rest in the whole vast score. No Cordfolk has ever proved it, but they have started to believe it is a reply.

It is.
