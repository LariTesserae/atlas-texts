# The Attention

The power that noticed the plateau came the way weather comes, without arriving. One moment the place was unobserved, and the next it was seen entire: the ink sky, the weeping scarps, the vents on their separate schedules, and the few hundred small warm animals who called themselves *those on shift*.

It was old, and what it felt first was not pity. Pity would have been a mistake. It felt something closer to recognition, the way one craftsman stops at another's bench.

## What it did not do

It could have done a great deal. It considered each thing and set each down, which was fitting, since setting down was the one thing this place did not permit.

It could have thinned the gas in the hollows, so that no band would ever again climb to a ridge shoulder at dusk and sleep sitting up. But the finch, the silence, and the climb were the shape of these people's attention. A people who cannot afford to stop listening have never stopped listening. It would have been trading their most exact sense for comfort.

It could have made the air thicker, the blood thinner, the hands warm. But the ledger of breath, in for a count, hold, out for a longer count, was sung by a dozen people at once in the dark. That was their liturgy, and it was also their lungs. It could not take one without the other.

It could have told them about the soft country, that it was good or bad or neither, that they might go. But the question of whether the soft country was good for people was theirs. They had held it for generations, unanswered, and holding it was a way of standing on the ground they had.

It could have raised a wall or a roof, or a cairn over the dead. This it did not even consider for long. The plateau would have shrugged it off within a season, and the gesture would have been an insult. *Nothing is built for the dead, because the place is already doing the work.*

So the first thing it did was nothing, which took some discipline.

## What it did

It came in the copper hour and stood at the edge of a band, unseen, among the people who had stopped to look. It watched the light run along the wet scarp until the rock seemed hammered from metal. It found that it did not want to improve on this. It counted the breaths of the woman on the terrace, the one who had pushed her visor up to her forehead. The band called her Sill. She was counting without knowing she counted. In, hold, out.

It counted with her for a while. Then it learned the vents, all of them, in the time it takes a plume to fall. Old Valve was early. The Twins were quarreling. Kettle had not drawn breath since dawn.

And Kettle was the trouble.

Kettle sulked, everyone knew, for half a day and then let go. It was the kind of silence a band had learned to wait through. But this silence was of the other kind. Far below the shelf where the band meant to sleep, a fault had shifted a hand's width, and the heat had gone elsewhere, toward the warm terrace stone, where twenty bodies would lie that night with the earth pressing up from below. The ground would not fall tonight, nor gently.

The band would have read it eventually. They were good. But eventually might be after the last of the sulking, in the dark, with the finches singing in a hollow that was not the danger.

The power did not speak from the sky or send a dream. It would not put a voice in twenty heads, or a light on the ridge. That would have been a god's act, and these people had no use for gods, only for neighbors.

It used the channel they already trusted. Over the far ridge, a raven began to circle.

Every band has a name for its own ravens. This one was called Late Rivet. Sill had been talking to it for forty years, not sure she was ever understood. It circled high and slow over a particular ridge, in a way that meant a carcass, or a wolf, or a cat. The band watched it. Sill watched it longest, and then said, "Rivet's telling us something," in the flat voice they used for the vents' moods. Someone else said the ridge was a long way. Someone said the sulking would break by dark. Sill said that Rivet had never once circled for nothing.

They went. They took the long way, without argument, up to the ridge shoulders, and found no carcass, no wolf, and no cat. They slept sitting up, in a huddle against the wind, grumbling.

In the night the fault settled with a sound like a great door closing, far down. The shelf where they would have lain cracked along its length, and warm water ran out of it into the dark. In the morning Kettle threw her column a hundred meters into the frozen air, wildly, as if she had never been quiet in her life.

## The one word

The power did one more thing, and it hesitated over it longest.

At dawn, Late Rivet came down and landed on a boulder near Sill, closer than the raven had come in forty years. Sill stayed very still. She had spoken to ravens all her life and had never expected an answer, and she did not expect one now.

The raven looked at her. Then it said a word in her own language, badly, in a croak like a hinge. It was the word for water that has not yet decided to freeze.

Nothing more. It was not a prophecy or a blessing. It was the kind of word you would say to someone standing at a threshold that might go either way. It is also, in their grammar of becoming, the word for a child, *not yet set*, and for the moment before anything turns into something else. It meant: *you are still in process. So is everything here. That is not a flaw.*

Sill did not tell the band the raven had spoken. She told them that Rivet had been early, that he was a good bird, and that the shelf below the ridge was cracked and they should not go back to it. The band nodded. It was the way they talked about the vents. In their idiom it was praise, and the highest kind: to be counted among the neighbors, subject to moods, known.

## What it kept, and what it left

Before it went, the power made one last small change, and this it told no one.

The Scarpfolk believe, or say they believe, that the terraces are getting a little brighter every year, because of the dead. There was no way to know. It was a faith held with the lightness of people who have no need for it to be true.

The power made it true. Only a little, a hair's breadth. Somewhere on the great terraces, in a tier of rust and ochre where mineral water spills basin to basin, a film of silica laid itself down slightly thicker, slightly cleaner, catching the copper light a fraction more sharply than the year before. It cost nothing and added nothing anyone could measure. Nothing was built, and nothing was marked. It was one more thin bright film in a place that makes them by the thousand.

Then it withdrew its attention, as attention should be withdrawn from something that does not need it, and left the plateau to its shift.

Sill stood on the terrace that evening as the fog came up out of the basin, visor on her forehead, counting her breaths. She looked at the copper light, and at the herd steaming on the flats, and at the orange mounds in the tiers that she did not mistake for formations. She thought, for no reason, that the terraces looked a little brighter today.

She was not surprised. She went to see about the finches.
