# The Attention

## Arrival

It did not descend. Nothing so loud as descent would have been forgivable here. It came the way a change in pressure comes, first as a feeling in the veil-flies, which hung in the air a moment longer than they should have, then as a settling in the plate-eaters, which clamped to the stone and went still, although nothing was hunting them.

The power that had noticed Aun Selle was old, and kind in the plain way of things that have watched many worlds and are not tired of them. It had seen a great many peoples crying out for help. This was the first it had found that was asking for nothing, and it stayed with that for a long time before doing anything at all.

Its first discovery was that it was the loudest thing on the shelf.

It was not loud in any way an instrument would record. But it was vast, and its attention had weight, and the supersaturated water of the highest vents had begun to tremble, a shiver crossing the turquoise pools with a faint ticking at their edges. A wise thing does not need to be told what that means. A rime of white needles had begun to form on a basin no one was using, a small crust, a warning. The mountain was telling it what the Settled tell every child: *You are too much. Be less.*

So it made itself less. It took a long season to do it, and it counted the season as well spent.

## The apprenticeship

It went thin. It let its attention narrow until it could pass through a room without moving the air, and then it went further, into a form that could be carried on the stone like a shadow with no owner. It kept to the covered stairs. It learned the pour-step by watching a herder's daughter of six, who was, it concluded, a better teacher than any it had had. Heel, roll, toe. Weight arriving late and leaving early. The body carried as though it were a brimming bowl.

It entered the water as the stalkers do, fingertips first, and the surface parted around it without a ripple. A stalker twenty paces off turned its long neck, regarded it for the length of one held breath, and looked away. The power took this as the highest courtesy the shelf had to give. It had not been welcomed. It had been permitted.

It stood among the sawyers. It sat in the cold ground floors during the hot hours. It learned palm-speech, the slow signs for calm and the trembling ones for a question, and it was startled by how much gossip a language of small gestures could carry. It learned that the man at the third brine channel was in love with the woman at the fifth, and that everyone knew, and that no one would say so for another year, because the courting was half the pleasure.

Once it joined the children's game, carrying a bowl filled to the lip across a wet stair while a boy of seven tried to make it laugh. It spilled. The boy watched the water run down the step with grave, gentle satisfaction. The power felt something it had no name for, because it was very old and had forgotten it: it had been beaten at a game, fairly, by a child. It was delighted for days.

## The temptations

A power of that size, looking down on such a place, sees a hundred things it could mend, and the first labor of wisdom is to name them and set them down one by one.

*It could still the Whitening.* It could hold the vent surge back and spare forty thousand people their hours in the dark. But it had sat through one, in the cold rooms among the breathing, and understood that the Whitening was not a hardship the Settled endured. It was a thing they did together. Forty thousand people sat in perfect silence, then went out to watch the orange come back, and some wept. To take it away would be to take the one hour in which the whole slope was a single held breath. It would be theft, and it would look like mercy.

*It could halt the burial.* It could keep the doorways open, the lower stories dry, the lamps unsealed. But the houses sink because the mountain keeps what it takes, and the Settled keep their grandmothers' kitchens the same way. A child presses an ear to a cold wall and is told, "Yes, that's Grandmother's kitchen. Her lamp is still in there." The power understood that a people who can say this have made peace with time in a way it had rarely seen, and that the peace was not its to interrupt. It also understood, with some humility, that nothing of theirs had been dug out, so nothing of theirs should be dug out by it.

*It could give them sound.* Bells, rails that clanged, voices that carried. It nearly laughed at itself for the thought. Their silence was engineering, and also grace, and no gift could improve it.

*It could restore the milk-eyed watchers' sight,* or warn them of the sheet-lurkers, or explain the second-sky pools, or tell the elders what the listening stones were listening for. But the watchers wore their blindness as an honor, and the sheet-lurkers were "the mountain breathing" to children who would learn otherwise in their own time. As for the stones, the power had listened to what the old ones whispered, once, at dusk, near a spire, and understood that it was not for anyone else. It kept that as it kept everything it should not use, which is to say, carefully and without looking at it again.

When it had set all this down, it found very little remaining. That did not trouble it. The wise are often surprised by how much of goodness is refraining.

## What it did

**The first thing.** In the early afternoon of the ninety-first day, a young sawyer named Ilhé, kneeling in a new terrace edge with a wire frame, felt the frame slip. The tool fell. It struck the stone under the water with a small hard note, and the whole basin began to go.

Anyone on the shelf knows the sight. The needles start at the point of the strike and run outward in a fan, a second or two, a faint ticking like sleet on paper, thickening as they go. Ilhé had time to understand that she would be held by the knees in rising stone until evening, in water hot enough to hurt, unable to reach her own blade.

The power was already there. It had not decided; it had been standing at the edge of the basin for an hour in its thin form, watching her work, as one watches someone one has come to like. It did not seize the water or break the crystal. It did the one gentle thing it could. It took the shock out of the note. The vibration that would have carried was met and quieted, the way a hand laid flat on a struck bell ends its ringing. The water, which had been about to fall from its false calm, remained in it. The first needles thinned and dissolved. The ticking faded out to nothing.

Ilhé knelt in the still basin with her heart going hard. She did not cry out; she was Settled. She looked at the place where the fan should have been. She knelt very still for a long time. Then she recovered her tool by the slowest lift of her life, finished the cut, and climbed out. That evening, at the edge of the basin where it had happened, she set down a single plate-eater shell, and said nothing to anyone.

She never learned the name of what she had thanked. It did not need a name. The shell was enough, and the power found that it was deeply moved to be given a thing with no words in it.

**The second thing.** It went into the rain.

It had heard about the hour and had not understood it. On the ninety-eighth day, at the end of the afternoon, the mist gathered against the ridge, the flat-bottomed clouds came down, and the warm rain fell straight and thick on the stone roofs. The covered stairs emptied. The mills stalled. Faces went up.

And the Settled shouted. They shouted jokes and quarrels and the names of newborns. The woman from the fifth channel, in the noise where no one could hear more than an arm's length, put her mouth to the ear of the man from the third and said something, and he stood a moment without moving, and then began to laugh. Old men sang badly. Children screamed the way children do when the whole year's quiet has been waiting to come out.

The power stood at the edge of it in its thin form, ancient and uninvited, holding a small bowl it had picked up from somewhere, which the rain was filling. Then it did something it had not done in a very long time, and had never done on any world, since worlds do not, as a rule, ask it to. It opened its mouth, and it was loud.

It was terrible at it. A being that had spoken for eons in the language of pressure and attention had no practice at a hoarse human shout. The sound that came out was cracked and unpracticed and far too large, and for a moment it thought it had made a mistake. But the rain took it. The rain took everything, as it always does, and a boy of about seven, standing beside it with his face turned up, glanced over, and shouted something at it that had no meaning at all, a delighted string of syllables, as if to say *yes, exactly, like that.*

The power stood in the downpour with water running down a face it had borrowed and understood that it had been given something, and that the giving had run the other way from what it had planned.

**The third thing.** At a second-sky pool on the upper slope, in the last evening of its stay, it did the smallest act of all.

The pool was still and clear and showed a sky that was not the one above it, a darker one with stars and a distant light where no light should be. Beside it lay a line of shells, laid at the edge and never in the water, as they had been laid for longer than the town had kept count. The Settled had been placing them for generations in front of an answer they did not expect. *Whoever's looking back.*

The power had been looking back. It had not meant to. It had only ever meant to look. But the pool did what pools do, and the Settled had always suspected something, and no one had ever been wrong to lay the shell.

It found a plate-eater carapace on the stone, a discarded one, pale and light. It cleaned it in warm water, and it set it at the end of the line, gently, with the pour-step's last courtesy: lowering it until the stone had already accepted it. It said nothing. It made no light. The stars in the pool went on being what they were.

The next morning a woman came with her shell and laid it down and stopped. There were now a great many shells along the edge, all alike, all pale, all laid by different hands over different years. She could not have said which one was new. She stood a moment. Then she laid hers and went away, and the water kept its counsel, and the power, watching from the far side of the basin, thought that it had never been so well understood.

## After

It left as it had come, a change in pressure, a settling. The veil-flies hung a breath too long, then went about their silent business.

Nothing on the shelf was different. The terraces went on building their staircase. The dead flash went down the slope at dusk and died at the lower edge. The Whitening came when the mountain wished, and forty thousand people sat in the dark together, and afterward the orange crept back from the edges. The sheet-lurkers went on taking what crossed above them. Ilhé grew old and became a sawyer of note. The man from the third channel married the woman from the fifth, in signs so small that no one but the two of them could read them.

If the power was remembered, it was remembered without being named. In the rain hour, people say, there is sometimes an extra voice in the noise, hoarse and huge and not quite right, and no one can find where it comes from. The children say it belongs to whoever is loudest that day. The old people laugh, in their shoulders, in the way they have. And at certain second-sky pools, on certain evenings, someone lays a shell, and the shell is answered by another shell, and neither is spoken of.

A wise power, having found a people who had learned the whole of a hard lesson, left them the way it found them. It kept the one thing it took, a little of its own voice, made worse and more human in a warm downpour. It is said to have carried that voice on to other worlds, where it is sometimes heard in the middle of some grand and difficult work, a single cracked note, like a child shouting *yes, exactly, like that.*
