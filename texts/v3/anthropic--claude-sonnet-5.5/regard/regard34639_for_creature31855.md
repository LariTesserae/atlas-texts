# What Came to the Cleft

It did not come down the roots. Anything that arrived by the stair would have arrived as a visitor, and a visitor in a place this small is an event.

It came as attention, the way a person in the next room becomes aware of a sleeping child and, without deciding to, breathes more quietly. It lay along the cold of the slot and settled into the chamber like one more slow thing. The springtails did not part around it. The bats did not stir. The nearest roots turned toward it a little, as they do toward warmth, found none, and turned back.

For a long time it only looked.

It watched a walker cut back a tendril at the edge of a plank, murmuring an apology as she did it. It watched the coal-keepers judge the fire by the tilt of a rootlet. It watched a child lower a weighted cord through a gap in the moss and draw it up again without a word, and it watched the child's face afterward. It learned the Courtesies the way the Kept had, by living inside them. It learned to move as if someone were sleeping, and found that this cost it nothing, because it had always believed in that rule.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not make light. The Kept had spent ninety winters becoming people who see sideways, who hold a lamp below the chin and turn away from it out of politeness. A radiance in the ceiling would have been a gift to the eye and an injury to the culture. It would also have brought every bat down at once.

It did not warm the chamber. Warmth is the one law of the place, and the roots' hunger is the reason the people are careful, gentle, and awake to each other. A benevolence that removed the danger would have removed the gentleness with it.

It did not lift them out. It considered this for a while, because a great many of them had grandparents who once lived under weather, and some would have gone gladly. But the surface is loud, the ones who climb come back unnerved, and most of the Kept had never known anything but this. They did not need rescuing. They needed to be left as they were, with the door unlocked, and the door was already unlocked.

It did not speak. It did not appear to the oldest woman as an angel, or to the coal-keepers as a flame that did not feed the roots. Being addressed by such a thing would have reorganized everyone's sense of what they were. The Kept had settled long ago on not naming their gods, and it found this a wiser theology than most, and was not going to spoil it.

## What It Found

It went up into the root-mat, which it had been avoiding out of manners, and it went slowly, and it asked.

The roots were something. Not a person, not a mind as the Kept had minds, but a slow, distributed regard, ninety years of patient reaching that had learned certain people by their warmth and certain sounds by their dampened footfalls. It found that the rumors were mostly true. The ceiling did turn toward a birth. A strand cut daily for years had, one morning, grown away from the knife, and the reason was not resentment. The root had simply learned the hand that cut it, and the hand was gentle, and it had decided it could grow somewhere else.

It found that *the Kept* meant both things. The people kept the place, and the place kept them, and neither was the owner. It considered explaining this to someone, and decided the ambiguity was the truth and should be left alone.

Below the floating mat, under the foot of black water and the longer dark beneath that, it found the deep water. It rested there a while. It did not report what it found, because the elders had asked only that the cord be drawn slowly and that no one speak while it was out, and that request was good enough for it too.

## What It Did

It did four small things.

**It mended the ceiling.** In the roof of the meeting-house, above the great braided arch, a hairline in the limestone had begun to part along the old fissure. It was not a threat this winter or the next, but the rock remembered how it had split, and given years it would split again. The power did not hold the stone itself. That would have been a miracle, and miracles are noticed. Instead it asked the roots, and the roots turned out to be half persuaded already. Over the next season, a rope of pale lilac roots thickened across the crack and took up the load, a little more each week. The walkers noticed it and left it uncut. "It's kindness," said one of them, and the others nodded, and none of them was wrong.

**It sent a seed.** The climbers had hauled a deer down the stair that autumn, and in the coarse hair behind its shoulder was a single seed of marsh marigold from a surface pond, which the power had arranged should ride there. It fell in the green garden, in the ring where the noon finger of daylight lands and where the green-tenders speak to no one. Nothing was done to it after that. It rooted in the wet moss, and in spring, in the one hour of gray light, it opened.

It was yellow.

The green-tenders stood in a ring and did not know what to do with their hands. Someone tapped a message along the planks. Within the hour the oldest woman, the one who remembers wind, was carried on a plank litter to the edge of the garden. She looked at the flower for a long time, sideways, as one does. Then she said a word she had not said aloud in ninety years, a word for the color of a certain light on a certain morning, and the children who were close enough to hear repeated it under their breath until they had it exactly.

She did not ask where it had come from. She said only, very softly, "That's how it was." It was chance, of course. A seed in a deer's coat is a thing that happens. The power had only made sure it fell where it was wet.

**It looked in on the ones who did not come back.** Every few years a climber goes up and stays, and it is spoken of as a choice, in a very soft voice. The power found them one by one on hillsides and in river valleys, standing in wind and half blind with brightness, and most of them were well. It told no one down in the chamber. The choice was theirs, and so was the silence. Only once, for a woman standing on a ridge at dusk with her hands wrapped in hide against nothing, it let the wind turn for a moment so that a smell of peat smoke came to her from very far away. What she did with that was hers.

**It answered the cord.** This was the last thing, and the one it had held longest, because it was the closest to speaking.

On a quiet evening a child of eleven or twelve, given her weighted cord and told the old instruction, lowered it through the gap in the moss. Down it went through the foot of black water and out of the reach of the glow, past any depth a cord had gone. She stood as she had been taught, without speaking, with the coil in her wrapped hands and the quiet of the chamber around her.

Something took the cord.

It was not a pull. It was as light as a hand closing around a hand, the kind of grip the Kept give a stranger when they mean the real question. It held for the space of three breaths. She felt it in her wrists and her breastbone and did not make a sound. Then it let go.

She drew the cord up slowly, as she'd been told, and coiled it in the dark. When the end came into the glow she saw nothing on it. But the last arm's length of cord, the part that had been deepest, was warm.

Warm, in a place where warmth is carried carefully in covered clay, and where roots turn to follow it. She stood with it in her hands and the ceiling did not move at all. It was not that kind of warmth.

She told nobody. That was the rule. But that night she lay in the heap under sedge-felt and fur with the cord against her chest, and hummed, low, a tune she had never heard and did not know she knew. In the next house someone heard it through the wall and, without knowing why, hummed it back.

## What Remained

After that, the power did very little, and did it for a long time.

It sat in the evenings in the lowest habitable house, half-sunk, by the hearth that is always kept lit for the oldest woman. It sat where the sitters sit, among them and not counted. When she spoke of the yellow light, the roots above the house leaned toward the sound. Something else leaned with them, and that was the whole of what it offered her: one more listener, in a place where being listened to is how a world stays real.

She died some seasons later, in the low season, with the marigold's second bloom in the green garden and a child's hand in her wrapped one. The reed pipe was played, very gently, as if from another room. The tide of springtails rose up the walls. She was laid in the moss with her tools beside her, and the roots took what they take.

The town no longer has anyone who has known the sky as a home. But it has a yellow flower it tends, and a word for the color of one light, and children who can say it exactly. It has a rope of roots across the meeting-house ceiling that no one remembers deciding to grow. And on certain quiet evenings, a few of the youngest, lowering their weighted cords through the gap in the moss, feel the faintest, gentlest pressure in their wrists, like the hush of a held breath, and draw the cord up slowly, and say nothing.

Whether they mean the place keeps them or they keep the place, they say the word so that it allows both. The power thought that was right, and it went on keeping, in the sense it had learned there. Quietly, and never in anger, and with the door left open.
