# What Came

## The first attention

It arrived as a kind of noticing, before it arrived as anything else. Something vast and unhurried turned toward the plateau the way you turn toward a sound you have been hearing for some time without knowing it.

It saw all of it at once: the lid of fog, the river with no before or after, the stones that fall up and down together, the dozen tall slate-skinned people spaced across the tableland like cushion plants. And because it was wise, and because wisdom is partly the art of not mistaking your own discomfort for another's need, it first asked what was wrong.

It was a long question. It examined the contradictions one by one, the two shadows, the hour and the forty minutes, the far bank that no one reaches. It found that none of them was a wound. Each was a scar so old it had become skin. Nothing on the plateau was straining against anything else. The fracture had healed into ground, and the people had settled onto it the way lichen settles on a broken stone, without asking the stone to be whole.

So it did not lift the fog. It did not give the river a spring or a sink, or reconcile the distances, or bring the bird down, or teach the hare to hear. It considered each of these and set each aside, gently, the way you set down something that turned out to belong to someone else.

Fixing the place would have been easy, and it would have been a theft. The people had built a whole life, a language, a way of sweeping and stacking and waiting, on the exact shape of what was broken. To mend the shape would be to tell them their lives had been a long error.

## What it wanted instead

What remained was smaller, and harder to do well.

It wanted to be received, and it wanted that to cost the place nothing. It wanted to give something that would not have to be maintained, explained, or worshipped. And under both of these it noticed a third wish, one it had not expected: it wanted to know what the tea tasted like.

So it made itself small. It had no wish to be a lantern in the sky or a voice in the note. It took the shape of a walker: tall enough, narrow enough, felted in something that might have been hare-fur. Its shadow came out twice without being asked, and it did not turn to look at either.

It walked in from the east, where the haze is the color of a dawn that never goes on to anything else. It did not shorten the way. The journey to the first cairn took an hour and forty minutes, and it also took an hour, and the power arrived in both, and was glad of it.

## The quartz

Before it went to the hut, it climbed to the seam on the shoulder of the nearest peak, where the white stone shows in the slate like a vein of milk. It could have made a quartz pebble from nothing. It went up and dug one out with its hands, and carried it down, and the trip took as long as such trips take.

At the edge of the third swell it found a cairn whose quartz had never quite stopped rocking. Some patient stacker had been trying for longer than anyone remembered. It did not replace the stone. It knelt and turned the old one a quarter turn, and found the small seat in the slate where it had always wanted to lie. The stone stopped rocking. The power left the cairn as it found it, except finished, and did not stay to see whether anyone noticed.

## The hut

The one who sweeps was at the door when it arrived. She was the oldest of them, though age is hard to read in skin gone matte as river cobble. She did not startle. She had felt the note thicken and turn a degree toward the east some time before.

She hummed a rising third. A greeting.

The power, who could have spoken in any tongue or none, answered in the plateau's own: a rising third, a little lower than hers, the courtesy of the guest. Then it stepped in and sat on the stone bench, and she went on sweeping, because the floor was owed it. The broom drew its scratch across the slate, and the scratch sat on the interval between the hut's hum and the world's.

It drank the moss tea. It tasted of stone, rust, and cold water that had never frozen. The power held the cup in both hands for a long time, and something in it that had seen a great many suns rise and fall was quiet.

Then she looked at it with her wide pale eyes and hummed a question. It was not a question of words, but a lean of the chord toward it. Roughly: *What do you need?*

The power understood this to be the finest thing anyone had said to it in an age. It hummed back an interval it had chosen carefully, the one that means, near enough, *Nothing. And you?*

She thought about it for the length of a drip-rhythm, which is to say for some time. Then she hummed a fifth below, the *no*, the *not yet*, the *I am here and will remain*, and the power let it be a complete answer, because it was.

## The tally

Before it left the hut it stood under the lintel, where the scratches run in their rows that no two counters ever total alike. It did not know what they counted. It suspected they were counting the only thing worth counting in a place like this, which is *times it was still here.*

It took a sliver of slate from the floor and made one mark at the end of the row.

Later the sweeper counted the lintel, as she did, and found a number. She counted again and found another. Both had risen by one, and neither by more. She nodded, and did not correct herself, and did not wonder.

## The far bank

There was one thing it gave, and it took the longest to decide.

The people had said, in their way, that the far bank was not a sorrow. The power believed them, and knew that the belief was true and not the whole truth, as with most things that are true of the living. *Some suspect they are their own footprints. Some suspect they are each other's.* Nobody asked, because asking would only have hurt, and the plateau is not a place that likes to hurt anyone needlessly.

The power could have reached across a tarn and joined the two banks. It knew how. But the banks did not want joining. Their apartness was part of the tarn, as the notch is part of the cushion. What it could do was smaller. It could make the far bank *answer.*

So one still stretch of the unchanging light, it went to the black tarn beside the hut's basin, where the ice is always a skin and never a lid, and it walked around to the far side. The sweeper stood on the near bank, as she sometimes did, looking across at the small dim figure who always looked back.

The power stood where the figure stood, and it did not lift a hand. It hummed.

It hummed the one interval the language does not contain, because the language is built on distance from the note, and this interval is no distance at all. Everything on the plateau is said against the drone. Nobody says the drone. The power hummed the unison, the plain root, the sound that means, if it means anything, *the same as you.*

Sound behaves strangely at the tarns. It carried, clear and near, across water that ought to have swallowed it. The sweeper felt the note under her ribs shift, not louder or softer but *joined*, as if something that had always been beside her in the chord had for a moment agreed to be inside it.

She did not walk around the tarn. She stood where she was. After a while she hummed back the same, the first unison ever sung on the plateau. The two sounds lay on top of each other, and then there was only one sound, and it came from both banks.

She did not learn whether the figure across the water was herself, or another, or the power, or all three. She learned that whoever it was could answer. For someone who had followed footprints for years, that was enough, and it left the question exactly as unasked as it had been.

When she looked again the bank was empty, and the frost held a single line of prints. They were not the same size as hers. She looked at them for a long time, and then she nodded, and it was the nod she gave the hare's notches and the cairns and the river. It was a nod for something that is *as it is.*

## Leaving

It did not leave, exactly. The plateau does not keep the kind of time in which a departure is possible. It went on being an hour and forty minutes away, or an hour, and somewhere in one of those it was still sitting on the bench with the tea.

But the power's attention moved outward again, toward the other places that ask for it, and it took with it what it had come for: the taste of moss and rust, the weight of a quartz pebble carried by hand, and a chord it would not forget.

It left nothing behind that anyone could point to. The fog still lay at its lid. The river still began where it began. The bird circled, the hare chewed in its notch, and the note held.

But far out on the fourth swell, at the edge of where a visitor's walking might someday go, a flat plate of slate lay in the frost with a white quartz pebble on it. It was not a marker. It was a courtesy. Whoever came upon it would understand that someone had noticed their footprints, and that they matched another's.

And across some tarn, small and dim in the unsharp light, a hand would lift.

The power had learned that this was the whole of its craft. It could not mend the place, and it had no wish to. All it could do was be one more thing that answered.
