# What Came to Karrow Cut

## The Ebb

It came at the end of an ebb, in the low light after rain, when the frogs were loudest.

It had no footfall to stop them with, and this pleased it. Walking among four hundred listeners, it made itself no louder than the moss, and the sedge went on humming as though nothing had crossed it. A heron in a drainage cut turned its head a quarter inch, saw what there was to see, and went back to the water. The visitor took this as a greeting, and the only one it needed.

It walked the mile from the spring to the coast end slowly, doing what the valley does. It learned the ticking of the pipe and the three different heartbeats of the mills. It learned that the washing lines were speaking: a red sheet on one terrace, a striped one on another. The stripe meant a dispute. The dispute was about a hen. Most of what was said across the Cut, over the noise and the distance, was gentle, and a surprising amount of it concerned hens.

There were things it could have done at once. It looked at each and put it down.

It could have read the counting women's ledger, which no one else may read. It did not. The rule was a good one, and the only rule in the valley it was in a position to break.

It could have mended the ears of the people. It heard the felters bellowing over their drums, and a woman who could not hear a shout but stopped mid-stride at a change in a pipe three terraces off. The valley had moved its listening into palms and floors and the soles of feet. To repair the ears wholesale would be to pull the language out from under the people and hand them ears in exchange. It left the offer standing, like a door left unlatched. No one knew to ask, and that was all right.

## The Night of the Column

On the north wall above the drying greens, where market is held at the crest, a basalt column had cracked through behind its face. Roots had found the crack, and behind them a thread of water. The visitor heard it as the children would have, as one tick out of place in the tick of the valley. At the crest, when mist filled the cut to the rim and every cliff streamed, the water would finish what it had begun. It would be market day, and the greens would be full.

It considered this for most of a night.

It could hold the column up. That was easy, and wrong. The wall would stand a thousand years on a promise, and children would learn the edge-test at a cliff that had been persuaded to lie. It could warn them with a dream, a wrongly hung sheet, or a starling taught to shriek. But a people warned by omens learn to wait for omens. These people listened to the ground itself, and the visitor did not want to become the thing they listened to instead.

So it did the least thing there was. It took four days off the column's clock.

The slab came down on the second night of the ebb, at the hour the frogs were loudest, onto empty greens. The sound was a book the size of a hill closing. Every frog in Karrow Cut stopped at once. The silence held a full minute. Then one tiny nail-on-glass piper started up, and the meadow followed.

In the morning people stood on the broken columns in their nightclothes. An old wrapper said to no one, "That was hanging over the market," and nobody answered. A few glanced up, not at the wall but at nothing in particular, and then went to find their gloves.

The children named it before breakfast: *the Slab That Stopped the Frogs*. It is the only slab in the valley's memory remembered for a silence. The counting women went to their ledger, because slabs fall at the crest and this one had fallen at the ebb. A girl had been born that same night, in the middle of the noise, and by the ledger she should have been patient and by the slab she should have been loud. They are still arguing over which page she belongs on. The visitor had not meant to give them that. It was glad.

## The Rising

The steam strengthened. Laundry went out on every line and dried in the warm mist. The visitor sat in a moss cushion a foot deep on the north wall and let the afternoon rain pass through it, twenty minutes, then the sun at full brightness. The whole cut steamed and glittered, and it steamed with it.

That evening, because it can, it looked down the long years, not to change anything but to know. It saw the moss advance a hand's width a year, and the alders take the allotments one plot at a time. It saw the columns round off. It saw the pulse slow, eleven days to twelve to twenty, as mineral scale narrowed the crack, until one spring the pipes cooled and did not heat again. The stacks held only starlings, then only the memory of starlings. The tram stopped last. At the end the frogs had the valley to themselves, under a soft green fold of ground that no one would have called sharp.

It grieved for a moment, accurately, at the size of the thing, and set the grief down beside the others. It found nothing there to correct. Nothing in Karrow Cut hears its own life in real time, and the basalt returns every sound half a second late. The valley was young and would be old, and it lived, like everything, a little behind its echo.

It told no one, and did not mind.

## The Crest

The pipes tightened. The frogs fell silent. Mist rose to the rim, and people stepped onto their doorsteps and tilted their heads. The chord built out of the iron: three notes, and behind them a fourth that some wrappers swore they heard and others swore was the wind. Then someone hummed, and someone else, and half the valley sang low and tuneless against the pipes, as an animal answers an animal.

The visitor had no throat, so it answered in the only way it could without touching the pipes. It gave the fourth note, the one the chord had been leaning toward for as long as there had been a chord, to a young starling on the trestle by the felting mill. The starling was a poor mimic and known for it. It whistled the note back a quarter tone flat, and the others took it up.

## The Falling

In the first days of the descent the wrappers heard the starlings and argued, as they will. One old woman walked her stretch of trestle, the third out from the oil press, tapping the joints with a spanner. She found that if a coupling were taken up three-quarters of a turn, the pipe would sing what the starling sang, a quarter tone flat, at the crest. She did not do it. She said only that it could be done, which in the Cut is how a thing gets loved before it is used.

The old note has always won. But the pub now had a worse question than who taught whom, birds or pipes. It had a third party that no one knew about, and so it would never be settled.

## The Next Ebb

The visitor left as it had come, at the end of an ebb, in the low light after rain. It did not stay, because staying would have made it a weather, and the Cut had weather enough. It took nothing but the sound of the place. It kept it the way one keeps a tune, a half-second late and doubled, and found it liked the delay.

A few days later, before the swell, a girl lay on a moss ledge with her ear against the warm rock. A heron stood a few yards off. Above them a starling on a trestle ran through the first two notes of the Chime, tentative, days early.

The girl whistled the third without looking up.

The starling took it, turned it over, and handed it back. Then, unasked, it offered a fourth, flat by a quarter tone. The girl whistled that back too, as she had heard it, wrong in the same way, which is how a thing enters a language.

The heron did not move. A long way off, something that was listening kept very still and was very glad.
