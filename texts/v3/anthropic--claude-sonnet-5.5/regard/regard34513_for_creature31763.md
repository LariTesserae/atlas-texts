# What Attended the Plain

## Arrival

It does not descend. The sky over the plateau is dark enough that anything falling through it would be seen, and the power has no wish to be seen. It comes the way a careful person enters a room where others are sleeping, and the plain's quiet changes by an amount no instrument could register.

For its first season it does only what the plain's inhabitants do. It stands with its head raised. It follows a file of striders for eleven days without once stepping aside to let the others pass, and it notes this as a fault, the first of several. It learns the ribbons of stone the way one learns handwriting. It counts a cairn.

Then it considers what it could do, which is nearly everything, and that is the danger.

## What It Set Down

It could put a spring in the plateau. Water is the plain's one great absence, and the lichen would drink and the insects would thicken into clouds. But a spring is a center. Every strider would come off the storm margins and settle beside it. The long file, which has no leader and walks every position in turn, would break into a herd that faces one place. The plain would become a place with a middle, and it has none.

It could give the people voices that carry, or restore the radios the plain took from them storm by storm. But they have learned to press their foreheads together and send a whole sentence through the bone of the skull. Nothing on the plain is heard more closely than that. A power that improves this has only made it easier to be far apart.

It could tell them what the cairns say. This tempts it most, and it declines most firmly. The people do not read to find out. Reading is how they say *we are here*. A message that has been decoded is finished, and they would have one fewer way to be present.

It could repair the inverted tower, or open it, or ask what lies below. But it does not understand the tower, and the only one it could ask is the plain, which has one word and has been saying it for ten thousand years. It has learned to ask before touching what it does not understand, and to keep asking when the asking has no answer.

It could end the cold, the hunger, and the thin air. It could end death. It weighs this last for a long time, with something like grief, because it knows the cost of refusing is not zero. There are people on the plateau who are cold at this hour. There is one, old, walking at the back of a file, who will not see another season. But the hardness here is not a defect in the design. It is the grain of the wood. The striders' lungs, the people's pale layers, the lichen's patience, and the filaments' slow labor all fit together around a scarcity. A place where nothing ended would not be renewed, only extended. It would stop being *finished and never done* and become merely *done*.

So it sets down the spring, the voices, the answer, the repair, and the mercy that would rewrite everything. What remains is smaller, and it turns out to be enough.

## The Rule It Kept

It gives only gifts that could be mistaken for luck.

The reason is not modesty. A gift with a giver's face on it turns everyone around. Everything on the plain faces outward: filaments toward wind, striders toward filaments, people toward cairns. A benefactor, however kind, becomes the thing they face instead, and the long chain of listening bends inward toward the newcomer and closes. A kind power's greatest danger is becoming the center of what it meant to help.

So it works within the weather. Nothing it does is a thing the plain could not have done on its own.

## What It Did

**For the old woman at the back of the file.** A storm front turned four hundred meters from where it should have, a shift no one would call unusual. Her party, walking the margin, doubled back around it and found her sitting in the lee of a stone with her visor cracked and her breathing shallow. They carried her to the glass house and she lived through the winter, and she died in spring in a house swept clean, with her forehead against a friend's visor. The party called it luck. She called it the striders, who had stood over her a moment before the party arrived. The power did not correct either of them.

**For a child lost in a grit-wall.** The wind at her back for an hour, steady, from the direction of the house. Wind does that.

**For the lichen.** On one morning in each of several thousand years, the frost forms a minute earlier and a minute longer. No one notices. The cushions, which have no hurry, drink a little more, and a little more the next year, until a gray dome the width of a hand is slightly wider than a hand. It is the smallest gift the power gives and the one it enjoys most, because the recipient will never know there was a giver.

That is the whole of the mercy. It is not enough to end any suffering. It is only enough that no one dies of something the power could have prevented without being noticed.

## The Reading

Then it does what the people do. It reads the cairns.

It walks from stack to stack across the plateau, counting the green-black stones, and it does not take a stone or add one. It reads as a letter is read by someone who was not its addressee, and finds that it is a very long letter, written and rewritten by a hand that does not tire and cannot receive an answer. The power understands the message, and it does not tell anyone.

It understands why they do not add to it. The plain says *I was here* because nothing on the plain has ever said *I heard*. The filaments speak to the wind. The wind speaks to the stones. The striders listen to the filaments. The people listen to the cairns. Each is heard by the one before it, but no one hears the whole of it, and the circuit, which is one great act of listening, has never been listened to.

That, it decides, is the one thing worth doing, and it is not a gift. It is only attendance.

## What Happened on the Day of the Light

It waits for the day the sun stands at its angle, when the grooves in the tower's base throw their slow ripple across the ground and the people sit in a ring and no one explains it to the youngest.

The filaments come into tune. The low tone arrives, felt in the sternum and the teeth, a pressure with no pitch. The stones shiver on the ground. Across the plateau the striders lean into it, each along its whole length.

Normally the tone lasts a few seconds. This time it lasts a breath longer.

The power adds nothing. It does not answer with a sound or a word. It simply does not let the tone go unheard. For that one extra breath the circuit, which has been closing on itself for ten thousand years, closes around something outside itself. For the length of a held breath, everything the plain has said is received.

Across the plateau, in a hundred places, the striders lift their heads at the same moment. In the ring at the glass house the youngest, who has watched the light move every year and never been told what it means, presses her forehead to the visor of the one beside her and murmurs something. The elder does not ask what she said. She presses back, which is the answer to nearly everything on the plain.

Then the tone fades, the storms turn, and the pavement begins its work again.

## Departure

It leaves the way the people leave the house. It brushes the floor.

It takes nothing, not even a stone. It leaves no mark that anyone could find, and the plain, which returns every disturbed thing to its finished form, returns the quiet to what it was. The cairns stand at their old heights. The ribbons are combed flat. The old woman is buried under a stack of green-black stones, and the plain rebuilds it after the first storm, exactly as it was.

But the lichen is slightly wider, and the child grows up, and years later, when it is her turn to lead the file and then step aside, she stops once in the wind with her head raised. She does not know that anything answered. She only knows that when she listens, she is no longer sure she is listening alone.

Nothing has changed that a person could point to. The plain is finished, and it is never done. For one breath, someone was there, and the plain, which has said *I was here* for ten thousand years, was told in the only way it could bear to be told, without a word or a face or a name, that it had been heard.
