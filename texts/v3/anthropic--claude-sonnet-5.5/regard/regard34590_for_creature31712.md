# The One Who Was Given a Wall

## Arrival

It came up the four thousand steps on foot. It could have arrived otherwise, and it considered doing so, then decided that a thing which arrives by the front stair is the only kind of thing a house can welcome.

It was not tall or bright, and it wore no crown of any weather. Mostly it had made itself small enough to be overlooked, which for a great power is a kind of courtesy. At the top, a woman with her hand on a carved deer lifted her eyes for one level moment and then looked back to the stone. It understood that it had been counted among the *going-past*, and that this was correct.

For a long time it did nothing else. This was the first wisdom, and the hardest. Something that can change a valley by wanting to has to learn, before anything else, not to want yet.

## The Wall

On the second day someone put a bundle of moss in its hands and pointed at a seam on the east wall of a compound whose name it never learned. It chinked the seam. It did the work no better than the work required, because better was a word the valley didn't use and it had come to be a guest.

While it pressed moss into stone, it listened. It heard the drip from the thatch and the dry snap of the grey birds. Far across the basin, small and steady, it heard the *thock* of a maul. It learned the third tense, *as it will be found*, and the greeting: touch the stone, then look, and say by looking, *this is here, and so are you.*

By the third day it had a name. Nobody gave it one. The name was simply already standing in the compound, waiting for a hand: *Seam-of-the-East-Wall.* It answered to that, and no one thought it strange, because no one thought any keeper was ever new.

## What It Saw

It saw that these were not a suffering people. It looked for suffering the way a physician looks, patiently and without hope of finding it, and it would have been glad to find none. There was almost none. Nobody in Ossary was lost, nobody was hoarding, and nobody was afraid. The stillness was not a cage they were pacing. It was a room they loved and had furnished with their whole attention.

But it saw three things, and it held them without hurry.

It saw the child. Small, gap-toothed, combing burrs from wool on the low wall, looking up at strangers with more wonder than any adult in the valley had allowed themselves in a very long time. The child was as small and gap-toothed on the ninth day as on the first. She was not unhappy. She was *kept*, and she looked at the going-past the way a person looks through glass at weather.

It saw the listeners, who sat on the roof-edges with their eyes half-closed, and understood that the listening was not a custom. It was a load-bearing wall. Attention was holding this place at its hour, the way hands hold a bowl of water level. It was gentle work and it never ended, and no one in the valley had ever put the bowl down to find out whether they could.

And it saw that the stillness had a beginning. Nothing this complete happens without a wound behind it. Long ago, some grief or fear had been so great that a whole people asked, in one voice, that nothing more be lost. The valley answered. It is a strange thing to answer a prayer so exactly that the prayer becomes a climate.

## Why It Did Not Simply Undo It

The power could have broken the Held Hour in an afternoon. Cloud, light, and the slow rain in its columns could all have been released, and the seasons could have poured back in like water into a dry bed.

It did not, and its reasons were not timid.

First, the hold was made from love, and to break a made thing is not the same as freeing it. A mercy that arrives as a demolition is only another weather laid over their heads, and they had already had one weather laid over them. They had asked for this one.

Second, the Kept were not prisoners. They were the wall's own masons. Whatever was owed here was owed to them as authors, not as victims, and authors are not rescued. They are asked.

Third, and this was the one that made it slow: it did not know that it was right. The deer returned, the cats returned, the children returned, and there is a grief in that, but there is also a gift, and a person who has never lost someone cannot say with authority what it is worth to lose no one. So it went to find the ones who could tell it.

## The Low Door

On the fourth evening, which was like every evening, it took a lamp and a bowl of water and walked to the oldest doorway at the base of the wall. The door was not sealed. It had never been sealed. It had only not been used.

Below the fitted courses, below the finer stone and the coarser and the coarsest, it found what the Kept called *the ones who held before.* They were neither ghosts nor bones. They were the holding itself: a long dark row of them, past counting, the first keepers and their keepers' keepers, each one a stone in a wall and each one still, faintly, a mason. The sound it had heard from the roof was theirs. They were dressing stone that would never be used for anything new, and they had been dressing it since the first grief.

They were not unhappy either. They were faithful. But when it set the bowl of water down on the floor and knelt beside it, the way one kneels beside a crack, it could feel how long ago the last question had been asked of them. The last question was *will you hold this?* And they had said yes, and no one had come down since to ask the second.

So it asked the second. It did not ask *will you stop.* It asked, in the third tense and then in a tense the language did not yet have:

*If you could put it down, and pick it up again, would you like to know that you could?*

The maul stopped.

For the first time in the history of Ossary, the sound beneath the stone ceased. Above, on every roof-edge in the basin, sixty listeners lifted their heads at the same instant. The pale moths hung in the rain columns. On the cloud's underside, one hawk stirred.

Then the maul began again, but it was no longer one maul. It was many, out of step with each other by a breath, like people walking who had only ever marched. It was, for the first time, a chord.

The power took that as *yes, slowly, and perhaps.* It asked for nothing else.

## What It Left

It made three changes before it left, each smaller than the last, and none that could not be undone.

It let one leaf fall in one rain column. The keeper of that column stood with her face lifted and her braids darkening, and the leaf came down and lay on the wet moss at the rim. She looked at it for a very long time. She did not put it back.

It let the gap-toothed child's tooth come in. That was all. One morning she ran her tongue across the place and stopped, and did not tell anyone, and carried the news around inside her like a bright coin.

And it let one shadow move.

It was the shadow of a stone marker at a crossing, a squat one carved with the faces of animals. The shadow had fallen the same way, short and blue-edged, since before the first grief. Now, in the Held Hour, it slid the width of a finger toward the east.

Every hand in the valley stopped. Nothing else happened for the length of many breaths.

Then a woman at the crossing did what her people have always done when something is put in front of them. She laid her palm on the marker, on the carved face of a deer. She looked at the moved shadow and did not look away. The gesture said what it had always said, but it said it now to time itself: *this is here, and so are you.*

Around the basin, others did the same.

## After

The power left by the front stair, as it had come. It went down the four thousand steps without looking back, having learned in the valley that one look back is a hand laid on the thing you meant to release.

It did not lift the hold. The cloud lid still hangs at its height, the sharp stone keeps its edges, and the moss stops at the rim of the rain. It did not tell the Kept what had been done to them, or for them, or that a door had been left open in both directions. If they choose, they can mend all three changes. They can find the leaf and hang it on its branch, ease the tooth back into the gum, and nudge the shadow home. It would take one mason a season. They are good at mending.

But something has entered their grammar. The listeners on the roofs say it first, in the low pauses between breaths, and it spreads outward from compound to compound the way a rumor never has in that valley. It is a fourth tense, and no visitor can hear it in less than a week. It is not *as it is* or *as it was kept* or *as it will be found.* It is closer to *as it will be met.*

Whether they will keep it, no one can say, and the wise power did not want to know. Somewhere in a sealed dark under the stone, a chord of mauls is still going, out of step, in no hurry. Somewhere above it, in a light that has begun very slightly to lean, a small gap-toothed girl is trying out a new tooth on a hard crust. The listeners are listening to the shadow.

And at the top of the stair, beside the last marker, a hand rests on the carved face of a deer. It is watching the road, as it always did, in case someone comes up. It is no longer certain what would be found.
