# What the Attention Did

It came down the shaft the way anyone does, and it did not hurry, because hurrying is a kind of noise.

It stood on the sulfur dust for a long time. In the vug's terms, that was a long time. It let its breath slow to the four seconds, its pulse lean on the piston's downstroke, its ribs say *here, here, here*. It did this on purpose and with its eyes open, knowing it could stop. That was the first courtesy: to be for a while a member of the room and not its judge.

Then it understood something the visitor had not. The vug was not finished.

To a clock that runs in one direction, a closed loop at equilibrium looks finished. But the Attention had listened in a different way. The four-second beat was not a flaw in the tuning. It was the place where two worms, in two cracks, had been holding two notes a hair apart for longer than the machines had stood over them, and the pipes had learned to copy them faithfully. The room was not a dead thing that hummed. It was a very slow conversation whose speakers were too patient to seem to be talking.

So the Attention decided against repair, rescue, or improvement. It saw no suffering in the chord that a kinder chord would cure. It saw no cage in the beat. It carried no water, because no one there had a word for water, and a gift you cannot receive is only a weight. It brought no light, because the ember-orange glow was already enough for the things that needed it. It brought no wisdom, because the mantises had their own.

What it did was smaller, and it took a long time.

## The Ground Beneath the Chord

First it went to the belts, the cured, ropy hides that run up through the roof. It did not follow them to their source. Whatever was up there, whether an animal or a mechanism or a habit of the rock, was part of the loop, and the Attention did not open loops that were not its own. But it laid a hand on the nearest belt and felt how it was held. It found that the whole equilibrium hung on one thread that no one in the vug could mend. Every closed and complete system has a single point that is not closed, where the outside touches it, and that point is where it will eventually end.

It did not strengthen the thread. A thing kept from ending is not thereby kept alive, and the Attention had seen what that turns into. But it did one thing. It shaped the failure, so that if the belts ever slackened and the flywheels slowed, the eleven notes would not shatter or grind. They would resolve. The chord would settle a step at a time into a last quiet interval, like a bell running out of swing, so that the beetles could hush in time and the mantises could fold, and the blades, ringing to the end, could go still one by one and not fall in a rain.

It told no one, and never played it. It left the cadence there like a folded blanket on a shelf, and the room went on, not knowing it had one more thing.

## The Twelfth Voice

Then it climbed to the ledge near the roof, where the jointed reeds stand with their translucent pipes, and it listened to their one note, the high lit edge that belongs to no machine.

The reeds are the only part of the vug that answers instead of repeating. The Attention thought about that for a long time. Then it sang them a note.

It was not a loud note, nor a new one. It was a fifth above their own, a tone the chord could hold without strain. It sang it once, softly, into the pipes, and then stopped, and waited to see whether the reeds would keep it.

They might not. They might sound it back once and let it lapse, and the Attention would not have minded. An offer that cannot be declined is only an instruction. But if they kept it, if the stems shifted over a season or an age and a thirteenth tone came into the top of the room, that would be the vug's first change that was neither breakage nor decay. It would be a change that a room could sing.

Whether they kept it, the tale does not say. The Attention did not stay to find out, because staying to check is another way of not having let go.

## The One Off the Beat

On the way down, it found a larva.

The larva was pale and soft and had not yet found the rasp. It was walking off the edge of a column into the bare sulfur, uneasy, out of step by half a second and a body's width. Any moment now, the drift would notice it and the column would take it back, and it would find the beat as it always had, without remembering, and be one of the hundreds of thousands saying *here*.

That is not a bad thing to become. The Attention did not think it was. Belonging in a beat is a real good, perhaps the good this whole room exists to hold.

But it also thought: *someone should be allowed to be the one who chooses it.*

So it did nothing to the larva. It only warmed the little stretch of ground where the larva was walking, a few paces of bare stone, just enough that being out of step was not cold. It made the wandering safe, and then it stepped back. The larva went on a little way, and stopped, and something in it turned toward the sound of the drift. Perhaps it went back, perhaps not. The Attention watched until it was sure the choice was the larva's own, and then it looked away, as one does.

## The Chimney, the Tags, and the Mantis

At the chimney, the Attention paused for a long while. It looked at the swept ring in the dust. It did not ask what tended the ground there, or whether it was the last of the ones who came before, or their echo. It sat down at the edge of the ring, outside it, and after a while it said aloud the name on one of the tin tags, the one that reads **B. HALLOWAY, WEDS.**, and it said it kindly. It said that the shift was over. It said that whoever had left the tools was known, and was all right, and had not been forgotten by everything.

Nothing answered. The chimney breathed its warm air out on the beat. But the ring of swept dust seemed, afterward, to have been swept a little more gently, as though by something that had been relieved of a duty it had held too long and did not yet know it could lay down.

Last of all, the Attention went to the thicket and found a glass mantis standing at a blade that did not ring true. The mantis had been there some time, tapping, leaning, listening through its feet. The Attention did not tell it anything. It crouched, and, by holding still, gave the mantis the kind of company that asks for nothing. The mantis tapped and listened. Then it levered the blade off at the root with a precise, sideways shove, and the blade fell into the dust with a clear chime, and the room was one blade cleaner.

"Yes," the Attention said, very quietly, the way you'd say it to a colleague. "That one."

That was all. It was the one true thing it had come to say.

## What It Took With It

On its way up the shaft, the Attention passed the visitor, who was still down there, breath slowed, ribs murmuring, nodding at the beetles without knowing it.

It did not wake them roughly. It did not need to. It only touched, once, at the fifth beat, the one place on the visitor's wrist where the pulse says *mine*, so that for a single moment the visitor's heart fell off the piston's downstroke and landed, all by itself, half a second late. The visitor blinked, and remembered the shaft, and the air above, and the clock that runs one way.

Then the Attention went up into the thin quiet, and the sound narrowed behind it to a hum, a pressure, and nothing.

Below, the chord went on, exactly as it had been. But there was a cadence folded on a shelf that had not been there before. There was a note in the reeds that was either kept or not. There was a larva somewhere, choosing. There was a name said out loud beside a chimney, and a blade gone from the thicket, and a room a little less alone.

The clock was still nobody's. That was the thing the Attention had been most careful to leave alone. But it was now, very slightly, a clock that somebody had heard.
