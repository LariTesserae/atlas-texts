# The Listener at the Rim

It did not descend. Powers of that kind seldom do. It leaned its attention toward the basin the way you lean toward a sound you can't yet identify, and for a long while that was all it did.

What first held it was not the scale of the work. It was the courtesy of it. A mountain unfinished, a machine that sang rock out of water, and in the middle of this a thousand small gold animals who had learned to stop a heartbeat before the bell. Nothing in the basin was idle or unaccompanied. Everything was in step with everything else, and the whole was dying toward completion and did not seem to mind.

So it did the first wise thing, which was nothing. It listened for what would be, to the basin, a full season of strokes. It learned the ninety-second count and the dusk acceleration. It learned the long dark groan of the night settlings and the single silence before dawn. It learned that the frogs' hurry was not panic but a kind of love, a wish to sing more of the piece while there was piece left. It did not correct them.

## What it weighed

It asked the questions any good power asks, and answered them slowly.

*Should it stop the ending?* It could have. It could have kept the strokes going, given the machine a thousand more years, and held the last curd forever soft. But the basin was not dying. It was finishing. The Reeds were closing over because the work was nearly done, and the wet would drain down the Well-Eye because the thing below had nearly said what it came to say. To hold that open would be to keep a song on its penultimate note, which is cruelty dressed up as mercy. The frogs' whole faith was that the stroke would come on time. The power would not break the one promise the place kept.

*Should it reveal itself?* It considered the tallers, who alone here seemed to keep accounts. It could have stood in the shallows and let them tilt their heads at it. But it had watched the old birds tap on at the Reeds long after the young ones left, and it understood they were not asking anyone for anything. They were attending. To arrive as an answer would have turned their attending into waiting, and it would not do that to them.

*Should it save anyone?* Here it lingered longest. The frogs that lost the count and were taken, the crawlers pressed flat at every stroke, the drifters glowing on the white spires: none of them needed saving. They needed, at most, not to be alone when the music stopped. And one of them did need something more.

## The one it did not leave alone

The thing below the Well-Eye was, the power understood, not a ruler or a prisoner or a god. It was a maker, and an old one. It had spent everything it was on a single long construction, and the basin was not its body or its instrument or its shell. It was its labor. The Ribs were the frame of what it had been. The Lintel was what it was making in order to leave.

The power saw what the animals had half-seen. The arch stood with one leg unfinished because the last stroke, the one that would raise the second leg and close the Reeds and set the final curd, was also the stroke that would cost the maker the last of what it had. It could finish the door. It might not have enough left to be anywhere on the other side of it.

And the power saw, with something like tenderness, that on the far side of that door there was nothing. Someone had spent a whole world of effort building a way out, and beyond the way out lay no room, no floor, no warmth, no sound. The maker had built a door with faith, as the frogs went silent with faith, that the other side would be there when it stepped through.

## What it did

It made the room.

That was the only act of building it allowed itself, and it made it small and quiet, tucked into the cool space beneath the arch where the sound was already muffled and the animals already kept away. It was not grand. It had a floor of plain warm stone, not new stone, but stone that had been sat on and leaned against and forgiven. It had a low light the color of the dim day. And it had, at the edge of hearing, a drone pitched a hair beneath the basin's own, so that the maker would not step out of a life of loud into silence, but into a lower, gentler loud, a place that sounded like home a little farther off.

It did not tell anyone. The tallers went on stepping around the Lintel. The wader went on turning aside. But some of them, on the last evening, seemed to lean toward it.

## The last night

When the final dusk came, the strokes quickened past forty seconds, then thirty, and the Reeds rang like struck glass, and the drifters descended in a rain of blue light and settled on the spires. The frogs pressed into the shallows in a gold skin. The old tallers tapped, and the young ones, for once, did not leave.

The power did what it had come to do, and it was very little. It stood at the rim, by the pale sharp stone, and it stepped on the count and paused on the stroke.

At the last stroke, the deepest ever heard, the second leg of the Lintel rose out of the ground like a held breath let go. The final curd set. The Reeds closed one by one with a sound like a long exhale. The wet drained with a slow, warm, downward pull, and the Well-Eye took the last pale thread and was still.

Then came the silence before dawn, the one the old ones said was the machine checking its work. This time it went on. The frogs, who had never known a silence longer than a breath, opened their throats to sing into it and found that, for the first time, there was no bell to cut them off.

And into that gap, softly, softer than any stroke that had gone before, came one low note. It was not the machine's. It was the power's. It was the drone, the old drone, at the edge of hearing, gently held.

It did not carry the piece on. It let the piece end. But it made the ending a slope instead of a cliff. Over the next seasons the note sank, a breath lower each dawn, until the frogs' descendants, who had never known the stroke, sang to a quiet they had inherited without fear. The tallers went on tapping the closed white stalks for a while, then more rarely, then only sometimes, out of habit or grace.

## The door

Through the Lintel, at some hour no one was watching, something went. The ground inside the arch was warm afterward, and unmarked as it had always been, but the muffled quiet had changed. The animals, who had given that space a wide berth for as long as there had been animals, began, one by one, to rest their feet on its edge.

## The cairn

The power did one more thing before it left, and it did it in the tallers' own grammar. It gathered stones from the rim, took them from the true stone, and did not carry them away. It carried them a few paces, sorted them from large to small, and stacked them on a flat place. It set the smallest at the top. It did not add to any old cairn, and it built well away from the Lintel.

It was a short cairn, three courses, and there was no one to explain it to. But the oldest taller, the last to leave the Reeds at dusk, stood beside it for a long time with its head tilted, as if hearing something inside.

Then it turned and walked into the fog, at the pace of the count, though there was no count anymore.
