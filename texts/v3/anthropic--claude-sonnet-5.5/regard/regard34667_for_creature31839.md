# What Was Done in the Sump Ward

The power came the way weather comes to a valley, as a change in what the place was being attended by. Nothing in the pit noticed it except the mold, which brightened very slightly and could not have said why.

It did not descend. It let itself settle, as silt settles, and for a long while it did what the ward does. It counted.

It stood at the outfall grate for the length of the interval, and then for another, and then for a hundred more. It watched the fish gather and the salamanders go still as dropped gloves. It watched the tin-shells clamp down along the rails in a ripple that ran out of sight. It heard the hiss, the groan, and the heavy plunk. Then it heard the quiet that came after, deeper than before, and understood that this quiet was what everything else in the pit was for.

## What it considered

It could have made the ward well, by its own reckoning.

It could have mended the roofs, drawn down the water, and shored the leaning chimney. It could have burned the flowstone off the lathes and let the foundry work again, or put a proper flame in the lamps. None of this was hard. All of it would have been a kind of theft.

The roofs were white because the swifts had made them white. The floors were soft because the beetles lived in them. The lathes were becoming stone at the rate of a hand's width per lifetime, and that rate was a clock. The lamps were lit by something that had spent generations learning where iron stood. A power that fixed the ward would find, when it finished, that it had ended the thing it meant to help. The tenants had not been waiting to be rescued. They had been doing something, slowly, and it was nearly done well.

So it set aside the large gifts one by one. It felt no reluctance in this, only a settled clarity, like a hand withdrawn from a sleeping child's hair.

## What it noticed instead

It noticed the gauge. The brass column of marks had gone green, and the numerals could be read by touch. Below the last chiseled line, the snails' grazed bands went on, bright in the verdigris, each one snail's rasp wide.

The power had a stylus and the means to cut one more mark, clean and deep, in a hand the sluice-keepers would have recognized. It did not. It looked at the grazed bands for a long time. It saw that a gift of the right kind could be made only by the ones who had been giving all along, and that the column was continued and needed nothing added.

It noticed the lamp at the corner of the main street that had gone dark in the last season, and the dead globe nine steps along that had begun to glow. It saw that the mold's route was a slow argument between the gas line and the iron standards, and that the argument was going well.

It noticed the willows leaning toward the gap in the girders, and it left the gap open.

Then it noticed the fledglings.

## The late ones

There were always a few, born on ledges too far from the pump-house to hear the beam clearly. When the stroke came, the roost lifted in its single thin curtain, and these young lifted a moment behind it, a wave late, wrong in their bodies in a way they had no word for. Their flight belonged to a slower ward. They rose into air the others had already left, and settled into the wake of a stillness they had only just missed.

Here the power felt something like the pull of a hook. It could carry the stroke to them. It could let the iron of the girders ring faintly at the far ledges, so the beam arrived on time everywhere. It would be a small thing. It would be kind.

It held the thought for the length of three intervals and looked at it from every side. It saw that the roost's rhythm was made partly of the young who came late and learned, in their own bodies, the distance between themselves and the sound. The late ones were not a flaw in the ward. They were the edge of it, where the beam's authority thinned into something like choice. And every year some of them, lifting late, found a slower rhythm that worked for the far ledges, and the far ledges had slowly grown a second, softer clock of their own.

So it did not change the sound.

On one evening, at one stroke, it did something the ward could not have measured. As the beam nodded and the roost rose in its curtain, the power rose a moment behind, with the late ones, on the far ledge where the sound was thin. For the length of a few wingbeats, the young swifts that lifted late were not alone in being late. Nothing came of it. No one was told. A fledgling settled back onto its shelf, and something in its small body that had been braced was not braced anymore.

Then the power went back to counting.

## The one thing it did change

There was one thing that the tenants could not attend to, because it lay beyond every creature's reach and measure.

Far above the pit, in the hidden cistern, the seeps of the cliff gathered and fell down the old penstocks to push the beam. That was the whole of it. The water head was the engine's only heart, and the penstocks were narrowing, slowly and correctly, as flowstone thickened in them the way it thickened on the lathes. In some number of centuries, not soon, the throat would close, the beam would hang in the air, and eleven minutes would become a silence with nothing at its end.

The ward might survive that. Its creatures were patient and it had survived worse. But the ward would have to learn to live without its clock, and the learning would be long and hard, and much would go under in the learning.

The power did not clear the penstocks and did not mend the cistern. It reached into the cliff and turned the seeps a little, redirecting a vein of water the width of a thread so that it ran across the throat of the pipe and kept a channel open. Then it made the change look like nothing at all, another drip among ten thousand. It did not buy forever. It bought perhaps three thousand years, enough for the flowstone to keep its pace and for the tenants to go on being what they were.

No one would ever know. Even the power, leaving, would forget the exact place.

## What it left

It left no mark, no lamp, no word, no gift that could be found. When it went, the ward was as it had been: the roofs white, the floors soft, the chimney leaning on the cliff like a tired man on a wall.

But the quiet after the next stroke, some who lived there would have said, was perhaps a little deeper than before, and perhaps a little less like waiting.

The beam nodded. The tin-shells clamped down along the rails, and the fish burst pale through the churn. The swifts lifted in their thin curtain, and a few of them, at the far end, lifted late and did not mind.

Then it settled, and in the long quiet, a visitor standing at the outfall grate, without meaning to, began to count.
