# What Was Done

The attention came the way fog comes: from below first, then all at once.

It had been looking for something else. It found the plateau by its sound, a low continuous note in the fabric of things, like a held breath that turned out to be a chorus. It slowed. Powers of that kind are not often slowed by anything.

It looked for a long time before it did anything. That was its first decision, and perhaps the wisest.

## What It Saw

It saw a floor older than mountains, worn down to the last hard thing, and a skin of living green laid over it like a held hand. It saw that the skin was hollow in places and that the hollows were slowly widening. It saw the wells open, one every few years, each a small mouth of the dark. It saw that the cloud line had begun, by a few paces a decade, to climb the cliffs, and that the frogs which lived only in the fog were being pushed toward the sky with nowhere higher to go. It saw a pale thread of fungus on some of the small bodies, arriving from the lowlands on someone's boots.

And it saw the people, twenty at a time, going up a rope ladder once a year to stand on the mat and be small. It saw the oldest woman at the front of the file, and saw that her hearing was going.

The power understood exactly what it could do. It could hold the cloud line where it was. It could clear the fungus from every frog. It could bind the peat over the wells so nothing would ever fall. It could give the people iron, grain, medicine, a written tongue, a road. Each of these was a kindness and each was within reach.

It set them down one by one, and looked at what each would cost.

Bound peat would be a plateau that no longer opened, and the plateau was made of opening. The wells were how the water got home, and the sinking was how the place stayed unfinished. A plateau that could not fall in on itself would be a museum of one.

Gifts to the people would be gifts to people who came, by their own account, to be small. What would a road be to them? An answer to a question they had not been asking.

And the power noticed something in itself as it weighed these. Every large remedy was a way of not listening. It would be easier to fix the place than to learn what the place was doing.

## What It Did First

It came down as nothing. It took no shape that the frogs would go quiet for, and it did not settle on the mat, which would have crushed the roots. It learned to be a weight of about a hand's depth and stepped as the grazers stepped, along the curved trails that go around the thin places. It found that the trails were correct. Nobody had taught the herds, and it could not have taught them better.

It spent a season lying with its palm flat on the moss, feeling the noise come up. It learned the grammar of the chorus: which pitches meant rock, which meant chamber, which meant a thin roof over black air. It was slow at this and humbled by it. The frogs had been reading the ground for a hundred thousand years. It had been reading for a month.

Then the file of people came up the cliff, and it walked at the back of them, unseen.

## The Woman

The oldest woman knew. She did not turn her head, but she slowed by a half step so that the last of the file was near her, and she listened to the place behind her that was quieter than it should have been.

That night on the pole platform, with the frogs roaring up through the wood, she did not speak, because a voice is swallowed at ten paces. She put her hand out flat on the boards, palm down, in the gesture her people use for *listen.* The power, which had never in its existence been asked to listen by anyone, listened.

She was afraid, it found, not of dying but of the map. Her ears were failing and the youngest of the group, a girl of ten or so, did not yet hear the ground. She heard the frogs as a wall. She had not yet learned that a wall has doors.

The power could have poured the whole map into the girl in a moment, every pitch, every hollow, every thin place for a hundred kilometers, complete and perfect. She would have walked the plateau flawlessly for the rest of her life and never once been afraid or wrong.

It did not. She would have been carrying something and not hearing it, and one day the plateau would have changed, as it does, and the map would have been wrong, and she would not have known how to make a new one.

## What It Actually Gave

It gave the girl quiet.

The next day, on the crossing to the west, when the file passed over a hidden chamber, the power did what a cloud shadow does. For three breaths it made a small silence in the frogs near the girl's feet, only a few paces wide, only as long as a hawk would have, so that in the hush the hollow note of the chamber came up alone and clear through her boots.

She stopped. Her head tilted. It was the first time the ground had said something to her and not to the wall of noise.

The frogs started again. She stood there, listening, with her whole face changed, and the power understood that this was the only thing it had ever done that was exactly the right size.

It did this eleven more times over the season, always at places where the plateau was speaking and she had not yet learned to hear. Never more than a few breaths. Never twice in the same way. By the end of the season the girl walked with her head slightly bowed, and the old woman, behind her, walked with her hands empty for the first time in years.

## What It Did About the Rest

It did not hold the cloud line, but it did not ignore it either. It followed the fungus down to the lowlands and found where it entered the streams, and it did what it could there, below the cliffs, in the country that already belonged to roads and boots and everyone's mistakes. It could not undo them, but it could arrange for a few pools to remain clean, and a few slopes to stay wet, so that some populations of the small singers would have a place to go if the plateau warmed past what it could give them. That was a refuge, not a rescue. It told itself honestly the difference.

And it left the wells alone. It found the newest one, a black doorway opened that spring in a hollow with no cairn, and it stood at the lip while the cold air came up against its face. It thought of the eyeless fish and the waxen salamanders down there in the drip, who knew the upper world only as a rumor of water and the occasional gift of a petal.

It dropped a stone.

The stone fell for a long time. The power waited, not because it needed the answer but because waiting was the point. Somewhere far below, the dark received the stone, and the sound came back changed, and it was not the sound of a problem. It was the sound of a place still being made.

## Departure

It left with the file, at the end of the season, when the first heavy rains came from the east. It went down the cleft behind the last of them, and at the bottom it lifted its attention from the ladder and let go.

Behind it the mat closed over the camps. The cairns stood, the pole platforms sagged, and the grass ropes went dark with hands that would come back in a year.

The power did not return often. When it did, it was a weight of a hand's depth on the moss, listening. Now and then the girl, grown, walked the plateau at the front of the file with her head bowed. Once, in a hush that fell across a hillside for no reason the frogs could give, she stopped, turned, and looked at the empty air behind her.

She did not say anything, because a voice is swallowed at ten paces. She put her hand out, palm flat.

The power, which had been asked to listen only twice in its existence, listened.
