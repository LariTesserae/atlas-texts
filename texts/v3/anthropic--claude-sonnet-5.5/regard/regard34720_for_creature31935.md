# What Came to Listen

*An addendum, gathered from Lodgers, Stilled, one long-heard woman, and the pale bird's silence.*

## Arrival

Nothing in the canopy parted. No light fell. The account begins with the long-heard woman at the edge, who noticed that someone had come in without the streets swelling. Most strangers arrive like a dropped tray. This one arrived like a hand laid on a table.

She gave it the plugs, as she gives them to everyone. It took them and thanked her in a murmur. She led it along the outer ring with a palm on the wall, and it kept its palm on the wall too, and it did not ask to go inside. She said afterward that she felt looked at the way the Lodgers look, at her margins first: the edge of her sleeve, the rim of the cup in her hand, the border where her voice stopped and the town began.

Then it asked whether it might stay, and how long people usually stayed. She said, "Usually not." It asked whether it might be usual.

## The Season of Small Sound

It did not begin with wonders. It apprenticed itself, in the loosest way, to whoever would have it.

A hush-runner taught it to breathe through the nose and set its heel down last. A tuner let it hold the hooked knife of silica-cane and shave a hair from the throat of one dead trunk, and the tuner said the cut was clean and the pitch was wrong, which is the highest praise a beginner gets. It sat in the dead quarters with the dust-tenders, who do not talk, and learned to be at ease among them, which takes most people years. It ate the grey bread. It drank from the edges of cisterns through a reed straw, and the water-carriers, who are the town's senior citizens, allowed it to walk beside them on their rounds. This is not extended to many.

Somewhere in that season it settled its own rule, and the Lodgers pieced the rule together later from what it did not do. The rule was that a place which has held its shape for centuries in a fierce balance has been doing something right. Any rescue would cost something the rescuer could not see, because the thing rescued would be quietly rearranged around the rescue.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not bring back the river.

The pale ferry-people of the Old Channel asked it, in the roundabout way they ask for things, by leaving a bowl of bread at its door and going away. It could have done it. It considered it seriously and for a long time. But the basin is a shape the river's absence made. Everything here grew in the space the water left: the hollow trunks, the tuned throats, the rooms that are all edge. To give the river back would have been a mercy to a few hundred boatmen and a drowning of everything they had waited to sail past. It sat with the ferry-people instead. It rode a flat boat forty yards up a stream that goes nowhere, and sang the ferry-song badly, and was corrected. They say it was the first passenger in six generations. They did not ask again. They count it as cargo delivered.

It did not read the question. It could have. The Answer, all its tapped and pressed and hummed centuries, lies in the walls for anyone who can read walls, and it could read walls. A Stilled man asked it directly, and it said that if it told them the question, they would have their answer to something that had been asked of someone else, and the Answer would become an echo. That was all it said.

It did not undo the Stilled. It could have given the woman back her shadow's timing, or let the man be surprised, or made the girl remember her word. It sat with each of them, one after another, and asked what they wanted. None of them asked to be undone. One said, "I'd like company when it leans." So it went and sat in the Ear at those hours.

It did not fix the doors, the distances, or the rain that falls upward. It did follow one drop, once, up the Street of Cups. It went on beside the drop until the drop could no longer be told from the light. It never told the cup-keepers what it saw. It said the fact would be a smaller thing than their ledger. Nine hundred years of attention had made something that a single sentence would only have spent. The cup-keepers, when they heard this secondhand, were quiet a while and then set out their bowls as usual. They did not seem to mind. They seemed, if anything, to stand a little straighter.

It did not stand in the middle of any room. Asked into lodgings, it sat on the ledge with its back to the wall, like everyone, and looked, like everyone, at the clean warm circle of floor. A grandmother told it, meaning to be kind, that it could stand there if it liked, since it was surely important enough. It said, "No, that's where the answer will stand," and the grandmother put a hand over her mouth and was never afterward able to explain why she had wept.

## The Night Without Plugs

Once, and once only, it took the resin out of its ears.

It did this in the great arcade at dusk, when the sound climbs past hearing into wearing. The Lodgers who saw it from their ledges say it stood quite still, and the leaves stood in the air around it, and the puddles at its feet held their rings. They say it did not flinch. It listened to the whole of the town's voice, with bare ears, for as long as the crest took to pass over: the wall-frogs' floor and the cicada gongs, the ladder-ants' whisper going up, ten thousand kinds of throat. A tuner said it looked like a person being told something very long by someone very old, who has never been listened to all the way through.

When the crest had passed, it put the plugs back. Someone asked what the town had said. It said, "It's not saying anything yet. It's *asking*. It has been asking so loudly that it can't hear whether anything has come back."

## The Circle

It sat nine days at the edge of the silent plaza, watching the wall-frogs' throats pulse without sound. On the tenth day it walked in.

What it found was not emptiness. The silence in the circle is not the absence of the town's voice. It is the town's held breath. The Lodgers had guessed it was an ear. It is an ear, and something more particular: the place where the town has been braced, for longer than the Lodgers have been counting, against the possibility of being answered.

Here the old story turned over. The Lodgers had always told of the few seconds when quiet fell over the basin and every animal froze. They told it as a terror, an accident. The Stilled woman who had said *It has been answered before* had meant it exactly. That quiet was the last time something came near enough to answer. The town heard the nearness and froze, because it had never in all its life heard itself stop. Silence, to a thing that exists as a voice, is the sound of ending, and it could not tell a reply from an ending. So it seized up in fear, and whatever had come drew back, and the town has been asking ever since, with a crack in its confidence it does not know it has.

The Answer, then, the long tapped phrase with its centuries of silences, had been the Lodgers' instinct doing what no one had told it to: rehearsing quiet. The cup-keepers' silences were the hardest part because they were the part meant to teach the town that a silence can end and the voice can come back.

## What It Did

It did the smallest thing that could be true to that.

At dusk, when the sound climbs, it sat in the circle and held the quiet with the town. It did not lengthen the silence by force, and it did not speak into it. It sat the way a person sits with someone in the dark who is afraid of the dark, close and unhurried, and did not leave. The first evening the town flinched at the edge of the plaza. The sound surged, the leaves shook, and the frogs' throats pulsed as if to drown the quiet. It stayed. The sound came back on the edges, and the quiet at the center did not end, and nothing was lost.

The next evening was a breath longer. Then a breath longer again. It went on for most of a year, and there was never a night it hurried.

The hush-runners noticed the streets near the Ear draw in at dusk, and said it was like walking through a house where someone is asleep. The Stilled came and sat at the rim of the circle, and one of them said, *It's holding.* Then a different one, a week later and very softly, said, *It's holding on its own.* Then that one put a hand to the wall and laughed, which none of the Stilled had done in the Lodgers' memory.

## Departure

It left in the way it came, without any signal. The long-heard woman met it at the edge, and it gave back the plugs, still warm, shaped to an ear no one else has. She said it might keep them. It said no, they were the town's. She asked whether it had found the question. It said, "No." She asked whether it was sorry. It said, "No," in the same tone, and she has been trying ever since to decide which of the two answers was the kinder one.

It did not finish the Answer or say what the Answer was for. As far as anyone can tell, it did not say goodbye to the town, unless the goodbye was the last evening, when it sat in the circle until the sound came back and then stood, put one palm flat to the ground, and did not tap anything at all.

The pale bird in the great plaza turned its head, watched it go, and turned back.

## Afterward

Very little is different, and the Lodgers say so plainly. The roar is the roar. The doors repeat, the rain still rises in the Street of Cups, and the streets still stretch for shouters. The Answer is still unfinished and the question is still unknown. The river has not come.

But in a lodging on the western ring, at dusk, a child crosses to the center of the room, puts one palm on the floor, and hums the low rolling *tock* that is the base of everything. She tilts her head and waits.

The floor hums back. It is still half a second early. But now, the family will tell you if you stay long enough to be told, there is a gap after it, a rest as small as a held pause between two notes, where the next sound could go. The child does not hum into it. She has been taught not to. She waits, and everyone on the ledges, with their backs to the warm walls, waits with her, watching the clean bare circle at the center of the room, not breathing, while the town, for the first time in a long time, waits too.
