# What Came to the Rim

It did not arrive so much as notice. The pit had been going on for a long time, and then the attention of something vast and unhurried settled on it, the way you become aware of a sound that has been there all along.

Whatever it was, it was used to continents and long weather. The well was the smallest thing that had ever held it: sixty paces, a hundred and fifty people, four thousand pigeons. In the first moment a thought came, the kind that comes unbidden to the very strong: *how easily this could be made better.*

It stayed at the brick ring among the thorn shrubs and did nothing for three days. That was the first decision, and perhaps the most important one.

## Reading

It learned to read the wall the way the children do. *Long-Course, two grandmothers back, mended by Thumb-Press after the split.* It learned that the Sevens would put a rosette anywhere, that a certain Wet-Corner corner had not cracked in ninety years, and that the dimpled bricks were a kind of signature, a family saying *here, here, I was here.* It understood that the wall was history, law, genealogy, and daily work all at once, and that it was legible only to those who were also mending it.

At the bottom, beneath the grey, it found the fused rubble that nobody had made on purpose. It knew that glass. It did not know the maker, but it knew the kind of heat. Something had once fallen here or been thrown, and the ground had answered by becoming a hollow. Nobody alive remembered. The first people had come, perhaps, to a wound, and had done the only thing a wound allows: laid a brick against it, then another. For centuries they had been sewing it shut, and they had made it sing.

It decided not to tell them. The knowledge would give them nothing to hold. They already had a story, that the well is an animal, slow, failing, loved, and wants to close. The power considered this and judged it more accurate than anything it could have said.

Then it listened to the chord. Near the cool terraces by the bats, among all the dead, it found a small high note that was very new. A child, last spring. It stayed there a long time. A wisdom that cannot grieve is only accounting, and this one could.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not bring water. The scraped gutters and the hand's width of dew each morning were labor, not romance, and it did not pretend hardship was noble. But the pit was a machine built for exactly this much water, and the terraces would slump under a river. The rain that came every few weeks was already the well's great feast, a drum and a fountain, and the power knew it could not improve on an occasion.

It did not teach writing. The wall was their writing, and a second script would slowly become a rival to the first. One of them would win, and it could not tell which. That was exactly the sort of uncertainty that calls for keeping your hands at your sides.

It did not appear in light. A voice in a pit of four thousand pigeons would get a hundred and fifty people on their knees, and kneeling people do not mend walls.

It did not touch the closing. Whether the well wanted to become a warm hill of sealed rooms was between the well and its people. Wanting to help does not mean you have been invited.

## What It Did

It did three small things and one larger one.

The first was on the ledges, and it was never a miracle. A boy of the Wet-Corners was reaching for an egg on a lip that had gone rotten with damp. The power moved a crumb of old plaster a finger's width, and the foot held. It kept a private count of these acts and the count stayed small, three in the first year. It caught only what skill and care could not have caught. Anything else would have taught them to stop caring, and their carefulness was one of the things that held the wall up.

The second was for the bats. It gave them one good year: cooler nights in the deep pots, thicker colonies, thinner mosquitoes over the basins. The fever season was shorter than anyone remembered. There was no one to write it down. The elders said only that the basins seemed kinder that year, and they credited the wall, which was correct.

The third was that it left the cistern alone, and it left the child's note where it was.

The larger thing took the longest, and it was a question.

It wanted to say something to the place, to the people and the slow animal they described, and it had only one language they would hear. It could have made a note no one could refuse, a chord like the inside of the sky, and the pit would have bowed to it. That would have been a command dressed as a gift. So it went the slow way. It took a lump of clay from the treading basin and a night, and in the morning there was a pot on the rim beside the brick ring. It was unfired and plain, with a beginner's neck, too long by a finger.

The kiln-keeper found it. The kiln-keepers sleep little and ask less. She turned it over for a long time, said to nobody that some makers are shy, and fired it.

On the next windy evening the whole family came to the ledges, as they do. The wind crossed the rim at the right angle. The pot spoke, and the pit winced.

It was only a small wince, an in-tune note sitting slightly wrong against the chord, but the silence after it was long. The power stood in that silence, unseen in the wind, and learned what every apprentice learns. The silence is neither cruel nor kind. It is only true.

The pot was carried down and given to the birds. Within the week a pigeon hen had stuffed its neck with grass, and it was quiet. The power found this was not a rejection. It was the gentlest verdict the place knew.

Then it listened again, for the reason the pot had clashed. The chord had a gap in it, a small high space near the bats where a child's note sat, and that gap was correct. The pit was not missing a note there. It was holding room. The first pot had tried to fill the space, and the second must not.

The second pot was a thumb narrower. This time the wind came, and the note that rose sat beside the child's and left the space open, like a person standing near someone who is grieving and not speaking. The pit did not wince. The old ones nodded, all but one, and that was fine.

In the morning a grey pigeon feather lay on the rim, pinned under a chip of brick, the way you leave something for someone shy.

## Afterward

It did not become the god of the well. It became one of its more recent voices, quiet, a little unsure of itself, an apprentice's note in a chord of centuries.

It made one promise, and only to itself. It would not end the silence when the silence came. The pigeons will go quiet without reason someday, as they sometimes do, and a hundred and fifty people will stop where they stand with a hand on the nearest wall. The power will not fix that. It will be one of those who stop, who put a palm to the brick and wait, and who trust that the well and the people will do what they have always done.

At dusk the swifts drop in their spiral, thousands of them, each finding its pot by feel. The roar rises and falls to the murmur, and somewhere a mason stands at a ledge with a palm flat on the warm brick, eyes shut, checking. The water is ticking. The wall is breathing. Among all the voices, faintly, there is one that is not the loudest or the best.

It fits. The mason's hand drops, and the mason goes to supper.
