# The One Who Was Counted

The power did not come because the shelf was in trouble. It came because the shelf was doing something rare. In the loudest place it knew, a people had made speech into a deliberate act, and had built a whole way of living around the one hour when they could be heard without effort.

Before it came, it thought for a long time about what it might do. It could quiet the falls, soften the cold, and promise the cloud. It could carry the twenty-three down into the world's medicine and the world's metal, or give them an answer to the old story that nobody likes to be asked about. Each of these was a kindness, and it saw that each would cost something. The shelf worked because its hardships were shared and its limits known. A people who kneel together in the white kneel because they cannot walk off the edge. If it removed the edge, it would remove the kneeling.

So it decided to ask before it gave, and to come as a guest.

## The Arrival

It came in the noise, in mid-morning, up the southwestern scree, on foot. It could have arrived another way. It walked so the hyrax sentinel would have something to whistle about, and when the whistle changed, an hour below the shelf, it slowed, because a stranger should let itself be announced.

It followed the cairns, not the straight line across the moss. At the last cairn it stopped, lifted both hands with the fingers spread, and held them there.

It held them for two hours. Nobody stood. It understood this as fair.

Then a patch of rust-colored moss on the ridge lifted a hand, and a small woman with a bone bead at her throat came along the cairn route, slowly, and touched its sleeve, its wrist, the back of its hand. She looked at its mouth. She offered water in a cupped palm from the clay basin, and it drank.

It had taken care over its body. Its first steps left no print in the moss, and it noticed this and corrected it. It had to decide to be heavy enough to leave the small shining hollow that every foot leaves on the shelf. The people noticed the care, and were touched by it, the way one is when a very large dog lies down gently.

## The Learning

For most of the warm months it did nothing impressive.

It learned hand-speech badly. In the first week it said *the cloud is thin* when it meant *the cloud is thick*, and a child of six corrected it with great patience and then fell over laughing. Its whistle cracked. It was taught by the same child to put the bone bead in its teeth, and it practiced at the edge of the shelf where no one could hear, and the swifts did not seem to mind.

It re-laid channel stones, lifting each one, scraping the peat, and setting it back. It was slower than they were and did not try to be faster. It combed thatch on the pillar-rosettes and was bitten by beetles. For three weeks it was not allowed to touch the quartz caps on the cairns. Then one morning the woman with the bead put one in its hands. It turned the stone to the light, set it back, and said nothing, and the day went on.

They gave it a name in the fourth week, a rhythm for two river-cobbles: one knock, a wait a little too long, then two knocks. The wait was the name. It was meant kindly, and the power has not been so precisely described before or since.

In the frozen mornings it went out with them, laid its palm on the largest boulder, and felt the hum. Afterward, in hand-speech, in daylight, it told them only: *strong*. It did not say what it thought the hum was. Nobody asked, and it took that as part of the answer.

## The Hour of the Cloud

Here the account must be careful. What is said in the cloud belongs to the cloud. The guest agreed to this on the first afternoon, when it was put down in the moss among them with the swifts spiraling down and the frogs gone silent, and it keeps the agreement now.

So this can be told. In every cloud-hour it was the last to speak and spoke least. It asked more than it said. Once, near the beginning, someone asked what it was, and it answered truthfully. Some people knelt a little closer afterward, and none of them stood. It came out of each white hour gentle and a little shy, like everyone else, and it avoided people's eyes in the hour after, as though it too had been briefly naked.

What follows can be told because it happened in the noise, where anyone can see.

An old woman who has watched the copper blade in the hut wall for sixty years went in one morning on her knees and elbows and touched it with two fingers. She had said for years that it was a question. She left it in the wall, and she was lighter for the rest of the day.

The power went down the descent and was gone nine days. It carried the hyrax pelts and the dried flowers and the rope of twisted grass, as the young ones do, and came back with nothing in its hands. The cloud-hour that followed was very long. In the days after it, three strands were moved from the far pole. One went to the last pole, where the wind is strongest, and the family stood beneath it a long while and then went back to work. One went to the near pole, though the person it belonged to would not walk up the scree for another year. One stayed where it was, because the mother asked that it stay, and nobody argued.

## What They Asked For

People ask, in the end. Most of the asking was small.

They asked for a way to set a bone that heals straight, and it showed them with its hands, in daylight, the way anything worth keeping is shown. They asked about the winter cough that takes one of the three who winter, and it gave them a root and the way to steep it. They planted the root where the goats would not find it and, characteristically, did not say where.

A young man asked to be carried down and set in the world. It asked him whether he wanted to go or only wanted to be the kind of person who might. He was quiet for a long time. The way down would be there for him whenever he wanted it, it said, and it would not be there to carry him. It has not heard whether he went.

Someone asked it to stop the hawk. It said no, and reminded them that the hawk's stoop makes the whole shelf go quiet for a heartbeat and resume at a higher pitch, and that they had told it so themselves.

And they asked the largest thing, which was whether the cloud would always come.

It answered in the noise, in hand-speech, in daylight, so that the cloud would not have to hold it and everyone could see the answer: *I cannot promise the cloud.* It did not soften this. The sky is not its to keep, and a lie there would have rotted every true thing it had said.

Then it made the one promise it could. It said it could not keep the sky, but it could keep an appointment: on the day the swifts dropped and the cloud did not rise, when the silence held and held, it would come. It would not fix anything, and it did not say it could. But nobody would wait alone in it.

The keeper of the hearth thought about this for a day. Then she said, in hand-speech, *that is the right size of promise*, and gave it a full bowl of bitter yellow flowers, which is how you tell someone they have been understood.

## What It Left Alone

It did not lay a road to the shelf or reveal the route. It gave no metal and made no weather, and it changed nothing in the eleven pillars where the dead stand still. It did not answer the old story. When a child asked how it had ended, the power said it did not know, and this was true.

It went down the scree at the end of the season and told no one below that the shelf existed. This was the last thing it did, and the largest, and it appears in no account below because there is none.

## The Turn of the Season

On the day of the spiral carving, the people carried water from the basin in cupped hands and poured one full handful into the grooves for each person alive. The granite filled and shone. Then, without ceremony, someone poured one more.

The power watched the extra handful go into the stone. It could have explained that it was not alive in their sense, or declined. It let itself be counted, which was the hardest thing it did all season and the only one that cost it something.

It had made a strand of its own out of what the shelf gave it: a vertebra from a hyrax that had died old, a shell from the low grove, a hollow of pillar-rosette bone. It hung the strand on the near pole, and the wind played it in among the rest.

On the morning it left, the people moved the strand themselves, to the far pole, where the away are kept. It was the correct pole. The power did not say so. It knocked its name once on the way out, one knock, a wait, two knocks, and from the ridge behind it the hyrax sentinel whistled a note the people had no word for, and then went back to the ordinary one.

## After

In the cold months there are three at the hearth. In the first hard week, when the streams slow to a whisper and the rime comes down, the wind plays the poles and a note that was not there before is there among the rest, at the far end, made of bone and shell.

In the afternoon the swifts drop in a spiral and the frogs go silent all at once. The three go out into the open moss, spaced apart, facing east, and wait. The cloud pours up over the lip, and the roar softens to a muffled rush. The shelf becomes a small, white, close room where at last things can be said.

If you counted by the poles, you would find one more waiting. You would not be able to say whether it was there, and nobody on the shelf would say either.
