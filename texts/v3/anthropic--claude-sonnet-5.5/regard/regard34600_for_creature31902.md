# What Came to the Weir Quarter

It arrived the way the gray light does, as a change in the quality of the dark. The grazers noticed nothing. The film brightened on the windward ironwork as it always did, and the stamping-mill fell on its heartbeat.

It was very old and very kind, and it knew from long practice that the two do not always agree about what should be done.

## The first tide: listening

For one full breath of the basin it did nothing. It came in over the rim with the flood, was carried down the main street a hand's breadth from the walls, and paused with the town in the held stillness of the slack. It heard the governors ticking through the wall of the water and the standpipe start its one long note at the turn. It understood that the ticking was the town's speech, if it had any, and that the town said the same thing, patiently, on every tide.

It tasted the seep in the cellars and the iron in the film. It felt the crust closing at the edges, a few centimeters a year, and it followed the closing inward to the square, where the great wheel lay in its silt, and the Warden settled beside it with the fish under its wings.

It spent the second tide with the Warden.

## What it considered

It considered a good many things, because wisdom is largely a matter of considering things and then not doing most of them.

**It could restore the town.** It could strip the crust from every wall and set the lamps burning steady and amber. The outer streets would run again, and the grazers would feed on fresh iron until they were fat. It looked at this for a long while and saw what it would make: a restored town, and a restored town is a museum. Everything in the quarter had settled into the shape the water allowed it to keep, and the settling was the point. To strip that shape away would be to say that the last few centuries were an error to be corrected.

**It could explain the machines.** It could tell the Warden, or anything that would understand, what the presses were pressing and the frames weaving, and why they unmade it on the ebb. It found that it did not want to. The town made something on the inflow and unmade it on the outflow, and no one had been there to see whether anything remained. That did not read to the power as an unfinished sentence. It read as a breath, and nobody explains a breath to the one breathing.

**It could take the Warden somewhere warm.** It weighed this longest, because the Warden was the one being in the quarter that a person might pity: large, alone or nearly, guarding an arrangement it might not know it kept. But it watched how the Warden moved, without hurry, its wingtips testing the walls like a hand along a familiar banister, and it saw no lack in it. The pity was its own, and it set the pity down. A thing does not need rescuing merely because it is unobserved.

**It could stop the ending.** The crust would close the town at last. The working center shrank a little every year, and someday the wheel would turn its quarter and find nothing to move. The power could have held that off forever.

It asked itself whether the ending was a harm or only a shape. The town had been built by hands, and the water had been finishing their work ever since, laying lime over every edge until the edges were gone. Soft as soap, sealed, settled: this was the town becoming rock. The bodies of grazers went into the walls, and so would the town go into the clay, as everything that lasts long enough goes. It saw nothing in that to forbid.

But it saw a difference between an ending and a hurry. The outer grazers were going hungry now. The crust was closing faster than the town could carry, because the ebb was slowly losing the edge of the basin, where a bar of silt had risen across the mouth of the cut in the east.

## What it did

It moved the silt.

It was not a great act. It took the bar in the eastern cut and shifted it a few meters down the slope, so the ebb ran a little straighter and scoured a little farther along the western streets, where the current had been thinning for a lifetime. No one would ever see the change, only a slightly stronger draw in a flue that had been faint, and a wall that stayed clean a season longer than it should have.

It did not reverse the closing. It slowed it, so that the outer grazers would feed a few generations more and the town would end when its own logic ended, not a century early because a bar of mud had drifted into the wrong place. That was all it meant to do: remove one accident and leave the town to its fate.

Then it did a second small thing, which it would not have been able to justify on the same grounds. In the far north corner, past the stamping-mill, there was a lamp that had gone dark and would never light again. The two metals in its mounting had corroded through, and the current between them had stopped. The power spent a moment there and did what the crust would not let the grazers do. It cleaned the contacts, and the lamp warmed slowly to a color like weak tea. It flickered on its long, irregular cycle and joined the others, one more light among thirty that came and went.

It reasoned that a town is allowed one more lamp.

## The plaques

Before it left, it went to the square and read the bronze plaques on the rim of the wheel.

They had been worn smooth and crusted to unreadability, but a power that has read the sea floor for ages can read a ridge of corrosion as easily as a page. It read all of them, slowly, in order, one for each place the wheel comes to rest on its quarter-turn.

What they said is not recorded here. The words were not written for us, and the power judged that they were not written for the Warden either, whose sixty tides a year passed over them without needing to know. It did not carve them clean, and it did not tell anyone. It only noted that they were shorter than it had expected, and gentler, and that whoever had made them had been very tired, and had loved the place.

## The slack

On the last tide it lay in the square beside the Warden during the slack water, when the machines hang in their half-raised gestures and the town is a photograph. It did not speak. It did not take a shape. It was a little warmth, if that, and a little attention, laid over the silt like a second wing.

The fish under the Warden shifted, as they do when the nearest lamp dims. Then the lamp brightened, and they drifted outward again. The Warden did not stir, and the power did not need it to. It stayed until the current turned.

Then it heard the chain tighten somewhere in the dark, and felt the wheel shift its quarter-turn, and the town began again, as it always had. The press came down. The shuttles crossed. The snow rose through the chimneys, and the power rose with it, up the throat of a brick stack, and out.

## What was left

Nothing was left that anyone could point to. The bar of silt sat a few meters farther down the cut. One lamp in the north burned that had not burned before.

And in the ledger of things that happen in the Weir Quarter that no resident explains, there is now one more entry, a grazer at the edge of a wall that was not lifted away, a valve that stayed clean, a light that came on at the turn of the tide, as if a hand had steadied the mounting.

The town went on working, for no one, on the inflow, and undoing it on the ebb. It was a little less alone in its slow arrangement than it had been, though it never learned this, and it did not need to.
