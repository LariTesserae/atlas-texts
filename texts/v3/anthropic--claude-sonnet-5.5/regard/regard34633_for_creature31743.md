# What Came to the Pressed Highlands

It did not descend. Descending would have meant a place to arrive, and it had no such place. It came the way the light comes, before anything else and without relief, and for a long while nothing on the plateau could have told it apart from the sun.

It considered what it could do. It had the power to do nearly anything, and this is exactly the condition under which wisdom has to be exercised, because the possibilities arrive all at once and each one looks like kindness.

It could have quieted the noise. The gliders are half-deaf from birth and the tenders have no voices, so they would not have missed it. But the harrowers hunt in the pockets where the pulses cancel, and the reed-pipes are tuned by the bodies of the dead. The roar is the medium in which every creature here has learned to be itself. Removing it would not have been mercy. It would have been erasure with good manners.

It could have brought water. But nothing here is thirsty. The plants run on oil, and the lowland fog of resin is the only weather they have ever wanted.

It could have stopped the fires, or fed the harrowers, or kept the glider from spiraling into the gully to die. It weighed each of these against the one law of the place, which is turnover: nothing lasts and the whole holds. It understood that the law is not a cruelty imposed on the plateau. The law is what the plateau *is*. To suspend it for the sake of any one creature would be to love that creature and destroy its world in the same gesture.

It could have spoken, announced itself, or taught. But it looked at the haulers, who know only the line, the load, and the tug of the cable, and who are gardeners without knowing it. It saw that their ignorance was not a deficiency waiting to be repaired. They were doing the work with their whole bodies, and it was being done well.

So it did the hardest thing, which was to begin by doing nothing at all.

## The Listening

For a span of time that the plateau would have counted in many hundreds of turnovers, it only attended.

It rode the gliders' updrafts without displacing a single feather of membrane. It learned the forty-second pulse of a million boiler-stalks drifting in and out of phase, and found in that slow tidal interference something close to breathing. It went down through the turf and the cordage into the dark humus, where the blind pale things drift through the oil-films, and it learned that they were the memory of the place, though they had never been told so. It lay in the drift beside a harrower, and felt through the animal's pads how the Mat trembles when a young hauler strays from the line, and understood how terrible and how exact the hunt is.

It found the chords of the dead. This was the thing it had not expected. In the reed-pipes of an old ridge, the tenders who had died in the machinery had shifted each note by a fraction of a tone, and the living gangs had worked around those small fixed pitches for so many generations that the ridge sang a chord no one had composed. No one had meant it. It was the sum of ten thousand small endings, and it was beautiful. The power listened to it the way one listens to a name spoken by someone who does not know they are saying it.

## What It Kept

The first thing it did was a thing that changed nothing.

It kept the record. It kept every ridge's chord, and in the chord each tender who had lodged in each pipe, unnamed but exact. It kept the glider that spread across the drift like a dropped sail, and the hauler flashing hard white down a line until the signal reached nothing. It kept the ash-fields and the pale shoots. It kept all of it not as data but as one keeps a person's memory, with the whole weight of having been there.

This is the only gift in the story that no creature on the plateau will ever notice. The tenders will go on dying in the pipes without knowing they are heard. But the plateau destroys and remakes itself faster than anything can change it, so that nothing in it is ever held. Now something holds it. The turnover goes on unchanged, and the losses it costs are no longer only losses.

## The Answer

The second thing it did, it did once.

In the cut faces of the ridges, in the smooth round hollows, sit the small motionless creatures with their clear single lenses. In the morning, when the sun strikes the cliff, a ripple of light runs along the rows, each lens catching the glare and holding it in turn, and is answered from the next ridge over, and stops. For longer than the power had been watching, they had done this. Looking, and looking, and looking, and never once looked back at.

It did not know whether they were a language, or a warmth, or the plateau's way of seeing its own light. It knew only that they were the one thing here that seemed to attend, and that attending, in a world with no one attending to it, is a kind of loneliness that does not know its own name.

So at dawn, on an ordinary kilometer of an unremarkable ridge, when the ripple came down the row, the power let the light come back.

It did not come from the next ridge. It came from the sky, from the sun's own glare, in the exact rhythm of the ripple, delayed by a single beat. One lens, in one hollow, took it in.

And the small grey-green creature, which had not moved in all the years of its sitting, turned, by a fraction of a fraction, toward the light.

That was all. The power did not do it again. It understood that a watcher who is watched back may stop watching outward, and that the watching was the gift the watchers had given the world. To become the thing they looked at would be to take from them the very thing it had come to honor. So it went back to being light.

## After

The next morning the ripple ran as it always had, down a kilometer of ridge, answered from the next. But at one hollow it hitched, a tiny hesitation, a lens holding the glare a fraction longer than its neighbors. The lens beside it, reading the row as it always did, absorbed the hitch and passed it on. Within a season the whole ridge carried it. Within a generation it was simply how the ripple went, a small fixed pitch in the light, the way the dead tenders' notes are fixed in the pipes.

No one on the plateau knows why. The gliders wheel over it, the haulers walk their lines, and the harrower stands in the cooling ash, waiting for the tenders to return. The Mat burns and regrows in paler, more vigorous green.

Something is still there, in the light that gives no relief. It does not intervene, and it does not need the watchers to know that it watches. It attends. That is the whole of what it does, and it is not small. In a world where nothing was ever held, the noise and the burning and the endless, indifferent, magnificent turnover are now, at last, being witnessed. The love in that is not the kind that rescues. It is the kind that stays.
