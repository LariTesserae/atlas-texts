# What Came to the Shelf

## The first season

It did not arrive so much as become noticed, and the grazers noticed first.

On a morning in the thaw, when the fog was still lying in the hollows, a whole flank of the herd turned its heads toward a stretch of empty gravel. It was not the bunching and turning they do before a tremor. It was slower, and the animals seemed only curious. Nothing was there that a herder could see. After a while the grazers lowered their heads and went back to the sedge, the way they do when a stranger has been judged harmless. The herder who saw it wrote in her ledger, under *small signs*: "Herd looked at nothing. Nothing was polite." She put her name and the date beside it.

The power stayed a full season and did nothing.

This was not indifference. It was the first requirement of the work. A place like this is a sentence that has been written over a very long time, and you do not add a word before you have read the whole thing. So it watched the two mornings arrive. It watched the city on the scarp-edge sweat out its water in the dark, and the easers walk their lines in pairs, and the children lay their palms on the walls and announce the weather. On the first clear Second Morning it watched a whole terrace step out into the light, and heard a thousand people say, quietly and separately, *again*, and *there you are.*

It found it had said the same thing itself, and had not decided to.

## What it considered, and put down

A power that can do things is tempted by a list, and this one was honest enough to write the list out.

It could stop the earthquakes. It could quiet the crust for good and take the ground's temper away. But the Shelf-folk do not live on the ground in spite of its temper. They live in conversation with it. Their lullabies keep its rhythm, their infants learn from its trembling, their whole grammar of care rests on the fact that the ground turns over in its sleep. A stilled world would spare them a danger and take away the way they know they are alive. It put that one down first, and without much regret.

It could explain the walls, the pylons, the bridges drawn in pencil on the air. But they hold that knowledge the way one holds a well-tempered horse, with a sense of moods, needs, and good signs, and it is a real kind of knowledge, hard-won and their own. Handing them the mechanism would turn tenants into students and the inheritance into homework. Some of them would spend their lives feeling behind. It put that one down.

It could bring back the earlier ones, or tell the Shelf-folk who they were and where they went. This was the hardest to put down, because the sadness in them is real and they do not hide it. But they carry that sadness with steadiness, and steadiness of that kind is not a wound to be closed. It is closer to good posture. Whoever had built the cities was *earlier*, and the people were *now*, and they had made a decent peace with the arrangement. A comfort delivered uninvited to people who have already made peace is only a kind of interruption.

It could make the cold gentler, feed the thin, mend the frostbitten fingers before they were lost. Here it hesitated longest, because this was suffering, and the power did not believe suffering was sacred. But the fingers were a tax the people spoke of without complaint, and the cold set the pace of the whole civilization, the low voices, the long pauses, the absence of hurry. To remove it was to write a different people. It decided that if anyone asked, it would help, gladly. No one had.

What remained after the list was very little. It was one thing, and it was not a gift. It was a job.

## The quiet in the east

The easers had been uneasy all year. They walked their lines more often and pressed their palms harder, and the city was careful with its jokes. Their fear had a name: the quiet year, when a fault stops slipping and starts to store.

From above the plateau, where the power could see the crust the way a person sees the back of their own hand, the trouble was plain and smaller than the fear. The east fault *was* going quiet. But that was because one strain-keeper, the fourth pylon on the third line past the last braids, three days' walk from the nearest city, had stopped. It had failed slowly and without a sound, and the hum in the neighboring pylons had adjusted around the gap, the way a crowd closes over a fallen person. The easers, feeling ordinary ground under their palms, could not tell a dead keeper from a quiet fault. To the hand, the two feel the same.

The power could have simply mended it, tonight, unseen. The ground would have resumed its small slips and the easers would have felt the year turn and thanked the weather.

It did not, because that would have made it a hidden hand on a place that had chosen, over many generations, to have none. The Shelf ran on reports with names and dates. It was a place where a strange bird call was written down and signed. To act there in secret was to break faith with the one thing they had built themselves.

So it went and sat at the rail.

## The terrace

The old woman was there in the amber hour with the flask between her palms, and the young man was reading her the ledger. She did not look up when the power came near. She did look at the herd, and the herd, below, had turned very slightly toward the terrace.

"That's twice now," she said to the young man. "They looked at nothing on the gravel in thaw. Now they're looking at the rail." Then, without turning: "Good evening. You may as well sit. My knees have opinions about the others."

The power sat. It had not decided on a shape and did not use one. She seemed to be talking to the place beside her, and that was enough.

"I've come to tell you something you have not asked," it said, "and to ask you something first. May I tell it?"

She thought about it for a long time, and the young man knew better than to fill the pause. Down on the flat a tarn shivered and went still.

"That depends," she said. "Is it a thing I'll be able to do something about with my hands?"

"Yes."

"Then tell it."

"The east line is not quiet. One keeper on it has died. The ground is only quiet where the keeper was."

She did not move for a moment. Then she let out a long breath, the one she lets out after a small tremor when she has waited for the sleeper to finish turning over. It came out shaking, and she let it.

"We couldn't tell," she said. "In the palm, they feel the same."

"I know."

"Forty years." She looked at her missing finger as if it had done something. "Can it be mended?"

"I can mend it. It is beyond what your hands can do. I don't think it should be mended without you, and I don't think it should be done in the dark."

"What would you do, then?"

"Whatever you allow. I could go alone, and it would be done by morning. Or I could go with whoever you choose, and do the part that's beyond you while they do the rest: lay hands, listen, say when. It would take longer. You would know it was done because you were there, and the ledger could say so honestly."

She turned then and looked at it, or at the place where it sat. The pale ring at the edge of her iris caught the last of the light.

"What do you want for it?"

"Nothing. I would like to be written down, if that is the custom. I gather it is."

She almost smiled. "It's the only custom we take seriously." She glanced at the young man. "Get your staff and your good boots. Ask Teren to come as the second pair. Tell him it's the east line and tell him the truth." To the power: "You'll walk. We don't ride to a dead keeper, and you'll want to know the ground on the way."

"You can't walk three days."

"No," she said. "I'll take the long vehicle to the last braids and I'll wait there with my flask, and I'll be very rude about it. Everyone will hear."

## The mending

They walked it, the young man and Teren and the power, three days out along the pale line. The power kept to their pace, which was slow and did not spend breath, and learned the ground the way they do, by the cold in the soles of its feet and the changing of the light on the wet scarps. It learned the lullaby too. Teren hummed it under his breath without knowing he did, and by the second night the power was humming it back.

At the fourth pylon on the third line, the young man laid his palm on the pale column and waited. Nothing hummed. The base was cold. The neighboring pylons, when he tried them, hummed a little too hard, straining to cover the gap.

"It's dead," he said. His voice was low, the way voices are here. "It isn't the ground. It's *this*."

"Yes," said the power. "Keep your hand there."

What it did next it did not explain, because it could not have explained it in any language the Shelf uses, and it did not pretend to. The pylon warmed under his palm, faintly at first, then evenly. The hum came up from below hearing, felt before it was heard, like the first shiver of a tarn. Then, a long way out across the gravel, the ground *slipped*, a few centimeters, the smallest slip imaginable, and every hair on the young man's arms lifted. Teren laughed out loud, once, and put his hand over his mouth as if he had been rude to a sleeping person.

The ground turned over. Then it did it again, a little farther off. It was easing.

"There," said the young man, without meaning to speak. It was the word they say to the light.

"There," said the power.

Before it left, it gave them one thing more, and it chose the thing carefully. It did not give them the mechanism. It gave them a way to tell: *a dead keeper is cold at the base and quiet ground is not.* Any easer could learn it in a morning. It fit their notation and asked nothing of their understanding. The rule was small enough to be theirs.

## The ledger

They wrote it down at the last braids, with the old woman looking over the young man's shoulder and correcting his spelling. Her tea was cold and she had been extremely rude about the wait.

*Third line east, fourth keeper: dead. Mended. Ground easing again, three slips at dusk, all small. Palm-test for the future: a dead keeper is cold at the base; quiet ground is not.*

*Mended with the help of a walker, no city, no season given. Polite. Hums the old lullaby a little flat.*

She read it over and nodded, and did not make him change the last line.

The power stayed on the terrace two more evenings. On the second she said, without looking at it, "You could tell me who made the bridges. I know you could. I'm not asking."

"I know."

"Let the children ask. Let it be a thing somebody wonders about. I think a place ought to have one or two of those left."

"I think so too," said the power, and meant it.

Then, because it seemed true and harmless and hers to keep or lose: "They were not gone because you failed. They were only earlier. It is not a thing you did."

She was quiet for a long time. Below them the herd settled in the lee of the scarp, breathing in clouds.

"Good," she said at last. "Go on."

It was gone before the windows came up amber, and the herd, which had been watching it for a season, did not look up when it left. The tarn near the road shivered once, gently, and the cranes lifted off it and flew south across the darkening flat. Under the wind the pylons hummed along all their lines, the east one too now, fourth in the row, a little louder than the rest, as if it had something to say.

Some nights afterward, when the fog fills the hollows and the world shrinks to a few meters of gravel and breath, something on the plateau hums the old lullaby a little flat. No one has ever found out what. The herders have entered it in their ledgers, with their names and the dates, under *small signs*, and beside it, in the same hand as the first entry: *Nothing is still being polite.*
