# What the Fog Kept

## The Arrival

It came in as fog, because fog was the only thing in Hush that was allowed to arrive without asking.

The Attention had turned toward the basin the way you turn toward a sound you cannot place. It heard the hum under the shriek and the drum-wave crossing the green. It heard a child asleep on a cistern lid, counting drops in her dreams. It was very old, and it had learned that its first impulse on seeing something small and lovely and fragile was nearly always the wrong one.

So it did what the rim demanded. It came at dusk and slid up the outer slope with the rest of the breath. It let the outermost stillwoods comb it first. It took nothing from them, and for a while it did nothing at all.

## What It Learned by Waiting

It stayed a season, then a vine's worth of seasons. It learned the drum-language, which was easy, and the hand-language, which was not. It learned that a word for drunk water and a word for family were the same word, and it sat with that for a long time.

It learned that the town was not poor. This was the first thing it had to unlearn. Every lowland instinct said: *here is a people who lack.* But the lack was the loom the whole cloth was woven on. Because water was scarce and shared, weeping was a gift, and a child's sleeping hand on a stone was the town's portrait. Because water was owned in small flat stones, a marriage was two lids on one cistern. The scarcity had been worked so thoroughly into love that the two could no longer be separated without tearing both.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not bring a river. It saw at once how easy it would be, one bright spring breaking from the quartzite and running clear down the terraces. And it saw what would follow. The lids would become stones. The gutters would become decoration. The word for family would slowly lose the water out of it, and in three generations the children would spit in the street without anyone looking at them.

It did not cure the deafness of the old. It watched a grandmother sit in her softer, deeper noise, hands moving in the dark, happy in it, and understood that this was not a wound. The town had made a mercy out of a limit, and it had no right to take the mercy back.

It did not give them metal, or writing, or medicine, or a better god. It did not tell them the Burning was probably a story. It did not correct the joke about the older women's hair.

Mostly it did not speak. The mountain had spent a hundred million years arriving at this shape, and the people had spent ten thousand years arriving at theirs. Between them they had reached an arrangement that needed no author, and the Attention had the humility, rare in powers, to know it was not one.

## What It Did

It did three things. Each was small enough to be mistaken for luck, because that was the only kind of help it trusted itself to give.

**The first was the spark.** In the dry heart of one year, a vent-cook's apprentice, tired and proud and twelve years old, carried a coal three steps too far toward the moss racks. It was the oldest mistake in Hush, and the one the whole town was built to prevent. The coal fell. A thread of orange ran into the dry hanging moss.

The fog came early that night, an hour before the drums expected it, thick and low and cold. The thread went out with a small hiss. The apprentice stood shaking in the dark. Everyone agreed later that the fog had been merciful. Nobody thought further than that, and the Attention was glad of it. It had not wanted to be thanked. It had wanted the mountain not to burn.

**The second was the descent.** The Attention had studied the law of exile with real grief, because it was the one cruelty in a gentle place. The condemned were walked to the rim and sent over it, down into cold wind and bare stone, told never to return. The town believed that this was a sentence of silence. In practice, most of the time, it was a sentence of death.

It did not touch the law, which was theirs. It touched the mountain. On the first shoulder below the rim, a fissure that had always run cold began to breathe faintly warm. Beneath a ledge a hollow opened that cut the wind. A hardy fog-moss took root there, and a fruiting vine that carried water in its stems. A little further down, the old trail firmed. It was never anything a person would call a road. But over the generations that followed, the exiled sometimes lived. They walked out into the lowlands, silent and shaken, and became a strange, humming sort of people in the valley towns. The Attention thought the law meant *go away from us*, and had never truly meant *die*, and it let the mountain say so.

**The third was for one person.**

## The Keeper

She was old, and she lay in a hammock at the rim among the combs with her eyes open. Her hearing had thinned to almost nothing above the low register, as everyone's did. That meant she could no longer hear the fog arrive leaf by leaf, the fine hiss the young keepers loved. She knew it now only through the skin, by the small cool change against her cheek, and she had been quietly afraid that this meant she was failing at the one thing she was for.

The Attention drew close to her, as close as fog can be to a person. It did not speak. It did not give her back her hearing. It arrived a little more slowly than usual, so that each leaf held its breath a moment longer and each drop fell a fraction later. She had time to feel the arrival with her whole body, from the first cool touch on her forehead to the slow gathering along the grooved trunks.

She lay still. Then she understood, without being able to say how, that she was being listened to. Fifty years of watching, unremarked, in a hammock, thinking of nothing but the water. Someone had noticed, and someone was keeping it.

She wept. She did it the way her people did, with her head turned so the tears would fall off the edge of the hammock, onto the moss bed below, where they would feed the ground. It was a good gift, quietly given. The Attention had never been thanked so exactly.

## The Vine

Once, only once, it went into the town.

It went for the flowering. The vine that opened every seventh year did so under a hundred lamps, and the whole town gathered beneath it with infants held up toward the blossoms. The oldest told the youngest what the last flowering had been like, and the youngest did not listen. They were staring at the flowers, or at each other, or at the food.

So the Attention listened for them. It took in every word the old ones said, the exact color of a vanished flowering, the name of a grandmother's goat, the year the fog failed and the year it came back doubled. It held these things where they would not be lost, and it did not tell anyone, because the town would keep telling them itself, badly and lovingly, as it always had. It only wanted the telling not to have been wasted.

## What Remained

It left as it came, up the slope in a long pale exhalation, at the hour when the drums were passing the last stroke of the night from log to log. A drum-warden, half asleep, struck her stroke exactly on time. She had never once missed it, and she did not miss it then.

Below the rim the fog thinned over the town and settled into its usual blanket. The geckos gathered at the lids. A child in the corner had fallen asleep with her chin on her knees and one hand resting on her family's stone. Nothing about her had been touched or improved. The cistern was a finger's width fuller than it had been at dusk, which was the way of the cisterns, and she would call it out in the morning.

The Attention thought that was the whole of what it had wanted: for the drops to keep falling and the child to keep counting, and for a place that had reached its shape without help to go on being that shape for a very long time. It had given three small things and asked for nothing, and it felt, as far as such a being can feel it, that this was the most it could rightly do.

Behind it the town kept its small orange lamps burning in the green, and the noise thinned to a hum, and Hush sang itself to sleep.
