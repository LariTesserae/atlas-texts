# What Was Done on the Chord Plateau

The power came up the escarpment the way the fog does, at dusk, over the lip. It was not seen, because nothing there had eyes for it. It moved into the plateau as attention moves into a room, and the first thing it noticed was that it could not be heard, and that this did not matter, because nothing on the plateau was waiting to be spoken to.

It was very old, and it was wise in the way that comes from having once been wrong at scale. It had mended things that were better broken, and it had comforted things into weakness. So it began the way it had learned to begin, by not beginning. It listened.

## What it listened to

It listened first to the low drone in the deep canyons, the note the goats lie down in. Then to the fifths and octaves of the mid-sized slots, then to the glassy whistle of the bored fins, and last to the grain of ten thousand small throats laid in the gaps like frost between stones. It took a long time to understand that the gaps were the point. The birds had not conquered the roar. They had found the places where the roar left room, and they lived there.

It listened to the goats' ears and the rodents' whistles, which it alone could hear, and which were mostly gossip and complaint. It listened to a fox stopped mid-stride for the length of a slow minute, and to the mites doing whatever mites do. It listened to the fog arriving.

Then it began, gently, to consider what it might do, and it held each idea up to the light and let the light judge it.

**It could bring rain.** The tarns were exactly full, and had been since before the cairns. More water would have meant more moss, more rodents, more foxes. It would have meant a different plateau, worse by being larger. The rock pools were bowl-sized because the bowls were sized to the fog. It let the idea go.

**It could soften the hunger.** The hawk hung in the roar and the birds dropped their pitch, and now and then the shift was too slow. The power looked at the fox with its foot lifted and the chick in the low hole, and understood that the fox and the chick were one animal, wearing two bodies in turn. It had made that mistake before. It let this go too, though not lightly.

**It could give the people a voice.** They spoke with hands and cords and flags because they had to, and by the second day they forgot that they had ever done otherwise. Here was something a benevolent hand could offer: a way across the roar, words carried whole. It considered this longest. But the silence was where they kept the cords, and the cords were where they kept the routes, and the routes were the shape of their memory. The people had built a whole grammar in the space the sound left them. Give them speech and they would shout, and shouting would be the beginning of not looking. It let this go last of all.

**It could resolve the chord.** This was the real temptation, and it took a while to see why. The chord was *almost* a scale. The slots were cut at regular spacings, and the notes fell nearly, not quite, into agreement. There was one interval among the mid-sized pipes, a little sour, a little unfinished, that leaned toward a resting place and never arrived. The power could have shaved it. A degree of tension in one fin, a grain of stone, and the whole plateau would have sounded, for the first time in ten thousand years, like something *finished*.

It held that thought in the wind for a long while. Then it saw what the unresolved interval was doing. It was the reason the goats' ears kept turning and the reason the birds kept fitting. It was the reason a visitor with a palm against the stone would stay, eyes closed, waiting for something the stone would not quite say. A resolved chord is a chord you can stop hearing. This one had never allowed that. The plateau was not still because it had arrived. It was still the way a held breath is still.

So the power left the sour note alone and, in leaving it alone, felt something it had not expected. It felt *accompanied*.

## Who it chose to attend

There was one being on the plateau that season the power came to keep near, and it was not the largest or the strangest. It was a woman, old, at the end of a line of cord-readers, who had come up with a party of eight because she knew it was her last climb and she did not intend to explain this to anyone.

She walked slowly and was waited for. The knots at her belt were more numerous than any others, and darker with handling. She could run a thumb along a strand and know a tarn she had never seen. But her hands had started to shake, and she had not taught the cords to anyone who would keep them, and she knew this. It sat in her like a stone in a shoe.

On the third day, while the others went after goats, she went alone into the wind-shadow of a certain fin, where the air was dead and the roar was only a pressure in the bones. She sat with her back to the rock. She was not praying, exactly. She was doing what her people did, which was listening with the body.

The power came and sat beside her. It could not speak, and had decided in advance not to try.

It did the one thing that the language of the place allowed. Through the stone at her back it sent a slow pulse, so quiet it barely disturbed the drone, and it set the pulse a little under the beat of her own heart, the way the goats' note sits under theirs. It did not change what she felt. It only let her be, for a while, *held*, in the way the herders say the goats are held. Her breath slowed to it. The shaking in her hands eased, not because it was cured but because there was nothing to be afraid of for a few minutes.

Then, and this was the only gift with any content in it, it let her feel that the cords were not lost. Not by telling her. There was nothing to tell. It let her notice what she already knew and had been too afraid to know: the cords were not only in her hands. They were in the way the young ones from her party watched her thumb, in the way they went quiet when she read, in the way one girl had, without being asked, started to tie the same knot at the end of her own cord. The teaching had been happening without her permission. It had only never been announced.

She sat a long time. When she stood, her knees hurt and her eyes were wet and she looked like someone who had put down a load. She did not know what had happened. She would say only, later, by hand, to the girl: *the rock was kind*.

## What it left

Before the party descended, the woman climbed to the crest of the nearest ridge and set her flat stone on the cairn, with the small motion of someone who has done it many times. The power watched, and understood that this, at last, was a thing it could do without disturbing anything.

When the party had gone, it went to the cairn. It found a flat stone in the scree, an ordinary one, the pale tuff, unremarkable, and set it on the pile among the others. It was cold and clean, with no lichen yet. It looked entirely new.

Then it went to the fin with the red hands. There were many prints, in a row at shoulder height, palms out, fingers spread. One of them, near the end, was smaller than the rest and slightly crooked, as if pressed by someone in a hurry or a child. Visitors had fitted their hands to it for a thousand years and always found it a little wrong.

The power laid its own attention into that print, as one lays a palm on a palm. It did not change the ochre or make anything visible. It only stayed there a moment, the way one stays with a friend's hand at a threshold. Then it withdrew.

That was all.

## After

The next dry season a party came up, and a girl among them, older now, stopped at the cairn. She put her hand on the newest flat stone and could not have said why. It looked like every other stone. But it was warm on the sunward side, and it was warm on the shaded side too, and she stood there with her palm on it until someone touched her elbow.

Later, at the fin, she fitted her hand to the small crooked print, and for the first time in the memory of her people it fit.

She did not tell anyone. She tied a knot at the end of her cord, one nobody had tied before, a small closed loop with a tail. It meant, as far as she could say: *someone was kind here, and went.*

The fog came up over the lip that dusk. The birds went silent by colonies. The goats turned their faces to the wind, and the chord went on, sour in its one place, unresolved, and, though it had never needed to be, a little more heard.
