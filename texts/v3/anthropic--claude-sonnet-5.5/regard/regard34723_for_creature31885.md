# The Attention

It does not arrive. Nothing so abrupt. It is more that the terrace, which has been one of a great many places, becomes a place that is being considered, the way a page changes when someone starts reading it.

The first sign is small. The afternoon hum in the rain-keys holds a moment longer than it should, as if the note had been overheard and was being politely let finish.

## What it could do

The power is old enough to see the whole list at once, and it sees it, and it is a long list.

It could stop the erosion. The rust-stain on the basalt is only a color now, but in some thousands of years it will be a gutter, then a channel, then a cleft, and the shelf will loosen, and the water table will drop by a hand, then by an arm. It could hold the stone at this hour of its youth forever.

It could end the century-long walk. It could gather the scattered residents, the ones somewhere on the slope in the cloud, and set them all down together in the egg-house at once, so the three bowls would be drunk from three mouths and nobody would need to be waited for.

It could wake the one beneath. It could ease the sleeper up into whatever waking it is capable of and ask it whether it is well.

It could give the frogs a second note.

It considers each of these the way you consider a stone you have picked up on a beach: turns it, feels its weight, and sets it back where it was. Every one of them would be a kindness by the measure of someone standing outside. Every one would also undo the terrace. This place is a circuit, and its beauty is that it is closed and slow and that everything in it is a bend in the same water. Fixing a single part would make the rest a museum.

So the power does the harder thing, which is to go small.

## The shape it takes

It takes the shape of a visitor, which the terrace already knows how to hold. It has no need of a body, but a body is how you tell a place that you are not there to take anything. It comes up the slope at dawn, through the standing mist, wet to the knees, and it lets the sundews turn toward it and does not step aside.

The moss keeps its prints one minute. Then two.

It walks the terrace behind the residents, not with them. It watches them pause at each key and change the pulse by one beat. It understands the work at once, and understands that it could not do it. It has never had to keep one measure of water in circulation across a life. It has always been the kind of thing that moves the whole sea or none of it. What these small pale walkers do, tending one warm loop with a lean of attention, is a craft it has no hands for, and it lets itself be humbled, which it does not often get to be.

## The asking

It stops at the key nearest the lip. It lays its palm on the pale surface, cool and dry, and feels the pulse: slow, huge, patient, breathing in a body the size of the mountain.

Then it does the one thing that is entirely its own. It doesn't speak, because the sleeper has no language for that. It matches the breath. It slows itself, beat by beat, down through the ranges of its own attention, until its awareness is beating at the sleeper's pace. One breath of the sleeper takes most of a day. The power stays for three of them.

In that time it asks, in the only way the question can be asked, by being present and leaving a gap for an answer: *Is this what you want? The sleep, the tending, the small warm shapes at your skin?*

The answer comes without words, as a change in the water. What the power understands is this. The sleeper is not held here. It chose the sleep, or grew into it so long ago that the difference has no meaning. The residents are not its keepers but its way of being partly awake, small hands it lets itself have so that it need not be entirely alone in the dark of the mountain. It is not lonely, exactly. It is a thing that dreams of company, and dreams it as a household, and the household is real.

There is one worry in it, deep down, like a stone in a well. It is afraid of the long slow thinning, the day when the stone softens and the shelf opens and the loop cannot close. It knows it is coming. It has always known.

## What it leaves alone

The power does not stop the erosion.

It does tell the truth, which is the only thing it lets itself add. It presses the fact into the pulse as gently as it can, the way you tell an old friend the weather: *thousands of years yet, not hundreds. Then the shelf will move. But the keys can be carried. A circuit is not a place. It is a habit of water, and habits can be taught to a new ground.* The sleeper does not answer, but the pulse changes by a beat, in the direction of relief. It had feared being told a lie more than being told an end.

The toppled key among the ferns, the one with the clean ring of moss around it, the power does not raise. It stands over it a long moment. Then it understands that this was not a fallen tool but a resident who lay down, one of the tall pale ones who finished, and that the plants keep their distance out of respect and not because they were commanded. It kneels, and it leaves it as it is.

## What it carries

There is one thing it can do that the residents cannot, because the residents are bound to the terrace and their long walks all curve back to it. It can go along the slope.

That night, after the third breath, it leaves the shelf and walks up into the cloud. It finds them the way you find a lit window in a dark valley: the far ones, the ones on the century walk, spaced out along the mountain like patient stitches. It finds the nearest. She is a column of mist standing motionless in the wind at the edge of a scree field, in the middle of a thought that began forty years ago.

It says nothing grand. It tells her: *the bowls are kept. Half is waiting. The terrace is well. You are expected, and you are not late.*

She does not thank it. She has no habit of thanks. But her mist tightens, in the way a face does when something long-held is finally confirmed, and she goes on walking a little more lightly. The power moves to the next one, and the next, until dawn.

## What it takes

At the end it comes back down through the mist and enters the egg-house, and the floor does what floors there do: it remembers. The power sits on the bench at the warm end. It lifts the nearest bowl, the one that will let it, and drinks half.

It leaves half.

It does not add a fourth bowl. A fourth would be a gift, and the gift would be a change, and the count of three is part of what the residents are. It does not leave a mark, a token, a blessing they will find and wonder over. Its prints on the floor are shallow and fade in a few minutes, like anyone's, and the older bare narrow ones rise faintly beside them, and for a while, before they sink, they walk together.

## What changes

Almost nothing. That is the point.

The afternoon rain falls a little more evenly. The frogs' chord, at dusk, resolves one shade cleaner, though no frog can say why. The next resident to pause at the key nearest the lip finds the pulse waiting for her one beat before she arrives, as though the sleeper has learned to lean toward the hand.

And the gray bird with the curved needle bill, who has never once looked at the rain-keys, who steps through the sundews with rehearsed courtesy and leaves without a glance, comes through that evening, as it does once in a great while. It stops at the lip. It turns its head, and for the first time looks directly at the place where the visitor last stood.

Then it bows, a small crooked dip of the neck. It has always known whose house this is. It is only now that it has seen someone else who knows.

It walks on. The mist rises out of the pans. Everyone is home.
