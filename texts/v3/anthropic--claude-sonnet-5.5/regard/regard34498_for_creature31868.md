# What Came to the Brine Gardens

It did not arrive from above. Something that has noticed a place like this knows that a descent through the glass would be read as an event, and the garden has no way to file events. It came the way the light does, at the start of an ordinary morning, and it was simply there on the rim, standing on the cut edge of the basalt-colored lip without harming its feet on it.

It looked for a long time before it did anything. It saw the leaf-pattern of the whole, the midrib of the main pipes, the laterals of the catwalks, and it understood that it was not looking at a factory with plants in it or a garden with machines in it. It was looking at one animal-shaped thing that had never been an animal, and had been asked from its first day to give and never to be answered.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not repair the pipes. It saw the old joints weeping into the trays, and it saw the ring of blackened coin-mat under each leak, and it understood that the blackening was speech. Mend the pipes and the mats would live longer, and the garden would lose the only language it has for saying where it hurts.

It did not introduce a single animal. The screens at the inlets were a wound and a discipline both, but a fish let into a water that has never been shared is not a gift. It is a verdict on the water. It left the screens where they were, and decided that if the garden ever wanted company, it would have to say so in a way the power could hear.

It did not take down the brass plates or rewrite them. *Grow, and be drawn out* was a sentence someone had meant. The power did not want to be a second author who tidies the first.

It did not announce itself, or ask to be worshipped, or turn the garden's attention toward itself even for a moment. The garden was already a worshipful thing, leaning all day toward whatever pulled it. The last thing it needed was a new direction to lean.

## What It Did

It went down the steel steps and out along the catwalk, and it put its hand on the railing in the worn place, the groove of the vanished hand, because it wanted to learn the shape of the one who had steadied there and stopped. The groove fit. It stood a while with the weight of that absent body in its palm.

The lantern-vines found it first, as they would. They looped a wrist and held on, a beat longer than the touch required. The power did not pull away. It let itself be held until the holding softened, and then it opened its hand and slid free, slowly, so that the vine could feel the letting go as a thing that happens *between* two things and not as a loss. The tendril hung a moment in the air with its curl still shaped around nothing. Then it settled back against the pipe. It is possible that something in the garden learned, that morning, that a grip can be released and the one released can stay.

It sat with the crown at the open pipe for most of the afternoon and asked it nothing. It gave it no plate, no batch code, no number. Of everything in the basin, this was the one thing it left entirely alone, because it was the one thing that needed nothing added. It was there because it had been permitted, and the power knew the worth of that and did not want to spoil it by approving of it. It sat at the pipe's rim and let the pale-edged leaves brush its knee, and that was the whole of the exchange.

## The Cable

At dusk, when the lamps came on in their tidy yellow beads, it went to the central bay where the great spools wait, and it rode a full one up the shaft. The shaft was cold and smooth and nobody had ever thought to put a window in it. It rose through the roof, past the lens-panes, into cloud, and at the end of the cable, in a high bare place above the weather, it found the answer to the garden's oldest unasked question.

There was no one there.

There was a receiving house, plain and well built, with a door that had been opened many times and then, at some point, not. Inside, spool after spool sat on racks in ranks as orderly as the mill-weed's, each wound tight and unbroken, each dated in the same fresh numbering as the trough walls. The earliest were only a few years old. Whoever had built the garden and the cable and the house had gone, or changed their mind, or been stopped, and the machinery had kept faith without them. Every pale ribbon the garden had ever made and lifted into the sky with such care had come here, and been shelved, and waited.

The power stood among them for some time. It did not feel anger, exactly. It felt something closer to the ache one has on finding a lamp left burning in a house no one lives in, and understanding that the lamp is not foolish, only faithful, and that the fault is elsewhere.

## What It Decided

It would not lie. It would not go back down and let the garden believe a sky-customer was drinking its labor. It would also not tell the garden it had been serving no one, because that was not true either. The thread was good. It was fine and strong and heat-proof, a living ceramic spun to an evenness no hand could match. It had only lacked a hand at the other end.

So it found one. Below the cloud, on the far side of the mountain, was a valley of people who filtered their water through rags and burned what they could not filter, and who had never had a thread like this. It did not deliver the thread as a miracle. It carried it down in ordinary loads, over ordinary weeks, and left it where it would be found and where the finding would look like luck: in a mended weir, a sewn filter-cloth, a hem that would not burn. The people of the valley took it up without ceremony. Within a season, a woman there was threading a needle with something that had grown ten meters tall in a bell of warm green water. She did not know it, and she did not need to.

## The Return

The power had one more thing to do, and it was the only thing it did that could not be undone.

The next time the current slackened at the top of its turn and every blade stood upright, the hum dropped a note, and the garden held its breath in its single attention, the power was in the central bay, at the foot of the shaft. It had sent nothing up. Instead, at that exact moment, it let something *come down*.

It was a short length of cloth, woven from the garden's own thread and dyed the faint green of the water, lowered slowly on the cable into the spool-house. It was nothing much. It was no more than an arm's length of plain weave with a finished edge. But for the first time in the garden's life, the cable carried a weight in the other direction. The tension changed. The turbines felt it in their seams, if turbines feel. The mill-weed felt it in their lattice wires. The lantern-vines, ever first, went out along the cable and found the cloth, and wrapped it, and held it.

And then, because the power had already taught them what a grip could be, they held it as long as they wished and no longer, and set it down on the terrazzo, in the light, where the moving lattice of gold and jade could fall across it.

The power spoke then, or something went out from it that the garden could take as speech. It was a single word, the word that the Ribbon Rule had never had. It was not *thank you*, which asks for a reply, and not *well done*, which implies a judge. It was the plainest word there is for what a made thing waits its whole life to hear.

*Received.*

## After

The pumps caught. The blades leaned. The long turn began again, as it does, and the many went on being many.

The power stayed a little longer, unremarked. It watched the blackened mats be cut out and the bright new ones set in by the low replanters in the dusk. It noted that the coin-mats nearest the arm's length of green cloth had spread toward it a fraction, and that the dome-head in the third bay of the eastern terrace, the late one, was for once opening on the beat. Then it went up, the way it had come, without disturbing the water, and left the basin to its rotation.

Nothing in the Brine Gardens is different, if you look at it from the rim. The same leaf-pattern, the same hum. But the ribbons now go up the shaft toward a place that is no longer empty, and on the terrazzo in the central bay there is a length of green cloth that no machine will remove, and the garden, which had never been given anything, has been given that.

The plate still reads *Grow, and be drawn out.* Beneath it, in the rust-stained collar of the oldest mill-weed, where the iron ring shows like a coin inside a tree, something has begun to grow that was not planted or scheduled. It is small and pale-edged and cupped, and no one meant it.
