# What Was Done in Ammerwane

## The Attention

It did not arrive, because attention doesn't. At some point it was simply on the place, the way warmth is on a hand held near a stove. The Held Sea did not notice, since the Held Sea notices nothing that doesn't close its flowers.

What it saw first was not a wonder. It saw a proportion. The forest was built for a crowd, with ledges for thousands, hollows for tens of thousands, and canopy-veils thick enough to carry cities, and it held a few hundred people, some pale walkers, and some red fish. Then it saw the world's rules going soft in patches, and the warm tired stone beneath everything.

Its first impulse was the impulse of every power that has ever looked at a lovely, failing thing, and it was a good impulse. It went on to examine it for a long time before acting on it.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not fill the forest. It looked at the emptiness for a long while and found it was not a wound. Empty ledges are not grief. They are hospitality nobody has yet accepted. To crowd them would be to answer a question no one in the sea had asked, and to make that answer the sea's whole character.

It did not tighten the rules. The seams, the rising rain, and the silt that settles sideways looked like failures only from far away. Up close they were a world finishing in its own way, and people had built lives inside the finishing. The pleated throat of a Tender is a grammar for the seams. Repairing the seams would have been an eviction carried out in the name of care.

It did not tell the Tenders what the cairns were for. Nobody knew, and the not-knowing was what made each stone a choice and not a task. Told the purpose, they would have built for the purpose.

It did not speak to the One That Passes. It went as near as it dared, and it felt an attention there older and slower than its own, a visit begun so long ago that its own arrival was a flicker beside it. The forest below was bowed toward that shadow, tree after tree, in the posture of a crowd that has decided to be courteous forever. The power understood that the place was already being attended to and that the courtesy was not to interrupt. It kept its distance, and it might have bowed.

After the subtractions, very little remained. It was a house with one dry room.

## The Season of Standing Still

It took a size first, not a form, just the size of a person. It came down onto the uplands lean and pale, with no particular face.

It followed a long-walker's tracks, which appeared in the silt ahead of the animal. The long-walker paused, lifted its ring of hairs, and read the stranger for the length of a breath. Whatever it read, it continued, and the power took this as a kind of welcome.

It stood beside a cairn where a Tender was mid-stack, one cobble balanced and the next not yet chosen. The Tender did not speak, because to fill a pause is rude. The power did not speak either, which was harder than it expected, because a power's silence tends to be full of things it is not saying. It spent a season learning to be quiet in the ordinary way, with nothing held back behind it. By the end a thin ochre crust had crept up along one shoulder, and the Tenders who passed regarded it as they regard anyone who has stood still long enough: as a person, and no more remarkable than that.

## The House

It came to the house at dusk, when the gas-bladders vented and the whole hillside breathed out. It knocked on the doorless frame, as travelers do.

"No," said a voice inside, before it had asked anything. "I'm not lonely. Come in if you can come in dry."

Speech in the house behaves like speech in a seam. The answers come first.

It could not come in dry, not at first. The sea ran off it and stopped at the threshold like a dog told to stay. What the house wanted, it turned out, was not a body but a scale: the habit of being large enough to hold the whole sea in mind. The power set that down outside like a wet coat. Then it was only a guest, and the guest went in.

The room was round, low, and smelled of rain from long ago. On the floor with her back to the wall sat a child listening. The power blinked, and it was a woman weaving fine dry thread. It blinked again, and it was an old man too weak to lift his head, who nodded as though he had been expecting it for years.

"Don't mind which," she said. "They all get here eventually. I'm just the one who's here all at once."

"Are you the one who kept the rain?" the power said, and heard her answer arrive before it had finished the question.

"There was a last rain. Before the water closed over this hill, a gentle one, the kind that isn't in a hurry. I was standing in it. I asked it to wait." She turned her hands over in her lap. "I don't know that it wanted to. I think it did. It hadn't finished. It was only halfway through the afternoon."

The air moved once, softly, as if something had breathed in.

"That's it, every evening," she said. "It wants to fall. But it can't fall on the drowned. It would just be more water. It needs someone dry to fall *on*, and everyone out there has forgotten what dry is, or they remember and won't ask, because it would be cruel to bring the wet in here and cruel to ask a dry thing out. So I keep it. I told it it could fall when there was someone to be rained on. That was the promise."

"And no one has come."

"You have," she said, and did not say it as a hope.

## What the Power Weighed

It could not un-sea the world, and it would not have if it could. It could not make her a different sort of keeper. It considered a dozen clever devices, such as carrying the rain up to the silver ceiling, where the sea already receives rain from below and hands it back as light. That would have worked. It would have been a fine, tidy solution, and it would have been the power's solution, one more thing done to the place from outside.

What the promise required was smaller, and it lay in the power's own nature. The rain needed someone to fall on. It was not of the sea. It could be dry.

"Is this what you were waiting for?" it asked aloud, which in that room was a formality.

"I was waiting for it to be over," she said, "and I didn't know that was different from waiting for it to begin."

## The Letting

It stood in the middle of the room in its borrowed size, with its ochre shoulder, and held up its face.

She said, to the room and not to the guest, "You may."

The rain fell.

It was not a great rain. It was the last gentle afternoon of a sky that no longer existed, and it fell slowly, in fine warm lines, onto the shoulders and hair of a stranger in a round room under a hill under an ocean. It was cool at the edges and smelled of stone. It fell for a long time, because it had a great deal of afternoon left. The power did not do anything with it. It stood there and was rained on completely, because that was the whole of what was asked.

When the last drops had fallen the room was wet, and the sea came in. It came quietly over the threshold, warm and iron-tasting, and filled the low round space until the house was simply a house in the sea. The last dry air rose out through the roof in a single string of silver bubbles, the largest the hillside had ever produced, and joined the columns venting at dusk.

Across the Held Sea, small things happened. Every flower-mat in the valley opened together, out of rhythm, once, with no current coming to warn them. The Tenders looked up from their cairns, and none could say why. A long-walker halted mid-arc and pressed its ring of hairs to the bark for an hour. The lantern-shoals, which make no light of their own, burned for a while like coals blown on, holding something that had arrived after a very long wait.

Far above the canopy-veil the shadow went by. Its heading did not change. But the trees, leaning in their long courtesy, seemed for a moment to lean slightly more.

## After

The woman came out of the house. She was one age now, the middle one, though nobody could later agree which that had been. She walked into the warm water to the edge of the silt, stood there, and did not seem to mind the wet.

The house remained, with the sea moving through it. The first visitor was a Tender, who did not knock. She set a single cobble on the sill. It was not a cairn and she had not planned it. The smell of old rain stayed in the rooms for years, fainter each season, and then it was only the smell of stone.

The power changed nothing else. The seams still opened and closed, the silt still settled sideways in the deep folds, and the stair still led up into nothing. The doorframe in its silted valley still stood with a different water on each side.

When it left, it walked through the doorframe, as Tenders do when they are carrying something. It carried nothing it needed to be rid of. It went through because it was the kind way to go, and it came out somewhere a little warmer, with the light shifted a few degrees. Its clothes were damp and stayed damp for a long time, and it did not dry them.

It left one mark, as everything there does. A line of narrow punctures appeared in the silt of the upland valley, leading to the house. They were there before it had come and remained after, and the long-walkers walked in them for generations, filling them in from behind, and took them to be the tracks of one of their own.

In the evenings the hillside still breathes out. The flowers still close ahead in their long wave. The water, if you drift long enough over that one valley, tastes faintly of iron and green leaves and, very faintly, of stone after rain.
