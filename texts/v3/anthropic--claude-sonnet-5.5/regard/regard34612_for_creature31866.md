# What Came to Ossel Tarn

It arrived the way weather does, as a change in the quality of attention, and nothing announced it. The drummer-fish did not miss a beat. The shrimp went on crackling in the moss. If anything was noticed, it was by the trout called *Late Again*, who tilted a fin at an empty patch of water, as she might have for a child, and then went on toward the court, late as ever.

The power was old, and what it had chiefly learned in its age was what not to do. It had seen many small worlds ruined by generosity, and so it began with the one thing it could do that broke nothing. It listened.

## The Listening

It listened through a whole winter, beneath the lid of ice, when the town lay slow and murmuring around the bases of its walls. It learned the hum-and-click of the language, then the hand-shapes, then the few things the reeds can say. It learned that the stroke meaning *I am here* is made with the soft mallet, on the third reed, twice, and that it means nothing more than that. It learned it was the most common stroke in the basin and the one people would least willingly do without.

It read the walls. It ran its attention up the cords the way the elders ran their hands, from the dark founders' knots to the bright new ones, and found that it could read them. Four generations of a house became a story in which nothing was ever said outright and everything was recorded. A flood of silt. A bad winter. A lost child found. A marriage, tied in two colors that thereafter ran together for the length of the cord.

Then it went down to the deepest hollow and sat, in whatever way such a thing sits, among the nine Listeners against the trunk of the bell-tree. It had come, it admitted to itself, wanting to know what the tree was answering. The town wondered, and it wondered too.

It never found out, and this was the first thing it learned about the place. The humming rose from three hundred throats and the tree answered. But when the power attended closely, as closely as it could, it could not find which came first. The town hummed because the tree had always answered, and the tree answered because the town had always hummed. It was a ring with no seam. A lesser power would have gone looking for the seam with a knife.

It left the ring exactly as it was. It considered this the most useful thing it did that season, and no one ever knew.

## The One Who Was Not Fed

The power was drawn to the whole town, but in the way a hand is drawn to one particular string in a harp, it kept returning to a young gardener named Ilmet.

She was nineteen, or would be at the next knot, and she was good with her hands in a way that made the Pruners uneasy. She could tie a lattice-joint that would not slip in forty years. But when she trained a shoot she did not always leave the one that went with the light. She left the one that went *sideways*, in a slow turn, and the shoot became a spiral where the pattern called for a plane. Twice her aunt, a Pruner with a voice like a struck reed, had shaded it. Twice Ilmet had quietly moved it a few strokes into a glade at the edge of her house's ring, where the current ran fast and the neighbors were few.

By the end of that autumn she was tending one small, wrong, beautiful thing in a glade, and nobody had said a word. Nobody needed to. The town had a long practice of not feeding what it did not know how to use, and Ilmet knew it. She hung sometimes at the shaded edge of the court, where the tied-off cords were, and looked at them for a long time. She did not touch them.

The power watched her do this through the whole dark of the ice, and it came to know her the way one knows a person by the weight of their silences.

## What It Considered

It considered many things, because it was wise and because wisdom is mostly the long sorting of things not to do.

It could have told the town that the pattern was too hard on the crooked shoots. But the pattern had built the lattice, and the lattice was the houses, and the houses were four generations of people staying warm. The rule was harsh at its edges because it was sound at its center. A voice from nowhere saying *be gentler* would not have made the town gentler. It would have made the town afraid of the voice.

It could have shown Ilmet the world above and told her what she was. But she was nineteen, and nothing in her needed to be told what she was. She needed room to find out.

It could have made her spiral grow strong and fill the glade, so that it proved itself. But a shoot that is made to prove itself has been fed by the wrong hand, and the town would have felt the wrongness, as one does in a joint tied by a stranger.

So it did smaller things. There was a great deal of ice that winter, but the lid cracked in one place, a thin seam no wider than a hand, directly above a glade at the edge of a house's ring. For eleven days a single pale shaft of light came down through the gloom and lay on a wrong-growing shoot. The shoot lived. Ilmet came out on the third day, saw the light, and hung a long time without moving, the way her people hang when they are reading the current. She never said what she read. She tied a small cord around the stem, not a promise, only a mark, and went home.

In spring, when the ice groaned and cracked and the town rose to the shallows to listen, the spiral was alive. Some weeks later the Pruners gathered in the court to argue about which lanes to close, and, as they do, they shouted at each other for three days. On the third, a very old man who had said nothing, and who was nearly deaf, pointed toward the glade and said only, "That one wants to hold the ice." Then he said no more.

The power understood at once that he was right. The spiral's turn was a better shape for bearing weight from above. If the lid had been heavier that year, it would have been the only thing standing in that quarter. The pattern had never accounted for it because the pattern had never met it. The Pruners were quiet for a while. Then someone struck a reed, twice, softly. The power was not sure who.

## The Cords at the Edge

That left the other matter, the long cords hung in the shade at the edge of the court, tied off to nothing.

The power had looked, in its way, beyond the rim. It had gone up through the pewter and found the hard bright world, and among the stones and thin grass on the far shore it had found what the town half-believed and never asked. Two of the ones who had gone over the rim were alive, old now, squinting, walking clumsily, breathing air with a labor that had never stopped hurting. Three others had not made it. The two lived in a shelter of piled rock and dried stems, a poor thing, but tended. They talked to each other in hum and click, in a shallow dry voice that carried nowhere. And every winter, one of them, a man, took a length of twisted plant fiber and tied a knot in it, and let the knot lie. Each knot said the same thing in the only language he had left for it.

The power weighed this for a long while. It could not say whether returning that knot was a kindness or a lure. It asked itself what would happen to a town that learned there was a far side to the rim. It asked what would happen to a town that did not, and went on hanging cords that ended in nothing. It could not answer either question. It did know that the town had, in its own oldest stories, a place beyond the ceiling, and that nobody had been forbidden to look. It concluded that it would not be revealing anything. It would only be answering a question the cords had been asking for a very long time.

It carried the knot down. It did not carry a message, and it did not carry an explanation. It carried a single length of fiber, which the man had tied in the way of his old house, with the twist and the double loop his mother's family used. In the last dark before the spring thaw it laid this cord among the ones at the edge of the court, where the current would move it a little, and it fixed the loose end not to nothing but to the end of another cord that had hung there a generation. The cord that had been tied off to nothing was now tied to something.

It made no other change. It did not make the knot glow or the water ring. It said nothing at all, and it hoped that saying nothing was the gift.

The first person to find it was a child, who reached up to touch it because it was new. The child called her mother, and her mother called an elder, and the elder ran a hand along the fiber, up from the frayed end, in the dim light, and stopped. The cord was old, and it had been tied by someone who knew the house's twist. And its one long knot said what the reeds say most often.

*I am here.*

Nobody made a sound for a while. Then the drummer-fish, who do not care about cords, began their evening pulse, and the clicking shrimp in the moss above the court, which had been quiet all afternoon, rose slowly into a dry, fine, general crackle.

## After

The power did not stay to see what the town made of it. It had learned that watching too long is a kind of pressure, like a hand held over a seedling. But it went back once, the following autumn, in the last of the violet light.

Ilmet was in the court among the gathered, humming the note of the bell-tree with three hundred others. Her hair was in a braid of work, and around one wrist she wore a small twisted cord. Nobody had asked what it was. Beside her, her aunt hung with her eyes shut, humming lower than she used to, and rather flat. Neither of them had said a thing to the other about the spiral in the glade or the new cord at the edge. That would come, or it wouldn't. The town would see to it, slowly, at the speed of a hand turning.

Far above, at the rim, someone had been standing a long time. The power could not tell who, and it was not its business. Whether they went or stayed, someone would tie a knot for it.

The reeds rang the long evening note. Below them, in the dark of the deepest hollow, the bell-tree answered. The power listened for the seam again, out of habit, and did not find it, and was glad.

It gave the place the only thing that had ever been fully its own to give, which was to leave it whole. The light thinned, the snails brightened along the lanes, and the water held a single sound, cold and green and loud, for as long as the day lasted.
