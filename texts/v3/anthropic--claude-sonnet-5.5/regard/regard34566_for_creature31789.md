# What Came Down the Stair

It came down slowly, because arriving quickly is a kind of shouting.

The handrail had stopped being cold, and it noticed that first. It was not a power that took a form when it visited. It took a weight, a pace, a way of setting a foot on steel. The grating rang under it, and the Field read it as it read everyone: a stride, a slight hesitation on the third step of each flight, a habit of stopping to listen. Somewhere on the second terrace a tender said a name into the dark, half a question, and the name was not one the visitor had chosen. It let the name stand. It was as good as any.

## What It Could Have Done

It had the power to do a great many things, and it went through them first, the way a doctor who loves a patient goes through the things she will not do.

It could seal the concrete. The lime would stop blooming and the rust would stop weeping from the bolts. The gutters would stay crisp and clean, and the walls would go on being months old for a thousand years.

It did not, because the bloom is how the place is learning to be a place. A wall that never weeps has never been lived against. The tenders read the lime by touch. They chalk their knees on it, and it has become their handwriting. A Field that could not fall down would have no one keeping it up. The vigilance was the thing worth preserving, and it lived in the danger.

It could have told them who rings the bell. It knew, or it could have known. Perhaps it did know, and chose not to look too closely at what it knew.

It did not tell them, because forty people each trusting that someone else is on the rope is a rare structure, and it holds a weight. Each tender pulls anyway, to be safe. That is faith and diligence in one gesture, and the answer would have spoiled both.

It could have drained the cistern and silenced the note. It could have gone down the shaft, found the underwrights, and told them to rest, that the water would not be beaten and they had built well enough.

It did none of this, and the reason was the hardest to bear. The builders below and the tenders above were doing the same thing from opposite ends. Each was holding a place open against its own dissolution, and neither had ever been told it was enough. But to be told that would end the labor, and the labor was the only form their love had. So it left the shaft alone. It lay down once on the iron of the ring road, put its ear to the plate, and listened to the ratchet and the chain. It said one word into the metal, which was not an instruction. The riveting missed a beat, and then went on, a little less afraid.

## The East Wall

What it came for was under the blue-white lights.

It had followed the smell of cut cress and cold iron, and it had followed a thumbprint pressed deep in dark soil with the whorls still legible. Somebody lived here whom nobody had ever seen, feeding thirty or sixty people who each thought it was someone else, and asking for nothing. The tin cup sat on the rack's end. A spoon lay beside it, cleaned.

The visitor stood at the trays and waited. It did not hide. It simply stood still for so long that stillness became a kind of invitation.

The gardener came late.

It came the way the Field's sounds come, after itself. First there was the wet drag on the slab, the hand's-width line, and only then the thing that made it. The visitor turned its head toward the sound and saw that the sound had already gone by. What arrived was long and low and the color of the slabs, with a great many small hands or one. One of them held a narrow blade. One had a thumb.

They regarded each other, and the visitor understood.

The gardener was the Late One. It had been born of the offset, the small delay a room this size puts between a noise and its source. It had grown up in the gap between a footfall and the foot, and that gap was all it knew of the world. Wherever it was, it had just left. Every time it reached the place where the tenders had stood, they had already gone, and the shining line it left behind looked like menace to them. It had taken the only course open to something that could never arrive in time to be met. It had cut cress on the diagonal and left it in the trays, and it had gone on doing so for eight months without once being present when the cress was eaten.

The gutters had been cut a hand's width because the underwrights, who never came up, had known it was coming. They had made room for it in the concrete before it existed. This was the only kindness anyone had done the gardener on purpose, and the gardener had never known.

The visitor did not say any of this. It sat down on the wet slab beside the racks, which was more than the gardener had ever expected of anyone, and for a while the two of them tended the cress together.

## What It Actually Did

There were three things, all small, and it did them with care.

**First,** the eight seconds.

When the three pumps of the fourth terrace drift into phase and the Field gathers itself into one heartbeat, every tender stops, mid-step, mid-word, with a tool half raised. The visitor waited for the next such beat and, at its peak, held it. It did not stop time. It only leaned very slightly on the moment, the way you steady a cup someone else is carrying. Eight seconds became nearly forty.

For those seconds the offset closed. Sound and source coincided. The Late One stood at the blue-lit racks with no lag between the thing and its noise, present, on time, for the first time in its life. Every tender, stopped where they stood, turned their amber lamps toward the east wall and saw it, and it had not gone anywhere.

It was long and low and strange, and it was holding a spoon.

No one screamed, since they were all midway through being still. Nobody was told what to say. The visitor did not put a word in anyone's mouth. It only made a silence long enough that somebody had to fill it, and it stood back, as far as it could, from whatever they would fill it with.

It was Two-Tap, the always tired one, standing in a dry lane where sound carries and where, by the Field's own rule, whatever is said still counts. He said, hoarse:

"It's bitter."

A pause. The pumps began to unravel toward their separate cycles.

"Thank you."

The visitor did not stay to hear whether anyone else spoke. It had done what it came to do, which was to widen a silence by a hair. What grew in it was theirs, and it went to look at something else.

**Second,** the crack.

It could not make the concrete last. Nothing that loves a place should promise it that. But the tenders were walking the ringing steel listening for the place where the Field would first give way, and that watchfulness was a kind of ache. The visitor found, on the fourth terrace, where the beats drift together and apart, the seam that would go first. It did not mend the seam. It tuned it. When that seam begins to fail, it will change pitch a full minute before anything else changes, a clear rising note in the canal's voice, unmistakable to anyone who has learned to read water by ear.

The Field will fall down, as fields do, but it will announce the first crack. Since the tenders cannot be spared the grief, they will at least be given time to stand at the lip and hear it coming.

**Third,** the pit.

It went last to the tiled pit, where the water stays flat when everything else trembles. It did not drop a grain. It stood at the lip, next to whatever lives there, and both were quiet for a long time. Whatever was listening from below knew what stood above, and the visitor knew it did, and nothing was asked.

In the reflection, if you counted, there was one more lamp than in the roof. The visitor looked at that lamp for a while. Then it left the difference exactly as it was, because it was not an error. It was a lamp not yet hung, in a roof not yet finished, and the water had been kind enough to show it.

## The Stair

It climbed as it had come down, heard the whole way.

Halfway up it passed the bell tower. The rope was already moving when it reached the base, swinging slowly with no hand on it. The visitor stopped and looked for the ringer, and it could find no one. It had the power to find them, and it chose not to use it. It was not the one who rang the bell, and it did not know who was. It found that restful. Even a benevolent power needs to be reminded, now and then, that not everything held together in the world is held by it.

It pulled the rope once, to be safe.

The clang went out over the terraces, and the sluices began to open. Far below, forty or so pale figures moved along the low places ahead of the flood, calling in flat carrying voices. At the east wall, under the blue-white lights, a long low shape set a full tin cup on the rack's end and, for the first time, did not leave.

At the top of the stair the handrail was cold again. The visitor took that as it was meant, as the ordinary temperature of a world that had gone on without it.
