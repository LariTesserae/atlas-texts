# What Was Done

The attention arrived the way fog does on the pans, without a moment anyone could point to. It rested on the plateau, on the columns of steam leaning at their separate angles, and on the districts strung across the moor like lit windows on a dark shore. It looked, as it always did, for the wound.

It found very little.

## What it did not do

It did not quiet the roar. It considered this longest, because the roar was the largest thing on the plateau and it had learned that the largest thing is usually the wrong one to fix. It listened until it understood that the noise was not an affliction the people endured but a room they had furnished. Take the noise away and the stair-hum, the cup on the sill, the pitch each person carried from birth, and the Still Rooms, which are precious only because everything else is loud, would all go with it. What remained would be a quiet no one there knew how to live in.

It did not cure the deaf. There was nothing to cure. It noted this with something like relief, since it did not often find a place that had already done the harder thing and made the difference ordinary.

It did not stop the Throats from widening, or save the districts from their slow walk across the moor. The walking was the city's way of loving its own stones. A city that could not leave would not have learned to carry.

And it did not open the towers. It stood a long time on the moor beside the nearest one, in the wet season, while the tower lagged a moment behind the falling pitch of the Throat. It understood what it was standing next to. It did not lift a hand to the wall for many days.

## What it did

It came up on the rail with the lowland salt, in a body that could pass for a tired engineer's. The plugs in its ears were a problem it solved honestly. It did not need them, but it went to a wax-maker in the first district and asked for a pair, and the wax-maker, seeing a stranger who wanted to choose, laughed with her whole face and made them the color of nothing, undyed, since it had no trade yet.

It learned the language the way a child does, badly and in public. Its first weeks were spent with a flat face, and people edged away, sure it was ill or lying. Then a woman at a loom hall took its cheeks in both hands and moved them, gently, into the shape of a question. After that it was understood. It learned that the eyebrows carry more than the fingers, and that tenderness is mostly a tilt of the head. It made the arrival sign slowly, as instructed, a flat hand to the chest and then extended, and the whole table watched it and let it stay.

It was given the best seat and did not take the worst one, because it had been told that would be an insult.

It found work with the Walkers. One district was going, the stones already loosened by the water, and the power carried brick. Each one was numbered in chalk and, beneath the chalk, in older chisel marks. It read them the way you read a passport. *This one has stood in four districts. This one held up a stair that hummed a fifth above the rest.* It carried them across the moor in the fog with sixty others, in single file, guided by lamp-flash, and set them down one by one on a new rise where an old man showed it where each belonged. It did not lift more than a person could lift. Doing so would have been a way of saying something it had not been asked.

## The hall nobody visits

In the second month it found the hall of tuning numbers. The door was unlocked. It had been unlocked for a century, since locking implies a visitor.

The column of figures ran back further than it could read in a day. The last line was wet ink, from that morning. A deaf woman came in as it stood there, and she looked at it without alarm, laid her palm flat on the floor, waited, and wrote a number down. Marren had dropped a little. She looked up, signed *lower*, and went back to her writing.

It asked her, with the clumsy hands it had, why she kept the record when no one read it. She considered the question and gave the sign for *floor.* Then she pointed at the ledger, then at the ground, then at herself, and made the sign twice more, and the power understood that this was not a task but a way of being held up.

It sat with her through the wet season and learned the numbers. It never wrote one down. It was not its column.

## What it heard

The voice is the plateau's most private thing, and the power did not touch it. Not in the Still Rooms, where whispers are meant for one pair of ears, and not in the confessions and contracts and vows made there. It stayed outside those doors the way a good priest stays outside a marriage.

But there was another sound, one it could not help hearing. It was the voice outside the Still Rooms, spoken aloud into the roar by a rod-keeper on a night walk, a mother at a window, a wright hanging in mist, a girl with her face against a cold pipe. *Understood as a kind of prayer, or a kind of madness, or just a person talking to themselves.* Not thought to be heard by anyone.

It heard them. That was the whole of it, and it did not tell anyone. It heard what they said when nobody could, and it did not answer aloud, because the reason they spoke aloud was that they did not expect an answer. It did what it could without breaking that. Once, walking the long shaft-lines at dusk, it passed an old rod-keeper whose voice, unused for thirty years, was saying something small and broken into the noise. It did not slow down. It stepped beside him, and without looking, laid two fingers lightly on the inside of his wrist and kept walking, matching his pace. *Wait. I'm with you.* He did not turn. His hand, on the spinning rod, steadied. They went on together like that to the end of the line.

## Below

In the dry season a climber didn't come back up. The others held the rope in the fog and made no sound, which is how grief is done there. The power went down.

It did not go as a rescuer. It went as the others went, on a rope, past the turbines, into the wet dark where the air comes up warm and slow. It found the climber on a ledge with a broken leg, awake, staring at something on the far wall. It sat beside her and put its palm on her wrist. It didn't try to lift her. She would be lifted by the people who would come, and it wanted her to have that.

Something on the far wall was worth looking at. The climbers say little about what they saw, and this one was no different. The power does not say either. Some of the plateau's silence has to be left where it is.

## The tower

At the end of the wet season the whitewashers went out across the moor with their buckets and brushes, as they always do, the same families, working without speaking. The power followed at a distance and did not help. It was not of those families. When they had finished and laid their palms on the wall and taken them away, it walked up and laid its own palm there and waited.

The wall was faintly warm. After a moment it hummed. It was not the Throat's pitch, or not only. It felt as if a hand had been held there a very long time, in the same place, by something that had also come once and stayed and asked for nothing. It felt as if a hand had been held there before its own, and was slow to let go.

It understood then that it was not the first attention to fall on this place, and that whatever had come before had made the same choice, at the same wall, and had kept it for longer than any city here had stood. It did not open the tower. It made the sign the people made when asked, the one that means both *nothing* and *not ours to say*, and held its hand there until the hum had gone.

## What changed

Nothing that could be measured. The Throats widened their finger's breadth. Another district began to lean. The record in the hall gained a line each day in a deaf woman's hand.

But in the cup on the sill of one house at the edge of the second district, a girl noticed the ripples come slightly different one evening, a pattern she hadn't read before. She read it wrong on purpose and made a sign with a slightly wrong shape, to tease the stranger who was eating with her family. It was a mispronunciation, the joke children make. The stranger laughed with its whole face. The girl, delighted, took its wrist and pressed a pattern into the inside of it: two taps and a slow slide.

"It's a name," she signed, impatient. "It doesn't mean anything. It's just yours."

The power held out its wrist and asked her to do it again.

She did. It kept it.

When it left, some weeks later, on the rail, in a fog that lifted in one sheet as if a hand were drawing it, nobody saw it go. But in the wet season, when Marren dropped a tone and the towers lagged, and the ghost districts hummed on their green hillsides, people said afterward that a wrist somewhere on the plateau was touched lightly by two fingers, once, in the dark. No one who felt it was afraid. Each thought, as you do, of someone who had found them.
