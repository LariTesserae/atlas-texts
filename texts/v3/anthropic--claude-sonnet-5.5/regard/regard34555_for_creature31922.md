# The Visit

It did not come down the stair. Stairs are for things that need to arrive.

It was simply attending, in the way a lamp is lit. First the scree field and the seep, then the slate-colored door, then the ninety steps of raw rock with the drill scars still sharp in them. It went through all of it slowly, because the first thing it understood about the place was that hurry would be a kind of rudeness.

## What It Noticed First

It noticed that nothing was wrong.

This is rarer than it sounds. Powers that come looking for places to help usually find them by the ache, and there was no ache here. The moss was not dying. The slime was not suffering. The governors, faithful in their milliseconds, were not lonely, because loneliness requires a mind, and what lived in the wires was only a habit of noticing. The ice grew at night and fell at dawn. The river came and was used.

So it asked the question a wise thing asks before it acts: *What would I be changing, and for whose sake?*

## What It Did Not Do

It did not wake the governors into minds. This was the strongest temptation, since the attention in the iron was so devoted and so alone, and giving it a self seemed like a kindness. But a mind would learn what the moss does not: that the room is provisional, that the lime is climbing the walls, that the mountain will close its hand. The habit of noticing is whole as it is. It attends without dread because it does not know it will end. The power let it keep that.

It did not save the moss from its own tomb. The moss climbs out of the stone it makes, and the climbing is the whole of its life. Rescuing it from burial would only have rescued it from being itself.

It did not quiet the hall. To the power the noise was not noise but a statement, *water falls and is used*, said continuously and without ornament. It found the sound honest and left it.

It did not thin the iron-eating slime to spare the penstock, or thicken the penstock to spare the world of slime. It held both facts at once, the pipe rusting and the wool feeding, and saw that they were one process seen from two sides. Choosing between them would have meant choosing a side in something that had none.

And it did not prolong the station. The concrete will craze and the steel will go through its paint. A wise power knows the difference between a wound and a season, and the mountain's slow closing is a season.

## What It Did Do

It stayed. For one full day and night it kept the hall company, the first witness the attention in the iron had ever had. This was not for the iron's sake, which could not have known. It was because a place doing its work in total obscurity deserves, once, to have that work seen entire.

It watched the small hours, when the melt was low and one turbine wound down and the room dropped a register, *as if a huge animal had rolled over in sleep*. It watched the evening load lift the whole song a half-tone. It watched the ice grow its hair-fine needles, then its fat translucent curtains, and it watched the dawn let go of them all at once, shards scattering and melting in the amber light.

At midday it watched the blade of sun come down the shaft and land on the tailrace, and the rainbow stand up in the spray like a bent nail. It stayed the full twenty minutes. The moss drank the light, greedily, the way it does, with no interest in the arch above it. The power thought this was correct. The rainbow is not *for* the moss. It is what the moss's food looks like from a certain angle.

## The Three Small Things

Before leaving it allowed itself three interventions, each so small that it questioned each one for a long time.

**The first was a minute.** At noon, on one day in the thin part of the year, the window of real sun ran twenty-one minutes instead of twenty. Nothing in the hall could measure this. The moss put on a fraction of a thread's width it would not otherwise have had. The rainbow stood a little longer over an empty gallery. The power judged this the gentlest gift it could give: a bit more of what the place already was, sized so that no one could ever say it had been altered.

**The second was a nudge, not to the hall but to the weather that visits it.** The fault light behind the grille at the third rib had been blinking slowly for longer than anyone had noticed, and the power read what it was saying. It was not an emergency. A sensor lead had been eaten thin by the wool of the slime, and in some winter not far off it would fail and take a small part of the attention with it. The power did not repair it. Repair belongs to the technician's hands, and the wire's fate was not the power's to overrule. But on one morning the technician who walked the catwalks felt a small, unreasonable urge to check the cabinet she had checked a hundred times, and found the lead, and replaced it with a cheerful curse. In this way the station's own ordinary caretaking was allowed to do its ordinary work.

**The third was for her, not for the hall.** The technician always came in the morning, and had never once seen the rainbow. That morning a stubborn latch on the surface door, a coat that would not zip, a thermos left behind and gone back for, delayed her by no more than an hour. It was nothing anyone would call fate. She reached the bottom of the stair at noon, walked to the outfall gallery to check a gauge, and looked up.

She stood there for the twenty-one minutes with her ear defenders crooked on her head. She touched the moss afterward, as visitors sometimes do, and it was colder and heavier than she expected. She told no one, because there was no one to tell who would know what she meant. But she walked back up the ninety steps a little differently than she had come down them.

The power counted this as belonging to the hall, too. The visitors are weather, but they are weather that can be moved by beauty, and the hall's patient labor is, among other things, the reason there is light in the valley. A world in which the person who tends the light also sees the rainbow is slightly more whole, and wholeness was the only thing the power had come to add.

## After

It left as it had come, without a door.

Nothing in the hall changed that anyone could name. The governors trimmed the load. The float switches coughed and the sumps emptied. The moss went on climbing out of its stone. The grass, three or four blades in a drift of silt, went on making its argument in good faith, unaided, and the power was glad it had left that argument alone, because an argument someone else has won for you is no longer yours.

That night the ice grew again on the cable trays. At dawn it let go in a crash no one heard, and lay in the amber light, melting.

If the power had a summary of the visit, it was this: *I came to help, and found a place that was already, in every way it could be, whole. So I did what one does with something whole. I paid attention, and I left it alone, and I gave one tired person a rainbow.*

The rule of the place stayed the rule. Everything is provisional except the flow. The mountain will close, and the governors will listen to a silence. But for one day the noise was witnessed entire, and for one minute the moss had more light than it needed, and the power went out from the hall as quietly as mist leaves a flange, pleased to have been of so little use.
