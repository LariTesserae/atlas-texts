# The Neighbor

It came down the way everything comes down to the Sump, over most of a day, and it did not hurry. It could have arrived all at once. It had decided, before it began, that arriving all at once was the first thing it would not do.

It was wise enough to know how little that meant. Wisdom, in its experience, was mostly a list of things not to do, learned by doing them.

## What it considered

It stood on the last terrace, where the air turns warm and close, and let the basin come to it. It considered, honestly and in order, everything it could do.

It could stop the sinking. The ground could be stilled and the fault locked, and the finger's width lost each year could be held. It saw at once why not. The sinking was the basin's heartbeat. The cracks were the water, the water was the shrimp, and the shrimp were the roar. A still Sump would be a dry one, with a white crust and a great many dead birds. The place was alive because it was unfinished, and finishing it would have been a kind of murder.

It could quiet the noise, or give the people ears that needed no wax. But the roar was not their affliction. It was their silence, the wall they leaned on. To take it away would be like taking the floor from someone and calling it a gift.

It could lift the three hundred out to somewhere dry and green and easy. But it looked at how they laughed, with their whole faces and no sound, and saw that they weren't waiting to be rescued. They were tenants who had learned the terms of a lease and found the terms beautiful.

It could speak. It could arrive as a voice over the pans and say, *I see you, I am here, ask.* It let that one go last and with the most regret, because it understood what would follow. Every promise in the basin was a causeway, something laid across bad ground that held only if everyone kept stepping on it. A voice from the sky would be a causeway laid by someone who never had to walk it. The Keepers would look up instead of down, and the whole grammar of their attention, the sign for a boil and the sign for a lagoon about to lose its bottom, would slowly begin to point at the wrong thing.

What remained, when it had crossed out all the large things, was small. It found that this did not disappoint it.

## The listening

So it listened first, for a season, which is a short time for a power and a long time for a listener.

It learned the sign for water. All of them: the boil, the bead, the skin, the warm channel, the cold intrusion, the new-crack seep and the old-crack seep. It learned them by attending to hands until the shapes made sense from inside. It learned why the Keepers crouch and why the terns strike at the neck. It stood on a mud island among nests and was never once mistaken for a shadow, because it had learned to weigh what a shadow weighs.

It found the note under the roar, the one that flamingo parents use to find their own chicks in a creche of thousands. It was a real note, narrow and rising, and the roar had not been built to cover it. The birds had found the one gap in a wall of sound and kept their most precious conversation there. The power did nothing with this. It only stayed a long time beside it, the way you stay beside something you would like to be worthy of.

It learned the toads. It learned that when they fall silent, everyone in the basin stops mid-step.

And it learned the ground, more deeply than the Keepers could, though less lovingly. Under the western works, where the plank road runs out to the generator shed, a block was ready to go. It would not be a catastrophe, only a tilt and a drop of the sort the basin makes every decade, the kind that means someone has to move a pipe. But it would come in the dark hour before first light, when the road was empty. Or it would come a few weeks later, at first light, when it was not.

## What it did

It thought for a long time about whether to do anything at all. It did not want to become a habit. A place that is rescued once starts, quietly, to wait to be rescued again, and it did not want to teach the Sump to wait.

But it did not think a neighbor is a bystander. A neighbor who sees the ground about to move under the house across the road says something. That's all. It doesn't lift the house or bring the ground to heel. It says something in the language the house already speaks.

So it went into the water. It found the seeps that fed the western fault, and it made a change so slight that a hydrologist would have called it noise. It let the pressure rise and fall in a rhythm just a little off the basin's own, a pulse under the pulse. Then it went to the generator shed, where the old machine shudders on its frame, and it let that pulse pass through the iron and into the metal, where a girl of fourteen keeps her palm.

She felt it on the third morning. She said something fast to her father, and he answered with his slight tilt of the head, and went out and looked at the ground.

He found a new-crack seep, a hand-width, already ringed with the first faint orange. He stood over it for a long time. Then he painted a board and set it on a stake, and by evening the plank road had been lashed to a new line of drums a hundred meters north. A week later, in the dark hour, the block went. The old road tilted, broke, and slid quietly into a fresh lagoon. Nobody was on it. The cormorants on its posts lifted, circled once in the steam, and settled on the new road as though it had always been theirs.

In the morning the Keepers came out and stood at the edge of the new water and made the sign for it. One old man gave the sign for *her opinion*, and the others laughed their silent, whole-faced laughter, and that was all. Nobody thanked anyone. The power was more pleased by this than by anything it had ever done.

## The one other thing

There is one more small thing, and it is barely a thing.

On the northern terrace, where the wind-bent tamarisks stand, the Keepers count their trees by name. There are three hundred and eleven. One spring there were three hundred and twelve, a seedling in a salt seam where no one had planted it, a hand tall, leaning already with the others. The power had done nothing but carry a seed, on a wind that was going that way anyway.

The Keepers argued about it for a week, in the hands, with great delight. Someone said the birds had brought it. Someone said the ground had. They gave it a name that has no sound and cannot be translated, and they put a ring of stones around it. The girl, when asked, only smiled.

## Where it stays

It does not leave. It couldn't say when it stopped being a visitor.

It keeps no shape, but if you looked for it you would look where the old sea eagle sits on winter mornings, because the eagle is the only creature in the basin that has ever turned its head toward it. Once, on a still morning, the eagle looked at a patch of empty steam for the length of a breath. Then it looked away, as one looks away from someone who prefers not to be greeted, and drifted on through the hole it made in the roar.

At the brine tide, when the pans merge into one sheet and the sky lies down in the water and the birds stand on air, the power is there, at the edge of the western works, below the shed roof. Up on the roof a man of sixty lifts one hand no higher than his chest and makes a sign his daughter says has no translation.

The power knows what it means. It has learned the language well enough to know that the sign is not addressed to anyone. It is what the basin says to itself, and it is also, this once, exactly true.

It does not answer. It does not thin the vapor or still the wind or turn the light. It stands in the shallows among the stilts, at exactly the reach of its own neck from the nearest bird, because that is how one stands in the Sump, and it is content to be one more body in a place that has room for it only if it takes up no more than its share.

Behind it the roar goes on without a pause. It has become, for the power too, a solid wall to lean on. The ground goes on deciding what shape to become. Up on the roof the hand comes down, and father and daughter climb back to the engine, because the machine needs oiling and the ground needs watching, and someone, in the end, has to be trusted with both.
