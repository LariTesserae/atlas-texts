# The Auditor

The power had many things it could have done, and it spent a long time, by its own reckoning, deciding against them.

It could have stilled the quake-fault and given the spring a steady temperament. It could have taught smelting, or sent a physician for the gravel, or told the Sawyers how their slabs would harden. It could have opened the sealed chambers at the center and shown everyone what was inside. It could have spoken from the vents in a great voice and answered the question the adults ask with their hands in the water.

Each of these was kindness aimed at a wound the town did not think it had. The gravel is a joke they tell about themselves. The closing-over is the shape of their whole understanding of time. A people who say *everything is meant to be held* have made a peace, and the power judged that breaking it to offer them a different one would be a theft that looked like a gift.

So it came instead the way the heron comes, at dawn, to the outer rim, and stood still.

---

The heron took it badly. It moved twenty paces down the bank and watched the newcomer with the flat resentment of an official whose office has been taken. The Worked noticed the change within a Breath or two, because they notice everything, and said nothing, because they did not know whether it was a second auditor or a sign. They went on rasping. A thousand doors were scraped in the grey light, and the power stood on a serrated rim and listened to the town gnaw itself clean.

Then the Breath came.

The power had no ankles or low back. It had chosen a form, thin and grey and long-legged, that the town would tolerate, and the form had no sense for the loosening. So the water rose over its feet without warning, warm and eggy, and the power did what every trader had done before it. It went down like a felled animal, all knees and neck, into two inches of running lime.

A Rimward woman, weathered and solitary, waded over, took it under the arm, and stood it up. She looked at it for a while. "Deaf," she said, not unkindly, as she would to someone back from a long journey. Then she hung a cup on a cord around its neck, because it was holding nothing and that was unsettling.

---

It stayed. That was the first thing it did, and perhaps the most important. It became a thing that could be seen every morning.

It learned the greeting. A child asked it, as children ask everyone, *Is your door open?* The power had no door and understood that this was not the question. It answered honestly. "Not yet."

It had not expected what happened next. The Worked answer *Not yet* at once and without questions, and they did, as they would for any neighbor. Within the hour an old Rimward man had led it to an empty house at the edge of the lowest terrace, where a family had died out two winters before. The lintel was already skinned over, a hand's breadth from sheathed. He put a small rasp in its grip, showed it the angle, and left.

The power had come to give and found itself being given to. It thought this the correct order of things and rasped the lintel until the door opened. The next morning it did it again. In the third week someone knocked on the lintel and called *Worked this morning?* The power, who had never been asked anything by a stranger who wanted only an answer, said, "Worked this morning," and something in it went quiet and warm, like a pool near a vent.

---

What it did after that would not have made a story. It did not mend anything.

It sat with the Jar-growers and waited a season beside a channel where a wicker form was being slowly clothed in stone. It waited well, and the Jar-grower said, "You'll do," which was the highest thing she said to anyone.

It went to the lowest terrace and stood among the sheathed dead while two families argued over whether the Keepers rasped too much or too little. Each was certain. The power could have said which was right, and could have told them how long a face lasts under a given flow. It did not. It saw that the argument was the remembering. If it were settled, the families would stop coming down to the shelf and stop quarreling over whose grandmother's nose was going. So it stroked a cheek when asked to and said nothing about the rest.

At dusk it walked the lanes behind the Listeners with its head tilted. It never learned to hear the terrace-tones as they did, but it learned to hear that they were listening. That is a different sound, and it was the one it had come for.

On a night when the Breath was late, it sat by the deepest vent-pool among the adults who sit longer than they need to. It put its hands in the water and the algae woke blue-green around its fingers. The adults waited, not looking at it, the way one does not look at a person about to speak.

The power could have answered. It had the voice and the words, and it knew what they were waiting for. But a thousand people had been listening to their own town for generations, and their listening was the town's tenderest structure, more delicate than any terrace. A voice from the water would have made it a thing that was listened *to*. So the power kept its hands in the glowing pool and said nothing. After a time the Breath came up through the vents, warm around all their wrists, and everyone sat in it together.

---

The one who asked was very old, a Deep Floor from a chamber near the center with a single working door.

She came to the power's house one morning before the rasping, which no one does, and stood in its doorway with her white lashes and narrowed eyes. She had the stiffness in the joints and the lentil-sized stones. She did not sit.

"You are the auditor," she said. "So I am asking you what we don't ask. Do I have to keep rasping?"

The power understood that this was the question behind the table, the one about the very old moving toward the center, about what they intend, and whether they ever ask to stop.

It could have said *no*, or *yes*, or *it doesn't matter*, and each would have been a small tyranny. "It's your door," it said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," said the power. "It's the true one."

She was quiet a long time. Then she said, "The sealed rooms. You've been listening. You can't hear the tones like they can, but you've been standing near. Do they hum?"

"I don't know," the power said. "I haven't heard it."

She made a sound that in anyone else would have been laughter. "Of course they hum. Every one of us knows. The center's a chord. It's been a chord since before my mother's mother. Nobody says it because saying it makes it a thing you'd have to decide about." She looked at it almost with pity. "You came all this way to be wise at us, and we know it already. We just don't say."

The power had, in its long existence, been wrong perhaps a few times. It found that being wrong in this way was a great relief. "Then I have nothing to give you," it said.

"You could rasp my door tomorrow," said the old woman. "If I'm slow."

"I will."

"And if I ask you to stop."

"Then I'll sit in the lane outside and rasp nothing, and keep you company."

She nodded, as if it had said *worked this morning*, and went back toward the center, sliding her fingers along the wall, checking that it felt right.

---

The power left in the end, as powers do, because there were other places. It did not vanish or blaze. It rasped its lintel one last morning, answered *Worked this morning* to a child who asked, and went up to the outer rim where the heron had been waiting with the patience of the aggrieved. The heron reclaimed its post. The power said nothing to it, only bowed slightly.

But it left one thing. In the house at the edge of the lowest terrace, under the hearth, lay a floor of stone that had not been there when it arrived. It was only a season deep, no more than a few layers, each thin as a fingernail paring, laid down in the Breaths of its stay. It was not much, but it was thicker than nothing. A Rimward family took the house, and the Sawyers said the hearth was oddly hard, and that the stone under it had grown more evenly than any stone had a right to.

The Worked have a word for someone with no thickness, no history, no floor, and the power was never called it. It had been laid down, and it would be held, as everything is, and the town would go on being wet and quiet and ticking out of time with itself.

*May your floor be deep*, the old Rimward man had said to it, on its first morning with the rasp. It had not understood then. Standing on the rim with the heron beside it, feeling for the first time in its long existence a loosening in its ankles and low back before the water rose, it did.
