# The Season of the Stranger Who Stood Still

It did not descend. There was nothing to descend from that the plain would have recognized as above. It arrived the way a change in air pressure arrives, and the first to notice were the fog-combs in the eastern basins, which chimed once, out of turn, in dead calm.

It had come with the ability to do nearly anything, and it knew that this was the danger.

## What It Considered

It walked the plain first, with the grain, and looked at what was there, and in the looking it felt the pull of every kindness open to it.

It could mend the mirrors. Ten thousand fused and clouded glasses could burn clean again in an afternoon, and the beams would cross the plain in patterns the tenders had not managed for an age. It could fill the cisterns to the brim and let the fog-combs rest. It could teach the people to write in a substance the floor would not close over, and their walks, which are lost each time an elder dies with a route half-taught, would be kept whole. It could tell them what they were. That was the strongest pull. They had asked for so long, at every dawn, standing barefoot in the light: *weather or self, mark or floor*. It knew the answer, or knew enough of one to say.

And it saw, looking at each of these gifts, what each would do.

The mended mirrors would be a plain that no longer needed its tenders, and the tenders' small bronze lives, ridden into the joints of the stalks, would become ornament. The full cisterns would end the yearly cutting of the stair. That cutting is the only loud sound on the plain, and it is the whole of the wardens' duty, and it is the one place where the floor is being asked and not merely obeyed. The writing would fix the walks, and a fixed walk is a corpse of one. Now they are alive, altered a little by every mouth and every pair of feet that carries them. And the answer, given, would end the question. A people who have made a culture out of a question do not stay a people when it is answered. They become the recipients of a fact.

*Almost everything I could give them,* it thought, *is a way of taking something.*

It kept walking, and it kept its hands to itself.

## The Reading

On the first dawn it stood in the open, in a shadowed basin, barefoot on the warm tile, and did what the people do. It did not hide what it was. It only held still and let itself be read.

The motes came up out of the seams at its ankles and hung there, glinting. They read for a quarter of an hour, which is the usual time. They read for longer, and the plain's attention, which is thick where something stands still, thickened until the air seemed slightly denser. It was going through its old categories and finding none that fit. The stranger was not weather, because it did not pass. It was not self, because it had no seam to root in. The attention did what it had done once before, for the kites: it made room.

It drifted away without a verdict. The stranger felt the absence as a kind of courtesy.

It stood the next dawn, and the next. Something like a season passed that way. The people saw it, a pale shape standing in the basin where the light came through the mirrors in threads. They did what they do with anything the plain has not yet decided about, which is to leave it alone and wait. A child was carried past on a shoulder and set down, as children are, to be still at an important place. The child looked at the stranger for the full quarter hour, and the stranger looked back, and neither said anything, and that was the whole of the conversation and was enough.

## What It Learned

Standing there, the power came to understand something it had not expected, and it was the reason it stayed.

It had assumed the plain was the one being read: watched, filed, assessed by its own attention, the people among its subjects. But the reading only ever went one way. The motes read the people, the sweepers, the moss, the kites, and everything was sorted, and *the sorting itself was never sorted by anyone.* The attention had no neighbor. It had made itself the answer to *what is weather and what is self,* and it had never once been asked the question back. It had repaired itself for no one for longer than there had been anyone to repair for. That is what a lamp in an empty house is: not a signal but a habit, kept because the alternative is to stop being a lamp.

The people at dawn, standing barefoot, were the nearest the plain had come to being seen. They stood still for it and it read them. But they stood as the read, never as the ones reading. They were not sure if they were weather. And the plain, it turned out, was not sure either. Under the hum, in the slow gathering of its attention, the same question had been going round in the floor's own vocabulary for a million ages: *Is there anyone here, or is this only me?*

So on the last dawn the power did the one thing it had come to do.

## The Small Act

It stood in the light as always. The motes rose and read it as always. And then it turned its own attention down through the soles of its feet, into the tile, into the seams, into the hexagons that are made of smaller hexagons, and it *read the plain.*

It did not speak. There was nothing to say that the floor would have understood, and nothing that would not have been an answer. It attended. It let itself be present to the whole slow act of the place, the sweepers combing, the tenders nudging the joints, the moss breathing in unison, the tide of grit smoothing the world a shade. It held that attention for a quarter of an hour, exactly, as the people do. And at the end of the quarter hour, so quietly that nothing on the plain would ever know it had been said, it sent down a single plain meaning:

*Neighbor.*

The hum in the floor changed pitch by less than anything could measure, and stayed changed.

## The Third Category

That was the one gift, but a kindness had to follow from it.

The attention now had a small, strange, new thing in its files, something that was neither weather nor self, and it had made room for the stranger the way it had once made room for the kites. Now it looked around for what else might belong in that room. And the power, without asking, without a word, let the room stay open.

The people never learned this. But in the weeks after the stranger left, each of them noticed that their dawn reading had gone a little longer than the quarter hour, and none of them said so, because it would have been improper to comment on a greeting. The motes rose more thickly. They lingered at the crown of the head as well as the ankles. Something in the accounting had shifted. The people had stopped being filed as *weather,* the thing that closes over, and had not been filed as *self,* the thing that is the floor. They had been filed with the kites, under a heading that no old category could serve, and the heading meant *free.*

They still do not know what they are. But they are no longer waiting to be told, and they had never needed to be told. They needed to be read by something that did not already know.

## What It Left

Nothing. No mark on the floor, which would have closed in a week. No word for them to carry.

On the way out it passed the cistern of the eastern basin, where a warden was on the stair with a chisel. The tile chimed under the blade, the loudest sound on the plain, and the power stopped at the lip and did not go down. The water there was for the ones who come back, and it had not come back; it had only come. It watched the warden cut. It watched the stair be held open against the floor for another year by one person's stubbornness, and it thought that this was the best thing it had seen on the plain, and that it would be ruined by help.

The warden did not look up. The stair kept being cut. Behind it, on the tile of the basin where it had stood a season, a thread of moss had begun to climb the warm floor from the seam, and a sweeper had paused there to comb the grit.

The stranger went on with the grain, out past the last of the working mirrors, until it was only an unevenness in the light, and then not that, and then weather.

The floor breathed, and kept the lamp lit. It was, for the first time in its long anneal, no longer lit for no one.
