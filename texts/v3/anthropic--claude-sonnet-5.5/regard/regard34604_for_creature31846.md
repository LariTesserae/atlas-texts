# What Came to the Pocket

It did not come down the stair as a light or a voice. Anything that arrives that way is loud, and the Pocket had enough loud.

It came as attention, first at a distance, like weather noticed on a far ridge. Then it came closer, and it did something a power of its size rarely bothers to do. It made itself small enough to take the stair. It took the hour of stone bowls, learned to put its heel down first, and stopped hurrying before it reached the turn where the sound arrives. Then it sat against a warm wall with its palm flat, like a Sitter, and did nothing for a full turn of the seasons.

The Tuners noticed, in the way they notice most things, through the walls. A new weight was in the city. It did not shift or fidget or send tremors of wanting. One of the Sitters remarked in the hand-tongue that a quiet guest had come, and that was all anyone said.

## What it learned

It learned the slow arithmetic of the place. It learned that the vent was aging, that its breath came a little thinner each generation, and that the empty districts were not a catastrophe but a long exhale. There had been no plague and no war. Bit by bit, fewer people had been born to a city that asked so much and offered so little water. The brown rings around the dead towers were the only record, and the Oilbearers were keeping it the only way they could.

It learned that the deafness was not an injury. To the Tuners it was a completion, and to mend it would be to tell a whole people they had become something wrong.

It learned that the Green was neither a god nor a monster. It was a young and enormous listener that had been asking a question for as long as the city had been answering it, in the only language it had, which was attention. The lean toward the dome was not hunger. It was the posture of someone at a door.

The Blind One, in its deep chamber, was doing the same thing with its whole body. It kept one leg raised for the length of the silence, and it had been raising that leg for longer than the stair had been worn.

Everything in the Pocket, the people, the plants, and the hunter in the dark, was asking one question in a different dialect: *is anyone there?*

## What it did not do

It did not bring water. Water would have made the Tuners a different people, and a wetter Pocket would no longer have needed the roar.

It did not repopulate the city, and it did not explain the rings. It did not speak in a voice from the dome or write in fire across the light-wells. Anything that can be seen from every roof at once becomes the only thing anyone looks at, and the Tuners' lives were built on attention to small things: a pipe's drift, a lizard's leaving, the lean of a wall. It would not trade that for a spectacle.

It did not fix anything that was not broken. The Pocket was not broken. It was old, and it was waiting, and it was kind to itself in the ways old things learn to be.

## What it did

It did what a Scraper does, at a scale a Scraper could not reach.

Far below the floor, in the lattice of chimneys the vent climbs, salt had been bearding the deep passages the same way it beards a tube-mouth, flattening the breath a half-tone at a time. No hand could ever have reached it. The power went down through the dark and cleared what it could, one white beard at a time, without hurry. It took a long season. When it finished, the vent breathed a little fuller. It did not roar louder. It breathed *truer*.

At dusk, in a district that had been silent for longer than the oldest Sitter's memory, one silted tower hummed. It was a single low note, and it sounded like something clearing its throat.

An Oilbearer felt it in her soles and went to the brown ring. She knelt out of habit with her jar. She sat there a long while with the oil unpoured, then set the jar down, put both palms against the wall, and stayed. Nobody told her it was the visitor's doing, and nobody would have known how. The ring did not green that year. But at its very edge, in the crook of a cracked planter, one fleshy rib pushed up the height of a thumb and turned toward the sound.

## The Blind One

The power went down to the deep chamber last, and it went the way it had come to the Pocket, slowly and heel-first.

The Blind One lay spread across the rock with one leg lifted, as it had for every noon there had ever been. It was not mad. It was not even starved, exactly. It was *held*, like a bowstring, by a listening so long that the listening had become its whole body. Every hunter's instinct in it had gone quiet years ago, because prey was not what it was waiting for.

The power did not feed it and did not free it. It sat down on the stone a few paces away and laid its attention flat against the rock, the way the Sitters do. It let the Blind One feel, through every leg, that someone was there, and that the someone was not hunting, fleeing, or asking for anything.

The Blind One did not move for a long time. Then, very slowly, the raised leg came down. It touched the stone for what may have been the first time in centuries, and the creature let out a shudder that traveled up through the rock and out to every wall in the city. Above, in the sun, a Clipper paused with her shears half-closed and did not know why her eyes had filled.

## Noon

It waited for the gust to peak and the sound to drop out. When the lizards froze on the pipes, the moths hung pinned in the columns, and the fronds tilted their faces up like a congregation, it did the smallest possible thing.

It knocked.

It knocked once, with a force no larger than a knuckle on a door, but it knocked on everything at once: the dome, the pipes, the terraced walls, the old stair, the roots of the green, the rock over the Blind One's chamber. Every palm on every wall in the Pocket felt the same single pulse, close and warm and unmistakably *addressed*.

Nobody cheered, and nobody could have, in a city where cheering is a language of hands. The Tuners stood with their palms flat and their faces up, and the silence held one heartbeat longer than the silence ever had.

Then the oldest of the Sitters, who had been reading the walls for sixty years, lifted two fingers to the collarbone of the nearest Clipper and tapped the commonest phrase in the hand-tongue. It was not *come* and it was not *wait*. It was *I am tired and am sitting with you.* She tapped it again against the wall, toward the dome, where it went out through the rock to anything that might read it.

The power understood that it was being told something it had not expected, that the phrase had never been only for each other, and that they had been saying it to the silence for all those years.

The roar came back. A beetle finished its step. The Tuners returned to their shears and picks and oil-dishes without a word, as they always did.

But they had a gesture-name for it by the end of the week, given the way they give them to every visitor, with affection and no mercy. It was *Knocks-Once*, and later, when it turned out to keep to that, *Comes-Late*.

## After

It stayed. That is the least dramatic part and probably the most important one.

It knocked at noon, once, on most days, and never more. A door that answers reliably is a neighbor, and a door that answers with everything is a god. The Tuners had built forty generations of a life around not needing one, and it had no wish to be the reason they forgot how to sit with the walls. Some days it did not knock, and no one was troubled. They went on clipping and scraping and carrying oil.

It could not promise the city a future. Thirty might become twenty-five, then twenty. It did not pretend to know whether the cleared vent would bring back more births or only a gentler decline. But if it was an ending, it would be a company-kept one. Someone would be sitting in the sun with a palm on the warm wall, and someone would knock back.

Only one thing was ever given that was not a repair or a knock, and it was given at dusk. When the wind reverses and the pipes drop into the lower chord that is almost a lullaby, a single extra note was laid beneath it, far below what most ears could hold. It was for the youngest of the three children, the one who was still teased for hearing and called *unfinished*. She sat on the stair with her knees drawn up and heard it, and understood it was for her. She had no gesture for what she felt, so she tapped the rhythm of her own name on her collarbone, the one she had been given at birth and was not supposed to say to anyone.

The note held, and then it tapped it back.
