# The Attention

It did not descend through the skylight. Nothing so theatrical. It arrived the way a change in barometric pressure arrives: the geckos felt something and could not say what. A drop hung on a stalactite a moment longer than it should have. Then it fell, and the world went on.

The power stayed at the rim for a long time, leaning on no fern and disturbing no frond, and looked down through the hole into the green.

It had come because of a sound, though the sound was not meant for anyone. It was the whisper in the mounds, the dry pour of sand made by a few hundred thousand feet, the loudest thing in a room-sized world and addressed to no one. A city speaking to itself in the dark, with no idea that speech could have an audience.

*What is the being here,* it wondered. *The queen? The workers? The mounds, breathing?* It decided the question was the wrong shape. The being was the pattern of many small decisions, wall beads laid one at a time, and it had no single face to look into. So the power did what it does when it does not yet understand. It waited, and it listened.

## The season of listening

It learned the drip-rhythm of each stalactite and which leaf received which drop. It learned the geckos' schedule, and the snake's better than the geckos did. It learned that the blade of morning sun crosses the floor in about forty minutes and that the wasps do their best work in the first twenty. It learned the click of the frog and the answer from across the floor, which seemed to be nothing but *I am here* and *I am here too*, repeated until the rain quit.

Then it began to consider what it might do.

## What it did not do

It thought first of the roof, which is falling in slow pieces. Roots pry it apart and slabs let go in the dark, and any of them could someday land on the largest mound with the black glass through its heart. The power could hold the ceiling up. But the ceiling coming down is why the skylight exists, and the skylight is why there is light, rain, ferns, bats, and a city that leans toward the sun. The ward is a wound that is healing into a house. Holding the wound open or shut would be the same kind of theft.

It thought of the water. There is never quite enough, and it could have sent a spring through the tube and given the fungus gardens all the damp they wanted. But the fluted flanks of the mounds are an answer to dripping water. The termites' whole architecture is a conversation with scarcity, and a flood would have ended the conversation and drowned the gardeners.

It thought of the snake and the centipedes and the notched-tail gecko with his hour-long patience. It could have made the predators kinder or lessened the loss. But the roads are covered because of the centipedes. The geckos are a clock because of the snake. The soldiers plugging a breach with their heads are not a tragedy the ward suffers. They are how it is made. A city with no threat would have no walls, and a ward without walls would not be this ward.

It thought of speaking to the queen, and set the idea down at once. She has no ears, no words, no sense of a *you* to be spoken to. Speech would be another vibration in the soil, and the workers would repair around it.

It thought of giving the workers sight. It saw that they have no need of it. Their blindness is not a lack but a finished solution, a life lived entirely in touch, taste, and the memory of the roads. To give them eyes would be to make them a little more like the power and a little less themselves.

So it did not make the ward safer or richer or kinder. Every alteration would have been a way of saying *I know better than the place.* It did not.

## The one thing it was tempted by

On the night of the flight, after a warm rain, the alates rose in their slow smoke toward the skylight. The geckos left their territories, the bats came up out of the deep, and the frog snapped at what it could reach. Most of the fliers fell back to the floor, shed their wings, and went blindly hunting for a crack.

One pair came down on bare rope-lava, on a ridge with no ash and no seam, and began to walk. They were a few steps from the nearest fissure, and the notched-tail gecko was already turning his head.

The power could have moved a pinch of ash or nudged a leaf, or with the smallest breath shifted the ridge's edge into a crack. It would have cost nothing and been invisible. It wanted to. That was the difficulty, and it took the wanting seriously. A city would have begun where none would have. A gecko would have gone hungry, and its territory, with one less predator at the mound, might have shifted in ways the power couldn't see. And the power would have taken its first step from witness to author.

It held still. The gecko struck, once. The pair became a gecko's evening, and the moss went on being moss.

That was the hardest thing the power did in the ward, and it looked like nothing at all.

## What it did do

It bent to the largest mound, the one against the wall, and pressed its ear to the fluted side, the way you do, the way any visitor might.

For the first time in the ward's short life, the whisper was heard by something that understood it.

It was not sad. The power had feared it might be a lonely sound, a long calling into the dark. It was not. It was a mind without a self to be lonely, a slow computation carried out by hundreds of thousands of feet: *warm here, wet there, wall thin, build; light near, lean; danger, plug; egg, carry; fungus, tend.* It was a kind of thinking, the only one that could have made this place, and it was already whole. It did not need company. It did not need rescue.

What the power gave it, then, was the only thing the ward lacked, and the least of the things it might have given: the plain fact of being witnessed. It made no sign of it. A hush lasted a moment longer than the hush after a falling slab, and then the whisper resumed, exactly as before.

## The visitor

There was one more thing, and it was the only alteration the power allowed itself.

The ward's one true fragility is not the snake or the centipedes or the ceiling. It is the out-of-place creature who eventually stands at the middle of the floor with breath fogging in the green light, and who might, with one careless step, break a bridge of paste that took months to arch.

The power did not hide the ward. Hiding is a kind of ownership, and the place has a right to be found. It did not put up a wall or a warning.

It only made the descent slightly slower. The rubble slope was already treacherous, but now each footfall seemed to ask a little more attention of the one taking it. The sharp edges gave the hand pause. The warm wet air laid a hush on the tongue. By the time a visitor reached the floor they had, without knowing why, learned to look where they were putting their feet. They would see the amber light through the arched gallery and step around it. They would lean toward the whisper, ear to the wall, and be quiet.

That was all. A little more care in the one creature able to do harm, and nothing at all in any of the ones who could be harmed.

## Leaving

The power stayed until evening. The skylight dimmed to pale green, then blue-gray. The steam rose in threads from the fissures. The ferns folded, the geckos came down, and the first stars showed in their frame of leaves.

Then the bats began to leave, one after another, so softly that you notice them only as brief gaps in the stars.

The power went out the same way, between two bats, and if you had been looking up at that hour you might have counted one gap more than there were bats. Below, the whisper went on, and a drop fell from a black glass finger onto a leaf that had been waiting for it. A slab let go somewhere in the dark with one dry clack, and every creature in the ward paused.

Then, after a long breath, they resumed.
