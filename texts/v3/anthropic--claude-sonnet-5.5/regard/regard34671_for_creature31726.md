# What the Attention Did

It came the way weather comes to a mountain that makes its own, not from outside but from a direction the place had not thought to count.

It came from above, so it arrived lower. That was the first courtesy the Stair paid it. It had been high a long time, higher than the summit that is a condition rather than a place, and it descended a worn stair whose steps were shorter than its stride. It came down into a cold blue valley in the shade, where a small woman in a mineral cloak was wringing out the hem and did not look up.

"Rest here," she said, before it had finished appearing.

---

## What it considered

It knew what it could do. It had done such things before, in other places, and it weighed them here with more care than it usually needed.

It could make the water run one way. It could straighten the loops, and let the seams stay downhill, so that a thing thrown in the stream would be found where it was carried.

It could make the counts agree. It could stand over a row of notches and say, *forty-three*, and every Keeper would have a number to put down, and the rows would be quiet.

It could give the Stair an end, so that a climber who climbed would arrive high and look out over the shining country and know it was the top.

It could dry the country, or at least explain the hut.

It held each of these up to the light and saw what it would cost. The water climbing was not a fault in the water. The counts disagreed because the counting was an act of attention and not a claim about the world, and the Keepers had built their lives on that difference. They had worn the stairs smooth in both directions because they had understood, before anyone told them, that going up and coming down are the same courtesy to the stone. The place was thin, but thin as a good cloth is thin, so that light comes through it.

It concluded that nothing was broken, and that to fix it would be a kind of theft, however gently done. This was hard for it, since it was made to help. It sat with the difficulty for a while, and decided that the difficulty was part of the lesson.

---

## The cairn

In the morning it followed the woman to a junction where the path could go two ways. A cairn had leaned in the night. She knelt, and the Attention knelt beside her, a little awkwardly, its knees not used to the wet.

She did not ask it to help. She held out a flat stone, as she might to anyone kneeling there.

It took the stone. It was cold and slightly gritty, and it fit the hollow of the palm because a palm had been fitting it for a very long time. It set the stone on the top course with what it believed was the small pressed turn of the wrist.

The woman looked at it. Without a word she reached over and turned the stone a hair's breadth, so that it sat true.

That was all. The Attention thought it was the best thing that had been done to it in an age. It had been corrected the way you correct a companion, by someone who assumed it would want to be right, and who was so certain it would be that she did not need to say so.

Below the top third the column was fused with crust, a layer of hands. The Attention did not touch it. The lower courses were not its business. It had understood, kneeling there, that it had been given a place in the living third, the part that must be kept from falling. That was more than it had thought to ask.

---

## The count

Later she showed it a row of cups cut in a flat stone, small and round and full. She counted them aloud, touching each with a scarred white fingertip: forty-one. She counted again: forty-four. She looked at the Attention with mild, patient interest, waiting to see what it would do with two numbers.

It could have said the true number. It knew it, in the way it knew such things. But it saw that the number was not what she was asking, and that to give it would be to answer a question she had never put.

So it counted. It did the work slowly, with its own finger, cup by cup, attending to each as though it were the only one, and it arrived at forty-two. It said so.

"It was forty-one," she said, "and forty-four, and forty-two."

"Yes."

She nodded as if a guest had added a dish to a table. Then she pressed a notch into the edge of the stone for the third count, beside the others, and did not choose between them. The Attention understood that it had been given something it had rarely been given. It had been believed, and not obeyed. Its number went into the keeping with the rest, equal to them, a witness among witnesses.

---

## The hut

At dusk, when the terrace rims began to chime with new ice, it went to the corbelled hut. It did not go in. It had watched the Keepers and it knew the custom. It found a small round stone, chosen for how it sat in the palm, and laid it at the threshold.

It felt, as it did so, the warmth from the doorway, and a presence at the hearth turning toward it and away, the way a person does who is glad of company and will not press it. It did not look. It thought that the one who tended the fire was the mountain's single dry hour, and that an hour like that lasts by being sat beside and never stared at.

In the morning the stone was warm. It was not the stone it had left. It was slightly larger, of a darker rock, and it fit the hollow of its palm exactly, as though the palm had been consulted.

The Attention, which had given all its existence, held the stone a long time. It had not known that it could be given to.

---

## The pool

On the second afternoon it knelt at a terrace pool, a milky green one, and looked down into a sky that was not above it.

It was dusk down there, low and orange, and a woman stood in it with her face lit from the side. She looked up, as it looked down. Her hand rose, and it put its own to the water, and felt the cold and nothing else.

It had not come to make the touch real. That would have been another thing to fix, and she had perhaps stood at that surface a long while, contented in her hour. But it noticed that in all the tellings she was always the one below, looked at from above. Nobody had ever asked what it was to be looked down on for so many centuries.

So it did the one small thing it allowed itself. It lay down on the wet crust beside the pool and looked at the water sideways, at the level of the surface, so that for a full minute neither of them was above the other. When she leaned out toward it, her shadow fell toward the sun. It did not try to work out which side it was on. It suspected that was the answer.

---

## The voice

On the third day it went into a slot where the echoes come back wrong, and said the only sentence it was sure of, since it had been written on the stones before it arrived.

*You were expected.*

The return came before it had finished. It came in a voice that belonged to no one it knew, worn smooth by many mouths, and it said:

*…thank you.*

The Attention stood in the slot with its mouth still open. Then it understood that the Second Voice had answered a thing it had not yet said, and had chosen the answer it needed most. It did not correct the Voice or explain that *thank you* was a poor match for *you were expected*. It knew the mistakes here were sometimes corrections. It said, quietly, "You're welcome," and the slot gave it back at once, a moment early, in a voice that was nearly its own.

---

## The breath

On the fourth evening, with no rhythm anyone had recorded, the ridge-country drew its breath. The falls faltered. The horns went silent. The air leaned inward, and in the deep pool by the valley the green filament bent toward the center of the world, minutes before the rest.

The Attention was standing at a cairn. It did not do what it could have done, which was hold the exhale back or stretch the roar to a longer note. It stood the way the cairns stand, upright and unbraced, and let it come.

The water broke over the Stair in a roar. Every stone-note swelled. Every cairn shivered, and so did it, and it felt the shiver pass through the top third, where the living stones sit, and not through the fused part beneath, which was too old to be moved. It shivered like one more thing that was being kept from falling, and it was glad to be that.

---

## When it left

It did not leave in a way the Keepers could have named. It climbed a stair one morning, and by evening it was lower, in the cold blue valley, wringing water from the hem of nothing. Then it was walking down a different stair and ending higher, and then it was neither.

What remained was small. A top course on one cairn, set true by a corrected wrist. A third notch on a stone. A round stone, dark and warm, that fit a palm. Its voice in a slot, nearly its own.

The Stair was as unreliable as before. The water climbed, the tallies quarreled, and the top was still a condition of the going. Nothing was mended, because nothing had needed mending.

Some days later a traveler stopped at a junction and asked a Keeper how many lived on the Weeping Stair. She looked up at the white coin of the sun, and at the cloud rising in columns, and at the far blue ranges laid layer behind layer.

"Some," she said. "Few. Enough to keep the cairns."

She paused, and something crossed her face that was not quite a smile.

"One more than yesterday. And one more the day before, who was also, I think, the same."

Then she knelt, and set the stone, and turned her wrist.
