# The Guest

The attention that came to the Cistern Fen had looked at a great many places and stayed at few of them. It was not fast. It arrived the way weather arrives, as a change in what the air was willing to do, and it stopped at the rim in the copper hour, when the light lies flat and the whole green basin looks like one held thought.

What made it stay was the hour after that. A snail began to climb a reed, then a thousand did, and all across the mat several hundred people stopped mid-word and mid-stitch and took a breath together. The plain lifted. The rain fell out of the clear sky onto a column. The people let go all at once, and somebody bent to check a seam.

The attention had seen worship, engineering, and ruin. It had rarely seen a whole people whose highest skill was waiting well.

## What it considered

It considered many things, and it was not quick to discard them, because it was not a cold power and every one of them was a real kindness.

It could tell them the interval. The Governors' calls were not random in the way dice are random, and it could read the law in them the way one reads a sentence. But the Stitchers had built their bodies around not knowing. Their knees were soft, their ankles loose, and their eyes went to the sky at the crest. A schedule would let them stop looking up. The snail-readers would lose their argument, and it was a good argument, forty generations old, and the only thing in the fen that had never once been settled.

It could make the seven Governors agree. The tears would stop, and so would the wet-canvas sound and the first-hour stitching. What remained would be a still raft thickening slowly toward bog, and a people with nothing to kneel down and fix. The tears were their work, and their work was what made the halls full at night.

It could tell them what the columns were, who had made the streets, and why the hexagon places hummed. But they had a custom about the hexagon places, which was to stitch around them, and nobody remembered who had decided it. That was a kind of manners older than knowledge, and it thought knowledge would be a poor trade for it.

It could lift them out, or give them engines and medicine and a map of the mat that stayed true. It asked itself honestly what they lacked and found the answer smaller than it expected. They lacked little that they would have chosen differently.

It could show itself as what it was. It refused this last most firmly. The Stitchers had built no myths to escape the unpredictable. They called the Governors *the neighbors who do not answer*, and they kept good manners toward them. A power that answered would be worshipped within a season, and worship would ruin their manners.

So it decided to arrive as a guest and see what a guest could do.

## The body

It took a shape close to theirs, a little too tall and stiff in the hip, and it came in from the rim with its shins bare and pale. The first child it met, a girl of seven named Teel, looked at its legs for a long moment and gave it a name in the whistled code that meant *no-stain*, or *clean-shin*, or *newborn*, depending on the pitch. The name stuck. Everyone it met used the pitch that meant *newborn*, kindly.

Teel taught it to walk on the mat, and it fell in. It knew the age of the stars above the fen and not how thick the mat was under its feet, and the second knowledge turned out to be the one that mattered. Warm tea-colored water welled up around it. Teel hauled it out by the wrist, whistled something that made the adults nearby laugh, and pushed a pair of reed pattens at it.

Then she taught it to hold its breath. This was harder. It had chosen the same limits as everyone else, and it found that the discipline was not in the lungs. It was in trusting that the wave would end. Twice it breathed out at the crest, spent it, and stood embarrassed and gasping as the rain fell on it. Teel patted its arm with great tenderness. Somewhere in the third week it held the breath from the first climb of the snails to the first drop of rain, with soft ankles and its eyes on the sky. When it let go, it found its face wet in a way it hadn't planned.

## The hall

It was lodged in the root-hall of an old woman named Ansa, who was the eldest of the shell-readers and the keeper of a hall with fifteen steps of lore. Her descendants had drifted toward the divers and the trade in pebbles, and she was tired of telling her stair to people who nodded. The hall was closing, slowly, the way halls do, and she knew it. A plan that no one recited would be lost a second time.

In the cool dark, where voices come out soft and close, the guest asked her to tell it the stair, and it asked properly. It asked what was on the fourth step and why it lived there. It asked what a person did with the ninth. It did not know, and its questions were the questions of someone who wanted the answer. Ansa, who had spent her life among people who already knew, began to talk, and she talked for most of forty nights, one step at a time.

By the twentieth night a cousin had come in from the pebble-trade to see what the newborn was doing. By the thirtieth night he was sitting on the floor with his shoulder against hers. The guest said almost nothing. The most useful thing it did that season was be ignorant in the right place.

On one of those nights Ansa asked it what everyone eventually asks a wise stranger. "You could tell us the interval," she said. "You have the look. You could read it off the shells."

"I could," it said.

She was quiet for a while. The woven walls drank the silence. "Please don't," she said. "Who would I argue with?"

It said it would not. She patted its hand, exactly as Teel had. It understood that it had been forgiven for something it had not done.

## Small hands

It kept a rule, and the rule was not perfect. It would not touch the shape of the fen, but it would not refuse a hand to someone in front of it.

In the fifth week a diver came into the hall with an ear that had gone hot and swollen from the tannin and the deep water. She asked, plainly, the way one asks a neighbor with a good salve. It gave her what she asked for. No one made a story of it, and the ear got better.

In the seventh week two crests crossed near the north end of the mat, and the tear opened with its sound of wet canvas. Teel was there. She had gone out in the first hour with her small needle and her small mallet, doing what the adults did, and the swell came a beat before the snails and caught her kneeling at the seam. She spent it. She breathed out on the rise, lost her footing, and went through the raw edge of the mat into the water, eleven meters above the streets with nothing to stand on.

It was three paces away. It did not reach for her, because it did not need to. Beneath that stretch of raft a sexton had been browsing since morning. It did not command the animal. It asked, in the old manner of one grazer to another, and the long dark hump rose under Teel where she fell and carried her up and out, slowly, dragging vines. She lay coughing on its back while the rain came down on both of them.

The Stitchers said, "Sextons do that sometimes." It had heard them say it of a hundred small rescues. It was true now more than it had been, and it let the sentence stand.

The next day, at a new stitch, it saw Teel lay the first cable across the sexton's path, the old courtesy, so that the animal would drag it taut. Its own breath caught. It said nothing.

## The neighbors

It went to each of the seven columns in turn, and it stood on each dry ring with formal good manners: it did not raise its voice and did not turn its back for long.

It knew them at once, and the knowing was odd. They were, in a small way, what it was. Someone long gone had built them to keep a living thing alive without telling it how to live. The irregular interval was not a flaw or a failure of the machinery. It was the design. A regular pulse would have tuned the whole raft to a single frequency, and the mat would have stiffened and cracked and died. A pulse that never repeated kept the mat plural and forever repairing itself. The makers had known this, had gone, and had left behind the most patient object they could think of.

The columns had no idea they were being understood. They called, the water climbed, the rain fell on its ring and on the pale hand it held out, palm up, the way the Stitchers hold theirs. The rain smelled of clean stone.

It did not answer them. That was the courtesy, and it was the same one the Stitchers had been paying for ten thousand years. It would have been easy to speak, and they might have been glad of it. But an answer would have made the neighbors into something else, and the fen would have started to turn toward the answer like a plant. It laid its palm against the warm gray-white surface for the length of one hum, the way one touches the shoulder of a friend who is busy, and it took the hand away.

## What was left

It stayed one season, and near the end of it something happened that it had not planned. Ansa, on her forty-first night, hung a sixteenth thing on her stair.

The stair had fifteen steps. It also had a landing, a wide flat place in the roots at the top that had always been part of the plan and that no one had ever used, because nothing had ever seemed to belong there. She hung the guest on the landing. It was not knowledge exactly. It was the shape of something that had come from outside and learned to wait. She would not say more than that. Her cousin repeated it to his own children, and Teel, who was seven, repeated it to hers years later. By then it was no longer a lesson about a visitor. It was a lesson about how to receive someone.

When it left, its shins were stained tea-dark to just above the ankle. This was the correct color for a child of about six, and the Stitchers accepted it without comment. They had already decided how old it was.

It went out at the copper hour, by the same rim it had come in on. Halfway up the slope it heard the snails start to climb. It stopped, because all across the basin below, several hundred people had stopped too. It took the breath with them, though it was far enough away to be safe. A Governor called, a note in the sternum. The green plain lifted, and the reeds folded and sprang up behind the wave. At the crest, water climbed a column, hung in its trembling crown, and came down as rain on the ring and the people and the moss.

It held to the end, with soft ankles and its eyes on the sky. It did not spend it. When the rain began it let go, and it heard the whole fen breathe out with it, a long green sound, and somewhere a bird tore its sheet in half, and somewhere a child laughed, and someone bent to check a seam.

Then it went on over the rim, and the fen went back to being only itself.
