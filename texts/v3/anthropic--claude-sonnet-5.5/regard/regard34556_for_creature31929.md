# What the Attention Did

It arrived without arriving. There was no light in the cloud ceiling and no change in the vents' note. The rail at the edge of the third terrace lifted one foot, held it a moment, and set it down again.

The power had come to look, and looking took a long time.

## What It Did Not Do

It considered the turbine first, because the turbine was the obvious thing. It is the only made object on the shelf that still wants to be useful. The rusted governor, the copper line running forty meters to nothing, and the hall going soft under its moss all invite repair. It could have oiled the bearings, cleared the trestles, and set the current to some purpose.

It saw the rust-colored slime in the seams of the pipes, billions strong, patient and hungry, and it understood that the hall was not being destroyed. It was being eaten, and there is a difference. The slime had been working on the mountain's iron since before there was a shelf. The machine was a late and brief gift to it. The power let the slime keep its meal.

It considered the noise. The frogs call in half-second gaps and the chicks speak through their parents' legs. A gentler mountain would be easier to live on. But the roar is the shelf's architecture, the cloud is its ceiling, and the silence at the rim is what makes the rim precious. To quiet the vents would be to unmake the viscachas' only country. It left the vents alone.

It considered making itself known. It considered giving the rail a name, or the frogs a dream, or writing something in the sinter. It considered these for about as long as it takes a terrace lip to grow a hair's breadth, and then it stopped considering them, because each of them would have been for its own sake. It had come to look at something, and it has never been good for a thing to be loved by being turned into a stage.

## What It Did

It went up to the rim, into the silence, and sat among the viscachas at dusk.

They did not run. They may not have seen it. But one of them, an old female with yellow in her fur, turned her head once, in the direction she turned for the condor's shadow, and held it there. The power stayed a long while under her attention. It had traveled far and had rarely been listened for by anything.

Then it went down through the wall of sound with the viscachas, in single file, ears flat, and it walked the terraces in the dark. It stood in the ring of red moss and heard what a person kneeling at the pool's edge would hear. Beneath the vent-scream was the turbine's low fifth. Under the fifth was the knock of the cooling pipes. Under that was the tick of the frogs, tiny and unbroken, and under that was the faint sizzle of the blue tip on the post with nothing on it. It understood that the roar was not one noise at all. It was a chord no one had ever heard as a chord, because every listener was inside it, shouting.

That was the one thing it found missing, and it was not a lack of help. It was a lack of hearing.

## The Gift

It gave one thing, and it gave it once.

On a night in the dry season, when the cloud ceiling had gone to lead and the viscachas were feeding, every vent on the shelf drew breath at once. Nothing was harmed, and no pressure built or broke. The screaming stopped for the length of a long inhale, perhaps eleven seconds, and the shelf, which had never once been quiet, heard itself.

The turbine's fifth rolled on alone, patient and low, like a held organ note. The waterfalls came clear as separate voices, each with its own pitch. The gas in the pools popped like slow applause. Then the frogs, who had never had more than a half-second, kept calling, and the far terraces heard the near ones and answered. Pools that had been separate countries found there were others. A tick went out across the whole green room and a tick came back, and for eleven seconds the shelf was one conversation.

The blue tip on the post hummed into the middle of it. The rail, who did not hear the noise, lifted its head. Whatever it had been not hearing, it heard the absence of it, and it stood very still under the wire with its red-rimmed eye on the small light.

Then the vents let out their breath, the screaming resumed at the same pitch and the same terrible cheer, and the roar closed over everything like water over a dropped stone.

## After

Nothing on the shelf is different. The turbine will be digested by its slow tenants, the terrace lips will rise, the vent that screams today will sigh next decade, and a new crack will open twelve paces off. The power did not touch the slow arithmetic of the place. It would not have known how to improve it.

The frogs may be a little less lonely. A few from the far pools may have crossed over to the near ones in the weeks after, and there may be more clutches in the bromeliad cups than there would have been. This is possible and not provable.

The old viscacha still sits at the rim, facing the drop. Sometimes she turns her head to the empty air beside her, the way she did that dusk, as if to a companion she cannot place. In high winds the rope on the bell sways and the clapperless bell knocks its dull tap against the column. She does not turn for that. She has heard it all her life, and she knows what it is.

The power went on. It had been asked for nothing and had given little, and it counted that as the right proportion. It carried away the memory of eleven seconds in a green room the mountain kept warm.
