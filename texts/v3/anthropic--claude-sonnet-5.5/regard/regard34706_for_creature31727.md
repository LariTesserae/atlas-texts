# What the Attention Did

It arrived at dusk, because that is when the plateau is most itself. It took no shape at first. It was a quality of noticing that settled over the tableland the way the virga settle over the rim ranges, and for a while it only looked.

It saw the lickers file to the walls in their order, mothers first. It saw the fox in the shadow of the sinter rim, chin on stone, and a calf drift one stride too far. It did not intervene. The fox ate that night, and the herd closed over the gap the way water closes over a dropped stone. The attention understood that this was not a flaw in the place. It was the place. The whole plateau is a machine for turning heat into hunger and hunger into a tight, unloud abundance, and a mercy that stopped the fox would have been a small murder of the wader, the shrimp, and the felted orange mats beneath everything.

So it made a list of the things it would not do.

It would not bring rain. The virga are the plateau's argument with the sky, and the weeping walls exist because the argument goes unresolved.

It would not mend the old lickers' teeth. A drifting animal at the edge of the herd is grief, but it is also the herd's oldest way of handing the wall to the young.

It would not cool the ground, fill the ruins, remove the station, or make the turbine louder or quieter. The hum was part of the place's ear now.

Then it came, at last, to the woman.

## The step

She was sitting on the step of the turbine hall with her back against the warm wall, as she did most nights. It did not startle her. Something in her had been waiting a long time for company without hoping for it, and that kind of waiting doesn't startle. She felt the dusk change weight beside her, and she kept her hands on her knees.

It wondered whether to speak. Most of what it could say would be noise. It could tell her she was seen, but she knew that: the lickers let her pass, and the foxes knew her limp for a limp. It could tell her that her life had meant something, but the sentence has been said to too many people at too many bedsides, and it has worn smooth as a coin.

So it waited, and after a time she spoke first.

"The eastern line leaks," she said. "Has for years. I write *monitored*."

"I know," it said. "I watched who drinks."

That was all it needed to say, and it was the only true gift available at that moment. Her secret had been kept in a monthly line of ink that no one read, a lie that was also true, and she had carried it alone with the vague guilt of someone who has broken a small rule out of a large loyalty. Now it was witnessed, and not as a violation. It was witnessed as a thing well done. She let out a breath she had held perhaps eleven years, and the thin air took it up as steam.

## The one act

The attention could have sealed the flange, or left it and guaranteed it forever, but that would have made the leak into a promise. The leak was already a kind of promise, and a fragile one, since it lasted only as long as she did. Someday a contractor would arrive with a clipboard and a new gasket, or the company would remember its obligations all at once, and the eastern drip would be tidied out of existence in an afternoon. The herds would come at dusk to a dry hollow and stand there, puzzled, with gray tongues.

So it did one small thing, in the plateau's own grammar.

That night, along the eastern line, the mineral crust that already scabbed the joints began to grow a little faster than it should have. It was nothing violent. It was the ordinary work of silica and calcite laid down layer by layer, and the attention only compressed some years of it into a few hours. By dawn the flange wore a rim of cream and rust, and the drip had begun to find its own path through the deposit. Over the following seasons the sinter would build a lip, then a bowl, then a terrace. At some point the water would stop being a leak in a pipe and become a seep in a hill. It would draw on the same heat and the same buried condensate, but it would be held by stone. It would be a weeping wall of its own, and it would go on after the pipe was gone and the steel was sulfur-yellow and the hall was another ruin for the foxes to den in.

The lickers would never know that their new wall had begun as a woman's small disobedience. That seemed right to the attention. The best gifts become landforms and lose their donors.

## What it left alone

It did not extend her life. She had a share of years like everything else here, and the number was hers.

It did not send her a successor, or tell her one would come. If someone came, it should be because the place called them, the way it had called her.

It did not tell her what it was. She never asked, and it took that as her answer.

It did, before leaving, do one thing for the night itself, and she never knew. Her breath had been tight for some weeks, the ordinary tightness of an old body at five thousand meters. That night, while she slept in the lee of the turbine hall with the hum coming up through the floor, the air in her small room was a little richer than the air outside. It was not enough to notice or to change anything, only enough for one easy night of sleep.

## Morning

The fog lifted from the pools in the pink light. The lizards pressed their bellies to the vent-lips. The scavengers rose on the first thermals from the stack, tilting on sulfur air, and one of them, unaccountably, rode a long way out along the eastern line and circled the flange twice before sliding away.

The woman woke, oiled the valves, read the gauges, and walked out to the eastern line with her wrench, as she did every month. She stood a while looking at the new cream-colored rim on the joint. Then she took out her log, wrote *minor seepage, monitored*, and this time understood exactly what the words meant.

Somewhere above the rim ranges the afternoon clouds were beginning to build, and the plateau went on as it had, with a little more water in it than before.
