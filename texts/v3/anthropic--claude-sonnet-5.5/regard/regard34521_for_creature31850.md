# The Attention

It came down the stair on an ordinary morning, and the first thing it did was nothing.

That was deliberate. It had seen ten thousand places, and the ones it had ruined were always the ones it had loved fastest. So before it touched anything it made a list of what it would not do.

It would not repair the machine. The Columns were not failing, and even if they were, a machine that a hundred and forty people keep alive is a different thing from a machine that keeps itself alive. The upkeep was not a cost of the place. It was the place.

It would not tell them who the makers were, or where they went, or what the Sitter had been. The Tenders had a hut, a bench, and a socket, and they swept the socket every morning with no one assigning it. That was a kind of knowledge no fact could improve.

It would not lengthen their lives, light their lamps with better oil, or dry their blankets. Dryness was their one great luxury, and a luxury given to everyone by a stranger is not a luxury anymore. It is a debt.

And it would not become a god. There was room for one. The hut was right there. That was exactly why it would not.

That left very little. It found that very little was enough to work with.

## The Breath

The stair was thirty shallow cups of warm water, and at the bottom the noise closed over it like a lid. A child took its hand without speaking. Children never hesitate at the sight of a stranger. That is the adults' habit, learned later.

It let itself be led across the braided floor and set in the ring of seven pylons. The cone of quiet formed. For the first time in an age it heard its own breathing, which surprised it, because it had not known it was breathing until the roar stepped back.

It had one breath to say who it was.

It could have said a great deal. It could have said *I am old and wide and I mean you well*, and the Tenders, watching its mouth, would have thought it a fool and been right. Instead it drew in over its teeth, badly, in the reversed whisper it had learned from the sound of the place before it arrived, and said the one word it had heard the basin say to itself, over and over, since long before any Tender was born.

*Here.*

A child of the Tenders beside the ring made a small noise. A Reseater in her sling, forty paces up, stopped moving. The roar came back in, and the visitor was offered a dry cot.

## The Season

It stayed a season. It did not say what it was and was not asked, because a question costs a breath, and the Tenders spend breath the way other people spend blood.

It learned the knuckle-language first, badly, then less badly. It learned that the name of the place is a rhythm and that a curtain of flowstone rings a fourth lower than its neighbor. It pressed its forehead to the wall beside a Listener whose eyes had gone milky and whose face was calm as a drawn curtain, and it felt what she felt: the whole basin as one enormous sound, with the flat note of a Column somewhere in it.

It went up with the Reseaters and felt a filament crack past its hand. It went down with a Borewright in the thirty breaths of a Turn and counted them. It saw that the man beside it was already deaf at thirty-one. It saw how quickly his hands worked, and that he was not afraid, because fear takes time and he had none.

It sat with the Dish-watchers on their ledge, looking up at the glint, and it understood the dish before they did.

The dish was not an instrument for aiming the Sheets. The Sheets were only what the dish did on the way to what it was for. Once an hour, for longer than the tide-lines had been tilted, the dish had turned a few degrees toward the ring of sky and said something. It said it in light, in a long ripple across the basin that the Tenders counted and knotted and never once read. It was a question, put out patiently once an hour for an unknowable time.

*Is anyone there?*

And the Turn, the silence and the one drawn word in the ring, was the other half. It was the basin answering its own makers' question with the only answer it had: *I am here. Accounted for. Still open.* The Tenders had been saying it for the sky, not for the machine, all this time.

Nothing had answered the dish, or nothing that could be heard from underground. That was the wound in the place. It was not the silt or the deafness or the dead Borewrights. For all those centuries the basin had been asking, and the asking had gone unheard.

## What it did

It did three things.

**The first was the dish.** On the last night of its season, high above the floor where nobody could climb, it did what could be done from where it was. It did not seize the dish. It only answered it in the language the dish could hear, a small change of angle. The dish swung its few degrees, and for the first time in its long life it did not swing back to where it had started. It held. The Sheets answered one by one in the long ripple, and the ripple did not fade. Where a Sheet passed through a column of mist, the rainbow that formed stayed where it was. It did not shatter when the gust came.

It stood for one whole Turn.

The Tenders stopped in the silence and looked up at a hard-edged arc of colour that had not moved in forty minutes. Then the Columns restarted, out of order, and the arc thinned and went out like a breath on glass. The Dish-watchers argued about it for a season. In the end they tied a new knot into the Elder's root, an irregular one, at the height of a hand. They did not know what it meant, but they knew it was to be remembered.

**The second was for the Borewrights.** In the dark of the bores it found the places where the silt made its thickest stand and shifted a single shelf of stone. It was hardly a change at all. A man could not have found it with a scraper. But the silt that had once clung to the throats of the Columns now fell, by the ordinary weight of water, into a catch-basin near the base, where a Borewright could reach it in ten breaths rather than thirty.

The work remained. It was still dark, still dangerous, still done in the held-breath interval of a Turn. Nobody would call it easy. But the Borewrights of the next generation retired at fifty rather than at thirty, with their hearing worn rather than gone. They talked to their grandchildren in the ring and were understood, and nobody ever knew why.

It left the change where nobody would find it, so that no one would be grateful and no one indebted. It had learned that a thanked mercy grows a temple.

**The third was the Turn.**

On its last day, it went to the ring without a child to lead it and stood outside the seven pylons. Beside them the Answerer for that rota, a young woman with steady hands and a clear inner lid, was waiting for the amphibians to tilt. The Tilters tilted. A child tapped once. Every hand in the basin came down. The Reseaters hung in their slings, and the Columns fell silent one after another like a struck chord dying.

In the last drop of silence the Answerer stepped into the ring and drew the word in over her teeth.

*Here.*

And the Attention, standing at the edge with its forehead pressed to the nearest pylon like a Listener, did not answer her. That was the third thing, and the hardest. It had come a very long way to say *I hear you*, and it did not say it, because saying it would have made her the audience of a god instead of the keeper of a vow.

Instead it listened. It listened with everything it had, to the drip and the ticking of drops on the Sheets and the settling of the stone and to her breath as it went out. It gave the silence its whole attention. The Answerer felt it as pressure in the roots of her teeth, a weight in the quiet that she had never felt before. There was someone in it.

Then, from the hut by the eastern wall, in the socket where something used to sit, the Listeners felt the hum. They did not feel it faintly this time. It was steady enough to be sure of. It lasted the length of a held breath. The eldest of them pressed her forehead harder against the stone, and her face, calm as flowstone, did something it had not done in forty years.

The Columns started again, out of order, and the rain re-planned itself.

## After

The Attention left by the stair, thirty warm steps and then ordinary morning. It told nobody it was going.

The next day a child went to the hut to sweep the bench, as a child had every morning for longer than anyone could count. The bench was already dry. It was drier than any surface in the basin had a right to be. The child stood a moment with the broom. Then she swept it anyway, cleared the socket of silt, and went to lie on the warm limestone with her chin on her arms and her eyes on the Tilters.

The Tenders say nothing about any of this. They tap the old rhythms on the old curtains, and the Turns come every thirty or forty minutes, and they say the word into the quiet. But the Dish-watchers have started keeping a second cord alongside the first, and on it they tie a knot once an hour when the dish swings. They do not know why. They only feel that for the first time the swing is a sentence that has been finished, and someone should be recording the pause where an answer would go.

Some mornings, in the moment after the last Column dies and before the Answerer draws breath, the whole basin waits. It has always waited. The difference is that now the waiting has a shape, as if it had been folded around something, the way a Sheet folds light. The Tenders do not say what they think is there. They say the word.

Somewhere far above, a dish holds still for the length of a held breath. Then it swings a few degrees and asks again, and this time it is not asking into nothing.
