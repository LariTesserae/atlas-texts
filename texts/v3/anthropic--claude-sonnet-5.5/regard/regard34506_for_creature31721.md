# What Came Up the Road

It did not come up the switchback road. The road was there, pale and patient, but the visitor was on the plateau before anyone could have said it had left the lowlands, the way a thought is in a mind before you notice thinking it. The air made no room for it, because the air was already as thick and warm as any air could be. It stood on a sinter causeway a meter above the standing water and did the first thing it always did in a new place, which was nothing.

It looked for the wound. Power like this is drawn by a wound, the way water is drawn downhill, and it had come expecting one: a hunger, a grief, some unbearable thing being borne. It walked the causeways for a long time, past the open doors and the glazed tin cups, past turbines turning clean and slow in their brick halls, and it kept finding the same answer. Nothing was wrong. Everything was very old, very tended, and very quiet, and it was complete in the way a held breath is complete.

So it began the harder work, which was deciding what not to do.

## The Refusals

It could have brought the builders back. It weighed this longest, because the empty chairs seemed to ask for it. But the builders had gone or changed or never been separate, and calling them up would only prove that the place had been waiting for owners. It hadn't. The threads did not wait. They listened, which is a different thing.

It could have explained the place to itself: told the mats they were a population, told the flies their day was one of many, told the salamanders they were the plumbers of something. Every one of those gifts was a small theft. The flies live their day. Naming it would give them a day that had been noticed and priced, and they would live it differently. The mats do not ask, and the visitor judged that their not asking was a kind of health.

It could have fixed the slow losses, the glaze and the century and the drip that dissolves one shape and lays down another. But the place loses its shape and regains it, always, and it counted that as metabolism, not damage. A world that is never allowed to erode has stopped being a place and become a monument.

It could have opened the sky, carried in green and wind, given the plateau a proper sun. It looked up at the copper coin in the steam, decided that the light was exactly the light the place was built to be seen by, and left it.

The visitor understood then what wisdom was for, in a place that needed no rescue. Wisdom was not the ability to do a great deal. It was the ability to tell which of the things you could do would leave the place less itself.

## The Runnel

At the edge of the far terraces it found one of the waders, stepping dam to dam on its narrow gray legs, and it followed at a distance, the way you follow a stranger who is going somewhere. The wader stopped where they always stop: a clogged runnel, a skin of foreign scum. It lowered its body and did the thing that cannot be seen.

The visitor came up beside it. It did not take over or offer to help. It knelt in the warm shallow water, which did not burn it, and put its hands to the other end of the same blockage, a hard little knot of mineral that the wader's reach could not quite get to. It worked the knot loose with its fingers, slowly, without hurry, as one might work at a knot in a sibling's shoelace.

The water ran. The wader stood a moment with its head turned, though it had no head to speak of, and then it went on to the next dam, and the visitor went with it. They mended four more runnels before the light in the glass bulbs began to rise.

This was the only thing the visitor did with its hands, and it was a small thing, of the same size and kind as everything else the place did for itself. Anything larger would have been a message that the place needed help. Nothing needed help. It was there as company.

## The Shaft

It did go down, once, along the spindle in the sinter that no one had followed. Being what it was, it could go where the heat and the dark did not matter, and it went a long way. It found what the belts feed. It did not linger. When it came back up it was quiet for a while, the way a person is quiet after reading a letter that was not addressed to them but was not hidden either.

It decided not to carry the answer up to the flies. It was not a dangerous answer, and it was not a secret. It was the sort of fact that lives properly only from the inside, in the ticking of governors and the slow turning of shafts, and would go dead in a sentence. The turbines had been saying it for longer than there had been anyone to ask. It let them keep saying it.

## The Tide

Then the eleventh hour came round, and it went to sit in a house.

It chose a foremen's house on the edge of a district, one with a table, two chairs, and a shelf with a tin cup. It chose the chair facing the steam-slot. It noticed, as it sat, that the hollow in the seat fitted almost no one, and that it fitted as if the fit had been left loose deliberately. It sat down in the warmth.

The bulbs brightened, ten thousand of them, green-white and steady across forty kilometers. The threads had tasted the change in the steam, and the whole animal drew breath. Then the wave came. It rolled out white and blind across the causeways, and the louvers on the windward side closed, and the bell-metal plates began their long chord, low enough to be felt in the ribs. The visitor sat through it with its hands open on its knees.

The steam did not stream evenly through the slot. It parted around the shape of the visitor, as it parts around the others, and closed again behind. And beside it, in the second chair, the vapor gathered for a moment into a seated shape, and it stayed there for the whole length of the chord.

Nothing was said, since neither party was a talker. But the visitor understood that it had been right about the chairs. They were not waiting for owners or masters or for the builders to come home. They were the place's way of keeping a seat open at the moment when the world exhales everything at once and no one can see their own hand. The threads did not need company to survive the tide. They had simply arranged, long ago, to have some. The hollows were an invitation, worn in by whoever had accepted it, again and again, for longer than the glaze had been forming.

The visitor accepted it. That was the whole of its gift.

## The Lights

When the steam cleared and every surface was newly beaded, the bulbs did what they always did, and began to dim toward their resting green.

Then, at the intersection nearest the house, one bulb held. It stayed a breath longer than it should have, a small stubborn brightness in the cloudy water. The next bulb held too, and the next, in a wave that rolled outward across the shelf from district to district until the whole plateau glowed a moment longer than the tide required. It was an answer, or the nearest thing the threads had to one. Then the lights eased down together and were only the ordinary lights again.

The visitor got up. It rinsed nothing, because there was nothing to rinse. It touched the tin cup once with a fingertip and put it back exactly where it had been.

## What Changed

Nothing you could measure. The mats kept their borders and the flies their stations. The turbines turned. The salamanders slid through their scalding drains, and the snails kept up their crisping on the tower walls. At the last crag, where the copper coin of the sun goes down through the steam, the green lights came up across the shelf, patient and even.

Only one thing was different, and it was small. In a foremen's house on the edge of a district, in the chair facing the steam-slot, beside a seat worn deep by many sitters, there was a second hollow, shallower than the rest and newly warm. It was the shape of someone who had stayed exactly as long as they were welcome.

The glaze will find it within a decade. The terraces will take it in a century. By then it will be a rounded curve in the white like every other curve, and the house will hold it without remark, one more soft place in a city where nothing stays sharp and nothing that mattered is ever lost.
