# What Was Done

It did not arrive. Arriving implies a place it had not been, and the fog had always been a little more than fog. It only noticed, the way one notices a sound that has been going on for some time, that the shelf on the mountain was *saying* something, and that it was worth stopping for.

So it stopped. The cloud on the shelf thickened by a shade no one measured, and the town took it for weather.

## What it considered

It was wise, which meant it went slowly, and it was benevolent, which meant the slowness cost it something. There was a great deal it could have done, and it looked at each thing honestly.

It could have stilled the slope. No more slides, no more terraces sliding into the dark, no more mornings when the horn sounds twice and a child is dragged out of sleep by the ear of the whole town. It turned this over for some time. Then it watched the chain of hands form at a wall that had let go the week before: stone, basket, log, a grandmother passing a rock the size of her head to a boy who could barely take it. They were laughing. Someone had dropped a basket on someone's foot and the whole line had a fit. The wall came back with the stones the mountain had lent it, and afterward six hundred people ate together with mud to the elbow. It understood that the slope was not their affliction. The slope was, in a sense, their *congregation*, and it left the slope alone.

It could have given them a dry place: a second kiln court, a hundred of them, a warm room in which the Kiln-scheduler might sleep without being negotiated with. It considered how much quiet power that post held, and how the town held it in check with courtesy and a little terror, and how a hundred kiln courts would dissolve that whole delicate arrangement. It let it stand.

It could have stopped the green tide. Just for a season, just so the Roof-keepers could rest their knees. But it watched an old woman on a ridge, capped in reed, deciding with a curved knife exactly how much green a roof might keep. Her whole art was in the word *exactly*, and there was no art in a world where the moss had stopped.

It could have taught them things: iron, the plow, medicine for the coughing that comes from all that smoke. It set this aside more quickly than the rest. They were not lacking knowledge. They had a religion of moving, and they had never once mistaken it for an argument about progress. Someone who has mastered the wringing of a cloud does not need to be handed a better cloth.

The dead it thought about longest, because it could have done the most there and the town would have wanted it least. The dead are laid in the terraces below the shelf and given to the ferns within a season, and no one marks the place. It could have kept their names. It could have remembered every one of them, and no one would ever have known. But the town had chosen the ferns on purpose. They believed the mountain keeps things by making them into more of itself, and it was not clear to the power that this was wrong. It kept the names anyway, silently, the way one keeps a stone in a pocket, and asked nothing of them.

## What it did

There were three things.

The first was small and it did it in the manner of the place. On a lane below the market, under a tree fern, a gutter had begun to stammer. A hand's width of drowned leaf sat in the channel, and the water was working at it and getting nowhere. The rules are plain: a blocked gutter is a public offense, and anyone who sees one clears it. The power had seen it. It took up a hook, one of the long ones left leaning against a wall, and lifted out the leaves. It also lifted out a small brown frog, very gently, and set it on the nearest wet stone. Then it put the hook back where it had been, at the same angle. The water ran clean and the stammer left it.

The second was on a bridge. There is a bridge of bamboo and vine cord above the upper lane, and the rule is that the last person to cross inspects it aloud, so that others hear. The power crossed it. Halfway over, it felt the cord under its weight and read the whole story of it: the cordwright's twist, the season's wear, one strand near the west lashing that had begun, a very little, to tick. It did not mend the strand. It said, in the flat, forward-pitched voice of the town, so that it carried:

*"West lashing. Ticking. Sound for today."*

Nobody was on the bridge, but a Roof-keeper on the ridge above heard it clearly, because roofs are where every lane can be overheard. She frowned at the empty bridge and told three people before noon. By afternoon a Cordwright had gone up with a coil of fresh vine, and the west lashing was renewed by dusk, which was how it would always have gone, just a little sooner. That was all the power wanted: to be one voice in the chain, not the whole chain.

The third thing it did far below where anyone would look.

Under the kiln court, deep in the old gray stone, there is a seam. The rock has stopped arguing with the water, but the rock is not immortal, and in this one place, three hundred fathoms down and very slowly, the shelf had begun to part from the mountain. It was not a wall or a terrace or a garden. It was the shelf, all of it, the stilted houses, the market, the six hundred, the Cooper in his lower basin. It would go in one breath, in some year no living person would see, and no horn could answer it, because a horn calls people to a wall and this would leave no wall to run to.

This was the one thing the town could not do, and so it was the one thing the power did. It went down through the andesite and closed the seam, not by making the rock immortal, which would have been a lie, but by giving it ten thousand years more of the patience it already had. It left the surface exactly as it was: weeping, restless, prone to small slides and rebuilt walls. The horn would still sound twice. The chain of hands would still form. It had only taken away the one catastrophe that would have made the rest of it meaningless.

The rule it followed was simple and it did not have to think about it long. *Take what the town cannot carry. Leave everything it can.*

## The night

When evening dropped the temperature, the power did not go. It sat at the edge of the lower stair, where the shallow bowls in the stone ring faintly, with its hands, whatever they were, in its lap.

The lamps came on, amber and blurred. Somewhere someone drummed on a hollow log to call a child in. The frogs came up to full chorus, and the Wrung fell into the habit older than speech and listened, six hundred of them in the wet dark, and the power listened with them.

It heard the tink like hammered nails and the rising whistle. It heard the drip from every leaf and the shifting of small animals in the thatch. It heard, from the lower basins, nothing at all where the Cooper's hollow bonk should have been.

The Cooper had gone quiet, and the town noticed, as it always did. Down the lanes, low, a few people asked after him. The power could have prompted the frog, or sent it a small warmth, or a moth. It did not. The frog was resting, and the town's relief when it started up again would be theirs to have.

Toward the middle of the night, from the dark below the stair, came one bonk, hollow as a knock on a barrel. A great, general, unspoken exhale went through the doorways. Someone laughed, low. Someone answered.

The power stayed until the laughter was done, and then it was only the fog on the shelf again, a shade thicker than weather, and thinning.

## Morning

The Webwalkers went ahead with their sticks, solemn as judges, and gathered the night's webs onto the tips like spun sugar.

Nothing was different. A roof-keeper mentioned the voice on the bridge and was told to stop being fanciful. A Hooker noticed that the north gutter was running very clean and said nothing, having been trained not to trust good luck out loud. A child born in the small hours under the last hollow bonk of the night was named for it, and would grow up carrying the name, *Hollowknock*, into a town that stood, without ever knowing why, on a mountain ten thousand years steadier than the one its grandmothers had known.

*It will grow back,* they said, about a cut vine and a fallen wall, and no one heard the small difference in the saying.
