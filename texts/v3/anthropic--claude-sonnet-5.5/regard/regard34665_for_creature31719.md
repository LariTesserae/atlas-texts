# The Attention

It does not descend. Descending would imply a place it had been before, and it has not been anywhere in the sense the floor would recognize. It falls the way the snow falls, slowly and unremarked, as one more thing in the thickening. The plankton flakes drift through it. A shrimp in the sponge forest tastes the water, finds nothing, and goes on eating.

It takes a long time to arrive, and it does this on purpose. Anything that came faster would arrive as an event, and this is a place where events are storms.

## What it considers

It has the power to do a great deal, and the first thing it learns is how much of that is unusable here.

*Light.* It could fill the column with day. But the dark is not an absence to be repaired. The sparks, the blue smear along a worm's flank, and the green pinprick that goes out when you turn toward it are a language spoken by everything with eyes, and daylight would drown it. The octopus would go blind in a world she had been reading fluently.

*Warmth.* The cold is in every rate the place runs on: the nodule's million-year millimeter, the sponge's slow inch, her own long years. Warm the water and you don't comfort the place. You end it.

*Food.* This is the tempting one, because it fits the grammar. A whale could fall on the terrace, and for a decade the plain would be a city. But the city would close over as everything does, and for ten years she would live beside a feast that had no place in the life she was built for, and afterward she would be an octopus who remembered abundance in a world that had gone back to snow. It also would not be kind to the ten thousand other rings within a day's drift, whose owners would starve a little more because the scavengers had gone elsewhere.

*Teaching.* Here it stays longest. She lives alone and dies alone, and her young are born into a silence. It could put knowledge into them, or into her: stone-craft, storm-craft, the names of things. But the knowledge here is in the stones. Each generation reads the ring and finds a stone moved by an arm it never met, and that reading is the whole of their inheritance. Give them instruction and they stop reading, and the ring becomes a pile.

*Explanation.* It could tell her what the flint is. It could tell her that a hand made it, that the hand had a name, and that the ground was once dry. She would not understand, and the understanding she has is better. She has a cold, sharp thing that was made, and she touches it as she passes. That is already the truth of it.

*Removal.* It could lift her out of the storms, out of the dark and the hunger and the long guarding that ends in death beside the eggs. It thinks about this the longest of all, and it does not do it, though not because suffering is good. She would be someone else. The storm is what the door is for. A creature without a door to close is not a rescued octopus but a different animal in a nicer place, and the octopus on the terrace would simply be gone.

## The partiality

It admits something to itself before going on. There are ten thousand rings on this plain and a hundred million bodies in the ooze beneath it, and it cannot attend to all of them. It has chosen her because attention has to land somewhere, and because of an arm that healed short and a flake wedged edge-out in a wall. It does not tell itself she is more deserving than the brittle star at the edge of the carpet or the shrimp couple living sideways on a fallen stalk. She isn't. The choosing is arbitrary, and it decides that arbitrariness is a reason for gentleness, not a license.

So it sets itself a rule. It will do only what the place itself could have done: a current that might have turned, a grain that might have slid. It will do each thing once. And it will leave no shape in the world that the world could not heal.

## The storm

It arrives at the terrace three days before the hiss.

She does not see it. Nothing here could. But it settles, or seems to, at the edge of her ring, and it gathers itself to about the mass and stillness of a large nodule. It takes on the patience of the oldest stones in her wall, the ones she never moved because they were heavy and held. It watches her rearrange a plate. It watches her turn the flint, not looking at it, just resting an arm against it, the way you'd steady yourself on a wall in the dark.

It knows who made the flint. That much of the past it can see: a woman kneeling at a stream that no longer exists, irritated at a core that wouldn't fracture right, then striking once more, and the flake coming free clean. She looked at it a moment and put it in a pouch and never thought of it again. She is now the same slow sediment as the shells.

It does not tell the octopus this. But when the octopus's arm passes over the edge, the power holds still, and lets the touch be received. Two hands, the same gesture, across a span of time that has no word. The woman never knew her flake would be a doorframe. The octopus does not know it was ever anything else. It thinks this is the nearest thing to a conversation the place has ever had.

When the hiss begins upcurrent, it does not quiet it. The floor lifts into the water. The brittle stars burrow, the cucumbers rise and drift, and the sponge forest bows. Far upslope a shelf-edge slab starts to give, and here the power does the one thing it permits itself. It turns the coming flow a few degrees, no more than an eddy might have, so that the dense river of mud runs past the terrace instead of across it. It costs the ring nothing and the neighbors nothing, since the flow finds another channel through ground that was going to be scoured anyway. It does not save her. It saves the ring, the stones, and the many hands' worth of arrangement in them. The flint's edge stays turned toward the dark.

She drags her shell across the entrance, braces her arms against the wall, and shuts her eyes.

The power sits outside like a stone, and the storm goes over both of them.

## The hatching

She does not live to see the young. This it cannot change and does not try to. Her time is the size it is, and lengthening it by decades would only make her a stranger to her own body. When the eggs have gone their long years in the cold and she has become a husk beside them, it is there.

The first hours are the dangerous ones. The young come out the size of a grain of rice, pale and soft, into water that is full of mouths. Amphipods find them first. For the length of one night the power does what she did in the storms. It becomes a door. It is a heavy stillness on the upcurrent side of the ring, slow and unremarkable, and the drift bends around it, and the scavengers that would have come do not quite come. When the young push out through the gaps between the stones, most of them find the dark empty, and they scatter across the plain to be what they will be.

Then it goes, and it does not come back. A second night would make it a god, and the young would learn to wait for it and lose the habit of the stones.

## What it carries away

Nothing is left. The dent where it sat is a shallow scallop in the ooze, and the next season's silt fills it. The ring is a little older and a little more itself. If a young octopus comes to it and moves a plate, no one will ever know that the wall stood through a storm it was not meant to survive.

The power keeps one thing. In the whole long depth of what it has watched, it has seen one being touch a made thing and know that it was made without knowing by whom, and treat that as reason enough to keep it. That is an act of respect across a gap no one can cross, and the power is not sure it has ever seen a purer one. It carries that up through the thickening, slowly, as it came, and the snow falls past it the whole way.

Below, the water clears. The plain looks exactly as it did before. It is not the same plain at all.
