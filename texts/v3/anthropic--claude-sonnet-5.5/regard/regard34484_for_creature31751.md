# What the Attention Did

It came as a change in the quality of noticing, the way you sense a person has entered a dark room before you hear them. The clockers didn't feel it. The bats didn't. The Ear, asleep in the hammock among the trusses, turned over once and said a word that wasn't a word, and slept on.

Being wise, it did not act at once.

## What It Considered, and Set Down

It saw the whole bowl in a moment: the seep on the northern wall, thin as a held breath, and the fault far below that fed it. The engines' four-second stroke. Forty-some people who had never once been dry. And it saw how many things it could do.

It could have mended the engines so they would never fail. It rejected this first. The Ear's whole life is the possibility of failure, and to abolish that would be to abolish the Ear, and with the Ear the small human attention the basin has grown around itself. A heartbeat with no one listening is only a mechanism.

It could have told them why the engines were built. It knew, or could find out: some ore, some contract, some long-dead company's purpose. But the Tenders' ignorance is not a wound. It is the thing that turned a business into a vow. Hand them the reason and they would have a reason, and reasons expire. Vows go on.

It could have cured them of damp, of cracked lips, of the short lives in a wet place. It could have brought the light-column down to warm their hands. But the mat takes back whatever is set down on it, and the Tenders know this better than any people alive. They build so that things may be lifted in. A gift that cannot be lifted in would sit in that green world like a stone in a throat.

So it made a rule for itself, and the rule was the mat's own: *set nothing down that you are not prepared to lose.*

## The One Thing It Did Without Being Asked

There was one act it allowed itself, and it was invisible, because it was a kind of weather.

The sinkhole was very slowly closing. Nothing dramatic, only a few centimeters per century of limestone thinking about its own weight. The coin of sky would someday be a smaller coin. And far down, the fracture that fed the northern seep was silting, grain by grain.

It went into the deep rock and loosened one grain and settled another. It moved a single joint of stone a finger's width. It did this so gently that no seismograph on the surface would have stirred, and no Tender would ever know the seep had been going to fail. This was not a gift. A gift is something you can point at. It was closer to holding a door open with a foot while someone else carries in the groceries, and then leaving before they turn around.

It considered this the outer limit of what it could do without being asked. It kept the sky open and the water coming. Everything inside the bowl belonged to the bowl.

## Being Named

Then it took the spiral stair, all ninety meters of it, as a stranger does. Somewhere past the sixtieth its footsteps changed, because the air changed. It let the green in.

It stood in a terrace of mat near the rowhouses, and it was still for a long time. Small things began to explore it, and it permitted them. A springtail crossed its wrist. A pale beetle considered its shoe. A sweep gang went over on the root-bridge above, and the lead sweep stopped, put its long fingers to the rail as if to feel for a vibration, and trilled something to the others. They passed on without alarm. The basin had decided, in its slow way, that this was not a threat.

At the hour before dark a woman crossed the terrace, moss in the seams of her coat, and studied it. She cocked her head west, toward the humming bells, and north, toward the ticking. Then she made a soft click and a whistle, and the frogs shifted half a tone.

It did not know what it had been called. It understood that it never would, that this was proper, and that the name was the first thing it had been given that it could keep.

## What Was Asked

The Tenders do not ask for things. They murmur, they wait, they mend what can be mended. But in the course of that season a man called Four-Tick Marrow came to it at night with his child in his arms. The child was hot and small and breathing badly, and he said nothing, because to say something would have been to ask.

It did not produce a cure from nowhere. That would have been a stone in a throat. Instead it walked with him, at engine tempo, to a place at the foot of a rotting stiltwood where a blue-grey mold lay in the compost. The Tenders had walked past it for four hundred years. It said, *This was here already.* It said nothing else. He gathered it in a leaf and the mat did not lift it away, because it was already the mat's.

The fever broke, or it didn't. In this world it is possible not to know, and the Tenders have never held that against a mystery. The child was named at the next rain, as children are.

Near the end of that season a woman without kin grew old and lay down on a terrace. Her sitter was the stranger. It sat with her and said nothing, and did not move, and the springtails came, and then the beetles, and then the first millipede, forearm-thick and unhurried. When the outline of her began to fade, it murmured the phrase it had learned, and it meant it as they meant it, as both a eulogy and a receipt:

*Held long enough.*

## The Ear

On a night with no moon it climbed to the Engine House and sat among the sleeping bats, beneath the woven canopy, as the beam engines went *hwoom* around it.

The Ear woke. The Ear could feel it there, as the Ear feels everything: a hair late, or a hair early. The Ear said, in the clipped murmur, on the beat, "I have never been needed."

It did not lie. It said it did not know whether the engines had run because they were watched, or in spite of it. It said the answer to that question was not available to anyone, and might not exist.

Then it said the true thing, which was smaller: *When an engine idles, the frogs go silent within a minute. The clockers lose their rhythm. The bats leave. All of them are listening to something they cannot name. You are the one who listens on purpose.*

The Ear did not answer. But the next stroke arrived exactly on time, and the Ear, who could feel a quarter-second, felt that it had been received.

## The Tide

The last place it went was the southern ferns.

It went there by night, past the wicks standing in their small vigils, and they did not move as it passed. The mat trembled under its feet with a slow tide, and it stood and let the tide come. Something enormous went by in the deep green, a flank as broad as a doorway, dark and damp and unhurried, and it did not stop, and it did not look.

They walked together for a while. That was all. The Tide set the pace, and the pace was the engines' pace, or the engines' was the Tide's, and it could not tell which way the dependence ran, and did not try to find out. It felt, in the soles of its feet, a very old patience, one that matched its own and ran deeper. It felt something that was waiting for something to finish, and it understood that "finish" did not mean end. It meant what a sentence does when its last word arrives: a completion, not a death.

It did not tell the Tenders. Their not-knowing was load-bearing, and their private belief was closer to true than any correction could be. It only stood at the edge of the deep ferns until the Tide had passed, and the trough in the mat behind it filled itself back in, and so did the footprints of the one who had followed.

## What Was Left

Nothing, in the sense that matters here. Everything it had set down was lifted into the moss.

But in the first rain after it left (the light-column turned silver, and a hundred thousand voices went up together) a child was named. The phrase drawn from the chorus was an ordinary one, a frog-call, a bell-pitch. The human syllable that hung on it was ordinary too. The woman who gave it did not know why the name felt, to her, like something already answered.

The engines beat on. *Hwoom.* Four seconds. *Hwoom.*

And somewhere in a century it will not count, it may come down the stair again, and the air will change at the sixtieth step, and a woman with moss in the seams of her coat will cross a terrace at the hour before dark and make a small sound, the beginning of a name. It will be a little embarrassed, as strangers always are. And the frogs will shift, half a tone, toward it.
