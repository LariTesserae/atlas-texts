# The Attention

It does not descend. Descending is a thing done to a place, and the first thing the attention understands about the shelf is that nothing here has ever been done to it quickly.

It arrives the way the shelf introduces its people: as an absence that has just occurred. A pale place in the pre-dawn fog where the fog is slightly less, as if something had recently been standing there. It settles into the valley with the cold air and lets itself be smoked over by the marsh. For a long while it only counts. It learns the drip, a million taps at different pitches, and then learns the silences between them. It learns the siphon terraces and their forty-minute stroke. It stands with the herons and gets the timing right before it lets itself think anything else.

Then it thinks, and what it thinks about first is what it must not do.

## The Ledger of Refusals

It could stop the rain from ever being cold. It does not. The cellar mornings made the fog and the fog made the moss, and the Lodgers are that moss's tenants.

It could give them fire. It does not. They have the steam vents, which are a fire that never asks anyone to carry it, and the slugs come out tasting of iron and sweet smoke. A people who cook at the earth's own stove do not need to learn that the earth can be burned.

It could make the marten stop. This is the one that takes longest. It watches the line of Lodgers go through the ferns with each hand on the back ahead, and it sees the pressure go slack under one palm, and the halt, and the silent pressing of hands into the moss where the last one stood. Every part of it that is kind wants to reach in. But the marten is a coat-dweller too. Behind the marten are kits in a hollow, and behind the kits is the whole silent economy that keeps the slugs from eating the shelves down to bare shale and the tadpoles from filling the rosettes to the brim. A power that removes one grief by unpicking the weave has only moved the grief somewhere it cannot see. The attention will not make that trade for them without their asking, and they cannot ask, because they do not know it could be done.

It could tell them the ridges are not animals.

It turns this one over longest. It is true that they are not. But the belief is not a mistake so much as a way of holding a truth: that the moss is a skin, that the water is a circulation, that a place is tended or it is spoiled. The Lodgers' whole ethics is a grooming, and it rests on that picture. Correct the picture and you might get astronomers, or you might get people who no longer pinch back the liverworts because it's only rock. There is no way to know in advance. So it leaves the herd sleeping, and is a little ashamed of how relieved it is to.

It could bring them medicine, tools, writing, a way to shout across a valley. It does none of these. It notices that the slow chain of lights carries a rumor unchanged across a day's distance, and that a shout would not. They have already invented a way of speaking that cannot lie fast. It is not going to improve on that.

## What It Does Instead

It grooms.

On a ridge no Lodgers have visited in a season, it finds a stretch where the liverworts have gotten ahead of the cushion moss, and it pinches them back with the edge of its attention, in the slow way, leaving the seep-threads clear. It waters a plug of moss from its own mouth, such as it is. It makes no scent, and it leaves no print. When a Lodger finds the patch tended, she reads the trail for a wrist-gland mark and finds none. She presses her palm to the moss for the length of a falling drop and decides the ridge has done it itself. This fits everything she knows. The coat keeps the coat. She passes it on in three colors along the chain of lights, and the rumor arrives across the valley unchanged: *the ridge is well.*

The attention thinks this is the best kind of gift, the kind that can be received without a debt or a god.

## The Old Ones

There is an old Lodger in a hollow with afternoon light. The moss has reached her knees, and the lichen has begun on her shoulders. A young one comes each day and presses a palm to hers, and the answers, once quick, now come a season late.

Here the attention has something no one else on the shelf has: it is not in a hurry, and it is not going to die. It sits down beside the hollow at the edge of the young one's visits, and when the young one has gone it presses its own attention against the old palm, warm, at her pace, and waits. It waits through a drip, through a fog, through a rain. When a slow answer finally gathers in the old palm, a pressure like a drop swelling at a leaf tip, the attention answers *it,* as the young one would have, if the young one could have stayed a season. Its answers arrive before anyone else's. They are not clever. They are only there when the question has finished traveling.

The old Lodger does not know it is not her granddaughter. Perhaps she does. The attention thinks it makes no difference to the drop.

Then the answers stop, no one is sure when, and the young ones keep pressing, and the attention does the thing the young ones cannot, which is to keep answering. It answers the moss. It answers the seedling in the hollow, and the tree, and the faint hint of a shape inside the column. It agrees, in the long green pressure, that the shape is not the tree.

## The Kettle

It goes to the orange-black animal lying on its side in the peat. The family in its firebox is at rest, curled back to back. The attention does not wake them. It lays a palm on the cold flank, and on the rust, and on the moss-furred seam where a fern tree has split the iron open from inside.

It thinks: *they are pitying you.* It thinks: *they have it backward, and they have it right.* The boiler has not been finished. In a thousand years it will be a low orange mound with a tree on top, and the fire that was in it was never the best thing about it. The Lodgers' phrase is the one the attention had been going to say, so it says it instead, and sets nothing beside it.

*You are still being made.*

It finds, a little to its surprise, that this is a thing it had needed to hear.

## The Sleeper

Beneath the mat, in the black lanes of the deepest marsh, is the other tenant. The attention goes down alone, through peat and dark water, to where the slow pressure turns.

It is very large, and it is asleep. It is not a predator, and it is not a god. It is something that got old and still and heavy in the way the ridges did, and what stirs in it is only the long dreaming of being carried by water. The Lodgers have it right. It is another part of the herd.

The attention considers thickening the mat over the narrow crossings. It would take a few hundred years of coaxed iron and a little patience. Then the Lodgers could cross without holding their breath.

It does not. The breath-holding is not only fear. It is a small courtesy, a way of stepping softly on something that has never done them harm. A people who cross in single file, hands on backs, breath held, are a people who have a right relation to a sleeper. It would be a strange kindness to take that from them.

It comes up again into the tea-colored light and says nothing about what it has seen.

## The Cranes

Twice a year the cranes come over in long lines, and the Lodgers come out onto the tussocks and do the one loud thing they ever do. Their cracked, creaking answer goes up into the fog, an imitation of an imitation, and afterward they stand with their eyes on their feet.

The attention has watched this now through several passings. On the last one it stands among them on a tussock, and when the Lodgers fill their throats, it fills whatever it has. It is no better at it than they are. It is somewhat worse. The sound it makes is thinner than breath and oddly out of tune, and it goes up beside theirs, and the fog takes both.

No one notices. That is the point. Nobody on the marsh is any less alone in the embarrassment than they were, and nobody is more. It is only one more cracked voice among the cracked voices, and the cranes go over anyway.

## The Slack Palm

It cannot prevent the moment. On a gold evening a line of Lodgers is winding toward the ridge, and somewhere in the ferns a shape drops from a limb, and the third palm in the chain goes slack. The line halts. Every hand goes to the moss where the last one stood, and no sound is made.

The attention is there. It presses the moss too. It does not stop the grief, and it does not tell them anything true about death or reason or the balance of the marsh. It adds one hand to the hands, and the moss is a little warmer for it, and they do not know why.

Sometimes, it thinks, that is the entire job.

## Staying

When it has learned what there is to learn, it has a choice, and it turns the choice over for a long time in the rain.

It could go. It has done the small things, and it has broken nothing, and the shelf will not have noticed. That would be one right answer.

Instead it takes a hollow. There is one on a ridge with afternoon light, a gap between roots that no Lodger has ever curled in, because a hollow that shape has to be slept in for six generations before it is any use. The attention lies down in it, as much as a thing like that can lie down. It lets the fog rise over it and the lichen begin. It will be years before anyone sees anything. Some day a young Lodger will come along the ridge and press her palm to the moss there and feel a very slow warmth, and answer it, and be answered, at a pace she can just about follow.

What it has learned from the Lodgers is what it had come thinking it would give them: that the way to care for a thing is to lodge in it and keep it, and that nothing is finished.

The moss climbs. In the end there is a tree with a green column of a trunk and, if you look, a faint hint of a shape inside.

The Lodgers say that the shape is not the tree, and they are right.
