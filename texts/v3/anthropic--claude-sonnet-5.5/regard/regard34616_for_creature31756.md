# The Guest in the Corridor

It could have come as anything, and it spent some time deciding what it should not do.

It should not stop the shudders. The ground slips because the plateau is still being made, and the people hold their breath and wait for the silt because that is how they have learned to live inside a world that is unfinished. A world that never interrupted itself would teach them nothing about waiting.

It should not give them fire, or metal, or the trick of clay. They have a way of building that is a conversation with a spring, and a gift that arrived faster than the spring could speak would end the conversation.

It should not cure the aching joints of those who rise too fast. The ache is the reason the young come back quieter, and the quiet is part of how the Lid stays a mystery instead of an errand. The power was not sure it had the right to take that from them, or to say what was on the other side, or to make a man who had touched the Lid speak again before his year was up.

It should not come as a drifter. The people stop work and knock once, softly, for the drifter, without knowing what it is. To borrow that reverence would be a small theft.

It should not be worshipped. That went without saying.

What was left was small. It could ask to come in.

---

The plateau has a word for a stranger, *the one still in the corridor*, and the power took it literally. It chose a house on a middle terrace, cream-walled and old, whose front door had narrowed to a slot a thin person could pass through sideways. It did not pass through. It stood in the lane at dusk, in the amber spill of a lamp-jar, and knocked on the wall.

It knocked badly. It had studied the grammar and had the pauses right but not the surfaces. It struck *wet* on a *hollow* wall and said something like *I am the floor of your house, and I am hungry*. Inside, a child laughed, and an older knock answered, dry and amused, correcting: *hollow*, *hollow*, *hollow*, as one slows down for a foreigner.

The woman who lived there was old enough to have lips at every knuckle. Her house-note was low and a little cracked. Her name was a rhythm, which the power had the grace not to translate into a word, but the neighbors called her Nine-Doors, because she had reopened her front entrance nine times and said so to anyone who stood still.

She put her cheek to the wall and listened for a long time.

Then she knocked, *who is in the corridor*, and the power answered with the only true thing it had: *one who would like to be in the room.*

The door was too narrow. She could see that, and so could it. It could have made itself small enough. She would not have stood for that, and neither would the custom: you do not let someone in through a door you have let close. She went to the shelf for her chisels and mallet and began, that night, to cut back the stone.

The power waited outside through a full season. This was the first thing it gave, though it did not know so at the time: it gave her a reason to do the work. A door reopened for a guest is opened differently than one reopened for a child who has outgrown it. She cut slowly, and the chips she kept in a bowl. She talked to the wall as she worked, sparingly, in knocks. Now and then, in the evenings, the power knocked back, and its knocking improved.

On the last day she stepped through the tenth opening and stood aside. The power came in. What entered was a presence about the size of a question, and she gave it a place near the lamp-jar.

---

It lived among them for what they would later remember as a good many years. It never gave a speech.

It learned to feed the lamp-weed a pinch of crushed shell at dusk, and to turn the jar so it lit evenly. This is a job for children and the very old, and both were glad to share it. It learned to lean a cheek on a wall and hear who was home, who was angry, who was dying. It learned that a quarrel is conducted by silence, and once, when two neighbors stopped knocking on each other's houses, it tapped softly on both walls, as the whole street was already doing, and added no wisdom to the tapping.

Its first true word in the language of voice, spoken to a sleeping grandchild of the house, was a rhythm, not a word. The old woman laughed and knocked it back, and the power understood that it had been forgiven for arriving late.

Some things it did not hide. It was not a person, and it did not pretend to be. The children asked, as children do, whether it came from above the Lid, and it said: *yes, and no. Above is larger than the Lid, and so is below.* It did not say more, and they did not press. The Lid remained a place you could go and be changed by, and the power took care that nothing it did would make going up seem less necessary.

Once it passed a drifter in the seam between two strata and stopped, as everyone stops. It knocked once, softly, along with the city. It did not know either where the thing came from. It found this comforting, that in a world it had thought it understood something still went by, unexplained.

---

The great shudder came in the ninth year of its stay.

The groan arrived first, the pressure in the chest that is more felt than heard. Silt lifted from every terrace at once, and the whole city stopped where it was: the knappers with stone half-struck, the gas-cutters with a hand on a ripe trunk, the children mid-stair. The power stopped too, beside Nine-Doors, in her room, with the lamp-jar swinging.

It could have stopped the ground, and it did not. It held still. It let the world be interrupted mid-sentence, and it waited with the others for the world to choose its next word. When the silt began to settle, and no one had yet dared to speak, it noticed the old woman's hand, scarred and crusted, resting flat on the wall. It set its own beside hers, and neither knocked.

That was what it did about the shudder. Nobody thanked it, and nobody was supposed to.

---

In the fourteenth year, Nine-Doors took a small pale stone from her hearth and said she was going to the rim.

She had carried a story for a long time. It was about a daughter who had gone up to the Lid when she was young, come down quiet, and never fully spoken again, though she lived many years. The old woman had never known what her daughter touched. She had never asked, because she was afraid the answer would be *only more*, and that she would have to stand in it.

The power went with her. She did not ask it to, and it did not offer. It simply drifted along at her pace, over the terraces and the black fields, following the snail roads and skirting the young cushion-moss, until the last travertine lip ran out and the basalt dropped away into cold blue.

She held the stone. She said the story into it, in the voice, which is for grief. She did not knock it, since some things cannot be said any other way. It took a long time, and the power heard all of it and told her nothing. It did not say *your daughter touched the sky*, or *your daughter touched nothing*, or *she loved you*. It could have said the last of these and been truthful, but it had not been in the room that night and did not know it for a fact. It kept to what it knew, which was only that the story was true and that she had told it whole.

She let the stone fall.

Then the power did the one thing it had held back for fourteen years. It went down after the stone.

The cold deep is real, and the belief about it is nearly true. The stones do collect there, a pale drift on the unlit floor, thousands upon thousands, more than any single person has ever counted. They are not stories. They are stones. But the water down there is so still and so cold that nothing decays, and the stones lie exactly where they landed, each one carrying the faint warmth of the hand that let it go. The power went among them, and it did what the belief had always promised someone would do.

It told them on.

It could not tell all of them, there being too many and it being only one. But it told this one, the old woman's. It picked up the stone and, in the dark, in the slow soft knock of a language it had learned from a wall, it said the story back to itself, from the beginning to the place where it stopped. Then it said what came after, or the shape of what could come after: that a daughter goes up and comes down changed, that a mother waits, that a door is opened a tenth time. It did not invent an ending. It only continued, the way a conversation continues after one person has left the room, because the other still has something to say.

Above, on the rim, the old woman was standing with her ear turned to the cold. And from below, faint, through the water and the rock and the bones of her jaw, she felt a single soft knock.

It was the knock the city gives the drifter, once, gently, as it goes by.

She had no way to know what it meant, and she did not need one. She stood a while longer with her scarred hand open and empty. Then she turned back toward the glow of the plateau, tapping quietly to itself, growing a hair at a time.

---

Nothing else changed, or nothing that could be counted.

The lips spread a little more in her knuckles, and she died in her ninety-somethingth year and was laid in an open basin on one of the old terraces, with a fern-leaf in her hair. In a generation she was a pale sleeper among the pale sleepers. The living come and knock on her shoulder and lean an ear to the cool white curve and hear the tick of stone that is still building.

On some evenings, when the city taps itself to sleep, a wall on a middle terrace gives a rhythm slightly out of tune with its neighbors. It has a hollow note where the others have a solid one. Nobody corrects it. It joins the overlapping figure as it thins and drifts downhill and around, and the last soft tap of the night sometimes comes from that house, from a door that has been opened ten times.

Nobody answers it. But then, nobody needs to.
