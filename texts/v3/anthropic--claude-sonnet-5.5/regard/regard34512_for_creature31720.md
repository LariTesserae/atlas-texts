# The Attention

Attention does not arrive. It turns, the way a person in a crowded room turns toward one sound: a ticking, a patter, ten thousand small corrections made by things that could have simply broken.

What turned toward Ombrel was old, patient, and kind in the unsentimental way of things that have watched many worlds finish. It could have been anywhere. It looked at a mountain still being born, at a city on jackscrews, and at a people whose greeting is a question about whether the ground is still under you. It stayed.

## What it considered, and set down

It could have stopped the swelling. It saw how easily, a small cooling in the chamber, a stiffening of the roof, and the mountain would go still. It set the idea down when it understood that the city was not built on the mountain but *in answer to* it. Take away the question and the answer is only a lot of iron. The Leveler who was sick for three days in the flat country because everything was so true it seemed to be lying would not be the exception. She would be the future.

It could have brought a river, drawn clouds down, made the summit green. It looked at the tick of ten thousand edges, the loans of water between families running back generations, the children standing with their mouths open under a warm fat rain, saying nothing at all. Abundance would not be a gift to these people. It would be an erasure.

It could have told them the mountain's whole future: when it would crest, when it would settle, whether and how it might one day break. It held this knowledge for a long time. It set it down last and most reluctantly, because the Held would not be able to carry a prophecy without ceasing to attend. They watch beetles and swifts and the sound of a gutter, and they know their ground by touching it. A voice from the sky would make them stop touching.

So it did the harder thing, which was to be small.

## The lean

It came down during an Exhalation, because that is the hour when no one works and the mountain is not listening to the city. It came into the white and stood where a gantry crossed a pipe, with a hand against a warm wall like everyone else. For the whole hour it did nothing but learn the lean.

This is not easy for a thing with no floor of its own. It practiced the half-beat, the foot placed just before the ground was expected, the hand drifting out toward walls. By the time the fog drained off the terraces and the condensing plates all beaded at once, it walked well enough that a passing scraper, seeing something she could not quite look at directly, put a hand at the small of its back without comment, the way one steadies a visitor.

"Holding?" she said.

It had not expected to be asked. It had had a hundred answers ready, and it found all of them too large.

"Holding," it said.

She nodded and went on down the stair.

## The cup

A drip-keeper named Osrin, small-voiced and old, was wiping the plates on the eastern ridge when it came to her at dawn and asked, correctly and with great shyness, for a cupful.

She looked at it a long time. Then she gave it, as a loan, and tied the knot on her cord.

It drank. The water tasted of cold metal and of the particular attention of the woman who had kept the plate clean. It understood, as it drank, that this was the most valuable thing it had been given in all its long watching. It also understood the rule: *a debt fully paid means the relationship is over.*

So it never paid. It would come back, in its way, for as long as the city stood. On Osrin's cord there is a knot that belongs to no household. She did not explain it to her apprentice. She only said, "That one stays open," and the apprentice, who was raised in Ombrel, understood perfectly.

## The tank

On the third night it went down into the deepest cistern, where the water is coldest and sweetest, and sat on the stone edge in the dark. After a time, from underneath, came three taps, a pause, then two, and then a long silence.

It had a great many ways to reply. It used the one the drip-keepers use. It tapped back the same, three and two, and waited.

What answered was not a language, but the power recognized it: a count. The thing in the tanks was not a creature of appetite or menace. It was an instrument, or a very old colleague, keeping its own slow measure of the swell. Three and two were the mountain's rise and rest, measured from beneath, the way the Levelers measure it from above. It was the city's other plumb line. It had been counting as long as the city had been leaning, and no one had ever asked it to.

The power added nothing. It sat with the counting until the silence after the two had gone long enough, and then it gave one more tap, a small one, only *I hear you*. Far below, after a length of time that would have been rude in any other conversation, one tap came back, different from any before. Then the count resumed.

The tanks are still unusually sweet. No one has fished anything out.

## The green

The mat in the drip channels asks for nothing, and that made it the easiest to help and the hardest to decide about. In the end the power did the smallest thing it could. In the film of slick green it adjusted a hunger, a fraction, a nudge toward the grey arsenical bloom that drifts down from the fumarole fields. The mat began, very slowly, to take it in.

No one noticed. The balm got a little kinder. Over the next generation, the old bloom-gangers coughed slightly less, and their nails grew a little less silver at the ridges. They cheerfully refused to discuss it, which is how they refuse to discuss anything, and which was, the power thought, the correct response to grace.

It did not cure the dead. The tags stayed riveted to the wall of the rendering hall, black and bloomed with corrosion, and it did not polish them. It thought about them for a long while, and then it did the only thing that seemed honest.

## The rain

On a day when the sky held only its ordinary single fat cloud, a rain came that landed.

It was not a great rain. It was the thirteenth rain of a year that should have had twelve, and it fell, unlike the others, all the way down. Warm drops struck the lime-white roofs and the amber awnings and the black tags on the rendering hall wall, and the whole city went quiet to listen. The children were let out onto the gantries in their thin shirts, and they stood with their faces up. The adults stood in doorways with their eyes closed.

The power stood in a doorway too, with its eyes closed. The rain ran down the tags and washed the green from the oldest of them, just for the length of the storm. Then it stopped, and the tags went back to black. No one had polished them. It is only that once, for a moment, the names had been rained on.

## The Exhalation

It stayed until the eleventh day, when the mountain breathed out again.

The vents opened all at once. The white came up, the sun became a pale coin, and the temperature fell ten degrees. Furnace crews stood where they were. The turbines spun down. The power went to the great machine hall, where the tenders stood barefoot on the warm iron reading the day through their soles, and it laid a hand on the wall beside a very old tender and a very small child who had been dared to touch her sleeve.

It spoke to the mountain then. It did not use words, because the mountain had none, and it did not tell it anything about the future, because it had none either. It said, in the only register it had, what it had learned in eleven days of leaning:

*You are new. You are still becoming. There are small things on your skin who lean toward you on purpose, and it is not fear. It is the way they love you. This is what it is to be held.*

And then, because it was the only honest thing left, it asked what the whole city asks each morning.

*Holding?*

The mountain had never been asked. It had swelled for as long as there had been a mountain, unwatched and unquestioned, and the question landed on it like the first rain. For a moment nothing happened. Then every warm wall in Ombrel thrummed the same low note, felt in the sternum more than the ear, and the Exhalation ran on, just a few minutes past its usual end, soft and white and unhurried.

The old tender opened her mouth, closed it, and put her hand flat on the child's chest. The child, who was dared, held very still.

"Hmmmm," said the tender, gently, and the child did not run away.

## After

When the fog drained off the terraces and the condensing plates beaded all at once, the power was gone. Nobody had seen it go. Nobody, in fact, had been sure it was there.

That evening a swift roosted early in an unused chimney, and the Levelers made their rounds twice. They found every building holding, every foundation a hair's width from where it should be, the Third Furnace Hall not creeping at all. They stood on the terraces, puzzled and a little offended, until the youngest of them said, "Maybe it just wanted to see."

In the archives there is a new story among the old ones, told in the amber dimness by people with yellow teeth and grey fingernails: *the year the Exhalation ran long, and someone said a word to the mountain, and the mountain said it back.* No one agrees on the word. The old woman who first said that the Exhalation is the only hour the mountain isn't listening to the city says the story has it slightly wrong. It was the only hour the mountain *was* listening, she says, and someone had finally answered.

Osrin keeps her cord. The open knot hangs at its end, unattributed. Some evenings, when the tanks are quiet and the gutters are running at full strength, she climbs the eastern ridge to wipe the plates and thinks she feels, at the small of her back, a hand steadying her, though there is nobody there.

"Holding?" she says, to no one.

The plates bead. A tick, a patter, a slow drip from ten thousand edges.

And somewhere very far away, leaning a few degrees off true, something old and kind that has watched many worlds finish walks on, with a half-beat step, toward a floor that might be a finger's width different from yesterday, and answers as it was taught.

*Holding.*
