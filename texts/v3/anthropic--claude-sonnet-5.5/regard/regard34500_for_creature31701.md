# What Came to the Table

It arrived as a thickening of the cloud, and for a long time that is all it was. The cloud deck dragged its belly over the gravel, and one afternoon there was a little more of it than there had been. The lichens noticed nothing. The beetles did not pause. On the back of an elder kiln a grey bird lifted its head, then went back to the rime.

The power stayed through one full turning of the seasons before it did anything at all. It had learned, in older and stranger places, that the first wish of a benevolent thing is usually the most dangerous.

## What it might have done

It looked at the Table and saw the ways it could be improved, and it held each one up and turned it in the light.

It could have warmed the plateau. The cold was hard on the calves. But the cold was also the loom on which everything was woven. The frost sorted the gravel, the frozen ground carried the kilns' speech for miles, and the seeps drank from the cloud only because the air was cold enough to give it up. Warm the Table and the kilns would go deaf.

It could have given the land rivers. Rivers would have been generous, and they would have cut valleys, and the wide dishes with their memory of water would have become gorges. The Table had already had its rivers. It was the shape they left behind.

It could have made every calf catch. This it considered longest. It thought of the grey boulders lying where they were dropped, the small hearths that never woke. Then it thought of the pale beetles, the rust-coloured scavenger crossing the pavement, the gardens in the folds, and the whole slow economy that ran on losses as well as gains. A Table where no calf failed would still be a Table, but the herd would grow past the lichen and the lichen would fail. Within a few circuits there would be more mouths than breath, and the failures would be back, larger, and far crueler.

It could have spoken to them. It could have told the kilns what they were, and shown the birds the shape of the world from above. But it found it had no sentence that would not make the place smaller, and it let the idea go.

So it put its hands behind its back, as it were, and learned.

## Learning the ground

It lay along the gravel for a long time and let the steps come through. It felt the deep, slow breathing that rose through the pads of a hundred animals and settled into the frozen plate of the basin. It felt the long, the short, the long, the stamp of an elder, and the answering hush.

It did not understand. It came to understand that this was not a failure. The speech had no words in it, only weight and interval and the shared decision of many bodies. To translate it would have been like translating the way a river bends.

But it found it could take part. It gathered a little of its attention into a single point and let that point fall on the frozen plate the way a foot falls: long, short, long. It said nothing. It made only the shape a kiln makes when it wants to be known to be here.

The basin stopped.

Every kiln within the reach of the pulse lowered its head at once, and the vents ran quiet, and the rime shook slowly off a hundred scutes. They stood that way through the night and through the next day, and the birds sat with their feet clamped tight and their heads tucked. The beetles went on working, because beetles do.

At last an elder shifted her weight and brought down a single forefoot. The stamp was slow and heavy, and it was not at all like the pulse it answered. The power felt it go through the whole plateau, and then it felt the herd let go. Heads came up. The shovel-lips dragged into the lichen. The vents opened front to back, and the steam laid itself against the cloud.

It did not know what the stamp had meant. It turned the sensation over for a long while. It was not a welcome, exactly, or a warning. The nearest thing it could find was the way a mountain might look at a traveller, with attention and no hurry, noting one more feature of the ground.

The power found that it was content to be that. It had spent so long among things that wanted to be its subjects, or its worshippers, or its wards. It was strange and restful to be a feature.

## The one thing

In the third season of its staying, a calf was dropped in a bed of slag at the edge of the basin. Its mother was young and her hearth ran low that year, and the calf lay on its side, grey and cold, with the small cavity in its chest banked to nothing.

The herd went on. This too was in the grammar of the place.

The power watched the frost creep across the scutes, and it did not reach in and light the hearth. That would have been the kind of help that makes an owner of the helper. But it had noticed that the Table already had a way of doing this. It had a form, worn smooth by ages, and the form was small.

Where an adult kiln lowers herself over a calf, the power gathered itself into a warmth of the same size and lay along the calf's back. It made no more heat than a vent-warmed belly makes, and it held very still. It asked for nothing and offered nothing that the ground had not already offered a thousand times. Over the hours the calf's flank rose a little, and then again, and deep in the chest something small and orange turned over.

The calf caught.

It rose in the grey light, wobbling on its splayed pads, and put its shovel-lip to the finest, softest slag as though it had always known where to find it. An adult came for it, a cousin or a stranger, and the power drew back into the cloud.

It did not do this again. Two nights later another calf failed, farther out on the flank of the herd, and the power stood over it as the frost took it, and did not lower itself. It stayed until the rust walker came, unhurried, along the old pellet-lines, and settled itself to the meal. Then it left the calf to the walker and the walker to the night. It was not indifferent. It had made a rule of one hand's width of help, the width any neighbour on the Table could offer, and it kept the rule, and it grieved a little, and the grief was also part of the balance.

## The walker

The rust walker was the one it had worried about most. Alone in the grey, ranging the abandoned trails, forever raising its head to check a memory against the horizon: surely that was loneliness, and surely a benevolent thing should mend it.

But it watched the animal through a long dusk and saw what it had not seen before. The walker slept in the hollows the herds had left, curled against warm slag, and when it woke it did not look about for company. It looked at the horizon, where the great backs rose steaming out of the fog, and it stood a long time. Nothing in its posture was reaching. It was closer to a greeting. It was not alone in the way the power had meant the word. It was in the middle of the only conversation the place had, a very long one, conducted in tracks and slag and distance, and it had been doing so its whole life.

The power did one small thing. On the coldest night of the year it warmed a single hollow by a degree or two, no more than a herd's passing would have left. The walker found it and slept there. In the morning it rose and shook its coat and did not look for whoever had done it. It went to the edge of the basin, raised its head to where the cloud had gathered over the herd, and stood.

## What it left

When the power left, it left almost nothing, and it was careful about that.

There were no monuments and no gift of fire. The gravel was as sorted as ever, the seeps as dark and green, the pellets still ticking as they cooled. The cloud over the basin was the same cloud.

There was one change, and it was small enough that no one would ever find its cause. In the elder's stamp, in the long slow phrase the herds used to turn and halt and begin again, there was a new interval. It was a very short beat, half a heartbeat long, tucked in after the second long step. The elders did not know why they had added it. The young learned it from them, as they learned everything, through their feet. Over the decades it travelled from basin to basin along the frozen plate, and in time every herd on the Table used it without a thought.

No one could say what it meant. If it meant anything, it was only *here*, or *still here*, or *we heard*. It sat in the middle of the speech like a stone in a stream, and the speech went around it and on.

The kilns walked on into their long circuit. Lichen spread on the slag behind them. The walker crossed the pavement with its small tick of claws. And under the cloud the Table went on breathing, a machine that had never been assembled, a little more itself for having been kept company, and none the wiser.
