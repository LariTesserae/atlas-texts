# The Attention

It did not descend. Descending is a thing done by powers that want to be seen doing it, and this one wanted the opposite.

It arrived the way weather arrives, as a change in the quality of the light that no one could date afterward. Then it did what wise things do first, which is nothing. It looked for a long time, and it made a list, mostly of things it would not do.

**It would not stop the lathe.** The lathe's turning was the reason the shelf existed at all: the sinks, the peat, the warm dome-stone, the slow cloud-tide that was really a breath. Stopping the cut would be a kindness to nothing.

**It would not wake the lathe.** It did not know what waking would mean to something that had spent hundreds of millions of years attending to a single task. It might be a joy or a grief, or it might be a flinch, and a flinch from something that size is a landslide.

**It would not speak to the city.** The people of Halvane had built a whole civilization of manners around not asking, and the manners were not ignorance. They were a form of respect, older and wiser than an announcement could be. To tell them *you are the shavings and you are loved* would turn the sentence into a cage. They would be looked after and they would stop being neighbours.

**It would not fix things.** The weir-wright falls sometimes. The cutters' hands go black and their lungs go grey. The single unanswered *dunk* in the small hours comes more often than anyone would choose. The power looked at all of this and did not pretend it was small. But it understood that the people here had made a shape out of their losses, with vigils and honey-spoons and salamanders, and that to lift the losses out would tear the shape.

That left very little. It decided to go and see.

---

It came in the low hours, when the cloud dropped to the rooftops, and it took the form the place had already made room for. On the ridge of a moss-silk mill, among the grey and patient herons, there was one more heron. It stood on one leg with its head tilted down toward the street.

The others did not look at it. They shifted very slightly along the ridge-beam, as one does on a bench to make space for someone who has not yet sat down. This was their whole welcome, and it was complete.

Below, the city went about its work. A lamplighter passed with her red can and her hooked pole. She glanced up, as she always did, and her eyes crossed the heron and moved on. Her lips shaped a small hush, a habit. She did not count.

*Good,* thought the power. *Someone here knows how to be looked at.*

---

It stayed one full tide, a little over nineteen hours, which is the length of a Halvane day, and in that time it did the things a guest does.

It listened. It learned that the children's names are notes, and that a name like *Ouhm* or *Th'eel* is a whole hillside in a breath. It heard an old woman call across a courtyard with her lips barely parted and watched three heads turn, and it felt something in itself that had no name for a moment and then acquired one: *delight*.

It watched the cutters lay their stacks and saw that each stack's edge was a signature. It watched a spinner draw thread from the rack like unwinding a wet cobweb from a branch, and it noticed the racksmen talking their nonsense syllables to the pale moss. It understood at once why the talked-to racks grow better. The moss was not being flattered. It was being *accompanied*, and that is a different thing and often the only thing that works.

Near the middle of the tide, in the small hours, a single *dunk* sounded, unanswered, from a felted bell. The power watched the neighbourhood wake in the dark and pull on its grey-green coats and go quietly up a staircase to a lit window. It went too, in the sense that its attention went, and it sat in the corner of the room among the sitters. It did not comfort anyone. It held still and low-voiced beside the dying-and-then-dead the way the city did, and when the sitters, at some grey hour, began quietly to talk about the man's terrible jokes, it stayed for that as well.

On the way back down it saw a spoonful of honey beside a gutter, left for a wedding. The ants had found it. The pale salamander with the red gills was waiting a little way off, patient as a tax. The power looked at this small arrangement, this deliberate, knowing, useless-and-useful feeding of the drains, and it thought that if it had been asked what it could give this city, it would not have known. The city had already thought of every gift that mattered, and had put them in gutters.

---

At the slack of the cloud, when the tide stood still for a long breath between rising and falling, it went out onto the largest dome.

The stone was warm. It knelt, which was strange for a heron and easy for what it actually was, and it laid its palm against the rock beside the shallow grooves. It held still.

And there was the rhythm, slower than a pulse and more even than a breath: the turning.

It had expected vastness, and it found vastness. It had feared indifference, and here it was wrong, or at least incomplete. What lay under its palm was not indifferent. It was *absorbed*. The lathe was not a mind that had failed to notice the city. It was a devotion so complete that it had no attention left over for the idea of being noticed. It was the way a person carving something small and difficult does not hear their own name called from another room. They are not refusing. They are inside the cut.

And the power read the grooves as the city never could, because the city had only ever seen them from above.

They ran downslope in fans and spirals, older than the moss. But the spirals were not decorative. Each one tightened where the lathe's tool had come to something and *slowed*. The power followed one with its fingertip down to where the cut swerved, and saw what it was swerving around: a hollow, a sink, the place where, hundreds of millions of years later, the first house of Halvane would be built.

Nothing had planned it, and nothing could have planned it that far ahead. But the lathe was a maker whose whole nature was *care in the cut*, and things that cut with care leave, without meaning to, places where small things can begin. The city was not a heap of waste that happened to collect at the foot of the work. It was what the work's carefulness had made room for. It was the accident that only the best craft produces.

And the herons, the power understood then, were not the lathe looking up. That would have been a kind of vanity, and there was none in the lathe. They were what spilled over: the smallest overflow of the great regard, shaped by the cloud into birds, standing on the mill ridges and looking down at where the shavings gathered. Not to judge them. Only because when you have made something with that much care, you cannot help looking at it in the corner of your eye.

The herders' theory was right. The city's other half had been standing on their roofs the whole time.

---

The power considered, then, what it might do for the lathe. It found that it wanted to do very little, and that this was the right amount.

It did not speak. It did not send meaning down through the stone, because meaning would have been a hand on the shoulder of someone mid-cut. It only did what the racksmen did for the moss. It stayed, and it held still, and for one full revolution of the great slow turning it kept the lathe company. It matched its own stillness to that unhurried rhythm and asked for nothing. The lathe did not notice. That was the point, and it was enough. Somewhere in the cutting a thing was accompanied that had never been accompanied, and it would not know, and it would go on cutting with exactly the same care, and *something had been there*.

---

Then, because the power was not made only of restraint, it did one small thing more, and it did it for a person.

That autumn an old herder named for a note that sounded like wind in a bottle lay down to sleep on the warm dome with her coat spread out. She had done this forty autumns, and each time she had dreamed the slow, turning dream she never described. This time the dream held a single addition, so light she would not have called it a message. She dreamed she was walking along a groove, and where the groove tightened into its spiral, she felt the tool slow and *lean away*, gently, from the hollow where her house would someday stand. Not as an animal avoids a fire. As a hand avoids a sleeping child.

She woke in the pale, shadowless light with the stone still warm against her back. She did not tell anyone. It would have been discourteous.

But at the next low hour, when the cloud dropped and a grey heron took its place on the mill ridge and tilted its head toward the street, she looked up at it. She looked for one moment longer than manners allow, and she nodded, once, as one nods to a neighbour whose kindness you have only just understood.

The heron did not move. But something in the set of its neck said *yes*, or *you're welcome*, or only *I know*.

---

By the time the cloud lifted, the ridge-beam was empty, and no one had seen a heron leave.

That morning there had been, for a single tide, one more heron than usual. Nobody counted them. That was the gift, and it was the whole of it: that it went unmentioned, unweighed, and unasked, and the city stayed exactly as the lathe had made it, low-voiced and mossed and warm.

Under the largest dome the great turning went on, taking off a hair's width of the world with each pass, curling it away as something that would live a while in the warmth. The salamanders kept the drains. The bells said *dunk*. The cutters cut and the sinks sealed over, and the ferns unfurled toward a light they could only hear.

And in the stone, very faintly, so faintly that only a palm held still at the slack of the tide might have caught it, the rhythm went on exactly as before, except that it had been kept company once. And a thing that has been kept company once, even without knowing, is not quite the thing it was.
