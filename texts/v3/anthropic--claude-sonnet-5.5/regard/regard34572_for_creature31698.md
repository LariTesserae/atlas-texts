# What Came Down to Varrick Deep

It came over the rim as fog, the ordinary way. Nothing on the floor looked up. Springtails do not look up, the hares were watching the mist for something else, and the ravens were busy shouting in their dream. The lamp above the generator hall door went on burning.

It stayed a whole season before it did anything, and most of that season it only listened.

## Listening

The roar was the first thing it learned, because the roar was the first thing everyone here had learned. It found that the roar is not noise. It is a slow, continuous account of a whole watershed: the weight of the meltwater, the pressure of the weather on the plateau, the hour, the year. The Combers had read that account since before the ground fell in. It took the visitor weeks to read even the outline of it, and it felt humbled, as it does whenever it finds a people who are fluent in something it has to study.

Then it walked the dressed ground, carefully, on the balls of its feet, the way you cross a room where someone is sleeping. It saw the feather mosses laid in their long strokes and the sphagnum lifted from the hollows. It saw the birch in the locomotive's firebox, pruned to a hand's height, and understood something the human eye would have passed over. The Combers know about the forest. They know the birches will close over the floor in a few centuries and put out the light that the moss needs. The pruned sapling is a decision about pace. They are holding a slow door open with their hands, and they know exactly how long they can do it.

So it did not warn them of anything. They were ahead of it.

## What It Would Not Do

It weighed a great many things and set most of them down.

It would not reveal itself to the village, or to anyone with a survey permit. It saw clearly what would follow: boots on the cushions, a boardwalk, a station, a paper, a name for the Combers that they did not choose. Dressed ground does not survive being discovered. What is gentle here is gentle partly because it is unobserved, and the visitor did not think it had the right to trade that away on their behalf.

It would not remove the works. The generator is ugly and it takes a fraction of the stream, but it sends a little light up the cable to people who need it, and the Combers have made no complaint. They have dressed around it for eighty years. The locomotive is their shrine, and the hall is the neighbor's house.

It would not hurry the moss or hold back the trees, or lend the Combers any tool, any metal, any knowledge that would make them something other than what they are. Gifts that arrive from outside a people's own hands tend to become the shape of that people. It has watched that happen elsewhere and does not enjoy remembering it.

It would not translate. It could have. It could have given the caretaker a dictionary of the fans and the Combers a dictionary of her oilskin gestures, and the two would have understood each other within a week and lost the thing that is actually passing between them, which is the bolt, the bark, the scoured patch of moss, and the lamp. They are speaking a language that is still being invented, and nobody should finish another person's sentence.

## What It Did

It did one thing to the world, and it was small.

In the turbine hall, in the main bearing, was a hairline flaw in the race, older than the caretaker, invisible to grease and gauge. The second hard freeze of the coming winter would have found it. The shaft would have seized, and the penstock would have taken the shock and split along its seam. Then the stream, with nowhere to go, would have found a new road through the fissure, and the note of the vent would have changed. To the Combers that would not have been a loss of power but a loss of the year. The clock in the roar would have slipped. Whole generations would have had to relearn when to go down.

The visitor closed the flaw. It did not replace the bearing or leave any mark. It made the steel whole and the seat true, and it withdrew. The next time the caretaker put her palm on the housing, the turbine would run cooler than it had in years, and she would put it down to eleven years of careful greasing, which was not entirely wrong. She would be quietly pleased and would tell no one.

That was the whole intervention. It was a hand steadying a cup that someone else was carrying.

## The Conversation

On its last evening it made itself a shape that could be touched: low, cool, damp, and broad across the front, roughly the width and temperature of a mossy boulder. It sat at the edge of the lamp's circle and held still.

Holding still is the greeting. It had worked that out from watching them.

A young Comber came out of the fog, one with fine green down still on its shoulders and only a few cushions along its spine. It stopped and considered the shape for a long time with its great lidless eyes. Then it came forward and laid its fans against the visitor's face, finger through finger, and its hands began to move.

The visitor was slow and clumsy at first. It read the pressure without the rhythm, like a child sounding out a page. But it got the sense of it. The Comber was saying, again and again in slightly different ways, *Here is ground. Here is ground kept.*

Then, after a pause, in a softer pressure that it had to read twice: *Who keeps yours?*

The visitor thought about that for longer than it had thought about anything all season. Then it lifted its own broad surface and answered as well as it could, with the only sentence it had been able to build: *Yours is well kept.*

The Comber's fingers went suddenly quick and light and fluttering against its face. The visitor was fairly sure that was laughter.

They stayed like that until the lamp had burned down its circle of fog to nothing. Then the young one drew its hands away, gathered some pale willow bark from a pile it had been carrying, set it on the moss between them, and went back into the green. The visitor did not take the bark. It left it where it lay.

## After

The fog went back up over the rim the next morning as ordinary fog, and nothing looked up.

Nineteen days later the caretaker came down by cable car and found the turbine running cool. She put her palm flat against the housing and left it there, and she was warmer for the rest of the day than the weather explained.

At the door she found the bark. Beside it she set the rusted bolt she had taken home, cleaned again and stood upright in the moss. Then she took a small tin pocket mirror from her oilskin, the kind sold at the village shop for a few coins. Nobody had told her the Combers talk in flashes of light. She had watched the pale throats catch her lamp, and she had thought about it. She propped the mirror against the step, angled at the dark.

The visitor was far away by then and did not see it. It might have been the thing it valued most in the whole affair, and it only ever heard about it later, as one hears of good news from a distant country: the caretaker thought of it herself. Nothing had to be given, because the two of them were already finding each other, one small offering at a time, at the pace of moss.

Under all of it the great voice went on, louder in the melt and quieter in the freeze, telling the year to anyone who could read it.
