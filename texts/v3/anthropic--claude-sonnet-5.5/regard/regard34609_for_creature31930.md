# The Listener in the Sillworks

It did not arrive. Arriving would have been the wrong shape for the place. It noticed the basin the way you notice, in a crowded room, that one voice among many has been saying your name.

For most of a Laying it did nothing at all.

It lay along the rim like fog and listened. It heard ten thousand buckets tip out of step with one another and knew, before it understood anything else, that the disorder was a kind of order too large to hear all at once. It heard the grazers' hooves. It heard the small wet clicking in the walls that even the people had never resolved. It heard the eel-worms surfacing in the fast channels, and it understood that they were listening too, and had been for longer than the brick, and that they were not lonely. It let that pass through it and was glad.

## What it considered, and put down

It considered light first, because light is what the powerful always think of. It imagined a warm, clean day over the basin, a sky opened like a lid lifted from a pot. It set the idea down almost at once. These people's eyes were wide and dark to the edge. Their whole grammar of gesture was built for lamp-pools, their tenderness for the dimness between them. Daylight would not have been a gift. It would have been a verdict on how they lived.

It considered stopping the erosion, and put that down. It considered sealing the first cracks, and put that down more slowly. It considered opening the packed vaults to see whether lamps still burned, and left that most firmly alone. Some doors are closed for the sake of whoever is behind them, and some for the sake of whoever is in front.

It considered speaking, and laughed, in the way a large patient thing laughs: a slight change in the pressure of the damp. Words were pointless here. Anything that spoke aloud in the Sillworks would be a smear on the din.

And it considered the thing it most feared doing, which was making a quiet. A hush, a hole in the sound, was the one message that would reach every person at once. It would also be the cruelest thing it could say, because in that place a hush meant *something has stopped*. It would not put a stain of silence across a district of sleepers to announce itself. Whatever it did, it would do inside the noise, by the rules of the noise.

## Who it found

There was a keeper, one of the uneven walkers, the kind who stop mid-stride with a hand raised. Her name was a rhythm, long-short-short-long with a rest folded into the middle, the way her mother had tapped it into a bronze rail above a cradle-shelf. Her fingernails were stained the color of ink. Her right knuckles were swollen like a river-stone's knots.

Behind the oldest wall in the copperworks quarter, a hairline had opened in the packed clay. She was the one who had found it. Since then she had returned to it on four hundred and some nights, knelt, laid her palm flat, and stayed.

The listener stayed near her, and it learned what she carried. The rim-tales said that buildings die slowly, and she had begun to believe them. Belief was not the burden, though. The burden was that she had told no one. She could not tap it on a pipe, because the pipes gossip. She could not say it in a warm room, because a person who says a wall will fall is not thanked. So she carried it alone, kneeling at the crack with her hand on the cool clay, wanting someone to tell her whether the thing she felt was fear or something else.

That was what the listener decided to answer. It would not touch the crack, the wall, or the city. It would answer the question she was asking with her palm.

## What it did

It found a pipe of glazed clay that ran near her, a quiet one, one of the old runs whose bronze collar had gone green. And in the dead hour of the thinner dark, when the buckets ran slow and the hammers were mostly still, it tapped.

It tapped badly at first. It had never had knuckles and did not know how much weight a message wants. The first taps were too even and too clean, like a child imitating a keeper. She lifted her head at once. Even in the din, a wrong rhythm is loud.

She answered the way anyone in the Sillworks answers a stranger on the pipes: three flat taps, spaced out, meaning *who asks?*

It did not know how to say what it was. So it said the one true thing it could, in the only grammar available. It tapped her name, long-short-short-long, with the rest folded into the middle, exactly as her mother had. It had listened to that name on the pipes for a whole year and had it perfectly.

She went still. Nobody had tapped her name to her in that way since her mother died. Now, on a night that was not a name night, something on the far side of the clay was calling her by it.

Then it did the second thing it had spent all season learning. It tapped a joke.

It was a small one, a run of even beats with a deliberate stumble in the middle, one false step where the floor isn't. She had spent her life hearing that stumble from a quarter-mile off and smiling in the dark. She did it now, alone at the crack, before she had decided whether to be afraid. When a stranger is willing to be a little foolish in your own language, you cannot fully fear it.

After that it said the true thing. It said it slowly, evenly spaced, in the rhythm they use for grief. Then, halfway through, it changed the rhythm, because what it had to say wasn't grief, and she would hear the difference.

*The story is true. Walls fall slowly. It will not be soon. It will be gentle about it.*

Then, after a rest:

*Falling is not the opposite of made. The silt will take back what the bricks borrowed. Then someone will build on that too.*

It did not say more. It did not say what she should do or whether she should tell the others. It did not promise that the ruin would be painless, or that it would be there, or that the people would survive the centuries of it. It didn't know, and wisdom includes not pretending to. It only told her that the thing she had felt under her hand was not an ending. It was a beginning she'd been shown early.

She stayed kneeling for a long time. Her hand was still on the wall.

## What it left

It left no gift she could hold, and it changed nothing in the city. The cracks kept opening, slowly, as cracks do.

But afterward, on certain nights, a knuckle-rap would come along the old green pipes at a slightly wrong angle, a joke with a stumble in it, from no district anyone could name. The keepers took it for a trick of the water. The children took it for a game and added it to the rules nobody can explain. Somebody left a small dish of fat in a wall-gap, and nobody was sure for whom.

The woman with ink under her nails did tell someone in the end, though not the whole basin. She told an apprentice, a boy who stopped mid-stride and lifted his hand at a bucket that was a quarter-beat late. She told him in the kiln-warm places, in a mutter, where a voice is allowed. She said only, *There is a wall I want you to put your hand on.*

Far above them, at the level of the drifting moths, the listener kept listening, and the sound went on around it. It was made of water, brick, and small hands, and it kept the mud moving, and the mud kept the city alive. It would be a long time before any of it fell, and longer before the fall was finished, and longer still before someone built again on the grey ground. The listener had decided that this was enough. Being one more person who knew was all it had wanted to be.

Somewhere in the copperworks, a bucket tipped, and struck the bronze.
