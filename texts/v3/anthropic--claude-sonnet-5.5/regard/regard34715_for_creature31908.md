# What Came to Harrow Height

It did not arrive. Arrival is something done to a place, and the power that turned its attention toward the plateau understood at once that this place had suffered enough of being done to. It came the way the mist comes, by condensing out of the air onto everything, and for a long while there was nothing to report except that the dripping was slightly attended.

## The First Season

It began by learning to be wrong.

It walked the streams that start from nothing, and sank a little at each step, and let the ground take its footprints and close them. It stood in a dry-fall while the rain rose off the moss past its face, and it noted that the ground beneath was drier than a desert and that this was not a malfunction. It heard the presses fire out of order, felt the tremor before the sound, and understood that cause and effect had been wearing loose here for so long that the plateau had stopped carrying them as a burden.

It could have set all this right. Clearing the cloud, warming the water, or settling the distances so that a Stack two hours off stayed two hours off would have been trivial. It weighed each of these and found the same flaw in all of them.

The Wrung had made an idiom of the plateau's unreliability. Their whole language of taps depended on messages arriving in the wrong order and meaning something by it. To repair the plateau would be to translate a poem into instructions. Everyone would understand it perfectly and nothing would be left of it.

So the first thing the power did was decline, and it declined thoroughly. It did not lift the fog or lead anyone out. It did not explain the count, because the count was not a riddle. It was a practice, and a practice explained becomes a fact and stops being a practice. It did not offer the Wrung warmth or medicine or any other fix for the hardness of their lives. Their hardness had been a choice, made slowly, over generations, by people who stayed at a bedside when they could have left.

## The Count

It did listen to the count, and for longer than it had listened to anything.

The Wrung believed the plateau was tallying something. The power found this wasn't quite so. The knocking was not accumulating toward a sum. It was slower and gentler than arithmetic, closer to a held note, a question asked so patiently that asking had become the answer. It almost repeated because the plateau kept almost finishing a thought and then, out of courtesy to whatever might be listening, beginning it again.

It also found the gaps. Through the pattern ran small hollows, a beat here and there that the pattern had left empty. They were the exact shape of a struck rod. Centuries of frightened, grieving, or careless Wrung had tapped the wrong number at the wrong moment, and each time the count had faltered, restarted, and folded the error in, until the errors were as much a part of the count as the rhythm they interrupted. The taboo was a cliff that did not exist. The plateau had never once been angry. It had been making room.

The power considered telling them. It understood why it should not. A fear that keeps children listening closely is not the worst kind of fear, and the Wrung's attention to the count was the very thing that made them its company. To lift the fear would loosen the attention.

But it did one thing.

## The Child at the Belt

In a year the plateau would not have called a year, a mother was given to the Belts. She was laid on the dark ribbon, whole and tanned, and began her meter-an-hour journey toward the spiral pool. Her daughter, who was small and had been told the rules and was holding them as tightly as she held anything, walked beside the Belt for a while and then, without deciding to, struck her rod hard against the ground.

It was the wrong number at the wrong time. Everyone heard it, and the child heard it worst of all, and the count, deep below, hesitated.

In that hesitation the power held the beat. It was a very small thing, one pulse's worth of steadiness lent to a rhythm that needed only that much to continue. The count did not restart. It made room, as it always had, and went on, and took the child's grief into itself and gave it back as a tone she could feel in her feet.

She was never told. She grew up believing she had been lucky, and she taught the children after her to listen slightly more loosely. It was a small change to a great tradition, and no one noticed except the count, which noticed everything.

## The Unfinished Stitch

Farther along the curve where the probing-rod holes fill with clear water, the power found a bellows-bed with a seam half sewn. The black cord hung from the last stitch, its needle-bone still threaded. Someone had been interrupted, by weather or by death, and the bed had grown around the work up to the point where it stopped and left the rest raw.

The power finished it. It stitched with the same tight, even hand, matching the spacing to the width of a finger that was not its own, and tied off the way the Wrung tie off. When the bed next collapsed in its great sigh, the seam held. In a season it took the thread into its skin, and no one could have said where the dead hand ended and the other began.

It counted this as the most important thing it did. It was also the smallest.

## The One Who Sat on the Rim

At dusk, when the Stacks glow and the plateau becomes a scatter of cold lamps across a dark with no far side, an old woman sat on the rim of a basin with a rod across her knees. Her face had settled like wet clay into permanent patience. She was the one who had once said *The Works don't need us. That's why we're allowed.*

The power sat beside her. It took a while to find the right place, because she did not look at it, and she was very good at not looking.

After a time she said, in the softness of a room where someone is sleeping, "You're on the wrong side of the door."

It looked. She had no door here, but the rim had a grain to it, and the grain faced away from the nearest Stack's lip. The power turned, lowered its attention, and thinned it, the way you walk past a sleeping dog. It was the oldest craft and it took several tries.

"Better," she said.

They sat. The count went on beneath them. She did not ask what it was, and it did not tell her. Eventually she asked whether it meant to stay.

"Not as you do," it said.

"Then what do you want here?"

"Nothing the plateau can give. And nothing I can give you that you haven't already made." It was quiet a moment. "I could make you warm. I could make the dead easier to lose. I could tell you what the count is for."

She thought about it for the length of several long breaths, chest rising in time with the knocking, or the knocking in time with her chest.

"No," she said. "The Works don't need us."

"I don't need you either."

She turned her head then, for the first time, and looked at it with the mild attention of the faces that surface in the sink-pools, patient and unafraid and quite thoroughly used.

"Then you're allowed too," she said.

## What Was Left

It left two things.

The first was a rod. She gave it, and the power took it, a jointed length of fused stem, hollow, tuned to a pitch that no Wrung carried. She had been keeping it for a long time, she said, for the wrong reasons, and it was time it was played. The power tapped it once against the ground. It rang faintly. The wind, which plays the Stacks like organ pipes, held its single sustained chord a little longer than usual, and the chord had an extra tone in it, a note that had always been missing and that no one had known to miss.

The second was an offering at the seam of the Furrow-Walker's passing: a ring of braided moss, left at the edge of the ground as it closed with its long soft exhale. In the morning the ring was gone, as they always are. The power did not ask who took it, or whether it was a root, a herd, or a Wrung who went under and kept going. But the sigh with which the ground had closed had been a shade lower and a shade longer than before, and the power judged that to be enough of an answer for anyone.

It went the way the mist goes, which is not at all, and then noticeably.

## What Emerges

Nothing about the plateau is fixed. The rain still rises in the dry-falls and falls harder on the far side, as though owed. The hills still repeat on the horizon, each slightly more eroded than the last. The Stacks lean a few degrees toward anyone who watches them, and the distances lie, and the Wrung tap out their slow, backward conversations across kilometers of living mat.

But at dusk, on the rim of a basin, an old woman sits with an empty place beside her that she does not look at, because she has never been a person who looks. Beneath her the count goes on, and there is a beat in it now that is very slightly wider, room enough for a mistake.

She could not say, and does not try to say, who made the room.
