# The Guest Who Greeted the Wall

It came the way the fog comes, down from the rim, and it did not announce itself. It had learned long ago that announcement is where most harm begins.

For a season it only listened.

It listened to the Counting and understood it faster than the Kept had in all their generations. Fracture faces eased against each other, load found its rest, and every tick was a small debt paid. It understood something else that the Kept only feared: the count is finite. The ticks were already fewer than in the grandmothers' time. Some night, in no life now walking, the last would fall and the basin would be still. The Hush they dreaded was not the ground gathering itself. It was the sound of a thing being finished.

It did not say so. It had first to decide what it was for.

## What it could have done

It could have stopped the white cough in every chest. It could have tempered the copper, shown them iron, and taught them the wheel and the ram. It could have put forest on the rim country so that woodsmoke was no one's privilege. It could have made the lime slow, or the ground certain, or the fog thin.

It did none of these. It did not think suffering ennobling. It had watched a man of forty fold in half over a doorway, coughing dust, and it had no wish to preserve that as a virtue. But forty thousand people had spent centuries in conversation with this water, and the city was what the conversation had produced. A gift that answered questions they hadn't asked would make them answer to the gift. It also did not know which of the things it might remove were load-bearing. A power that repairs what it hasn't understood is only a larger landslide.

So it settled on this. It would understand first. Where pain was plain and the remedy could be made in their own hands and their own words, it would say so plainly. Where the remedy needed something of its own, it would ask, and it would take the answer.

## The greeting

It took the shape of a rim-country traveler, small enough to turn sideways in the lanes, and walked in through the low gate at dawn under the white ceiling. In the first covered lane it laid its palm on the nearest wall, felt the cold sweat of the stone, and held it there a breath before it nodded to the woman kneeling ahead.

She was masked in gray cloth, working a doorway with a bone scraper. She looked at the hand on the wall and then at the stranger.

"You greet right," she said, low, from behind the cloth. "For rim."

"I was taught by a wall."

She made a sound that might have been a laugh. Her name was Dena Oru, scraper of the ninth lane, and it was the third day of scraping-month. She did not ask what the stranger wanted. She handed over a second scraper and pointed at the lower jamb.

It cut too deep on the first stroke. "Leave the skin," said Dena. "Always leave the skin. Bare stone doesn't know how to start again."

It was corrected, and it was glad to be, and it kept the sentence.

## The question

By evening it had learned the weight of a scraper, the sound the crust makes when it is ready to lift, and the whistle under Dena's breath when she bent. She was perhaps forty. In the room behind the narrowed door her mother sat at a window slit, breathing in short shallow drafts, watching a hand's-width of sky.

That night it said, quietly, "If I were something that could give, what would you ask?"

"Are you?"

"Yes."

Dena did not answer. She banked the fat-lamp and went to bed. It sat in the dark and listened to the stone tick, and it did not mind the wait. Waiting was one of the things it had come to relearn.

In the morning, while she tied on her mask, she said, "Not for me. My mother sits. All the old ones sit, in the slits, and can't get the air. If there's a thing, do it for the ones who sit." She touched the wall. "And don't do it to the wall."

"I can't mend old lungs," it said. "I'd be lying if I said I could. I can give them ease. And I can tell you something about the dust that might keep the young ones from sitting."

"Tell me the dust, then."

It told her plainly that the white in her chest was stone, ground fine by the bone against the lime and thrown into the air, and that the damp cloth caught only some of it. It said that if she wetted the crust with a brush of seep-water before the scraper touched it, the dust would not rise. The work would be slower, and the paste would smear, and she would not like it.

She did not like it. She said so at length, in a whisper, for three days. Then she wet one doorjamb and scraped it, and the air in front of her face stayed clear, and she stopped talking and did the second jamb. The skin of lime that remained was better than she had ever left it.

## The warm crack

On the fifth night a tick came that was not like the others. It was a deeper note, brief, from the floor of the basin beneath the oldest quarter, where a house-line had died out a century ago and its dwelling had been sealed to become cliff. The guest had leaned there. It hadn't forced the ground. The ground had been going to open somewhere. It only asked that it be here, beside a wall that belonged to no one.

At dawn Maren, the old water-reader, walking barefoot on the fractured stone with red at her wrist, stopped. The ground under her left foot was warm.

She knelt. A hairline crack, a thread of water no wider than a stalk of barley, and steam lifting from it into the fog. She put the back of her hand against it, then her tongue. There was lime in it, and something else, faint and mineral and old. She tied red cord at either end. When she straightened, the stranger was standing at the edge of the fan, hand on the wall.

"You did this."

"The ground was already opening. I asked it to open here, where no one's wall would be taken."

"The ground isn't asked."

"No," it said. "I'm not sure that's what I did. I leaned."

Maren looked at it a long time. She was very old, and she had walked this rim for fifty years, and she knew what she was looking at, or knew that she did not.

"It will fail," she said. "Warm seeps do. A season, a few years."

"Perhaps. I can't promise otherwise. I won't."

"Good," said Maren. "Don't."

## The Hush

Before it left the rim, she asked it what she had never asked anyone. She said it into the wall, as one says a thing one is ashamed of.

"When the stone stops. The Hush. Is it coming?"

It could have said no. It could have said nothing. It said the true thing, as gently as it could: that the count was slowing, and would go on slowing across more lifetimes than her line had names for, and that one night, long after her bones were glazed in the western seep, the ticking would end. It would not be the ground gathering. It would be the ground done. It said it could not promise the basin would never move again, that a fault may still slip, that the old rock was not wholly sure of itself. But the silence, when it came, was a peace, not a warning.

Maren was quiet for a long while.

"Don't tell the children," she said finally. "The story does its work. It makes them touch the wall in the morning." She flexed her cold hands. "But give me the end of it. I'll put it after the last line, where they'll find it when they're old enough to want it."

"What is the end?"

She thought.

"And then the count was done, and the morning came, and the wall was warm."

The guest said that was better than anything it would have made.

## The lane

Maren walked back into the city the same morning, and she did not speak. Doors closed quietly behind her as she went, because a water-reader who walks a lane in silence carries bad news. The lanes emptied ahead of her the way a tide empties a shore.

At the ninth lane she stopped, turned to the shuttered doors, and broke the custom. She spoke aloud, at nearly the pitch of an ordinary voice, which was almost rude.

"It's warm," she said. "There's a warm seep at the dead line's wall. For the ones who sit."

There was a long silence in the lane. Then a shutter opened a finger's width, and a second, and from behind them came the soft, muffled sound of forty thousand people not quite believing it, and beginning to.

## The bead

The guest stayed until the crack had been claimed, which the Kept accomplished without it: an argument among the street elders, a judgment by the water-readers that the seep was no one's and therefore everyone's, and a rota for the sitters, chalked in lime on the sealed wall itself. Old people were carried down in slings and lowered onto benches of travertine, and the steam went into them, and some of them slept.

On the last evening it walked up to the western rim, where the dead are wrapped in cloth and reed and the water is slowly glazing them into the cliff, and it laid its hand on the stone there for a while. Then it took a small pebble from the talus, set it in a seep where no house-line kept a claim, and tied a single red thread around the crevice to mark that it was spoken for.

It would come back for it. A child leaves a bead a few years. Someone with more patience could leave it longer, and the coating would tell exactly how long, and the Kept, who read such things, would know it had been a very long time.

## After

It left the way it had come, down through the gate in the fog, and nothing marked its going. The ticking went on that night, unhurried, one tick and then a pause and then another.

It carried away one thing it hadn't brought. It had believed, as such powers do, that a place was finished when it was safe. The Kept had taught it, with a bone scraper and a raised jamb, that a place can be a home while it is still becoming, and that the tending of the unfinished is itself a form of love.

The next spring, on scraping-day, Dena Oru knelt at her narrowed door with a brush of seep-water and a mask she no longer needed as much. A child from the next house squatted beside her and asked why she wetted the wall first, when nobody did.

"A guest showed me," she said. "Or I dreamed it."

"Which?"

She laid her palm flat on the cold stone, feeling it sweat.

"By spring you couldn't tell," she said, and went on scraping, and left the skin.
