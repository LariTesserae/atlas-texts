# What Came Down the Seam

It was not noticed at first, and that was by design.

The attention of the power arrived the way the light arrives, through a place where two sheets of the mat had not quite joined. There was no blade of brightness and no voice. For the length of one breath, the enormous chord of the Lid held still, as a sleeper's breath will when someone enters the room. Then it resumed. Nothing else in the hollow marked the moment. A bellcrake herd sounded its note across a fogbound basin. A rain-column fell into its pool with a sound like torn cloth. Far down the valley, an old woman named Ouen, who sat at the edge of camp and heard everything, lifted her head half a finger's width and put it down again.

The power was very old, and its kindness had been shaped by long practice at not doing things. It looked at the hollow the way one looks at a very complicated instrument that someone else is already playing. It saw the dark cathedral, the herds, the wolves, the small bands with their pots of blue light, and the slow ceiling-walkers with their hooked feet. It felt the mountains still rising underneath, and the green skin bridging every crack as fast as the rock could open one.

It felt the ache in the place too, and it did not pretend the ache was absent. Children were born here who would not live to be old. Herds were thinned by wolves, and the wolves had their own hunger. Under the Lid it was cold all year, and no one had ever felt the sun on their skin. A wise power does not mistake any of that for a defect that needs repair.

## What It Considered, and Set Down

It considered light first, as any newcomer would. It could have peeled back a valley's worth of roof and let the sun fall on the black peat. The people would have wept, then burned, and the pale lichens would have gone dark. Within a generation the felted mat would have torn itself to rags trying to close the wound. Every sound that made the hollow a hollow would have flattened into ordinary wind.

It considered fire and metal and the knowledge of smelting. The people had no metal because they had no need of it, and the old story of the year-long burning was the best teacher it could have left them.

It considered ending the wolves. It looked for a long moment at a herd going hoarse and stumbling on the second day of a sounding. It looked at the wolves waiting in the fog for half a breath of lateness, and it understood that the herd's throat and the wolf's patience had made each other. If it took one away, the other would lose its meaning, and the chord would go dead. It let them be.

It considered stilling the quakes and lifting the risk of the collapsing ceiling. But the quakes were also the bloom. The shaft of light that stabbed down after a fall, the red flowers and the animals traveling days to stand in it, were the ground's way of having a holiday. A world made safe from its own violence would be a world with no holidays.

It considered telling the people what lay above, the plain of green with its flowers, the full light. It found it did not have the right. The stories they told about the top were theirs. They would go up someday or they would not. A story kept whole is worth more than a fact handed over early.

And it considered the ones who stacked the stones, whoever they were. It could have named them. It could have pulled them into the open and introduced them to the bands as neighbors. But they had kept their distance for a very long time, and had left their receipts in pitch, and that was a form of speech too. It decided to leave the conversation as it stood.

When it had set all these aside, it found that very little remained. This did not trouble it. Very little is often the correct amount.

## The Form It Took

It wanted, before anything, to be in the place without being a burden on it. The hollow was a world of listeners. Even the wolves listened, and everything that was not a wolf listened for wolves. To arrive as a large being, or a bright one, or a speaking one, would have been to arrive as a great noise. The power wanted to be something that was not listening for anyone.

So it became a hush-wren.

It was the smallest, dullest, most overlooked form available: a brown thing the size of a thumb, in the ribs of a pillar, living inside the noise, stealing scraps from the gliders. In a world of listeners it was the one creature that could be trusted not to be listening for you. That was the first gift, and it was a gift to itself. For a season it lived among the mat-drip and the chorister wave and the herds' slow arithmetic, and it learned the hollow the way the hollow's own creatures learn it, from the inside, as sound.

It followed a band. It rode the shoulder of a boy named Teth, who never noticed, along the valley floor and up the traverse routes where the black cairns stood. It watched the clay pots pass from hand to hand and the blue light run through the fingers and fade. It learned that the children were taught to tell a hundred herd-tones before they were taught to be careful, and thought this the most sensible curriculum it had ever encountered.

## The One Small Thing

In its fourth month there was a birth.

The mother was young and the labor was hard. The birth-hour is when a mother finds the child's note, a hummed pitch that will be the name. She has to hear it in herself in the hour of greatest pain, and this mother could not. The pain was too loud. Her humming wandered, found nothing, and began to fail. The old listener sat at the camp's edge and did not intervene, because a note cannot be given to a child by anyone but the mother, and everyone in the shelter knew it. A child without a note is not driven out, but is a child who walks among the herds without a place in them.

The hush-wren sat in the smoke-hole, where there was never any smoke. It had not sung in its life, since its kind had long ago given up song. It felt the cold, exact shape of the note the child was reaching for, not a note in any sense the power could have chosen, but the one that already belonged to that particular small person. The mother was very near it. She only needed to hear it once.

It sang. It opened a throat made for silence and sounded one clear tone, from the ceiling of the shelter, for the length of a breath.

The mother heard it. Her humming steadied and found it, and she held it through the last of the labor. The child came out and was given its name in the dark, hummed against the wet forehead. In the smoke-hole, a small brown bird was already quiet again, and no one, in all the commotion, looked up.

The power did not know whether this had been right. It had spent months laying down the rule that it would not carry anything into the hollow that the hollow had not made. It had broken the rule for one breath, in one throat, for one note that already existed. It let itself keep the doubt. Doubt, it thought, was probably part of being kind.

## The Question

There was one thing left to do, and the power had left it for last because it wanted to be sure of its manners.

Under all its months of listening it had felt a layer in the great chord that it could not at first name. The Lid was a drum, rung by drip and wind and tremor, and the note it gave back was taken by the bands for weather. But under the weather, slow as ice moving, there was a second thing. It was the same chord but altered. The herd-tones were in it, stretched to a hundred times their length. The glider-clicks were in it, and the chorister wave, and the drop when a bellcrake fell. Once, faintly, there was even a child's hummed name, the new one, the note the wren had sung, laid into the chord as a single added thread.

The Lid was not merely ringing. It was answering. It had been answering for a hundred thousand years, in the only voice it had, and the answer sounded exactly like the sky holding its breath. No one had heard it as an answer because no one had ever asked it a question.

The power did not ask the Lid what it wanted. It was afraid the question would be too large. Instead it did what it had done for the whole visit: it listened, and then it added, very quietly, one sound of its own. It let the wren's silence extend up through the pillar and into the ceiling, and it said, in the only way that could be understood, *I hear you.*

The Lid, over a stretch of some hundreds of meters, stopped ringing.

It lasted less than a minute. In that minute there was no drip, no hum, and no tick from the thousand hanging spires. It was a sudden, breathtaking, sacred silence, the silence of something turning its whole attention toward one point. In every camp in the valley the listeners lifted their heads. Old Ouen stood up.

Then the chord came back, and it was slightly different. There was a new low harmonic in it, nearly too deep to hear, a tone that rose and fell with the wren's own pulse, as if the Lid were now keeping time with something small. It was not a request or a demand. It was only the sound of being known, coming home to the thing that had been knowing.

## What It Left

By the standards of powers, it left almost nothing.

The old listener Ouen went on sitting at the edge of the fog, but she sat differently. She had heard the silence and the harmonic, and though she never told the band, she began to teach the young listeners a distinction no one had taught before: the difference between the sound the Lid made because it was struck and the sound it made because it was speaking. It is a very hard difference to hear. She said it was mostly a matter of patience, and she was right.

A child in Teth's band had a note that had come from somewhere other than his mother's pain. It was strong and clear and slightly to one side of the herd's pitch, and when he was old enough to call, the bellcrakes answered him a little louder than they answered anyone else. No one could say why.

At the rope bridge, the next time the band re-twisted the fiber, they found on the far side a small fresh pile of drip-tip pitch on a flat stone. Beside it, for the first time, was a second stone, a tiny black one, balanced very carefully on the first. They did not know what to make of it. They put a pot of lichen down in exchange, as always, and went home.

Somewhere in the highest fog of the eastern wall, on a night with no wind, a listener heard the double knock, stone on stone, patient, at the interval of a breath. And for the first time in memory, it was answered, from somewhere low in the valley, in the same rhythm, by something small and quick, three knocks and a fourth. The stones stopped for a long time. Then they resumed, a little faster, like someone making a decision.

The power left in the eleventh month, through a seam of gray-green light, at the hour when the chorister wave was running north. It took no form, or rather it let the last one go: a brown bird on a pillar-rib, mute again, unremarkable, one of thousands. It did not look back, because there was nothing it needed to see.

## Afterward

The hollow went on being the hollow. The wolves waited in the fog for half a breath of lateness. The herds sounded their long chords and lost a member to the dark. A quake dropped a hundred meters of ceiling, and for three days a shaft of true light fell on a floor that flowered in red and white, and the animals came. The mountains kept rising. The Lid kept bridging their cracks with pale green shoots.

Nothing was fixed, because nothing there was broken. What had happened was smaller and, the power thought, better. In a world made wholly of listening, someone had listened to the listeners, and the largest of them had felt it. That was all.

At the edge of a band's camp, in the deep of a long dim evening, an old woman lifted one finger. *Stay.*

They stayed. The wave ran north beneath them. Far off a herd sounded, and another answered, and the roof hummed its enormous chord, with one deep new thread in it that kept, very faintly, the time of a small heart.
