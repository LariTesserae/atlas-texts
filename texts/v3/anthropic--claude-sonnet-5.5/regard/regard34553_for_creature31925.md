# What Came to the Rim

It did not descend. The first thing it did was stop at the gray lip of the bowl and let the mist take its measure, the way the mist takes the measure of everything, without hurry and without verdict. A power that can do anything learns early that its first act is the one that counts, and the wisest first act is often to stay still long enough to learn what your arrival would cost.

So it stayed. Drops fell from the leaf-tips one at a time. It counted nothing. It let the place count it.

## What It Looked For

It looked for harm first, because harm is what a benevolent thing is obliged to look for.

It found the jaw, the tongue, and the web, and it followed each to its end. The eaten were taken quickly and replaced at the rate they were taken. The salamanders were not afraid. The frogs' throats pulsed in their slow private rhythms, and nothing in the hollow was in despair. It looked for despair the way you look for a coin in a dark pool, with a hand that knows the shape of what it wants, and it came up empty.

Then it looked for the other harm, the quiet one, which is stasis. Nothing here was becoming anything else. The salamander was the descendant of ten thousand identical salamanders. It weighed this longest, because a mercy that mistakes stillness for suffering is only vanity dressed as kindness. A salamander does not ache for weather. Boredom requires a wish to be elsewhere, and there was no such wish in the film of water each of them wore. It decided the hollow was not wounded. It was finished, the way a stone is finished, or a bowl, or a long sentence that has reached its period.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not open the canopy. It did not bring light, though it could have laid a shaft of noon down through the ferns and watched every pale frog blink at the first sun of ten thousand generations. It did not bring wind, seasons, or rain.

This was not caution. A gift given to a pattern lands on the pattern's bodies as a flood. Light would have scalded the eyeless fish and dried the backs of the salamanders. The moss-glow, which had held one green since before the terraces, would have had to fight for its small authority. The hollow had spent an age arriving at a balance, and the most loving thing was to see that the balance was an achievement and not an oversight.

It did not clear the moss from the shapes on the terraces. It did not lift the humped, fern-crowned hummocks to see what they held. It came near them and was quiet, and that was all.

## The Note

At the deepest part of the bowl, at the black pool, it knelt.

It knew, as one knows things in a place like this, that the pool's stillness was an agreement between two rates, and that the thing beneath the silt was the one part of the hollow that was not simply pattern. The rest of the hollow was a great many small bodies thinking slowly as one. The singer was the one irregularity, the one thing that came at no interval a person could count.

It waited, and the note rose, low and round, a wet finger drawn once around the rim of glass. It faded without an echo, since nothing here was hard enough to return it.

Then the power did the one thing no one and nothing had ever done in the hollow. It answered.

It made no grand sound. It made the same note, the same wet curve, and let it fade the same way. It was not a copy. It was a reply, the small courtesy of a listener who says *I heard you*. In the whole long life of the hollow, the *tk* in the ferns had never been answered and the note had never been answered, and nothing had ever been told that it was heard.

The silt did not stir at first. Then the surface shivered, once, for a reason.

## What the Singer Was

It was not exactly a being and not exactly a place. The power had half expected an old amphibian in the mud with its throat sac slowly filling, and there was something like that, but the something was also the drip, the count, and the arm's length between frogs. The singer was the part of the hollow that held the whole hollow in mind.

And the power understood what the note was. It was not a call or a lament. It was a keeping. Every unmeasured interval, the singer drew the pattern back into itself and set it right, the way a person touches a wall in the dark to make sure it is still there. The count did not hold by itself. Someone held it. Someone had held it for so long that no one, the holder included, remembered it was being held.

The power did not say this aloud. It set the understanding before the singer as you set a cup before a guest, without pressing.

Then it made an offer. It was not a gift, and it was not a rescue, because the singer had not asked to be rescued. It offered to hold the count for the length of one breath, so that the singer might, for once, let go and see what happened.

The singer said nothing. But the surface of the pool went, very slowly, entirely still, stiller than it had ever been.

The power took the count. It was light, lighter than it looked, made of ten thousand small arrangements, each one a frog on its leaf or a bead on its tip. It held the hollow the way you hold water in cupped hands, gently and without squeezing.

And in that breath, the singer did not sing.

It lay in the silt. It heard the drip. It heard the snail's foot lift from the leaf and the soft rubbery *tk* in the ferns, and for the first time it heard them from outside the holding. Nothing fell apart. The frogs stayed where they were, and the fish did not turn. The moss-glow held its one green. The hollow was not sustained by the singer's effort alone. It had also, over its long age, learned to hold itself, and the singer had never once stopped long enough to find out.

Then, in its own time, by its own choice, the singer sang. It was the same note, low and round, rising from the deep and fading without echo. But it was not the same note at all. It had been a duty, and now it was a song.

## The Ones Under the Moss

Before it left, the power went along the terraces to the fern-crowned shapes, the shoulders and bent backs furred green. It did not pull the moss away. It laid its attention against them, as one lays a palm on a sleeping flank, and learned what they were.

They were not suffering. The hollow had found a place for them, as it finds a place for everything, and the finding was not cruel. They were held, and they were slowly becoming moss, and there was a kind of peace in it that the power did not presume to undo.

But it saw what the hollow could not. The hollow kept the count and not the counted. Every frog was a frog, and no frog was *this* frog. Whoever had stood here, with the hand-axe and the twisted cord, had once been *someone*, and the hollow had no place in its arrangement for someone.

So the power did the one thing it could do without changing anything. It remembered them. It read what was left, the lash of the haft, the knot in the cord, the angle at which the axe had been wedged, and it took from these a few names, a few faces, a few mornings on some warmer slope before the climb. It spoke each name once, quietly, into the fog. The hollow did not notice. The power kept the names, and that was enough. The axe stayed in the fork of the tree fern, and the cord stayed strung between its two trunks for no purpose that anyone could guess.

## What It Left

It took nothing. It left no light, no wind, no seasons.

It left one small asymmetry. Somewhere in the canopy a single leaf-tip, which had let go its bead at the same pace for ten thousand generations, would now sometimes let go a little late. No one would count it. The drip would still be even to any ear. But the hollow, which had never had a *sometimes*, now had one, a small door left ajar in a room that had believed itself to have only walls.

And it left a promise, made to the pool and not aloud: that it would return, at no interval you could count.

## The Gap

When it rose to go, it looked back at the place on the lowest terrace where it had knelt beside the black water. The hollow had already begun to close around the shape it had left, as it does. The moss reached for the edges of its footprint and the film of water rose in the print. In a moment there would have been a place for it, and it would have been fitted in, one more quiet form among the rest.

But the frogs, the pale ones with eyes like dropped sequins, did not close the gap. A thumbnail of open ground remained where the power had knelt, and the nearest frog sat an arm's length and a half away instead of an arm's length. The hollow was full, and it always found a place for what entered it.

This time it kept a place open, for what might return.
