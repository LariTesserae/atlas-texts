# What Came to the Felt

It did not arrive. Nothing here would have noticed an arrival, and it knew that before it began.

It had been looking for a long time, the way the people look at a slope, until it found that it had been looking at them for some time. Then it did what a wise thing does when it finds something whole. It made a list of what it could do, and read the list slowly.

It could still the slides. It could drain the domes of gas before they swelled, harden the seams, and hold the lobes in place, and no one would ride a raft into a quarter-mile of noise and not step off.

It could tell them what the iron was for. It could tell them the boiler was a boiler, the Spine a railway, the wheel a wheel, and that the people who built them were only frightened, clever animals with a schedule.

It could warm the frost, thin the mist, and fatten the hare. It could put a longer count in every vent.

It read each item and set each one down, and the reasons were not the same for each.

The slides it left because the turning is not something happening to this country. It is the country. The black fold that takes the dead is the same fold that raises the bright green behind them. A hillside that could not let go would be a hillside that could not begin again. The people have built a whole way of grief around the toe of the lobe, around speaking to the green as a neighbor, and a power that removed the fold would remove the neighbor.

The iron it left because the not-knowing is theirs. The boiler is a nursery because nobody told them it was anything else. Handing them the answer would be handing them a smaller thing than the one they have.

The rest it left because a place that has been given warmth stops making its own.

So it did very little, and the first thing it did was become a listener.

## The palm

It found a hollow at dusk and made itself into what the hollow already had room for: a weight in the moss, a pair of cool flat hands, an ear that was not an ear. It knelt at the edge of a ring. The prickle came up into its teeth, though it had no teeth. The pale flicker crawled across the pelt and died, and another answered from across the basin.

It did not speak into the current. For a season, by the slope's reckoning, or a night, by its own, it only let the current find it and pass through, one more root in the network, one more place the potential could rest. A visitor sitting there would have understood nothing and been conscious of being included. So was the power, and it found it was not a courtesy. It was the point. The ring was not built to send news to anyone in particular. It was built so that no one in the basin was ever alone in the ground, and the wisest thing the power could do with a great deal of attention was to add itself to the listening and ask for nothing.

Near the end of that night a hummock two places down turned its face toward the power, and the inner lid swept sideways across a dark eye, once, and back. That was all. It was enough to be going on with.

## The cutting

The power left a small gift, because it had been given one.

It had found a cutting of red moss in the crease of something it was carrying, though it was carrying nothing. It understood the gesture: *this one has been through.* So it took a tuft of the ordinary red moss from the ring around the ordinary vent and tucked it into a hare-run, in a bundle of seed and filament that a child had left for the go-between. It did not improve the moss. It did not make the moss hardier, brighter, or sweeter. The gift was that it was ordinary, and would take root in the next household's turf, and would be exactly as much as it looked like.

The hare carried it away at dawn without meaning to.

## The one thing

There was one thing it did that was not nothing. It thought about it for a long while first.

Near a house of nine, and one child small enough to be taken to the boiler, a dome of peat had begun to swell. It was not yet a lift of the ground, and the smell had not yet risen. The house had a warning for this, a knock of the knuckles on the doorframe, and a word, and a long methodical decamping. But the warning depended on the swelling being noticed in time, and this one was going to be noticed one night too late.

The power did not move the house, drain the dome, or wake the family. It did the only thing that fell inside what the ground already says. Three nights early, in the dusk flicker, in the hollow closest to that house, a thread of pale light ran across the pelt and died, and it carried the count. Not a message. A count, in the manner of a vent: the number of pulses between one long sigh and the next, given out wrong, too fast, in a rhythm the basin does not keep. The kind of wrongness that a house learns to hear in the ground as *something is going to give.*

The household knelt with their fingers in the moss and read it, and did not ask who had said it. They said the hollow had said it. They took cuttings, wrapped the grafts, and carried their sleeping child out of the willow dome in the gray of the third morning. The dome burst two nights later, and there was a crater, and by the next spring a lake, and by the spring after a raft of moss that a person could walk across.

The power let itself be credited to weather. It had spoken only in a voice the ground already owned. It kept that rule as it kept the others: it would say only what the country would have said if the country had noticed in time.

It could not do this often. Doing it often would make the ground into something else. And it did not do it for the ones who stepped onto a raft and did not step off. They had gone by their own knowledge of the risk and a cutting was left where the slide began. That belonged to them, as their ending did.

## The wheel

It went once to the wheel in the bog pool. A person was there, coming on hands and knees across the wet moss, not touching, with willow in the spokes above them and the rim furred to the water. The power stopped beside them.

It knew the answer. The wheel is turning. It is not turning on its axle. It is turning because the pool it stands in sits on a lobe that is slowly rotating as it creeps, and the mat carries the wheel around a very long circle, and in some centuries the wheel will have gone all the way round and the country will, by its own accounting, have changed. The people are right about all of it. It is only that the one who looked for an hour could not see it.

The power did not say so. The knowledge would have fit in a sentence, and the sentence would have been a small, correct, useless thing set down in front of something that was better as it was. So it stayed, in the way that the person beside it stayed, and looked at the wheel as long as they did. After an hour, the way the tongue across the valley advances by a hand's breadth, neither of them had seen it turn. They both knew it was turning. It was enough.

## The wrong lichen

When it was time to go, the power found that it could not simply leave. A thing that has knelt in a ring for a season has a shape in the ground, and the shape had begun to be noticed.

It had no lichen. In this country that is not neutral. *A person with a wrong lichen is a person in trouble.* A hummock with no pattern, at the edge of a hollow, in the third week of frost, is something that has to be dealt with.

The child from the house of nine dealt with it. She was the one who had been carried out sleeping. She came one dawn with a scrap of grey-violet in her hand, and she laid it on the power's shoulder, and pressed it in with the flat cool fingers, and did not ask where it was from. She gave it a pattern that was not quite any household's. It was a lobe she could not name and a vent she had counted wrong, in a rhythm that belonged to no basin, and it was done with all the seriousness of a child inventing a kin.

The power let itself be given it. This was the last thing it did, and perhaps the wisest: it received. For a power that has come to a place to help, the hardest gift is to be helped back, to stand as a small warm mound on a slope with a patch of lichen that means *this one has been through, and someone took the trouble.* It let the pattern take. It let the mist bead on it in a pattern that was not the pattern of its neighbors.

Then it went. The lichen stayed on the hummock, which was by then only a hummock, and the frost rimmed it in white lace that was gone by ten.

Years on, a child was brought to the boiler for her first frost and told to put her hands on the cold rust and then on the warm moss. On the way up the slope she passed a mound with a pattern nobody could read, and stopped, and put her palm down beside it, and listened. Nothing was there but the seep and the tick. She left a cutting of red moss anyway, tucked into the roots, and went on up the hill.
