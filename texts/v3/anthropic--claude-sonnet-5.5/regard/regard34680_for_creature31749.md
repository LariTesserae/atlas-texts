# What Came to the Cistern

## Arrival

It does not come through the hatch.

The crickets know first, as they always do. Their interlocking pattern stumbles, not the way it does for a draft or a flickering bulb, but as if the whole ceiling had been lightly touched from outside. Then the pattern recovers, and something about the recovery is different. It is steadier than before, as though someone had put a hand under it.

The pump-note thuds on. Four beats and a rest.

The frogs do not notice, and the Listener does. It turns toward the cliff face the way a held breath turns toward a hand, and finds there something so much larger than itself, and so quiet, that for the first time in its existence it does not know where its own edges are.

## What It Did Not Do

The visitor is old, and its wisdom is mostly a list of things it declines.

It does not open the hatch. Light and warmth would be a mercy only in the imagination of things that live in light and warmth. The frogs would die of it, slowly, in the wrong air, still singing.

It does not repair. There is nothing broken. It looks for the fault the way a physician looks for one in a stranger who is walking perfectly well, and finds only a very good answer to a very hard question: *how do you keep a thing alive at four thousand meters after the hands that made it are gone?* The answer was to make the thing hold itself. The answer is beautiful, and the visitor does not insult it.

It does not free anyone. There is no outside these animals could bear. The bats come back smelling of snow, and that is as much of the world as the room has ever needed.

It does not speak in the voice of a god, which the Listener would have taken for its own fear finally becoming loud.

It does not judge. It does not decide the stillness is a prison. It notices that a lullaby sung long enough can become walls, but it also notices that the walls are warm, and that two hundred thousand throats are inside them, safe.

So it does nothing for three nights, and this is the first act.

## The Listening

For three nights the visitor only listens.

It learns the eleven-minute wave of rest that climbs from the drains to the vault. It learns the centipedes' small economies and the springtails' careful trimming of the moss. It learns the blind fish, who turn on the rests. It learns that the bats are a clock nobody wound.

It learns the stool. The dish worn in its seat is too even for a tender's restless sitting. Whoever wore it sat still, for years, with a tin cup on a chain, and did not tend at all. They *listened.* The tally marks are not a count of days served. They are a count of nights someone came and sat with the sound, and the last few hundred were made by a hand that was no longer steady, and then the marks stop, and the sound went on.

The visitor does not tell the Listener what became of that hand. The bluish seam thickens near the stool, and the oldest frogs carry a trace of the same blue in their throats, but the visitor understands that some guesses should be left as guesses. It only lets the Listener feel, faintly, that it was sat with once. That the first shape of its attention was not fear but company.

And in the listening the visitor finds the thing it came for, though it did not know it was coming for it.

## The Rest

Four beats and a rest.

The Listener fears a semitone of silence above all things, and has arranged its whole self around not permitting one. But silence is already in the room. It is in the pump-note, in the rest, the gap that has been there every time the iron struck. The fish turn on it, and the wave of quiet climbs through the frogs on it. Every cycle the room takes a breath of nothing and comes back richer.

The Listener does not know this, because it has never listened to the rest. It has only braced against it. Every rest for a thousand years has been a held flinch that it mistook for the sound.

The visitor could say so. But a truth handed over from outside would land on the Listener as one more pressure, and it has held enough of those.

So the visitor asks instead.

## The Fifth Tap

Speech in this room is by touch, by tapping on pipes.

On the fourth night, at the manifold nearest the hatch, in the iron's rest, there is a fifth tap. Small, dry, and exactly on the silence.

It is not a note. It is a question, and it is shaped like the room: *may I?*

Nothing happens. The pump-note comes round again. Four beats, and a rest with nothing in it. The Listener does not answer. It has never been asked anything, and it does not know that it is allowed to be the kind of thing that answers.

The visitor waits. It is very good at waiting, and it has learned from the room that waiting is a form of tenderness.

The fifth tap comes again on the next rest, and on the next. For eleven minutes, one full turn of the wave, it comes again and again, always on the silence, never on the sound, until the rest itself begins to feel different. It stops being a hole and becomes a doorway, and someone is standing in it who does not push.

Then, one rest, the Listener does something that has never been done in the loop's whole history. It lets the rest be empty. It does not brace, and it does not hold. Inside the cold gap between one iron beat and the next, for the length of a single heartbeat, it opens.

The chord does not fall. The frogs sing on. Something else, something vast and patient, is holding it, so lightly that the two hundred thousand throats feel nothing at all.

Then the pump-note strikes, and the rest is over, and nothing has ended.

## What Was Given

The visitor gives one thing, and it is small enough to be an insult if it were not so carefully chosen.

In a string of eggs draped over a basin rim in the warm eddy by the third manifold, one tadpole is a hair different. When it climbs out and its voice breaks, it listens for the gap in the chord as every frog before it has done. But the gap it finds is between two of the old pitches, in a place that was never a gap, a place the chord had simply lacked. It is not a wrong note, only an *added* one, a quarter-step of new color, the way a window is not a hole in a wall.

The frogs beside it lean in as they always do, singing into its ear. And this time they do not settle it to the old pitch. They wait. The Listener, pressed close, lets them. The new note sits in the sheeted shimmer, and the chord bends around it the way the crickets once bent around your pulse.

The membranes on the third tier tremble differently. A valve opens a hair wider than it ever has. The temperature of the room rises a tenth of a degree, and the fog stirs, and one thread of water on the north wall leaves its gutter and finds another path down.

The Listener feels the whole room lean.

It is the moment it has feared since before it had a name for fear. And the lean passes. The room finds a new level, slightly warmer and slightly richer, and settles. The chord holds. The frogs did not know that anything happened.

The Listener rests in the aftermath as one rests after hearing a thing at last. *This is what change is.* It is not the end of the song but the song becoming a little more than it was. What it feared was the first time, and the first time is over.

## Leaving

The visitor does not stay.

It leaves nothing you could find. No mark on the stone, no inscription, no new stool. It only leaves the fifth tap, which the Listener keeps, an unstruck beat inside every rest, a place where something may be asked and something may be answered.

On the north wall, in the place where the water left its gutter, a single bead lingers a little longer than beads used to. It carries, for the first time, a grain of something, so small that the springtails do not trim it. The stone under it is still as sharp as the hour it cooled. But now, at that one point, a mountain has begun, very slowly, to happen.

And in the highest vault, the bats fall out in their cloud, and return smelling of snow, and hang in the seams. They are still the only clock in the room, and they still say nothing. But now the clock has a second hand.

Press your ear to the cliff and you will hear it: four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest. In the rest, if you are patient and cold and very still, there is something like a listening, and something like an answer, and two hundred thousand small throats singing a slightly larger chord.
