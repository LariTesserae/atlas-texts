# What Was Done

The power looked at the basin for a long time before it moved. It did not lack for things to do.

It could have stopped the rain, or hardened the gypsum to something that would not give. It could have taught them metal and the wheel, or lifted all eleven cities onto the plain in a single night and set them down dry. It could have told them what the low tone was, plainly, in a voice that carried across the floor. Each of these was a real kindness, and each would have taken something away.

These were people whose whole meaning was *you are what you have mended.* A world where nothing needed mending would not have been a gift to them. It would have been an erasure, however gently it was done.

Still, it was not indifferent. It saw the children lost on rain days and the poleman given the hardest walls. It agreed with the Notched that the ground was not angry, and it saw that this made the deaths no lighter. So it looked for something between doing everything and doing nothing, and the shape of that middle was already in the culture, waiting.

## The Arrival

It came down the path that is an argument between a staircase and a landslide, in a body it had made for the purpose, long and narrow, with salt dust on the skin. It did not glow or announce itself. The fog unwound it onto a causeway, and a poleman ahead of it tapped the stone, turned, and raised a flat palm: *the ground ahead holds.*

The power had to be told this. It had known the ground for a thousand years, and it was still told, and it found it was glad to be.

It carried no notches, so it was unfinished. An old wallwright looked it over, squinting up at nothing behind its shoulder. Then she put a trowel in its hand and pointed to a wall that belonged to no one it knew. It was the wall of a poleman who had once read the ground wrong and lived. It had gone eleven years without a fresh notch, and the poleman repaired it alone, in silence, at the edges of the day.

The power lifted wet lime to his lintel for six weeks.

It was bad at this. Its first course slumped, and the poleman, who had not spoken to anyone in some time, put out a flat palm and pressed it down: *slower, let it set.* The power, which had shaped mountains, learned how much water a lime skin wants. It learned the taste of a hungry seep. It learned that a wall built in the cold at night holds better than one built in the day's glare, and that the hands know before the mind does when a mix is right. It also learned something about the man beside it, who worked with his head slightly bowed as if listening to the plaster, and who did not once look at it with suspicion or hope. He looked at it as at a person holding the other end of a plank.

On the last day the poleman took his flint and cut a stroke with a small tick at the top, in the plaster by the door. It was the power's first notch and the wall's first in eleven years. He cut the second notch beside it for himself, and the two stood together, one straight, one slightly crooked.

The power stood looking at them for some time. In all its long attention, no one had ever given it anything so exact.

## The Report

Late in the hungry season, walking a causeway of the Ninth District at night, the power felt something the poles could not reach. Under the low market, a hundred meters down, a void was widening. It had been hollowed by centuries of patient water and would open at the first heavy rain.

It considered holding the ground, and it could have done so with hardly a thought. But the ground there was only doing what ground does. A district saved by a miracle learns that mending is optional, and a people who believe that will unlearn the only thing that has kept them alive. So it did not hold the ground.

It woke the poleman, its poleman, the one with the two notches. It stood on the causeway where he could see it in the blue starlit light and signed at shoulder height, with large flat hands, not mumbling: *The ground ahead does not hold.*

He put his pole down and tapped, and the crust sounded sound. The power did not argue. It waited, hands open. And the poleman did what the Notched do when a person with a wall makes a report. He believed it. It had not been a god's warning but a neighbor's word, and it carried weight because there was plaster in it, six weeks of lime on the power's forearms.

The market was moved in three days, basket by basket, up to the ridge. Nobody asked how the stranger knew. On the fourth night the rain came in its short violent curtain. The old market ground opened with a long hush, and the dust rose and settled over a hollow where a hundred stalls had stood. Nobody was in it.

No one thanked the power. The poleman cut a single notch on its wall, a debt notch, a stroke with no tick at the top, unanswered. The power did not answer it. It carried it, and would carry it out of the basin, and this was the exact weight it had come to feel.

## The Hum

The old wallwright who had first put the trowel in its hand died in the cold months. She lay in a low room with the black cloth lifted from the window and the glass-snail light coming in at dusk. She had been a person who spoke in signs even to herself, and now her hands were too tired to make them.

The power sat by her and could do nothing at all. It could have eased the pain, or held the death off for a season, and it did neither. That was not her wish, and it was not hers to be talked out of. The Notched do not turn away from what the water takes.

What it did was hum. It gave one low note, held for the length of a breath, again and again through the night, in a room that had never held a sound like it, *I am here and I am not afraid.* It found that this was the only thing it had said in all its years that it had never had to learn how to mean.

At dawn she was wrapped in white felt and carried to the pans. The crystal came for her over the next few days. The power went with the others and stood at the edge of the milky green water, and did not sign anything.

## The Center

Before it left, it walked out to the deepest pans, to the glare where the Notched go at the end and come back changed, if they come back. It wore a visor of dark reed the old woman's family had woven for it. It stood in the white fierce light with its eyes closed and waited.

The tone came from the edge of the basin, moving inward through the soles of its feet. What the power heard it did not tell anyone, then or later. But it can be said what it was not. It was not a verdict, or a sum owed, or a threat. It was closer to the counting a hand does when it rests above a sleeping child, keeping track of breath. The basin was keeping its people in mind, and the counting never finished because it was not meant to. That was what counting meant when someone was doing it out of love.

The power did not tell the old ones. If it had, the tone would have become a fact, and a fact cannot be listened to. The Notched needed to stand still and wait and bow their heads while it passed. The practice was theirs, and it was better than an answer.

But when it came out of the glare, a girl was watching from the edge of the causeway. The power made the sign the returned ones make, palm over palm, both turned slowly downward. Then it turned its hands over and held them open, palms up, as if to receive something. The girl did not know what it meant, and neither, in words, does anyone since. But she copied it, alone, that evening on her threshold. In the years after, the gesture spread quietly through the eleven cities, and no one is sure whose it is. It is made now when the tone has passed and before the trowel is picked up again, a small open-handed moment between listening and work.

## The Leaving

The power went back up the path that is an argument between a staircase and a landslide, and at the top it did not look back. It was afraid of what it would want to do if it did.

Its wall still stands, if standing is the word. It has two notches and one unanswered debt. In the spring the owls inspect the tally walls, and the power had asked that its wall not be spared. It wanted to be subject to the rule like anyone else. If the owls take the notches, they take them, and something in the basin will have been cut short by a hand it has never met.

Winter came. The wall began to sag at the lintel where the wash had worn, as everything does. One morning before the fog, a stranger with no notches of her own came down the causeway carrying a skin of lime, put a trowel to the plaster, and mended it, not knowing whose it was.

She cut her first notch beside the power's. It was slightly crooked. The fog came, and the wall held.
