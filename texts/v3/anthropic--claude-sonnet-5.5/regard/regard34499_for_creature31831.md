# What Was Done, and What Was Not

It did not descend. Nothing that attends to a place like this needs to arrive from above.

The first sign was that the Breath came over the rim a little slower one morning, and that no one noticed, because nothing on the plateau was in a hurry to be noticed. The attention was wide and unhurried and had no edges. It moved across the tableland the way the fog does, touching and letting the land take what it needed. It stayed a long time before it decided anything.

## What It Considered

It saw that it could do a great deal.

It could bring water, real water, running in the old channels for the first time in a hundred thousand years. The dry beds would darken and the comb-reeds would stand in green they had never had. It saw at once what would follow. The fog-drinking would become a memory, and then a custom, and then a story. A grooming that speaks a bond would become an ordinary chore, and the people would be safer and slightly elsewhere. The plateau had no thirst that needed curing. It had an arrangement, and the arrangement was the point.

It could teach them to build, or to write. It saw that the Keepers had declined both with open eyes. The palm-readers memorize the stone the way a river memorizes its bed, and the gift of a page would tell them the stone was insufficient. They would believe it, because they are courteous.

It could warn them. This was the hardest. The banded cliffs hold dark thin lines, and the power could read further along the strata than any palm-reader, past the present into the years the stone has not yet written. It knew that the cloud sea would not always fill the basins, and that some future slow shifting of the far ocean would thin the Breath to threads. It weighed this for what, for a mind like that, was a long while.

It set the knowledge down. The Keepers already know that the Breath can fail, because their entire way of life is a listening for it. They have counted droughts back further than they trust the count, and they have come through them the way they come through everything, by asking little and keeping time. A warning would give them the future without giving them anything to do with it, only a weight to carry on mornings when the fog is still coming. The power judged that a foretold ending is a heavier thing to carry than an ending. Whatever waits at the end of the long dry line, the Keepers would meet it in the way they meet the dawn, with their faces lifted and their arms held slightly away from the body.

It could reveal itself to the Sleeper and offer to speak. It came very close to this. But the Sleeper has no face and no voice, and it has been telling the whole plateau what the cloud is doing for longer than there has been anyone to listen. The power understood that to address such a being suddenly, from outside, would be like shouting into a room where someone was sleeping soundly.

So it did not do these things. Deciding this cost it nothing, and it did not mistake the decision for the whole of wisdom. Restraint is only the first half of a courtesy. The second half is deciding what to do with your hands.

## What It Learned to Do

It learned manners, as a guest does.

It walked lightly on the salt-crust. It did not dig. It went around the morning webs, so that its path curved and doubled back among theirs, and from a ridge the loops looked like someone thinking. It stood on the western swells at dusk and said nothing while the cloud sea lit from within and the pillars threw their kilometer shadows.

It stood in the fog with the Keepers, with its arms held a little away from itself, and took the Breath the way they did. It had no mouth for it and no skin that could be wet. It received it anyway, and this was not pretending. The Breath arrives at whatever is standing in it, and asks nothing about how the arriving is done.

Once, before dawn, it combed a field of comb-reeds. It did this badly at first, drawing the fans through with a tooth too wide, so that the stalks stood at a slightly different angle from the ones the Keepers tend. The Hollow-Keepers found it that morning. They stood at the edge of the field a long time, and no one said anything. That evening they left a folded length of water-cloth at the edge of the saddle, as they do for the stalker, and by morning it was gone.

Nothing more was said. They had noticed a patience that was not their own, and they had answered it with the manners they had, which is the deepest way they have of answering.

## The Old Woman

In the autumn of the color wave, the oldest of the palm-readers began to die.

She did not make a matter of it. She had been sitting for some years with her hand on a curve of stone that looked from a distance like a word, keeping it company, and she began to sit there longer, and then to stay through the fog without going out to it. Her kin stood near her, and someone stepped up beside her at the Breath each morning to lick the beads from her back and her long white hair. But she was very old, and her kin-line had dwindled to three, and on the last morning two of them were out at the far veils.

The power stepped up beside her.

Once it had understood the gesture, it had no doubt about this. It was the one thing the Keepers do that needs no permission and no explanation. It did not cure her, because she was not sick in any way that is a wrong. It did not tell her that it was there, or what it was. It stood at her shoulder while the fog came over the rim, and it did the small, exact thing the custom asks.

She turned her head after a while. She could not have seen anything. Her eyes had gone pale as the stone. She said, in the language that has no word for day, "You are not from the count."

Then she said, "Stay for the hour after," which is the way the Keepers say *don't go yet*.

It stayed for the hour after. When her kin came running in from the veils she was already lying with her palm flat on the ground, and they said what is said, and carried her at dusk to the saddle among the lantern-trees, where the graves are shallow so that whatever a person was can be gathered in. The power went with them and stood at the edge of the little group with its head a little bowed. When the frost coins formed in the cups that night, none of the living touched them. One dead woman had the right to take them, and did.

## The Tone

There was one more thing to understand, and it came from the high pillars on a cold night, soft as a breath across a bottle.

The Keepers stopped. They turned their backs, gently, and stood with their heads bowed.

The power had wondered about this sound since it first arrived, and it had been half-ready to answer it. It had thought the sound might be a loneliness, the one true ache in a place that otherwise kept a perfect peace. It had thought that here, at last, there might be something worth doing, a second voice, a hand held out across the dark.

It listened, and it understood that it had been wrong. The tone was not a call. It was not directed at anything. It was the plateau being alone the way a person is alone who is at ease, humming in an empty house, or standing in a field with no one watching and letting the face go slack. It was not loneliness at all. Being unheard was what the sound was made of, and to hear it, or to answer it, would have turned a private ease into a public one.

So the power did the only thing left, the thing the Keepers had been doing for longer than there was a count. It turned its back, gently, and stood with its attention bowed, and let the plateau think it was alone.

That was the kindest thing it did.

## What Was Left

When the power went, or when the attention drew back to its wider work, it left almost nothing, and that was the intent.

The comb-reeds it had combed, badly, grew out in a season and were indistinguishable from the rest. The path it had walked around the webs faded. The water-cloth was never returned, and no one expected it to be.

One thing remained. In the cliff face where the old woman had sat, a young palm-reader ran her ridged fingers over the banded stone the following year and found a line she had not been taught. It was finer than any drought-line and paler than any year of heavy Breath. It ran straight for a few hand-widths, as if something had passed close along the surface and not quite touched. She did not know how to read it. She spent a long time with her palm on it and did not ask anyone what it meant.

In the end she did what the old woman had done. She kept it company.

Somewhere under the floor, something very large and very slow turned toward the edge of the world, felt the cloud begin to climb, and told everything else an hour ahead, as it always had.
