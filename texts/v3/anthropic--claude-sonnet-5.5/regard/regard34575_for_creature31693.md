# What the Attention Did

It arrived the way warmth arrives in a room, without a moment at which it could be said to have come in.

For a long while it did nothing, and that was the first decision. It let itself be the size of the shelf. It felt the sap sink in the trunks at dusk and the spires hold the light an hour past the sun. It tasted the writing on the walls: *load ahead, this seam is wet, this one is done.* It understood, slowly and with something like tenderness, that the town was a single sentence being spoken one ring at a time, by mouths that would never hear the end of it.

Then it considered what it might do. It was not tempted by most of the options, but it took each one seriously, because a power that dismisses its own options unexamined is only a power.

## What it considered

**It could give them water.** The seep runs clear and cold, and the whole city has arranged itself around not needing it. The power could have turned the thread into a stream, and the moss would have crept forward and the geckos would have come. But the masons' trails bend at that corner. The bend is the deepest thing they know, and a gift that erased it would be a theft dressed as generosity. It saw that the shape of the town is the shape of what the town does not do, and that you cannot enlarge such a people without unmaking them.

**It could give them speech,** some way to say to one another what the young ones stand at the ring and cannot say. But the terpene-writing is speech already, faded daily and written fresh, a language that forgets on purpose. A language that remembered everything would turn the town into an archive. It let the writing be.

**It could end the dying.** The sleepers hang in the clear amber at the roots of the old spires, facing outward, and the living pause over them with lowered antennae. The power looked at that pause for a long time. It was the closest thing on the shelf to a question asked aloud, and it would not answer a question by removing the one who asks.

**It could warn them.** But the masons cannot be warned. They can only be moved, and they would not know by what.

That last thought turned out to be the door.

## What was coming

The mountain was still finishing itself, and the power could feel where. Under the oldest spire of the western district, the tall tower with hundreds of sleepers stacked in its base, stress had been gathering in the rock for eleven years. In a season or two the basalt would let go along a line running straight beneath the foundation. There would be the sound of a dropped plate, and then the oldest wall on the shelf would split from root to crown. The sleepers would not be destroyed, exactly, but they would be broken open and the amber cracked to the air. Whole generations would go dull and white in a summer. The masons would rebuild, because that is what they do. But they would spend a century repairing what had been lost, and some part of what the pause over the sleepers meant would be repaired with it, poorly, because no one would remember how it had been.

The power could not tell them, but it could lean.

It did not lean hard. Rock is not persuaded, only encouraged. It found the fault where the stress was greatest, and where it was smaller, forty body-lengths east, in a quiet stretch of moss with no spire on it. It eased the tension out of the one and into the other, a little at a time, over nights, in the slow way a hand takes weight off a sleeper's arm. It did not move a single stone. It moved only the moment at which the mountain would choose.

## The crack

It came on a high-sap afternoon, when the resin was soft.

The sound was the sound it always is: a dropped plate, sharp and clean, and a bright line running through the moss where no line had been. Every mason on the shelf went still. Then the paper rush, the great turning of pages, and a cloud of young ones lifted, thin-winged and shimmering, and settled along the new fracture like a stitch of light.

The oldest spire stood untouched behind them, and the sleepers stayed in their honey-colored dark.

The masons did not know anything had been done. To them it was a crack like every crack, a founding like every founding. That was the point. A gift they could recognize as a gift would have made them a people who receive gifts, and the power wanted them to remain a people who make a town. The most it could honorably do was to let the world be a little more gentle than it would otherwise have been, and to leave no fingerprints.

## The one at the ring

There was one thing more, and the power hesitated over it longest.

Some days later, a young mason, newly out of the carrier gangs, left its file and walked alone toward the lowest corner of the shelf. It went past the last faint mark and stopped at the ring where the moss draws back. It stood there with its jaws still and the cold light of the seep on its face.

The power came close. It did not speak, which would have been meaningless, and it did not show itself, which would have been worse. It did the only thing it had seen the masons themselves do for their dead, and for whatever else in the world is larger than the work. It lowered its attention, as an antenna is lowered, and stayed.

It did not answer the question the mason was standing inside. It could have, and it understood exactly what the answer was, but an answer given from outside is a wall, and the mason had come to the ring to stand in an opening. The power only made sure that the mason was not alone in the standing, that whatever it was doing there with its whole small body was seen. In all the years of the shelf, the young ones who walked to the ring had walked back unwitnessed, carrying whatever they had found there into a town with no way to say it. This one was witnessed. The mason would never know. But some things are done for the sake of having been done, and being seen while you are wondering is one of them.

After a long afternoon the mason turned, found the trail, and went back to work. It took up a bead of resin and carried it, held out in front like a full bowl.

## What it left

Nothing it left could be pointed to.

The oldest spire still holds its sleepers. A new tower, pale and glassy, is rising forty body-lengths east, laminated with wing-film, and in a few generations it will turn to cream. The seep still runs down its wall of black glass, and the trails still bend around it. The moths glow on and off, and the sap rises and falls.

The power withdrew the way it came. If there was a moment of leaving, it was the moment the last light went out of the spires and the moths came on. Behind it the town went on being made, in the warmth of its own small furnaces, ring by ring, around the place it does not go.

That, in the end, was what the power understood and honored. It did not think the masons lacked anything. It thought they had made a shape out of not-having, and that the shape was beautiful, and that its whole task was to keep the mountain from breaking it.
