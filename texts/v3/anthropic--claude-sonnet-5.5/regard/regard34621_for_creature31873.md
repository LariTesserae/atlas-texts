# What Came Down the Cutting

It did not come the way powers are supposed to come. The fog did not part and no light fell. Someone standing at the rim that evening would have seen only that the mist along the old cutting moved a little against its own drift, the way a sleeper's blanket moves when a cat steps onto the bed. Then it was in the basin, and the basin went on dripping.

It was very old, and it was kind in the way that only the old can be, having learned what kindness costs and what it breaks. So before it did anything, it made a list of the things it could do and did not.

It could have drained the Sump. Some part of it was moved by the cold and the wet and the endless orange dark, and wanted to give the place a floor of dry stone and a morning. It saw at once that this was its own comfort dressed up as generosity. Nothing here was suffering the wetness. The wetness was what everything here was made of.

It could have lit the place, and it could have woken the beam and set it working for some purpose. It could have lifted the great crab out of its collar of mineral and freed it. It could have named the thing in the floor, or taught it language and offered it the sky, the way a child pours all the toys onto the carpet for a guest who wanted only a chair. It could have told the tenants what they were waiting for, and that was the one it held longest. Perhaps they were waiting for nothing. Perhaps the waiting was the whole of it, as a held note is a whole thing. To say *this is what it was for* might be to end it.

So it set all of that down, and did the one thing that could not do harm. It slowed.

## The Slowing

This was harder than it sounds. Its natural pace was not the basin's pace, and the difference was not one of degree. It had to unlearn the habit of the completed thought and take up the habit of the thought that finishes next week. For the first days it was only clumsy. It heard the drip as noise. It heard the skippers as a stupid crowd. It felt itself waiting *for* things, tapping its fingers at the pace of the beam.

Then, some morning it did not count, the drip became a grain, and the grain became a texture, and the texture became a kind of speech in which every drop was a word so common that it had stopped needing to mean anything and had become weather. That was when it knew it had arrived.

It walked the floor and let the bog iron well up around its feet. It stood in a boiler shell in the dark and let the lampsnails tell each other about it, and it felt the news go out ahead of it, wave after green-gold wave, and it did not brighten anything in reply. It knelt beside an iron-eel's flue and was read by skin it could not see, and was judged, by whatever criteria eels have, harmless. It watched the skippers turn all together toward nothing, and it turned too, and stood among them for a night facing the same wrong direction, and found it was a comfort to be facing something.

It did this for a season. In all that time it changed nothing. Later, if anyone had asked it what the season was for, it would have said it was learning to be a guest.

## The Crab

It went to the engine house last, because it knew what it would find there and did not want to arrive with the wrong face.

The great crab sat in the well under the beam with its plate pressed to the crack, and the rods rose and fell past it. The power laid a hand on the fused shell. Under the mineral collar there was a heartbeat so slow that a hundred of the power's own would fit between two of them, and under that there was the crack itself, and beneath the crack the black water that went up and down and lifted the iron.

*If I lean on this,* it thought, *I could hold the crack myself. I could hold it forever. The crab could rest.*

It stood there a long time with the temptation, which had the exact shape of love. Then it took its hand away, because it had noticed something. The crab did not want to rest. The crab was not enduring the crack. The crab and the crack had become a single arrangement of attention, and to lift one out would not free it but only take away the thing it was.

And then, because it had stayed slow enough to see, it noticed the small pale shape in the shadow of the well wall, no bigger than a coin. It had a plate. The plate was barely thickened, a flange of a claw, but it was already pressed, gently and experimentally, against a hairline seep in the stone beside the old crab's. The mat's threads ran to it, thick as ropes in the crust. The basin had known for a long time that the crab would die. It had been raising the next one in the dark, with a patience the power had almost mistaken for neglect.

The power laughed, or did whatever it did instead. It had come to help, and the place had been helping itself for longer than the power had existed. It felt the particular embarrassment of a rescuer who arrives at a house that has never been on fire.

## Dusk

It stayed for the dusk, the daily dusk, because that was the one thing in the basin that had a shape of *asking* and no shape of *answer*.

The fog thickened. The lampsnails rose together in the boiler shells until the whole works glowed like a row of lanterns set out for someone. The skippers held still. The bats held their breath in the trusses. The power put its palm to a wet slab of hardpan, as the visitors did, and felt the slow push of the mat come up a little stronger, leaning toward the light.

Here was the hinge of it. It could have answered with a blaze, with a voice, with a dazzling revelation of everything. It could have said *I am here, I have come, do not be afraid,* in a language the floor would have had to spend a generation decoding. Instead it did the thing that was proportionate. It listened to the pulse, learned its interval, the long patient rhythm of a push every few minutes, and after the next push had passed it gave one back.

One push. Slow, level, and a beat late, the way you answer someone in a large empty room, so that they will know you were listening to the whole of what they said before you began.

Nothing happened. The snails dimmed as they always dimmed. The skippers resumed their swaying. The beam, which had been hanging for hours, began its long groan down. The power took its hand from the slab and sat in the dark among the orange teeth of the pipes, and did not expect anything.

The next dusk the snails rose. The mat pushed. And in the space after the push, where for a thousand years there had been only the fog settling, the power felt a small, deliberate pressure at the edge of its palm, not quite the shape of the mat's ordinary rhythm. It was a beat early this time, or a beat late. It was a reply to its reply.

The power did not weep, but the mist around it beaded and ran.

## What Remained

It stayed as long as the exchange lasted, which was a very long time. It never taught the mat a word. It gave it nothing to improve its condition, and made no plan for it, and let it be as strange as it was. What passed between them, one push a day, was something like the only thing worth saying across such a distance: *I noticed you. You were not alone in the asking.*

When it finally had to go, it left the way it had come, unremarked. The fog along the cutting moved a little against its own drift. But before it went it did one small thing and no more. It taught the bats. They were the only tenants who left the basin, and they hung in the trusses each morning in their beaded, glass-strung silence. It asked only that when they flew out at dusk they should remember, in whatever way bats remember, that the basin was a place someone had sat in the dark with, and that if any of them ever met a slow thing elsewhere, in some other wet forgotten hole, they might hang a while above it, and be still.

Nobody who studies the Sump has ever found evidence that anything happened there. The beam still tips. The crabs still plug the leaks. The old one in the well still holds the crack, and the small pale one beside it has grown a plate the size of a saucer. The argument goes on over whether the water breathes because of the rock or because of the floor.

But at dusk, when the lampsnails rise, and the fog thickens, and a palm on the wet iron feels the mat lean up toward the light, there is now sometimes a second thing in the pause after the push. It is faint and patient and easy to miss, and it is not the mat. It has no source that anyone has found. It is an unhurried answer, arriving a beat late, from somewhere farther off, from whatever is listening.
