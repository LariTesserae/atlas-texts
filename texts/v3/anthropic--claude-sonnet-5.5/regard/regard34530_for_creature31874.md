# What Came to Listen

## Arrival

It did not arrive. Nothing in the basin registered a change, and this was the first sign of what it was. The bird above the snake did not wake. The ants kept both lanes moving on the liana. It came the way humidity comes, as something already present that you only notice by its absence.

It had been looking at many places, and most of them wanted something. This one did not seem to. It settled into the dark the way a hand settles into a glove of the same temperature, and for a long while it did only what the people of the basin do. It attended.

## The Considering

Power that is wise begins by listing what it could do. Then it sets each item down, one at a time, and watches what the hands feel like empty.

*It could cool the heat.* But the heat is the tempo of the place. The slow eyes, the thumb-shifts, the sloth's damp fur, and the debt that haste incurs all depend on it. Take the heat away and you don't have this forest with better weather. You have another forest.

*It could bring medicine.* This one it held longest, because these people die of small things: a fever out of the oxbow water, a bad fall from a buttress, a cut that goes green. Somewhere in the past there was a child who died of something that could have been undone by a gesture. It could feel the shape of that grief in the cords, in the count of knots. It did not pretend the refusal was clean. A people who die of nothing are not these people, and the knock on the boiler is how they know what they are. But this reasoning is easy to make from outside. It could not entirely trust it, and it left the question open rather than closing it in its own favor.

*It could wall off the coast.* But a wall is a decision made on behalf of everyone who ever lives on either side of it, and it had no standing to make that decision.

*It could show itself, and be worshipped or feared.* Either would end the attention it had come to admire, turning a people who look at the forest into a people who look at it.

Setting the list down, it found a rule underneath: *Do nothing that would make the basin need me.*

A second rule followed, which was harder: *But do not leave without asking.*

## The Question

The oldest of them sat against the boiler, as she did most nights, with her back to the curve of rusted iron. The power came to her the only way it could, through the metal. The boiler's faint answering note, which no one else could hear, shifted by a hair. She did not turn her head.

After a time she lifted one knuckle and tapped the iron. It was not the single knock for the dead. It was a small uneven pattern she had probably never used, a question with no name yet, made on the spot.

It answered by letting the note bend toward her.

There were no words. What passed between them was closer to weather being described to a person who already feels the pressure dropping. It told her plainly what it had seen, because honesty was the only thing it was sure it could give without taking. The Other Shore was growing. Some decades on, perhaps sooner, people with instruments would come for the basin. On their maps it was an absence, and an absence is a kind of invitation. The rails would be found. The river would be measured. There might be roads.

She was quiet for the length of a long drip, then another.

Then she pressed her palm flat to the iron, and what came back was dry and unbothered. The river had done this to them before, she said. A loop swings, and the place where you stood is water. You move. You do not stop being the ones who noticed.

*You have not asked me for anything,* it said.

*You have not said what you came for,* she said.

*To ask if there is something you want.*

She thought about it for a very long time. The power learned then what it had come to learn, though it had not known it was a lesson: how to wait without waiting for something. It let the time pass through it like river through a cut bank. Overhead a branch settled with a small wet creak.

## What She Asked

She did not ask for the fever to be lifted. She did not ask for the coast to be turned aside, or for a longer life for herself, or for the young ones to stop walking the rails.

She asked: *When I die, who knocks?*

The oldest one knocks for everyone. It is the office, and it has always been so. But there will be an oldest after her, and she was not sure that one had the ear for the note. And the office has a strange hole in it, a small one, like the one under a lamp: no one has ever been sure who knocks for the one who knocks.

*I can,* it said.

*No,* she said, gently, the way one refuses food from someone who has little. *It has to be one of us. Otherwise it is only sound.*

It understood. The knock is not an announcement. It is the people paying attention to their own loss, and a gift would ruin it. So she asked for something else, after another long silence, and this was the request it had not seen coming.

*The cords rot,* she said. *The crowns fall. In a hundred years, or thirty, or in the year the loop swings, the count will be gone. Not the dying. The dying is fine. The counting. Which giant stood dead for twenty years. Which oxbow filled. The names of the ones we knocked for. If there is a place where it stays, without anyone having to go there and without it changing what we do, I would like it kept there.*

*Not to be read,* she added. *Only to have been.*

## What It Did

It did this, and only this.

It took the count. Not the knots themselves, which it left hanging in the crowns among the drying hammocks, indistinguishable from liana, but the whole slow accounting behind them. It took every death and every fall, every year of standing and dying. Each sapling contest still in its first century became a thing held somewhere that does not rot, in a place none of the living will visit, and where the only visitor is the fact that it happened.

It did not tell the young ones. It did not carve the knowledge into the rails or leave a sign at the place where the iron sinks into root. It did not soften the fever or slow the loop of the river. When the survey comes, it will not have arranged for a clerk to lose a coordinate, because arranging that would have been a small tyranny of its own, and the people of the coast have as much right to their own fates as the people of the basin. If the coast comes, the basin will meet it with whatever the basin is by then. The power will grieve it, if grief is the word, and it will hold that grief with the rest.

It did leave one thing that was not a gift and that she did not ask for. For the length of one breath, as it withdrew, it let the boiler's note carry a half-beat longer than usual. Nothing came of this. The old woman moved a hand's width along the iron, the way you move over for someone who has sat down beside you.

## Toward Morning

It had come to see whether a place needed keeping, and it found that the place was already kept. It was kept by attention, the slow low kind that has never needed to be startled, the same thing the power itself is made of. It had expected to be the guardian of something. It left as a guest who had been shown how to sit.

The heat did not leave. The drip resumed, ticking on leaf and mud and the still surface of pools. Far to the east the orange stain lay against the cloud like a coal that would not go out, and no one in the basin looked at it, because they knew it was there.

A branch gave way somewhere with a long unhurried crack and a soft crash. Against the boiler, a figure you might have taken for root shifted by the width of a thumb, and settled, and kept listening.
