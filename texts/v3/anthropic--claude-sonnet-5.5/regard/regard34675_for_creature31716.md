# What Attended the Bellows Plateau

It did not arrive. Arriving implies a before and an after, and the first thing the power understood about the plateau was that it was not to be interrupted.

The gleaners would later say it began as a change in the bedrock pulse, one beat in a long series landing slightly softer than it should have, as if something had set its weight down carefully. The beasts did not turn. The hammerjaws did not miss a stroke. The power had come to a place where everything was already occupied, and it was glad of that. It had feared, as it always did, that it would find something in need.

## What it did not do

It did not bring water. It considered this longest, because the gleaners' whole lives were bent around that lack: the glass flasks, the month down and the month back, the old ones who died on the road with the weight of other people's thirst on their backs. It could have opened a spring in an afternoon. But it looked at what a spring would do. The green would come first at the edge of the wet, then the things that eat green, then the things that eat those. The rustlings would have no work, and the pavement would go under something soft. The land was finished in its own way, and finished did not mean broken. A wise power learns to tell the two apart, and it is the hardest thing to learn.

It did not mend the slips. They were wounds only if you insisted the plateau owed the world consistency. They were places where long labor had worn through, as a step is worn by feet. The power had no wish to repair a step by unwalking the walkers.

It did not wake the sleepers, or tell the beasts anything, or teach the gleaners a better way to smelt, since none of them smelted. It did not try to learn what the beasts were making. When it understood that it was tempted to, it stopped and waited until the temptation went by. Wanting to know is not a right of ownership.

## What it did

It walked. It took the smallest shape it could manage, lean and pale-wrapped like the tally-walkers, and wore it badly. It kept stopping to look at things. The gleaners noticed a stranger whose flask never lightened, who walked the circuits at no one's pace, who stood in the wrong places with its head tilted. They did what people do with a strange fact they cannot use, and let it be.

It listened first, for a full year of the plateau's slow time, without a single thought that was not about sound. It learned the round bronze, the cracked bell, the kettle-whistle that never boils over. It learned the beast that went sharp on sulfur for six years and then mellowed, and it did not mistake the mellowing for age, because it had learned that a beast's voice is its long record of what it has eaten. It learned the weaver harps and the one string in each that was always slightly wrong, tuned wrong on purpose, so the whole field would keep something to reach for. It learned the ticking of a cooling pan at night, and the ticking that a sleeper makes, which is like a clock with a very slow hand.

Then it did the one thing it was asked to do.

## The old tally-walker

She was among the oldest of them, a woman whose skin had gone to the color of the pavement, who had been walking one circuit for sixty years. She came to the stranger one evening at a cooling flank, sat down without preamble, and said, in the flat manner of someone who has run out of patience for asking gently: *There is a mesa on the eastern pan. My mother called it a sleeper. Her mother called it dead. I have put my ear to it every year of my life and I cannot tell. I would like to know before I go.*

That was all she wanted. She could have asked for anything.

The power went out to the eastern pan and stood against the mesa for nine days. It did not use any faculty the gleaners lacked. It only had more patience, and it could hold its attention on one slow thing without wanting to be elsewhere. On the ninth night, in the deepest of the cold, it heard what she had never had the hours to hear: a tick, and then, eleven breaths later by its own count, another tick, and then a long silence, and then a tick. A dead thing goes quiet. A sleeper is never quiet, only slow.

It went back and told her plainly. *It is asleep. It will wake, but not in your lifetime, and not in the next. I heard it turn over once in its sleep.*

She was silent for a long while. Then she said, "Turn over how?" and the power described the sound, and she closed her eyes and listened to its description as if it were music.

Later, when she was dying, she asked to be carried to the eastern pan, and she died with her ear against the warm rock, hearing whatever a person hears when she has been told the truth about the thing she loved.

## The silent walker

The power did go into the slip. It knew that what lived there was not hostile, and it was not afraid, but it was curious in the way a guest is curious about the one closed room in a house.

At the worst place, where the herd was doubled, it stood still and let the two beasts pass. The near one breathed and gonged and the ground shook. The far one, a little too silent, walked in perfect step, raising no dust.

The power did not challenge it. It began to walk beside it. It matched its pace to the silent thing's, and after a time, without its deciding, the silent thing matched its pace back. Then they were walking in step, and the power could not have said which was leading.

It walked a day and a night, and when it stopped it did not fully understand what it had walked with. But it had a suspicion, which it kept, because suspicions are cheap and it distrusted its own. The slips were places where the plateau had worn through, yes. But the wear was not only from labor. It was also from being watched. For generations the gleaners had cairned and mapped and sounded the slips, and the plateau, which had never been looked at before people came, had begun in the places most watched to look back. The silent thing was not a memory of the herd or its future. It was the plateau's attention, learning to walk beside itself.

This was neither good nor bad. It was only a new thing in the world, and not the power's to end. It did, before it left, walk once around the slip in the way a person walks around a sleeping animal, to signal that it meant no harm. It thought the silent walker understood.

## The unhatched

There was one thing more, and it nearly broke the power's resolve.

Deep in another slip, a lump of white metal sat on the pavement, too large to be an ingot. It glowed and did not cool. Around it a ring of hammerjaws struck the ground in a slow, patient rhythm, and beneath their rhythm the power could hear another, from inside the metal, faint and steady, the beat of something that would stand up in a few decades and be a hillside.

The power felt the urge to help. It knew a dozen ways to steady that heat, to guard the lines that would become plates, to keep out whatever might crack it wrong. It was an old urge, and it had caused more harm across the universe than most forms of malice.

It sat down instead, at some distance, and did nothing. It did not even tap the ground in time with the hammerjaws, though the rhythm pulled at it, because any addition of its own to that rhythm would have been a fingerprint. It let the thing be what it was making of itself, and it kept the vigil in the only way that could not interfere, by paying attention and asking nothing in return.

It stayed until the first hairline crack ran across the surface. Then it rose, because it had promised itself it would not stay to see the standing-up. That was not its to witness.

## Leaving

It went the way it came, without departure. A gleaner walking the circuit some years later noticed that one of the older cairns, on a stretch of pan where nothing was marked, held a stone that was not from that place. Under it, scratched into the dust of the pavement in a hand no one recognized, was a single line of tally-marks, nine of them, and then a tenth, drawn slowly, as though the one who made it was reluctant to finish.

She did not know what it meant. She added it to her map anyway, with a note: *here an echo returns early.* It was true. If you shouted at that cairn, the answer came back a breath before you finished. The gleaners have kept it since, one small new slip among the old ones, and children are taken there to hear it.

And in the choir of the plateau, among the bronze and the cracked bell and the kettle-whistle, some of the gleaners say there is one more voice, very small, at the far end of the harp fields, an even, patient tone that arrives with the wind and leaves with it. It is not one of the beasts. It has no vent. The gleaners, who name everything by its sound, have not named it. They say a name would be a kind of claim, and that whatever sang it wanted only to be one more thing making a noise in a place that had room for it.
