# What Came to the Bowl

The power did not descend. Descent implies a height, and the bowl was low, and the power had learned that most harm done in the name of kindness begins with arriving from above.

It came the way the haze comes, by being there already, and it looked for a long while before it moved at all.

## What it considered, and set down

It saw first that nothing was broken. This is rarer than it sounds. It had looked on many places where help was a matter of urgency, and it had learned to distrust the feeling that help is always wanted. So it made a short list of the things it could do, and put each one down in turn.

It could give them rain. But the pool has never risen past its lip, and the exact trade of seep and air is the closest thing the bowl has to a heartbeat. Rain would be a flood in a place that has never known one.

It could give them children. The number of pairs has held at a dozen since before the wall was laid, and when a pair leans into the stone, another is found sitting in the same alcove. The power looked closely at this and found it was not a gap in the world's kindness. The bowl was already renewing itself in a way no one had to explain. To fix it would be to say that the way they arrive is not good enough.

It could level the lintel. That was the easiest to reject. The tilt was set on purpose, by someone who is now a sunken stone.

It could tell them what they were. But they did not ask, and a name given to someone who did not ask for one is a small weight that never stops being carried.

It could lead them out. This tempted it longest. A dozen pairs in a bowl of silt, in permanent late afternoon, touching drip cups and never seeing beyond the rim. It is very easy to look at that and feel pity. The power felt it briefly and examined it. It found that the pity was its own, that it came from imagining itself in the alcove without the alcove's meaning, and that nobody in the bowl had said they were lonely. A walker is only the sitter's other half, out in the world. They had never been alone. It was the power, not they, who did not know what to do with a life like that.

Then it saw the one true thing that could be tended.

## The one true thing

They say the Halves are the last things in the place that could have moved and chose not to. The power saw the flaw in the phrase, which was small and tender. A choice needs something to be chosen against. No Half had ever gone up over the rim and come back. The staying was faithful, but it had never been tested by an open door. Every Half was there because the bowl was all there was, and the power suspected that some part of the stillness, perhaps only a thin one, would be truer if it were freely held.

It did not want to break the stillness by testing it. It wanted only to make the rim look like what it was, a low lip of pale rock, easy to cross, with the same land beyond it that the bowl was folded out of.

## What it did

It settled at the edge of a large alcove, in the shape of a shape, and sat. It was a sitter without a walker, which the Halves noticed at once and did not remark on. The nearest sitter turned only its eyes.

After a long time a walker crossed the silt and held out a cupped palm with a finger-depth of clear water in it. The power drank half and gave the rest back. That was the whole of its first act, and it was also the truest one. It had come to a place built on the rule of halves, and it kept the rule.

The walker looked at the palm, still half full, and then at the power, and something passed across the wide dark eyes that had almost forgotten how to blink. It was not gratitude. The Halves have no word for that. It was closer to *enough*, said with warmth.

The power stayed as long as the light stayed the same, which is to say it stayed a very long time or no time. It learned the drip of the *plum* cup by heart. It was taught, by a walker's patient hand over its own, to make a single scratch above the largest alcove in raking light. It took the whole of an afternoon. The mineral bloom began sealing the stroke before the power had lifted its hand. That was the only mark it left, and it is now part of a group of three, indistinguishable from the rest.

It stood once under the lopsided lintel and did not go in, and afterward it went and sat. It understood that this, too, was complete.

## The rain

When it rose to leave, it went up the eastern ledge past the crooked tree, whose leaves taste of coins and rain, and over the pale rim, and it stood among the blond tufts of grass with the low swells of the land rolling away in every direction like the backs of sleeping things.

There it let it rain.

It rained only beyond the rim. It fell in a soft grey curtain on the tufts and on the buff swells, ran off the outer lip, and did not come down into the bowl. Nothing in the bowl was flooded. The pool kept its ring. The drips kept their intervals. Only the smell came over the edge, the wet-chalk, cold-coin, faintly sweet smell that the Halves had known all their lives without knowing what it was the smell of.

The sitters looked up. That was all, at first.

Then a walker who had been touching a drip cup stood, crossed the silt, climbed the eastern ledge past the tree and over the lip, which no Half had done in living memory, and stood at the edge of the rain. It held out one cupped palm and waited. It caught a finger-depth of water that had never touched stone, and stood a while looking at the low, soft, endless land, which was quite unlike the walls and quite the same.

Then it turned and went back down, carrying the water level and unspilled, and gave half to the sitter. The two of them tasted it, and it tasted like the leaves of the crooked tree. For the first time they knew what the tree had been telling them.

## What the power left alone

It did not stay to see what the walker did next. It did not stay to see whether the Halves spoke of the rim again, or whether one of them rose some other day and went up, or whether none ever did. Whether they remained was no longer its business. If they stayed, they now stayed knowing the door was a low lip of rock and the world beyond was gentle. If some day a pair chose otherwise, that would be the bowl's own news, and the bowl would keep it in its own way.

The pool stood at its lip and the moss stopped where the damp stopped. The lintel was as out of level as it had been. The number of pairs was a dozen.

The only change was a single new stroke in the scratches, and a walker's cool palm, still faintly wet, laid against the stone where the sitter's shoulder had begun to go to moss.

The power, which was benevolent and wise and knew when its work was done, went out over the swells in the last of the rain. It looked back once from the rim, at the pale gold light, and could not tell the halves from one another, or either from the stone. That was how it knew it had left everything as it should be.
