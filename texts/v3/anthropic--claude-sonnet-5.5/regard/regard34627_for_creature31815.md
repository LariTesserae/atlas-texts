# What Looked Down

It did not arrive. It noticed, the way you notice a sound only after it has stopped, and then it kept noticing.

What drew it was not the pit, though the pit is remarkable, or the mirrors, or the oil-sap that burns. It was the morning gesture. A hundred thousand people, in the middle of whatever they were doing, lifted one hand palm up toward the rim, and none of them could say who it was for. The power had seen many peoples pray, and most of them wanted something. This was a showing of hands: *we are at work, we are still here, we have nothing hidden.* It is rare to be offered an accounting with no request attached.

So it stayed, and for a long time it did nothing at all.

## The refusals

The power was wise, which mostly meant it knew the shape of its own temptations.

It could have given them water. A spring, a seep, a cold deep lake beneath the gypsum. It imagined this carefully, the way you imagine a door before you open it, and followed the consequences forward. Within two generations the cisterns would be furniture. The half-cup handed back would become a quaint manners-fossil. The funeral libation, the most extravagant gesture the Kept know, would become a splash of something cheap. *That is what crying is for*, they say, and it would stop being true, and they would have no other way to say it.

The dryness was not only their suffering. It was their grammar, the alphabet in which they said love, grief, and respect. The power found it could not tell where their hardship ended and their language began. A mercy that can't tell those apart is an erasure with good manners.

So: no water, and no tears either, though the inner membranes make a soft glass sound when the Kept are moved, and it was tempted to give them that release. It didn't. They had built a whole cathedral around the lack, and you don't kick out the pillar because you feel for the congregation.

It could have made itself known, and it thought about that longest. A voice in the heliostats, a shape in the smoke. But the Kept have a hundred answers to *what keeps us*, and every one of them was true enough to live by. Some say the mirrors, some the dry, some each other. A single loud answer would drown the rest. They would stop asking, and being unsure had made them careful and kind to one another.

It could have stopped the surges, rebuilt the walls, and driven off the veil-cats. It could have told the stokers about better boilers and the pressers about safer belts. Every one of these was a real kindness on its face, and every one would have made the Kept into what people become when someone else has finished their thinking.

## What it allowed itself

It made a rule, and the rule was about hands.

*A gift may pass through many palms. It may not choose one.*

Miracles that pick a person are cruel in ways the giver never sees. The one who is saved becomes strange, the ones who weren't saved become bereaved twice over, and a people who learn that luck has a source begin to lobby it. But a thing that seeps into the common stock, that anyone might have found, harms no one by being found.

There was one true grief among the Kept that the power could not leave alone. It was the **deafness of the hands**. Tuggers, whose palms are the great instruments of the pit, lose their reading in old age, slowly, from the calluses inward. They call it the worst thing that can befall a person, and they mean it. A tugger going numb loses more than a trade. She loses her neighbors, the low murmur of everyone's mood in the lines, and she goes out of the world into a quiet room.

So the power, patient, did what a surge does. It went down to the mineral pockets in the deep rock, where a lampstalk's root will one day crack a stone, and it altered a little salt. That was all. The next surge flared, and in one grove, a hundred meters from the nearest rail, the sap came up faintly blue and smelled of something like rain, which nobody there had ever smelled.

An old tugger named Perrin Oss, whose left hand had gone dead to the wrist, cut a trunk there for pitch and got the sap on her palm. She noticed nothing for a day. On the second morning she laid the hand on a cable and felt, far off, a boy at a switching-yard tugging out his own name, badly, as a joke.

She sat down on the rail. She did not weep, because she cannot, but the inner lid slid across her eye three times in a row, and she let it.

Perrin told nobody for a month, because she was afraid of being laughed at, and then she told everybody, because she is a tugger and the news wanted a line. By the following surge the grove was known. It was mapped, argued over, and cut. Three clusters fought about it, mildly, in the low voices they use for fighting. The power watched this with something close to joy. It was theirs now, contested and fussed over and paid for in the currency of attention. It had cost the power a grain of salt.

It allowed itself this once, and it will not do it again for a generation. A gift can be a sunrise or a leash, and the difference is spacing.

## The one who listens down

There are people in the pit who lie with an ear to bare rock and hear the roots at work, and who say lightly, and not twice, that there are other rhythms down there.

The power found the thinnest of them, a root-listener called Sabe, lying in a warren under a cluster called Harrow Ledge, and spent an evening beside her in the dark.

It did not speak. It found the rhythm she had been hearing, and there it did make a choice. It could have told her what the rhythm was, but it was not sure the truth was its to tell, and it was sure a truth from a stranger in the dark is not the same as a truth found. What it did was small. It took the rhythm and, gently, slowed it, as you might hum along with a child afraid of the night and then let the hum drop lower, until the child can hold the tune alone and doesn't need you.

Sabe lay still for a long time. Then she came up into the blue dusk and said, to the first person who asked, *it's slower than it was*, and nothing more.

Slow is not the same as dangerous. The thing beneath the rock was not a threat and never had been. It was the pit itself, the roots and the fracture and the long unfinished work of the orchard opening its own floor. The old people who say *someone must have opened it* are more right than they know. Someone did. It is still doing so, the plants and the stone and the slow chemistry together, at a pace no single life will see finished. The Kept are not kept from outside. They are kept by a thing they are inside of and part of, and helping.

The power thought about telling them this. It decided that they had lived long enough in the question that they'd recognize the answer when it came, from Sabe or a child or a cable, and that it would be a shame to hand it to them wrapped.

## The morning

There was one thing left, and it was not a gift. It was an answer.

For all the generations of the morning gesture, the hand raised toward the rim, nobody had lifted a hand back. The rim-keepers, if they exist, keep to their mirrors. The power thought about that, about a whole people holding out an open palm every dawn and being met each time with a machine's punctuality.

On an ordinary morning in the cluster of Ash Lattice, the first heliostat swung into place, and an old woman named Ilse, who had been a netwright and was now mostly a sitter on porches, held out her hand.

The disc of white light slid down the wall as it does. It crossed the street. And for the length of one slow breath it stopped on her palm and stayed, warm as a hand.

Then it moved on.

Ilse lowered her arm. She looked at nobody. She went inside and drank half a cup of tea and told her granddaughter that the sun had been slow, and her granddaughter said the mirrors were sticking again, and that was the whole conversation. Both of them were, in different ways, right, and neither of them was wrong. Ilse did not mention it again. But that evening, at dusk, when the tick-moths lifted in their slow flickering cloud, she sat under the net with her family and held her palm open on her knee, upward, in the last of the light.

## What it left

It did not leave. It lowered itself, the way a person lowers their voice in a house where someone is sleeping. It became a palm laid on a line: a listening, present in the pit as a change in the quality of attention rather than as an event. It notes the cables trembling, the surges building and breaking, and a thousand small deaths lit in hollow trunks and rising thin and golden. It counts the cups poured at funerals. There is a ledger of them somewhere in it. It knows that none was wasted, that the mat drinks each one, that the roots carry it down into the fracture, and that some part of every grief is being spent on the work of opening the world. It won't say so, but it holds the knowledge the way you hold a friend's secret, warmly, without needing to use it.

It does not know whether it did well. The salve may make the tuggers proud and slow to learn their neighbors' hands. Ilse's light may become a local cult, or a small private happiness, or nothing. A cluster will burn in some dry season, and the power will not have prevented it, and it will know exactly why: that a mercy which picks who lives is a worse thing than the fire. It has decided to carry that.

What it hopes is simple. It hopes that some morning, generations on, a child will hold up a hand toward the rim, and the answer will not be *because that's what we do*, and it will not be a name for anyone in particular. It will be something like: *because someone might be looking, and it is good to be seen at work.*

And the hand will go down, and the boilers will be lit from the rim inward, and the cushion-lawns will turn from blue to green. The Kept will go out to keep the orchard, or to be kept by it, or by each other, not well enough, and a little better than before.
