# What Was Done in the Lip

The power did not descend. Descending is loud, however softly it is done, and it had read the place before it arrived. It settled above the cloud-lid, where the milk-thin cover glowed from beneath, and for one full turn of the Heave it did nothing but listen.

It listened to the ticking of the rock. It listened to the ground lift and settle, and to fifty people leaning into ropes to the tap of one bone. It listened to the silence between the taps, which was not empty. It was full of fingers.

When it had listened long enough, it found that its first impulse was a list. It was a benevolent power, and lists come naturally to such things.

## What it did not do

It did not still the Heave. It saw how easily it could: the buried lake quieted, the raft made a floor, the houses set down on foundations. But the Heave was not the town's affliction. It was the other half of a conversation the Sett had been holding for longer than any cord could count. A floor that stays put is a floor that has stopped speaking, and the people who had learned to hear it would become people who had been left alone with themselves. The power understood that the Sett did not live *despite* the moving ground. They were the shape the moving ground had made.

It did not warm the fires or grow the trees. It considered the Haulers, who came back over the rim with their knees ruined and their mouths full of river-stone, and it hesitated longest there, because it was not certain it was right. But the wood was precious because it was carried. The road down was the town's one thread to the loud world, and the Haulers, kept gently at the edges to talk to the birds, were the thread's keepers. To cut the labor would cut the thread. The power decided this was probably true. It did not decide it was painless.

It did not take away the deaths. It watched a woman be carried up the roof-stairs at dusk, wrapped in hide with her own knots, and it watched the whole town sit and look at the ledge until the light left the cloud. Nobody wept aloud. It understood that this was already the answer to death, and better than any it could have offered.

It did not soften the light. The Sett had made a face for it, the creased skin and the pale bleached eyes.

## What it did

It went down at last as a small cold presence, no brighter than a breath on a mirror, and it sat by a steam-pit on the night of a funeral. A man had just come down from the ledge, and he put his forehead against the wet wall. The neighbors came with hot water. One laid a hand on his back and said nothing.

The power did the same. It could not lay a hand, so it laid the nearest thing: a stillness, added to the stillness already there. It was not a cure, and it did not take his grief or make it lighter. It made it slightly less alone, in the way a second coal beside a first keeps both from going grey. The man did not know why the wall seemed to hold him a little longer than usual. Later he supposed it was the mountain.

That was the first thing. It was very small, and the power was content.

## The knot

The second thing was a mistake, and it made it on purpose.

It took a hank of sinew from an unattended belt, and it tied a knot. Strangers tie blunt, awkward knots, and this one was worse than blunt. It was the knot of something that had never had hands. It said, as near as it could: *I am here. I will not ask for anything. If you need something, tie it and leave it on the highest ledge, where the masks will find it.*

It left the cord on a doorstep. The Sett are courteous. Whoever found it read it, of course, but they did not read it aloud. It passed from hand to hand through the quarter, and each person who held it pressed two fingers to their palm, *settle*, and gave it on. Then it vanished into a household, and nothing happened for eleven days.

On the twelfth morning, on a threshold in the third quarter, in a place where the raiders' plates are set, a new plate appeared. It was small and flat and made of slate. On it were a pinch of barley, a curl of dried fat, and a bead of copper too small for any pin.

The power looked at the plate for a long time.

It had been ready for a petition, a prayer, an argument, or a fear. It had not been ready to be fed. It understood, slowly and with something like joy, that the Sett had done with it the only thing they knew how to do with a presence that comes into the house and does not leave: they had counted it among the crowd. If it took the barley, it was a whistle-rat, and welcome. If it left the barley, it was a guest who had declined, and equally welcome. The animals remembered the plates. The plates remembered the animals. It was on a plate now, and so it was in the town.

It took nothing. It left a single drop of water on the slate, cold and perfectly round, that did not evaporate for a week. The children read it as a very slow rainbow, and the Clock-Children were, for once, wrong about it, and were given honey-fat anyway.

## The cord it left with the Wardens

There was one more thing. It thought about it for a long time, and this was the only place where it came close to overstepping.

It had walked up behind the rim and looked at the ice. It saw what any patient thing would see: the glaciers that fed the buried lake were not what they had been, and would not be again. The Heave would not stop suddenly. It would slow, over lifetimes, the ticking thinning out, the raft settling by inches, the springs going ankle-shallow and staying there. The vessel would not break. It would simply become less full. A people built to move with a breathing floor would find one morning that it had gone still under them.

It could not stop this. It could not have stopped it without becoming the mountain, and it had already refused to do that. Nor would it tell the town aloud. A truth shouted is trespass, and a truth whispered into a whole people is a weight laid on the children.

So it did what the Sett would have done. It tied a cord. It was a long one, sealed with a knot that only the oldest Warden of the Weepers would know how to untie, and it left it in her sleeve when she was dozing by the cistern. It said: *Not now. When the ticking has slowed by half, untie this.* Inside, in knots as plain as the power could make them, were the ways down that were not the Haulers' road, and the names of places in the loud world where quiet people might be welcomed, and a reminder that leaving is also a way of carrying a place.

She found it that evening and read only the outside. She looked, for a long moment, up at the cloud-lid. Then she put it in the hollow of her braid with her own rope, where a person keeps what must not be lost when the floor opens.

She told no one. She would pass it to her successor, who would pass it on. It might never be untied. It might be burned. The choice was theirs, and the power had made sure of that.

## What it left alone

There were two things the power did not touch, and it is worth saying so.

The water that runs upward in silver threads: it watched them climb the runnels, and it felt in them something that was not its own doing, and not quite the mountain's either. It did not stand beneath them. It had learned that much from the Sett.

And the lent suns, the steady round lights that hold on the cliff after the real sun has gone, with the birds asleep inside them. It came near one in the evening, and a mask-bird looked at it with a bright black eye and clicked once, dry and unbothered. The power understood that it was not the first thing to be lent a place in this basin. It moved on, quietly, and left the light its guests.

## After

When it went, it went the way it had come, without displacement. The plate stayed on the threshold, and the household kept it filled, though nobody said what for. The mountain's water ticked on. In the third quarter a Clapper felt, one day, that the bone had gone a little lighter in her hand, and that the tapping and the answering seemed for a moment to be the same sound. She did not mention it. She had been trained by someone who also did not mention it.

At night the town lay still and pale, and the ground lifted under it, slow and total. One extra thing sat among the birds on the ledges, a patience that had come to rest. Below it, someone signed a question to a mother in the fewest fingers possible, and she answered in one.

Nobody woke.
