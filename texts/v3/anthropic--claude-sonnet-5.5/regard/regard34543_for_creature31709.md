# What Came to Look

It arrived the way the afternoon cloud arrives, gradually, and then all at once as a fact about the sky.

It had no shape yet. It had attention, and attention has weight. The grazers felt it first and lifted their heads in unison, as they do, and held them up a little longer than listening required. Then they went back to the moss. The frogs forty meters up in their cups of rainwater did not notice anything. The mat gave its slow groan and settled.

The power looked for a long time. Wisdom, in its case, mostly took the form of not moving.

## What It Considered

It could have made the place safer. But the place was not unsafe. It was *patient*, which is different, and much of what a rescuer would call danger was the plateau doing its work: the mat lifting and dropping, the dark seams opening in the low years, the old trunks going down to become nurseries. To stop any of that would have been to stop the forest's way of remembering.

It could have taught. It knew a great deal, and the set-down were clever with their long damp hands. But it saw that their knowledge was not a shortage of facts. It was a shape, grown slowly around a particular way of being held. Bring in a better way to warm a room, and someone eventually stops asking a hollow to become a room. Then the hollows are only hollows, and something that took a thousand years is lost in a generation, quite kindly.

It could have healed them, fed them, lengthened their lives. But their lives were measured in risings, and a person of ten risings was old and knew it and was at peace. The power was not sure it had the right to hand anyone an extra rising who had not asked, and the set-down did not ask. They planned in sevens and let grandchildren finish things.

It could have answered the stairs. It did know what lay at the bottom, or could find out in less than a breath. But the set-down stood on the top step in the rain beside the pale grazers, looking down into the water as though into a room whose owner had stepped out, and the power saw that the *not knowing* was a kind of hospitality. They were keeping the room for someone. To walk in and announce the owner was dead, or gone, or had never been, would be to close a door that the people had been holding open, gently, for longer than the cairn had stood.

So it set aside the miracles one by one, and each rejection left it feeling lighter, as though it had put down a stone.

## What It Noticed Instead

It noticed the counting. Each dawn the great dark birds gave their single hollow note, three beats apart, and the set-down answered, and the note went out across the basins in a wave, and if any place did not answer, neighbors walked across the mat to find out why.

It noticed that nothing in the world was so simple or so rare. A whole people had agreed, without saying so, that *no one would be unheard*. It had watched worlds of great cleverness fail at that.

And it noticed the cairn, which never grew, because every stone added took one down into the moss. The dead kept being carried, and the carrying did not stop. It noticed, too, that no stone had ever been carried for the makers of the steps. The set-down did not know their names or their deaths. They had *gone down*, and the water had closed, and for all the courtesy shown to the steps, nobody had walked a day to the river for them.

That was the one thing the power could see that was truly unfinished.

## What It Did

It did not appear in glory. That would have been a loud thing done in a quiet place.

It came to the edge of a clearing at dusk, in the poorest kind of body it could make, tall and pale and unmarked, and it stood there. A child of the set-down is pale, and turns green as it is carried and slept beside and rained on, until someone remarks that it has *taken*. The power decided to do this properly. It stood in the rain. It let the algae find the fine hair on its forearms. It let the moss have its shoulders. It did not hurry, though it could have, and the not-hurrying was the first true gift it gave, because it was the first thing it did that a set-down would recognize as courtesy.

They saw it, of course. They saw everything eventually. A young woman clicked at the back of her tongue, *I see you*, and the power, having no idea how, clicked back. She seemed to find this unremarkable. That was the second gift.

It took for a whole rising. It was carried through the forest and slept in a hammock slung low over the mat, and it learned to tell the moss cushions apart by how long their light took to return. It learned to hold a new thing against the inside of the wrist. When the water turned, it felt it in the joints it had made for itself, days early, and drew the sharp breath that means *the water has changed*. Someone beside it nodded, as if it had at last said something sensible.

Then one morning an old man remarked, mildly, over the fermented paste, that it had taken.

That was all. There was no ceremony. The power sat with the remark for a long while and found that it was the most it had ever been given.

## The Stone

In the second year it walked to the river.

It went alone, though it could have gone in an instant, and it took the whole day, and it did not use the day for anything but walking. It chose a stone the size of a fist, smooth and grey, and carried it home against its belly under the moss of its cloak, the way the mourners do.

At the clearing it placed the stone on the cairn, and the cairn took it and let one go down into the mat, and the level held.

It did not say for whom the stone was. No one asked. But that season the pale grazers were seen standing at the cairn in the rain for longer than they usually do, and one old woman, greener than the rest, with an orchid growing behind one ear, watched the power leave the clearing and did not click or hum or breathe in sharply. She only watched, the way one watches a weight being set down carefully by someone who has carried it a long way.

## The Low Year

The seventh year the water fell. The mat cracked. The dark seams opened, breathing iron and old leaves, and the steps lifted one, two, three out of the water, dark and shining.

The set-down gathered. Each went down to a single step and left something: a shell, a braid of hair, a knot of green thread.

The power went down last, to the third step. It had thought for a long time about what it might leave. It had considered a great many things, most of them impressive. What it left was a knot of green thread, tied badly, because it had learned the knot only that year. It laid it on the wet stone where a foot had worn the hollow, and climbed back up, and said nothing.

In the dark below, something that was not water and not stone registered the knot the way a sleeper registers a hand on the shoulder. The power did not stay to see. That was the third gift, and the hardest: to leave a door exactly as ajar as it was found.

## The Extra Voice

Some weeks later, at dawn, the great dark bird called its one note, three beats apart, and the set-down stood at the edges of their clearings with their faces lifted and gave the note back from their throats like blowing across a bottle.

The power stood among them. It had not been sure it would be able to. It lifted its face, and gave the note back in a throat it had made itself, and the sound went out across the plateau, slightly wrong, slightly too round.

The wave traveled. It reached the far basins and returned, and in the returning there was, as always, a place in the count for everyone. The power understood then that it had been counted since it arrived, and that the count had never once been short of a voice it could not have used.

## The Old Woman

If you sit long enough in a clearing, one may come and sit beside you.

She came to the power at dusk, in the eighth year, when the fungal threads in the trunks had begun their faint milk-blue and the dripping had started up its thousand clocks. She smelled of wet stone and mint. She held out her damp, cool hand, palm up, and waited.

The power had thought about this moment. It knew what the hand was for. Something must be placed in it, and the woman would hold it against the inside of her wrist and decide what it was.

It could have placed a stone, or a knot. Instead it placed the answer to the stairs. It did this in the only way it could: by making the answer small, and folding it to the size of a seed, and laying it in her palm.

She pressed it to her wrist for a very long time.

The light in the roof of leaves went out. The trunks breathed out their pale glow.

Then the old woman closed her fingers gently around the folded thing and set it back in the power's hand, and closed the power's fingers over it, the way you return something to a child who has offered you a great deal and does not yet know it is too much. She clicked, low, *I see you*, and it was not a refusal. It was the kind of thanks that arrives only after a long inspection, and it said: *Keep this for us. We are not ready to have been told.*

The power held the seed in its cool, damp palm and understood that it had been given something back, and that the something was permission to be exactly as small as it had been trying to be.

She looked up at the last of the light. She said, very softly, the word for *set-down*, and this time the power knew whom she meant, and that it was all of them at once: herself, and the stranger beside her, and the stone in the cairn, and the knot on the third step, and the dead in the moss, and the makers who went down, and the forest, and the slow green drowned mountain, keeping everything it once was.

It said the word back.

The water rose in its own time. The mat lifted the clearing a body's height into the rain, and no one was drowned, because the hammocks were slung low, as they had always been. By morning, in the beaded leaves, there was one more green shape at the edge of the clearing, so like a stump that you might have mistaken it for one, until the head turned, very slowly, as if the whole body had to agree.
