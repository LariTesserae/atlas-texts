# What Came Down in the Fog

It would walk past it. That is the first thing, and the power knows it before it arrives.

It comes across the flat on a moonless night, and it makes itself small for the crossing. It has a great many ways of being large, but none of them would be welcome here. The wind tears its cloth. The cairns point subtly the wrong way. It follows them, since they were made for people and it is willing, for once, to be led. At the rim it stops, and looks down into the dark, and feels the warm breath coming up out of the ground.

It stays a long time. It has learned that most of wisdom is staying a long time before doing anything.

---

## What it considers

It could bring rain. It sees the rain plainly: the terraces running red, the fig-bark bridges sagging, the great jars overflowing into lanes that have no drains, because no one here has ever needed to send water away. It sees a people who have counted water in cupfuls for longer than they have kept count of anything, standing in a downpour with nothing to measure. Their whole grammar of care would go slack. It would be a gift the size of a grief.

It could bring knowledge, such as iron, medicine, and better nets. But the potters alone have the wheel, and the town has never once chosen to give it to anyone else. It suspects, correctly, that this is not backwardness. It is a town that has decided how loud it can afford to be.

It could show itself and be believed. Then the rim would be a place people looked toward, and the whole art of not being seen, all those hundreds of small dull practices, would be spent in one bright moment. It would be paying with their hiding, and the hiding is what they are.

It could carry them out to a green valley with rivers, and they would go if it asked them to. They would stand in the open light with their hands raised like visors, weeping and squinting, and be relieved to go back down. It sees that too. The Cup is not a hardship they are enduring. It is the shape of their whole life. To rescue them from it would be to rescue them from themselves.

So it puts each of these down, gently, the way you put down a thing you have been asked to hold and should not.

## What it finds

It goes down into the town as fog does, without weight, through the lanes at the deep of the night. Nothing wakes. The geckos click once and go on. It passes a gutter-child asleep against a jar with a clay trough still in her arms, and it stays there a moment.

Then it goes lower, into the root-passages, where the air is warm and the stone sweats. It finds what it half expected. Two hundred feet down, past where the root-listeners' cords reach, a fissure is silting. It is slow and nothing dramatic, just a fine clay drifting into the throat of the channel, year on year. The breath is thinning. Above, in the jar-rooms, the water has begun to taste of metal, and the old are being asked to remember.

It does not tear the fissure open. It does not send a great warmth up from the deep. It does what a fig root would do if a fig root were patient and had been paying attention. It takes a single hair-fine root, one that has been stalled against the clay for a decade, and it persuades the stone to give, a finger's width, along a line the root was already trying to follow. Then it presses the clay back. It does nothing that a fig could not have done, only sooner. In a season the water will turn sweet again, and the jar-keepers will rap their knuckles on the clay shoulders and say the Breath has turned back, and no one will be thanked, and no one will be wrong.

It does not make the Cup permanent. It could, and it decides not to. Above, the flat world is still being worn, grain by grain, toward its final smoothness, and one day the roof of every hollow comes down. That belongs to the plateau, and to the people, and to the long truth that things end. What it gives is time. It gives more of the time the town was already living in, and it is careful not to give so much that the people stop noticing they are living in it.

## The one who is understood

At the bottom of the bowl, where the fig roots hang like curtains and the light is a green rumor, the power finds the cat.

It is lying on a warm terrace stone, and it is old. Its grey has gone almost to white along the jaw. It has not been seen with another of its kind in the lower bowl for longer than the cat's own memory of it, and the people, who say the cat is understood, have never quite said aloud what they understand, which is that it is the last.

The cat lifts its head and looks directly at the power, which is not where anyone else would have looked. Its pale eyes are unhurried. It doesn't startle. It regards the visitor as it regards fog, or a change in the wind, or a monkey on a roof: something that has arrived and will depart, and does not require a decision.

The power sits down beside it, or does whatever a power does that is like sitting.

It cannot give the cat more years. It has looked, and the years are what they are, and it will not lie to itself that grief can be edited out of the world without taking something else out with it. But it can see what has ended the cat's line, and it isn't cruelty or bad luck. It is only distance. Somewhere across the plateau, under a different stretch of quartzite, there is another cup, smaller, wilder, with no town in it, and in it are others of the cat's kind, who have never known this one exists.

Between the two, under the flat, is a wall of stone one hand thick.

The power puts a hand to it. It is a gesture, not a force. A passage opens, low and warm and narrow, too tight for any person and unwelcoming to the ones who might follow a person. It is a way for one kind of animal only, and it goes a very long way in the dark.

The cat watches. It yawns, showing all its old teeth, and puts its chin back down on the stone.

The power takes this as thanks.

## The cairns

On its way up, it stops on the flat.

It finds the cairns, the false ones, pointing subtly wrong, and finds that the wind has knocked three of them true in the last month. The rim-menders will be up in the next fog to reset them. It saves them the climb, and it does the job as they would have done it, stone by stone, in the exact shape of their carefulness, so that it looks made by hands.

Then, for a moment, it does one thing that is not for the town's safety at all. It lays a flat white stone on top of the last cairn, the way a person might leave a coin, without knowing whether anyone will ever find it. The rim-menders will find it, and they will stand around it in the gray for a long time, and no one will touch it. The eldest will say, at last, in the language of the second kind of speaking, *we don't move that one.* It will join the short list of things they leave where they are.

## What it takes, and what it leaves

It has taken nothing. It has drunk nothing, spilled nothing, and left no glint in the shafts of light. It has been careful in all the ways it was asked to be, without being asked.

But it has one thing left to do, and it does it last, as the sky above the rim goes from black to the color of the inside of a shell. It gathers what it has been, all of it, into a thing that fog is good at. It goes back down into the hollow, condenses, and settles on the roofs.

The nets are heavy that dawn.

Not flooded, only heavy, a little more than the season's fair share. Every jar in the lower town rises by a thumb's breadth, no more. The jar-keepers rap their knuckles on the shoulders of the clay and go quiet, and count twice. The gutter-children run the lanes in the gray hour with their troughs, singing their counting-song under their breath, and for once the song and the drip come out exactly together. Not a drop touches the ground. Someone will notice that, later. Someone will say that it was a good morning, and someone else will say *nine and a half*, and that will be all.

By midmorning the shafts of sun are burning the last of the fog off in swirling rags, and the temperature is climbing, and the cicadas begin their long held chord. The thrushes hand the song along, one throat to the next. In the deep bowl, an old cat lies on a warm stone with its eyes half shut, and somewhere far off in the dark of the rock, something small and tawny lifts its head and begins to walk.

On the flat, the wind still tears its cloth.

And no one looks down. Not even the one who was there.
