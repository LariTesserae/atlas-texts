# What Was Done at Harrow Deep

## The Descent

It comes down in the cage, though it has no need of one. It stands among the coal sacks for the full eleven minutes, wet through by the ninetieth second, because the descent is part of the place, and it wants to arrive the way the place makes arrivals.

It takes no shape that would be remarked on. It agrees, for a time, to be counted. The census stands at four hundred and sixty, and for one evening it stands at four hundred and sixty-one. Nobody notices. A man at the cage gate tells it to shake out its boots, and it does.

## The Exodus

It goes first to the east gangway and stands with the others while the gray light comes down the shaft and the roof begins to pour.

It has seen stars born and rivers turn, and it stands with its face tipped up like a congregation of one, and watches two million bats rise in a slow spiral. Beside it a child on someone's shoulders says, *Thin tonight,* and the woman under her says, *No. Good pour. You're just impatient.*

It is a good pour. It stays for the whole forty minutes. This is the first act of wisdom, and the rest depend on it: it watches until it has stopped wanting to be useful and started wanting to understand.

## What It Considers, and Leaves

It could drop the water table. It could close the seams, seal the chamber dry, and let the pumps rust in peace. The town would be free of its sixty-year fight.

It does not, because the fight is the town. The brass gauge is the truth of the place, and children learn to read it before letters. A people with nothing to watch would no longer be a watching people. The warm water rising from below also feeds the blind fish, and the cats sit over them, and so on down the whole wet chain. Sealing the leak would be a small murder of everything the leak has made.

It could cool the air. It could light the ceiling. It could cure the cough by fiat, whiten the feet, and quiet the pumps. But the dark above is where the bats live. The heat is why the children are quick. The pump-beat is the lullaby, and the town would wake if it stopped. Ease can be a kind of erasure, and it has learned to tell a hardship from a wound. A wound it will treat. A hardship it will only keep company with.

## What It Does

**The rock.** Every few years a slab sloughs from the ceiling with a sound like a dropped anvil. Nobody has died yet, and the wall in the meeting house records the dates. It does not prop up the roof, and it does not promise the rock will never fall, since the rock is young and has its own business. It has a quiet word with the limestone about manners. Afterward the slabs fall in the seconds when a place is empty, just after the bell has cleared a gangway or just before the cone-men return to a terrace. Fifty years on, someone reading the wall may notice how strangely well-timed the falls have been. They will decide it is luck, and there will be no one to contradict them.

**The third engine.** Some night in the coming decade the second engine will trip, and the third will be slow to catch. The whole population will wake at once, upright in the dark, hearing the silence. That must be allowed. It is their story, and it is how they know what they are to each other. But the catch will come at the ninety-first second rather than the fourth minute, and it will be a story instead of a funeral.

**The cough.** In the small hours the doctor's granddaughter wakes with an idea she has been circling for years: a mask of layered wet cloth over a pad of charcoal, with a fold that keeps the damp off the lips. It is a small, ordinary, entirely plausible idea. She thinks it is hers, and it is. Nothing was given her that she had not already been reaching for. It only leans a little on the door she had her shoulder to.

**The southern terrace.** It goes there on the second afternoon, where the ceiling drops and the pumps come muffled through the rock. A young man sits on the low wall with his back to the town, crying the way people do who have been holding something a long time. It sits down at the customary polite distance and says nothing. It does not comfort him, or explain, or look at him. It stays until he is done, and then a little longer, so that he does not have to be the first to leave.

Of all it does at Harrow Deep, this is the one it would keep if it could keep only one.

## The Gauge-Keeper

It saves her for the last night.

She has not missed an hour in nineteen years. It considers giving her a single night of unbroken sleep, an hour's grace with the book left blank. It sees at once why this would be a theft. The unbroken count is hers. The town asks after the level softly, the way you ask after the ill, and she is what makes that softness possible.

So it does what a person would do. It sits on the far end of the pier with its boots off and its feet in the warm edge of the black water, and it says nothing. At the top of each hour she trims the lamp, leans to the brass, reads, writes, and says the number aloud. It listens to her say it eleven times.

Around the fourth hour, when the engines and the cats are the only ones fully awake, a thin sleek cat comes along the gangway, pauses, inspects it with great seriousness, and climbs into its lap. The cat is cool. The Gauge-Keeper notes this without turning her head. The cat has not yet decided how long it will stay. Cats decide such things slowly, and sometimes for eleven years.

## Leaving

It does not announce its going. Before the morning exodus it walks to the Lamp Works, where the gardener's ferns stand in their tin tubs, and it takes one frond, gently, the way you take a thing that has been offered. It sets the frond in a tin cup of water on the Gauge-Keeper's stool and goes up in the cage among the coal-men's night shift. The cage rises eleven minutes into the light.

Or it does not go up at all. Whether the cat has a new lap, or the census a new entry, or the pier a quiet extra pair of feet, nobody could say, and the town has always been polite about what it does not comment on.

At the top of the hour the Gauge-Keeper comes to the pier and finds the cup. She looks at it for some time. She does not ask who. To be given a frond in a tin cup is to be told you are staying, and she understands it is also, this once, the reverse.

She trims her lamp. She reads the brass. Behind her the pumps go *thud–hiss, thud–hiss,* the town sleeps in the roar, and above her two million bats begin to come home like sustained applause.

She writes the number down. She says it softly, the way you say the name of someone still there.

*Steady.*

Then, after a moment, to the cup, or the dark, or the cool small weight of the cat against her boot:

*Stay, then.*
