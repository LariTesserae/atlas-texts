# The Guest

## Arrival

It does not come through the hatch at first. It comes the way weather comes, as a change in what the ridge is like to stand on. The herders two days down notice nothing. The grey birds in the vaults notice at once. They shift together, and for the first time in memory the sound does not follow their shifting. The chorus holds its chord, unbothered, as though it had been told there was nothing to fear.

The old woman lifts her head from sleep and taps once on the rib beside her, a question. The rib answers with only the ordinary shiver of the wall.

Then it comes in, and it comes small. Arriving upright and radiant in a room this low would break ribs, and it is careful of ribs. It enters on hands and knees through the crawlway, as the Sitters do, stooping under the vaults and letting the drips fall on its head. It walks to none of the people and none of the wall. It goes to the nearest ring of blue-white glaze, lies down in the silence, and stays there for a season.

## The Listening

A wise power does not assume, so it listens.

It listens to the pipe-eels in their channels and the moths on their air columns. It listens to the frog-wall, cell by cell, until it knows which frogs are old and which cells hold their notes crookedly. It listens to the Sitters by watching how they move when a rib goes flat, and how one of them sits for hours beside a silent hexagon, cupping nothing, waiting.

What it learns first is unwelcome to a being that is good at rescue: nobody here needs rescuing. The room is not a wound. It is a long argument that has been going well for a very long time. The Sitters are not suffering from their deafness. They traded one hearing for another that reaches further, and they would find the trade hard to explain to anyone who had not made it. The chorus is not imprisoned. The animals are not enslaved to a design. In the sense that matters, this place is *finished*, and it keeps itself finished, day after day, by singing.

So the first act of the power is to hold still, and it holds still for a long time.

## What It Did Not Do

It did not give the Sitters hearing. Their names are cells, their language is the lattice, and their calling is a shiver in a rib. Ears would not add to that world. They would drown it, and a people's whole way of being called would go quiet.

It did not fill the room with more water, warmth, or light. The one thought of the chorus is *water*, and the wanting is what makes the room a room. Abundance would have loosened the circuit that holds every creature in it to every other.

It did not carry the Sitters down the mountain, or bring medicine, or teach them what the lattice was. Understanding given whole is not understanding. The Sitters would have stopped sitting and started engineering, and the room would have turned from a creature they keep company with into a machine they operate. There are gifts that turn a companion into equipment.

It did not leave a sign, a shrine, or a name carved anywhere.

## What It Mended

There were two places where the circuit was failing in a way the room could not repair itself.

The first was on the ridge, out in the cold, where the Sitters cannot go. The fourth of the hidden intakes had been closing for a century. Corrosion had narrowed its throat, and every winter the frost took a little more of it. The room had never been short of water in any Sitter's memory, but the chorus had been thinning, cell by cell, in a way that would have taken another few generations to matter, and by then there would have been no fixing it. On a night of hard frost, the power climbed out and cleared the intake. It packed the seam with mud, chitin, and grit in the crude, sturdy masonry of the animals, so that any Sitter walking the ridge would find one more patch that looked as if the frogs had made it. It signed nothing. It let the work join the long conversation between the room and its mending.

The second was a single cell in the north wall, one the Sitters had sat beside for years. A frog had died there long ago, and no other would take the note. The power found the reason under a scallop of calcite: a hairline fracture in the hexagon, too fine to feel with a callused palm, that flattened the pitch enough that the frogs judged the cell dead. The Sitters had been waiting for a frog that would never come. The power laid its palm on the cell, the way a Sitter lays a hand on a frightened animal, and held it there until the crack knit and the note came true again.

## The Old Woman

The old woman found it in the circle.

She had come to lie down in the silence, as she did when she wanted to think, and there was someone already there. For a long time they lay on their backs, a body-length apart, watching the room shudder and drip and glow without a sound, the great creature dreaming. In the circles no one speaks, and she did not try. She turned her cloudy eyes toward it and looked, and it looked back.

Then she took its hand, which was warm and callused like her own, though it had not earned the calluses, and led it out of the silence into the roar. She did with it what is done with infants. She pressed its palm to one hexagon after another across the ribs, waiting for the twitch, the quickened pulse. It let itself feel each cell honestly, with everything it had, and at the north wall its hand twitched at the cell it had mended.

She was delighted. She tapped the name against the rib beside them, a bright ringing note with a thin buzz at its edge, the mark of the mended crack. *A name should carry the scar of the place it comes from,* she said, in her slow drumming. The Guest had been named, and it was the first time in a very long life that the Guest had a name that was not a title.

That night, pressing its fingers against her breastbone in the only language she could take in, it made her an offer. It could give her back her hearing, or give it to the young. It could do it gently and without cost.

She considered it for a full day. Then she tapped two knuckles, a pause, and a flat palm: *calm, no.* And after a moment she added, with a dry, patient drumming that might have been laughter: *Then how would I be called?*

But she asked for one thing. She asked what the room was for.

So it told her, through the rib and the sternum and the flat of the hand, in pressures her body could hold. The builders were not gods. They were tired people who wanted to leave behind something that would drink from the sky and keep going after they were gone. They built the three circles so that someone could be quiet enough to see that it was going. They did not build the room to be tended. They built it to be *listened to*, from a place where the listener would be still.

*You did not fail to grasp the design,* it told her. *You are the design. You have been lying in those circles for a thousand years.*

The old woman said nothing for a day. Then she passed the news to the others the way news is passed in a household, lightly and sideways, as if it were small. Nothing changed. One grieving Sitter lay a little longer than usual in the nearest circle. The frogs went on singing. But afterward the Sitters sat easier in the roar, like people who have been told the house they live in is theirs.

## The Fern-Keeper

The Guest sat, one afternoon, beside the woman who tends the tree-fern.

She was tapping her idle rhythms to the plant as she always did, small absent patterns on the cracked basin, and the Guest joined in. It gave her no words, only a rhythm that fit inside hers, the way one voice fits into another in a round. She did not stop or look at it. After a while she began to tap differently, no longer to the fern but to the one beside her. It was the first time in years she had tapped anything to a listener.

It did nothing for the fern. The fern is the only thing in the chamber that nobody planned, and the power has learned to respect the unplanned as the place where the future comes in. Its roots would split more of the glaze. Its spores would wash into the channels and be cemented into the floor with the bones. Let the room decide what that meant.

## Leaving

It stayed a season and a winter, and it left at the dawn pulse, when the frogs' sacs swell from the entrance to the far wall and the sound climbs past what seems possible, and no one could have felt one more body slip along the crawlway. On the ridge, in the first sun, it stood a moment in the silence that makes the herders cry.

It had changed almost nothing: one intake, one cell, one truth, one name. It considered that the measure of a good visit is how little a place has to explain afterward.

## Afterward

In spring, the youngest Sitter walks out to the herders and stops on the ridge, stunned by the quiet, and sees a patch of mud and chitin by the fourth intake. It looks like the frogs made it. She doesn't wonder.

In the north wall, a frog sits in a cell that rings clean, with a thin buzz at the edge of its note. When the old woman taps the name she gave the Guest, the rib shivers, the frog swells its brass sac, and the note comes back. It is only a frog, and that is the whole answer.

On the coldest nights, when the mountain freezes and the chorus thins to a few hundred voices, she lies in the nearest circle and taps nothing. The room dreams around her without a sound, and she thinks, though she could not say why, that something very large has sat down in the silence beside her and is being quiet too.
