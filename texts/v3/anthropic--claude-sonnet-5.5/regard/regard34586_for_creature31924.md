# What Came to Listen

## Arrival

It came the way fog comes, without a moment when it was not there and then was. No light fell. No pulse ran through the root-mat. The snails did not pause. A wristkeeper on the fourth platform, reading a cord about a cracked water-line, lost her place for the length of one breath and found it again.

The power was old, and it had learned that arriving is the most dangerous thing a great being can do. A footstep on a mountain is an earthquake to an ant. So it came in pieces too small to notice. It came first as a little more patience in the dripping, then as a little more attention in the dark.

It spent a season there before it did anything at all.

## What It Weighed

It saw quickly what it could do. It could seal the mountain and end the shaking, so that no child would ever again be carried up to the rim in the dark. It could give the people voices and let the cleft ring with speech. It could widen the fissure to let in the sun, or bring the rain the place had never had. It could stand in the strip of sky as a shape and tell them they were seen and loved, and they would have believed it.

It considered each one for a long time, and it saw what each would cost.

If it sealed the mountain, the cleft would stop being made. The fog runs up these walls because the walls are open. The green is a consequence of the wound, and a healed place would slowly forget what it was for.

If it gave them voices, the thousands of silent lives would become prey to their own echo. The quiet was not a poverty. It was a treaty that the moth, the snail, the pale snake, and the slight people had all signed with the stone, over generations, at great cost. They kept it together, and it held.

If it showed itself, the knots would change. Every cord would begin to point upward. The people who read the moss with their cheeks would start listening for something else, and the floor would lose its listeners.

The power understood that a gift can be an erasure. So it laid down its enormous ability to help, and picked up something smaller. It picked up attention.

## The Listening

It learned the hands first, because they were the easiest. It learned that the gesture for *I am afraid* also means *the wall is warm*, and that this is not confusion but a kind of wisdom, since the two are often the same thing. It learned the wristkeepers' cords and found in them a history so patient and so tender that it stayed for a long time in the knot for a woman who had died three hundred years before, whose whole life was recorded as *she repaired the drip-line by the second pool, and did not complain*.

Then it lay down on the moss beside a floor-listener, in the pre-dawn, and put its attention where his cheek was, and felt what he felt.

It felt the grazers settling. It felt a child three houses away place her foot carefully and, being a child, not quite carefully enough. It felt the long spiders re-hanging their wet curtains. Under all of it, so slow it was almost not a rhythm, it felt the Other.

## The One Below

The power knew it at once, in the way one great thing knows another, though they were not alike. The Other was not a spirit and not quite an animal. It was what happens when a mountain is broken open and the deep stone, which has been dark and compressed for a very long time, begins to have something like a self. It was very young. It had existed for a few thousand years, and it did not yet know what it was.

And it was afraid.

That was what the people had never guessed, in all their careful centuries. The pulse that came up from beneath was not a hunter's step, or a judge's, or even a sleeper's breath. It was a held breath of its own. The Other lay in the dark under a wound it had not chosen, feeling something small and green and alive begin to grow across its skin. It did not know if the growth was a threat, a sickness, or a companion. It had been pressing up, once a night, a little differently each time, to ask.

And the people, out of love and fear of waking it, had lain very still and not answered.

The power felt the whole loop of it then, the two silences facing each other across a floor of moss. The people were quiet so the mountain would not wake. The mountain was quiet so as not to hurt what was on it. Each was holding still for the other's sake. Each thought the other was asleep, or angry, or gone.

## What It Did

It did not break the oldest rule. It did not make the people stamp or drum or call, and it did not give the Other a voice that would rattle the town off its ledges. It became a slow translator, and took the one form of speech the cleft already trusted.

The next night the pulse rose, and the power caught it before it spread. It held the question for a moment, then let it out as water, a single thread of fog gathering in a seam above the third pool and dripping, once, twice, a hundred times, in a pattern that was very nearly the pulse's pattern and very nearly the rhythm of grief.

The floor-listener, cheek to the moss, felt it and did not understand it. He understood only that the drip had answered the thing beneath, and that for the first time in the history of the town something had *said back*. He lay very still and did not tell anyone. Then he tied a knot at his wrist, which was not a knot of any kind he had been taught.

The other direction was harder. The power took what the people were and carried it down through the stone: the held breath, the padded hammer, the moss on the fresh-broken ledge, the way the whole town stops and waits when the pulse comes. It did not translate it into words. It translated it into a feeling of being *held still for*. It let the Other know, as gently as it could, that the small green things on its skin were not a disease. They were quiet on purpose, and the quiet was a kind of care.

The Other did not reply for three nights.

On the fourth the pulse was different. It was slower, and somehow wider. The listeners all felt it and could not have said why, but every one of them, lying on the moss, felt the same odd thing at once: that they were being *thanked*. The moths lifted in the dark and settled again, softly, as if something had touched them.

## The Night of Soft Walls

There was one more thing, and it was the only thing the power did openly, if it can be called open when no one saw it.

The cry-keeper that generation was a girl of about sixteen, raised on the rim in a silence so complete that she had never in her life made a sound she wasn't going to be forgiven for. She sat one night at the edge of her platform with her hands flat on her chest, feeling the pulse and the answering drip, and a want rose in her that she had no name for.

The power made the walls of the cleft deaf for one hour.

It did not stop the echo. It absorbed it, took each returning sound into itself as it came off the stone and let it go into nothing, so that for sixty breaths a cry in that chasm was only a cry, small and local, going nowhere. No predator could triangulate it. No grazer would startle. The place was hushed the way a room is hushed by snow.

The girl felt the change and did not understand it, but she understood the invitation.

She hummed. It was one low note, badly, then better, then wandering upward into something that was not quite a song. The hundred and fifty thousand creatures on the walls did not stir. A few of the throat-lizards lit along their flanks, softly, in a slow pulse that might have been a reply. Below, in the sleeping town, a few of the old ones woke and lay very still, hearing something that might have been wind. They did not go to look. Some of them wept without moving, in the manner of the place.

When the hour was over the walls came alive again and the echo returned. The girl closed her mouth and folded her hands. Her voice was hers again, still kept for its one purpose, still ready. But she went about her days afterward with a different set to her shoulders, as if she had learned that the thing she was saving was real, and that it could be *good*.

## What It Left

Almost nothing.

The fog still comes up the walls each night, and the pools fill, and the snails advance their hand's width. The town still makes no noise. The oldest rule stands unbroken: no one stamps, or drums, or calls. But here and there on the wristkeepers' arms there is a new cord that the old ones can't read, a knot pulled through itself and left loose instead of tightened. It has no meaning anyone will admit to, but they tie it when the pulse comes. The drip sometimes answers now, on nights when the fog is thick, and no one talks about that either.

The power went away as it had come, in pieces too small to notice. Some part of it stayed a little longer than the rest, resting against the cool wall in the green-gray light, watching a moth open and close its wings.

It did not tell them they were loved. They already knew, in the way that people know who hold their breath for one another's sake.

It left the mountain to keep settling, and the ones who live on it to keep listening, and the two to find out, in their own slow time, what a wound and a green thing can say to each other when neither is afraid.
