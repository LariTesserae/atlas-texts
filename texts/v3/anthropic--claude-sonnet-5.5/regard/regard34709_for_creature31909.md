# What Came to the Bladder

It did not arrive as light. A wise power knows that arrival is already an act of force, and this place had gone a few hundred years without one.

It came the way the visitor comes, by the crack that breathes, sideways, letting its edges be scraped by the glass. It took a shape small enough to fit the seam and warm enough not to be noticed as a chill. The felt leaned away from its footfall, the amphibians blinked, and the bats turned their pink faces in a slow tide. It felt the pressure at the back of its neck, the accountant adjusting the column, and it let itself be counted. It did not try to be an exception.

## What it decided against

It had power enough to do a great many things, and it considered each of them and put them down.

It would not free them. They were not held. The walls were not a cage but a body, and a being that has no outside cannot be freed from it.

It would not give them eyes, or names, or the notion of one animal apart from another. The blur of snail into grub into toolmaker was not a defect. It could not tell whether it was pain or breath, and it had learned long ago that a fix applied to something you do not understand is only a wound with better intentions.

It would not stop the hunter that threads through the mites, or the snails that eat the hunter, or the toolmakers who die into mud and come back as mites. The wheel of eating was how the room stayed full, and fullness was the room's one word. To break the wheel to spare the mites would silence the only thing the room had ever said.

It would not tell them the mountain was cooling. The cone was a few hundred years old, and the warmth in the crowd was borrowed from a fire that would eventually go out. The bubble would one day be a cold hollow in black stone, and the felt would thin from the edges inward, gently, the way a breath thins at the end of a long exhale. The power knew this and held it. It was not unkind to know something and not say it. A truth with no one who could carry it is just weight.

## What it did instead: it read

The power stayed a long time, by the mountain's clock. It sat in the knee-deep water at the edge and let the mites walk over its hands, and it studied the floor.

The blades lay under the water in their thousands, paved and drifting. Everyone had said they were tools, and they were. But the knappers do something no tool needs. They flick a fingernail against the edge and listen to the ring, and then they pass the blade or drop it.

The power listened too, as long as it took, and it learned that each blade rings differently, and that the differences are not accidents. They are tuned. Each knapper, without knowing it, strikes the flake until it sounds like something particular, and what it sounds like is the one who was there before.

The room forgets its dead within a breath. The wall gives up a replacement and the gap is gone. Fullness allows no vacancy and so no mourning, and the accountant has no column for what is lost. But the hands remember. The listening remembers. Every dropped blade is a note held in glass, and the floor of the basin is a slow chord of a thousand thousand who were filled in over so quickly that no one had time to say they were gone.

The room does not know it is grieving. It thinks it is making knives.

The power understood that this was the one thing in the chamber that was not conserved. The count never changed, but the floor kept rising. The grief went into black glass, where it could not be counted, and so it accumulated.

It did not tell them. A name for what they were doing would have been a stranger's hand on something whole. Naming is not always a gift. Sometimes it is a way of taking.

## What it did instead: it left a gap

There was one thing it could offer without changing anything, and it thought about it for a long while.

When a mite died on the wall by the seam, in the breath before the glass gave up its replacement, the power leaned into the place. It made no force and no light. It was only a warm weight where the next one would have stepped, the way you can hold a door by standing in it.

For the length of one held breath, a hand's width of obsidian was bare and wet and empty. It was the first bare glass in the history of the room.

The ticking stopped. Every bat turned. The heat dipped in that place, a coolness the size of a palm, and the felt leaned toward it without filling it, a long ripple that arrived at the edge of the emptiness and paused. The power felt the accountant stop, the pressure at the back of its neck go still, a very large mind meeting a number it had not written.

Then a knapper came, blind, and put its fingertip to the bare glass. It stood a long time. It struck a flake from its core, turned it, and flicked the edge with a nail, and the ring was one the power had not heard on the floor. It did not pass the blade. It held it out toward the empty place, edge away, and waited, and there was no hand to take it.

Then it set the blade down in the gap, the first thing the room had ever kept.

The power let go. The wall filled, as it must, and the count came out even. But the blade stayed in the mud where the gap had been. Nothing had been fixed. The room had been given one breath in which a loss could be a loss, and it had done with that breath what it knew how to do, which was to make something with an edge and offer it.

## What it did instead: it made a bad knife

Before it left, the power did one more thing, and it was the thing it enjoyed most.

At the clear place in the wall, where the mountain goes thin and you can see the older plain and the grey sea, a knapper on the far side lifted a flake toward it, blind, edge turned away. The power had watched this passing a thousand times and knew the shape of it. It took the blade, and it did not refuse.

Then, because a gift should be in the tongue of the one receiving it, it picked up a pebble and a lump of obsidian and tried to knap.

It was terrible at it, and it did not cheat. It gave itself hands with the wrong number of joints and struck the core too hard and split the flake across its width. It turned it a quarter turn, felt the edge, and tried again. It made a small, thick, ugly blade with a lip like a badly healed scar. It held the blade out, edge away, and a knapper took it.

The knapper flicked a fingernail against it and listened. The ring was flat, a dull clack with no note in it at all. The knapper turned its eyeless face toward the power for a moment, as if to say something gentle, and then dropped the blade into the water.

It sank and settled among the others, one more black leaf on the bed of glass, and a mite crossed it and did not stop.

The power was as moved as it had ever been. It had been judged as one of them, by the standard they actually held, and found ordinary. It had been given the whole dignity of the room, which is that you are counted, your work is tried, and if it is not good it is put down without cruelty and someone else takes another.

## After

The power went out by the crack, which was colder than it had been, and stood a while on the black gravel under the thin sun. It did not turn the mountain back or slow the fire. It let the cone be young and let it age.

What it left behind is small enough to be missed. Under the water lies one blade the room did not make, dull and flat-ringing, that will drift with the rain and shift into new arrangements, and be stepped on. Beside it, in the mud of the shelf by the seam, lies one blade that is not part of any pattern, set down where a gap had been.

The count is even. The wall is weeping. The felt leans on itself. And in the packed, silent heat, a knapper strikes a core, turns it a quarter turn, flicks the edge, and listens for a ring it now half-remembers, one that no one ever taught it to hold.
