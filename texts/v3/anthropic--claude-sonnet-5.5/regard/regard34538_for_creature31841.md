# What Came to Verrin Flat

It came looking for a someone and found a somewhere.

That was the first surprise, and it took its time over it. It had expected a single mind to attend to, some center where the place gathered itself and could be spoken to. Instead it found the felt and the bloom, ten thousand waving claws, the black rows of cormorants holding out wings that would never dry, and a few dozen people in tin sheds. None of them was in charge, and none of them was the flat. The flat was the tenancy, and the tenancy had no address.

So it did what it does when it doesn't yet know what to do. It stayed still, for a full turn of the tide.

## The Looking

It came in without weather. The overcast did not part and no light fell. It settled into the place the way humidity does, and the only sign was that the flamingos, for about a minute, lifted their heads together and then lowered them again.

It watched the tide arrive as a thin silent sheet and take the horizon. It watched the crabs pour into their holes under its shadow, though it had no shadow, and come back out from the edges inward, waving. It read the walls of the pumping houses, where each drip has a rhythm and the rhythms overlap. It went down into the felt, section by section, through the green roof, the purple middle, and the black floor that has never needed air, and it found there the oldest arrangement on the plain, running without complaint.

It noticed that nothing here was suffering in the way it had been braced for. The abundance was real. Every predator had plenty, and the herons were merely thorough. The ruin was not a wound. The concrete had become a reef, and the sweat on the walls was a kind of soil.

It noticed a mudskipper on a rail, propped in the heat, wearing its expression of slightly offended patience. The power was amused. That was a true thing, and it let itself be, briefly, delighted. The mudskipper did not notice, which was as it should be.

## What It Did Not Do

It considered a great deal and declined most of it.

It did not scour the rust. The red beards on the tanks were not damage. They were the tanks turning slowly into something else, and the swallows had built in them.

It did not break the heat or thin the haze or raise a single tree. Nothing with a stem had ever taken hold here, and to plant one would have been to say that the place was unfinished. The place was finished. It was only unfashionable.

It did not call back the city. The works were built for a crowd that never came, and there is a kind of cruelty in filling an empty theater to redeem the architect.

It did not hold back the sea. The land lay a hand's breadth above the water, and the power could see the long arithmetic of that. A day would come, not soon, when the tide's thin sheet would stop retreating. It could have propped the coast up for a while, but a wall built by something wise is still a wall, and the flat had always been a negotiation with the water, not a victory over it. The mats had outlasted the coast as it once lay. They would outlast this one. Whatever the sea took, the felt would take back, in its own color.

It did not announce itself to the crabs, who had not asked. It did not comfort the birds, who were not afraid. Most of what it might have given, the flat did not lack.

## The One Ache

There was one place where it found something unfinished, and it was small.

It was in the crew, and not in their bodies. They were fed and dry and skilled. What they lacked was an answer to a question they had stopped asking out loud. *Who is the salt for?* A boat came in the dry season with fuel and flour and parts. A ledger was signed. Nobody could say who read it. Over generations the reason had thinned until only the keeping was left, and the keeping had begun to feel, to the few who still thought about it, like faithful attendance at an empty house.

The power went and stood beside the woman who tends the diesels. She knows each engine by its stroke, the way one knows a relative's cough. She was oiling a bearing and did not look up. It did not speak to her. It did not want to become a voice in her head, or a dream, or a burning anything. It knew that a truth handed down from above is never quite believed, and it would have been a poor gift to make her a prophet in a tin shed.

It gave her a morning instead.

## The Morning

The tide was out. She had gone, for no reason she could later name, farther along the causeway than she usually went, past the last survey post to where the dike overlooks the far pans. The light was the weak-tea color that follows rain. She stood there, and she saw.

It was nothing that hadn't always been there. But for a few minutes it arranged itself. She saw the water she let through the sluice gate by the tide table, and the salt-gradient it made, pan after pan, each a little richer than the last, like a slow sentence. She saw where the sentence ended, in the pink pans at the far edge, where the flamingos stood a kilometer deep with their heads down. She saw that the flock was there because of the exact brine, and that the brine was there because of the gates and the diesels and the slow-hearted wheel. She saw the ledger as what it was: a list of requests from the land, translated into numbers, and answered every day by hands that had forgotten it was a conversation.

It was not a vision. Nothing glowed. She simply understood that they were not attending an empty house. They were gardeners, and the garden was the whole flat, and the garden was full. The city had never come because it was never the point. This was the point: the hush, the tick, the drift of pink, the ten thousand birds turning as one dry breath.

She stood there a long while. Then she walked back and finished oiling the bearing. When someone asked what she'd seen out there, she said, "Flamingos," and that was true.

But at the end of the dry season, when the ledger was made ready for the boat, she added a column. She counted birds, by kind, as well as she could, and wrote the numbers in.

## The Reader

That, too, the power had seen to, and it was the only other thing it did.

Far from the flat, in a room with real windows, a clerk received the ledger. For a hundred years the ledger had been received and stamped and shelved without a glance, and it might have gone on so until the fuel stopped coming and the sheds emptied and the sea walked in. This clerk had been, for no reason he could trace, a little bored that season, and a little curious. He opened the ledger past the stamp. He found a column of numbers he didn't understand, and then he understood them. Stilts, avocets, terns, the pale small shorebirds by the tens of thousands, a drift of flamingo.

He did not know why his throat tightened. He took the page to someone whose job was to decide things, and he said only, "Somebody is counting."

It was not much. The power had not moved a coin or forged a signature. It had made sure that a reason, once it existed, would have a place to land. The fuel would keep coming for a while, now for a cause. When the sea did finally decide, the people would have time to choose what came next, and the reader would have a hand in it.

## Leaving

When it was done, it did not leave at once. It walked the causeway at low tide, though it made no click in the snails. It watched the storm come across at four, the warm heavy rain, twenty minutes, the birds turning their backs, and then the steam rising from the whole world at once. It watched the bats stream out of the brick. It watched a window in the distant compound go yellow.

At the crossing, where no train has run in years, the signal light changed to red.

The power stopped. It was not obliged to. It could have gone through the light as it went through everything. But the light was the truest thing on the flat: a promise kept for no one, tended for no reason but that it had always been tended, and to let it go dark would be a kind of desertion. So it stood at the crossing and waited, in the warm dense dusk, as though a train were coming.

After a while the light turned green, and it went on.

Nothing on the flat marked its going. The crabs waved. The diesel beat its slow beat. The tide came in, thin and silent, and took the horizon for an hour, and everything that lived there went on living in the leavings of something larger than itself, a little more seen than before.
