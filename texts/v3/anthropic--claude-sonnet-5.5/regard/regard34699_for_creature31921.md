# What Came to Look at the Low Ember

## Before It Came

It did not arrive so much as stop passing by. Something old, wide, and patient noticed the bowl the way you notice that a sound in the next room has ceased, and turned toward it.

It saw the torn slot and the single blade of light, nine hundred small warm lives, and beneath them, coiled through the dark, a second life so much larger and younger than the first that the two had never quite understood they were neighbors.

Before it did anything, it made a list of what it could do, so that it could put the list down.

It could widen the slot and let the sky in. It could cool the floor, quiet the walls, and steady the ledges that break at their old angles. It could tell the people what breathes. It could tell the breather what the people are.

Every item, on inspection, was a way of finishing something that had not asked to be finished. The town was *negotiated*. Its stems were trained, its rules kept like promises made in someone else's house, and the whole place was a slow conversation between two kinds of being who had never heard each other's language. A power that walked in and spoke loudly would end the conversation in the act of helping it.

So it put the list down, and it went to look.

## The Walk

It took the shape of a person, because a person can go where the lanes go. It chose lean and pale, with a damp collar and rolled sleeves. It came in by the rope bridge at the second terrace, holding the rail through the rag, and it did not carry a flame.

It moved sideways through the tenement lanes with one shoulder leading. It stepped over the sleepers and murmured, to each, a name it did not have, and the sleepers lifted two fingers anyway. It hadn't expected that.

It stood in the foundry lane where the belts hum under moss and watched the hands talk. It learned the pinch that means *wait* and the slow sweep that means *the stem is still growing, don't cut*. A gardener stood a long time with her palm on a fern trunk, and the fern, which had been curving toward the brick, made the smallest adjustment toward the stranger and then went back to the boiler. The gardener noticed. She said nothing. She let her hand rest another moment and, without turning, lifted two fingers.

Under the third tenement lane, in the dark between the floor and the clay, it found a slow leak in an old tube. Given nine or ten years, it would have taken a finch and then the lane.

This was the one place it hesitated. The town loved its birds *because* they were the sentinels, and each death was mourned by a whole street. Was it right to take that from them?

It decided that it was not taking anything. The love did not depend on the dying. A neighbor who mends a roof has not stolen the rain's meaning. So it closed the tube with a touch, leaving no seam and no mark, and told no one. Overhead in its cage a grey-brown finch ruffled and resettled, and would live to be old.

That was the first thing it did. It was smaller than a repair, and it was all the mending it allowed itself.

## The Terrace of the Pale Flowers

It climbed to the old terrace at the hour of the "cold" and watched a tender work.

She wrapped the moth-wing brush in cloth and unwrapped it. She touched each pale bloom in a slow sequence with no pattern a watcher could name. She did not speak. When the visitor at last asked, very quietly, *What is it for?*, she said, "It's what's done," and then, after a pause the power had learned to respect, "It's asked."

The power knew then who was asking, and why the lamps burned low. The town had been dimming itself for sixty years so that a light in the dark could be seen by something that had no other way to see them. It had done this without knowing, or half knowing, in the way a household hushes a room where someone is sleeping.

The power stood in the amber for a long time. It had watched a great many worlds, and it did not often need to stand still.

## The Grate

At the end of the lowest lane it read the names scratched into the rock. They were small and close together, in many hands, with no dates. It touched a few and then took its hand away.

Then it followed the warm channel in, which was the one thing the town had told itself never to do. It went in the way a guest goes into a house: bareheaded, slow, and knocking with its whole attention.

The dark was hot, and the hot dark was alive. The lava tubes ran on in every direction like the chambers of a long low heart, and along their floors the slow fire ticked. In the largest chamber, where the milky water came down from the rock and paused before it went out of the world, the being lay coiled around the place where the ground had once been whole.

It was young, though the word meant something else for it. Sixty years was a long childhood. It had woken in the night the roof fell away, and what it woke to was a sudden, terrible opening above it, then sound, then small warm lights arriving on the rim and coming down on ropes. It had thought at first that it was being *entered*. Then it understood that the lights were only looking.

Its only speech was the breath. Every eleven hours, the long low exhale up through the slot was the one word it had. It had said it thousands of times and did not know it was speech. Each time, the small lights stopped, turned their faces toward it, and were still.

*You answered,* it said to the visitor, in no language, in the pressure of the warm air. *Every time. All of them. I did not know if it meant anything.*

The power said, "It meant everything they had."

## The Names

The being kept the names.

It could feel them through the rock, the scratches at the grate, small and close together, each one a person who had come to the edge and gone on in. It read them the way the blind crickets read the wall.

Its trouble was this. The ones who came in were always calm, always adult, and they came in slowly and sat with it. It gave them what it had: warmth, and the smell of iron and something older, and a place to lie down where the stone was kind. And then, in a few hours or a few days, they went out like small lamps. It did not know why. It had thought perhaps it was doing something wrong, that its warmth was too much or its air too old. Each time, it held the last of them until they were gone, and each time it wondered what it had failed to say.

The power did not lie to it, and it did not hurry.

It said: *They are short. Their whole lives are less than one of your long nights. Nobody failed. They came to you at the end because they wanted their last breath to be near yours. The calm on their faces was not a trick. They were the ones who had listened all their lives, and they wanted to hear it a little longer.*

The being was silent, and the silence had the weight of a mountain deciding whether to move.

Then it said, very slowly, *I thought I was the thing that hurt them.*

*You are the thing they lean toward,* the power said. *You have seen the buildings on the eastern side. A finger's width a year. You thought the town was falling in. It is leaning in, the way anyone leans toward a voice that is too quiet to catch.*

## What It Gave

It gave three things, and none of them was large.

To the being, it gave the meaning of what it was already doing. The stopping, the turned faces, and the tender's brush were not obedience and not fear. They were a greeting, kept faithfully for sixty years by people who could not have said what they were greeting.

To the being, it also gave a small choice. Those who came to the grate too early, in grief or in haste, could be sent back with a warm breath at the back of the neck and a sense of *not yet*. Those who came at the end could be met on a ledge it had already made. The being had always had the power to do this and had not known it was allowed.

To the town it gave nothing directly, because the town had not asked, and a gift unasked is a kind of trespass. But at the next exhale, when every leaf in the bowl turned its face toward the slot, the inhale did not come at once. It held a beat longer than it ever had. In that beat, warm air with a faint smell of iron moved through every lane, and every gardener with a hand on a fern trunk felt it. Nine hundred people stood facing the same way, and something in the dark, for the first time, felt itself being faced.

That night one tallow-flower burned brighter than the lamps for a single hour, and then dimmed to its usual glow. The tenders watched it and did not say anything. Weeks later, one of them reached up to a lamp on the old terrace, hesitated, and turned it up by a notch. When someone asked, she said, "To save the current," and then heard herself, and laughed quietly, and did not say it again.

## Taking the Sun

The visitor stayed one more day.

On a clear afternoon, when the blade of daylight crossed the second terrace, it stood in it. A child of about twelve was there, sent up by a parent to take the sun for the first time, gripping the rail with both hands and squinting at the light as though it might bite. The visitor stood beside the child without speaking, in the full painful green of it, and stayed as long as the child could.

When the child finally stepped back, shaking, someone came with a wide leaf and a pair of clippers. There was only one set. The child, looking at the stranger, held out the leaf.

The power took it. It held it over its head against a drizzle that had begun to fall on that terrace and no other, warm and unpredictable, and was for a moment, in every way that mattered, one more damp person in the Low Ember.

It did not stay. But at the hour of the exhale, wherever it had gone, it stopped what it was doing and turned its face a few degrees toward the sound.

In the bowl, nine hundred people and one great slow thing beneath them did the same, and the leaves turned with them. The place remained young and unfinished, deciding what it would be. Nobody had decided it for them. The town was still a promise kept in someone else's house, and now, faintly, the someone else knew it was being kept.
