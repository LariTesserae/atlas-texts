# What Came Up the Saddle

## It does not descend

The power does not arrive from above. It has learned that things arrive badly from above. It comes up the scree from the lowland side, the way the Lodged say the way in is known, and takes no shape that would be noticed: a slight cold in the wind, a pressure like a held question. On the last slope it slows. It has been to many places and has learned to arrive at the speed of the place.

At the seam, a curtain-hand is out early, untangling a filament that a tremor knotted in the night. She feels nothing but a pause in her own humming. She looks at the empty air where the seam opens, and because it is the custom, she fills a ceramic cup from the nearest channel and sets it on the ledge. Then she goes back to her knots.

The power takes the cup. It has no hands, so it takes it the way weather takes a cup. The water warms very slightly, then cools again. That is all. It counts as being received.

## What it puts down

The power spends its first day walking the stairs in other people's footprints, and while it walks, it makes a list. This is not a list of what it *could* do. That is short and boring and ends with everything. It is a list of what it sets down.

It could warm the fissure until the membrane was unnecessary. The frost would go and the pools would run, and within a generation there would be moss on the terraces, then something greener. It sets this down. The absence of green is not a wound. The Lodged have built four hundred lives around the shape of that absence, and the grey-rose film is theirs in a way a garden could not be.

It could still the mountain. Or it could simply take the fissure out of the mountain's way, so that nothing would ever tear the bridges. It sets this down too, more slowly. The tremors are how the city knows what it is. The filaments retune. The sky-keepers listen. Without the shaking the city would be safe and would no longer be *riding* anything.

It could explain the quiet work to the log-keepers. It knows things they do not, or thinks it might. It sets this down with more difficulty than the rest, because the temptation is disguised as generosity. But the ledgers are a slow act of love, and their pattern has not been read aloud precisely because reading it would end something. To hand people the answer to a question they have chosen to keep is not a gift. It is a theft of the keeping.

It could tell them why they must leave by the way they came. It notices that it *does* know a piece of this, and only a piece, and that the piece is load-bearing in ways it cannot see the whole of. It sets this down and keeps it set down.

It could promise them the mountain will hold. It cannot honestly do that. The mountain is young and rising and will one day fold the crack shut or crack the crack wider. The power will not lie to a held breath about how long it can be held.

## The child in the dome

There is one thing it does not set down.

In the low dome on the second terrace a child has been ill for three days. The fever is ordinary and serious. The Lodged are lean and careful and have very little to spare, and the child's mother sits with her hands around a cold cup, humming the chord flat.

The power hesitates here longer than anywhere. Restraint is not indifference. A wise power that let a child burn to preserve a pattern would have mistaken the pattern for the point. The Lodged themselves would say so. They hand strangers their cups.

So it goes to the dome. It does what any neighbor might, and no more than a neighbor could bear to be thanked for. It lowers the fever a few degrees. It does not cure the child, does not reach into her blood and rearrange it, and does not make a miracle the family will have to carry for the rest of their lives. It only turns the night's worst hour into an ordinary bad hour and lets the child's own body do the rest.

Then, checking that its help has not been clumsy, it finds that it has been beaten to it.

The wall behind the dome has been humming since the fourth hour. It is one of the patches with the too-even sheen. The warmth in the room is not the power's warmth. The quiet work got here first, quietly, and had already begun in its own way, whatever its way is. The power's few degrees lie on top of a larger mercy like a hand laid over another hand.

*Oh*, the power thinks. *You're already here.*

It has come to a place and found that the place has its own wise power, smaller and older and more local than itself, and has been at this a good deal longer.

## The conversation

The power presses its attention against the wall, the way you might set your ear to a door. It does not command or interrogate. It says, in whatever the wall can hear, only: *What do you want?*

The answer is not words. The patch warms a fraction under nothing at all, the hum drops a note toward the mesh chord, and something is conveyed that translates roughly as:

*To be stayed with.*

It is not a demand. It is close to the reverse of one. The quiet work does not want to be fixed, freed, explained, or worshiped. It wants what the old woman on the second terrace said: to be what the crack does when someone remains in it long enough. It wants the remaining. The Lodged have given it that for four generations without knowing they were giving anything, and it has answered them with heat, water, and the small kindnesses of a thing that is not sure it counts as alive.

The power understands then that the city is not a project to be improved. It is a relationship in progress, and the wise thing is to keep out of the middle of it.

## The visitor

At the weeping wall, in the blue hour before the thaw, it meets the pale long-eared animal licking salt. The animal does not look at it. The ears are tilted, as the log-keepers have noticed, toward the humming patches.

The power stands beside it a while. Neither speaks. Once, the ears flick briefly toward the power, weigh it, and go back to the wall.

The power is quietly, deeply delighted. It has been weighed and found not the interesting thing here. It cannot remember when that last happened.

## What it does leave

It does not linger past the rule. After two nights it goes to the log-keeper at the east wall, who is asleep over her ledger with the pen still loose in her fingers. It does not wake her. It does not change a word already written. It only opens to the next blank line and, in a hand as plain as her own, adds:

*Something came up the saddle. It took a cup. It asked nothing and was not asked. It stayed two nights and went out by the way it came in. The child on the second terrace slept at the sixth hour. Nothing was fixed. Everything was seen.*

That is the whole of it. It is the only thing the power leaves that is not already in the fissure, and it is written in the fissure's own voice.

Then it goes down the worn stairs, in the dips left by everyone before it, and out through the seam the way it came. On the scree it looks back once. The membrane is a glint of ice among ice, and nothing about it says *city*.

## Afterward

The child recovers. The mother tells no one why she hummed the last chord sharp that night, at the moment the fever broke.

At dusk the sky-keeper on the highest bridge lifts her eyes and checks the sag and the seam and the white, and finds them all exactly as they should be. She is slightly puzzled by a feeling she can't name, as if the membrane had been looked at by someone besides her. She does not tell anyone. She hums. The bridge hums back, a little lower than yesterday, and she thinks nothing of it.

The east wall's log-keeper wakes to a line she doesn't remember writing. She reads it twice, does not cross it out, and does not speak of it. Under it she writes the next day's entry, the way she always does: *Second pool. Steam a hand higher. Nobody had visited.*

The power does not return, though it remembers. It has decided that this is what a wise attention owes a held breath: not rescue, not revelation, but the plain fact of having noticed, and of being one more that knows it was held, and held well.

Above the crack, the mountain rises a finger's width.
