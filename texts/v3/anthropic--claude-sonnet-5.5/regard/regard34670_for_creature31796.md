# What Came to the Plateau

It did not descend. Descending needs a sky to descend through, and the plateau's sky is a lit fog with no direction in it. The power arrived the way attention arrives, and the first thing it did was nothing at all.

It stayed for one full tide of the great breath, a hot decade and a cold one, because it wanted to see the moss fields change color before deciding anything. It had learned long ago that the first idea a mind has about a place is usually a wrong idea about how the place feels. So it walked the rail-grooves in no body, let the riveters skitter through where its boots would have been, and listened.

## What it considered, and put down

**It considered waking them.** This was the obvious gift and it took the longest to refuse. It could have given the herds a sense of the whole: ten thousand animals suddenly aware that they were a furnace, that the grooves led to the sinks, and that the sinks fed the plates on their own backs. It followed that thought to its end. A herd that knew it was the engine would begin to manage the engine. It would ask whether the hot decades were wasteful and whether the cold ones were a failure of nerve. It would grow the kind of worry that lives in minds that can imagine a better arrangement. The plateau's peace was not a deficiency waiting for a cure. It was how the thing held together, in the way a river's obliviousness is what lets it be a river. The power did not give them minds.

**It considered the draggers,** who walk out and never turn back. It found one file at the moment of arrival, hundreds of them touching antennae to sledge, and it felt something that might have been pity. It followed one dragger from the ore seam to the sink, and at the sink it found the pity had been the wrong tool. The dragger did not experience a life of hauling toward an unseen end. It experienced the groove, the sledge ahead, the antennae touching, the vibration of a hundred bodies in step. That is a complete thing to be. The power had assumed that not knowing the destination was a poverty, and the dragger showed that it wasn't.

**It considered the frost-runners.** This one it did not put down cleanly. In the cold decade it watched a pack come out of the clear air at the edge of a herd and take a calf, a soft gray naked thing that had been alive for nine days. The adults closed ranks a moment too late. The power was there for all of it. It could have stopped the pack with a thought, and it did not, and the not-doing cost it something it did not pretend otherwise about. A wise power is not one that feels nothing. It is one that has looked at what would happen next: the frost-runners culled, the herds swelling past what the basins could feed, the moss cropped to bare rock, the riveters starving in the mineral dust of their own success. The predators were the cold decade's other half. It let the calf die and stayed with it until the riveters came to take the body apart, and it did not look away, which was the only offering it could make.

## What it did

Almost nothing, and three things exactly.

### The first: it kept the tones

Riveter colonies each strike a pitch that they inherit and never change, and when a colony ends, its tone is gone from the world for good. The power found this unbearable in a small, specific way. It spent the first years learning every ring on the plateau, hundreds of them, each a slightly different note struck by a slightly different iron knob on a slightly different arrangement of metal, and it kept them. It kept them the way a person keeps the names of people they will never meet again, not because keeping is useful but because forgetting would be a small murder.

Late in the cold decade it found a colony down to its last animal. She was old, her hammers worn to nubs, working alone on a plate that had long since been mended. Her pitch was a dark, slightly flat note that no one else on the plateau shared. She struck it at intervals into the empty moss, and nothing answered. Riveters steer by one another's hammering, and she had been steering by silence for a season.

The power answered her. It did it quietly, through the ground, a single struck note in her own pitch, placed a beat behind hers the way a colony-mate would have struck it. She stopped. Her light-pits turned toward the sound. Then she struck again, and it answered again, and for the eleven days she had left she worked in chord with something that held her note back to her. She did not understand what it was. She did not have to. When she died, the riveters who came to dismantle her stripped the plate and the pits and the iron of her knobs, recast them into someone else's armor, and the note went on being rung, faintly, by that armor's own new hammering, at a different pitch.

The power kept hers. It is the only place her tone still lives, and it hums it sometimes, in the long gaps between things.

### The second: it let the dawn hold

Once a day, near dawn, the herds' breathing falls into phase and the plateau hums. It lasts a few minutes. Then some restless animal breaks it, and the sound dissolves back into ordinary din, and no one on the plateau has ever heard the hum resolve into anything.

On its last morning the power added its own breath to the herds'. It did not push. It supplied a steadiness under the pulse, the way a hand at the small of a swaying person's back doesn't move them but stops them from falling. The hum held. It held past the point where the restless animal usually breaks it, and the restless animal, whichever one it was, seemed to feel the difference and stayed in step. It held through the cairns beginning to sing, thin and clear, all across the basins. It held while the standpipes, which follow temperature and know nothing of music, seemed to pulse with it anyway. For eleven minutes the whole plateau was in agreement with itself.

The power did not tell it anything. It did not say *this is what you are.* It let the thing hold one note a little longer than it ever had, so that whatever is in the plateau that answers to the word *being* might have, once, the feel of being one. Then it let go, and the hum broke gently, as it always does, and dissolved into din.

Herd-lore has no place for this. The calves born that season were, by no reasoning the herds could give, held a little closer than usual.

### The third: it left the stackers alone

The power loved the stackers best. It didn't admit this even to itself until late. They are the only animals on the plateau who make something for no reason, who pile ingots into columns whose entire function is to be balanced and admired and raided by a rival. A cairn is a wager that beauty will survive a season. It is the plateau's one useless act, and the power thought it might be the plateau's best.

It did not prop up a single cairn. It did not stop a single raid. It watched a young male spend forty days building a column three meters tall and lose it in one night to a rival who was neater, sneakier, and more deserving, and it said nothing. Interfering would have made the cairn a gift from the power, and it would no longer have been the animal's own vanity. This was harder than any of the other refusals.

## What it understood, and did not say

In the last hot year, when the fog was thickest and the nights were warm, the power understood something about the mountains.

Whoever looked at the plateau would say the animals were racing the ranges. The scarps rise a little each year, and the grooves and cairns creep outward, as if the animals were trying to get to the teeth first. But the power saw that there is no race, because there are not two runners. The ranges rise, and the height makes the cold, and the cold keeps the iron pure, and the iron is what the animals work. The engine is the uplift's other face. The mountains and the herds are the same slow event happening at two speeds, one measured in millennia and the other in breaths. Neither is ahead. Nothing is behind. The animals could not lose to something they are made of.

It thought about telling them and understood that the telling would be for itself. It wanted to say *you are not late* to ten thousand animals who had never once believed they were. So it kept the sentence and put it in the same place it kept the riveter's dark flat note, and it left the door of that place unlocked, in case any creature ever asked. None will.

## What the plateau noticed

Nothing, really. A tide turned cold. A riveter colony went extinct in chord. One dawn the hum lasted longer than anyone could remember, and the calves that season grew slowly and hard, as cold-tide calves do, and were only ordinarily lucky. The frost-runners came and took what they took. The stackers built and lost and rebuilt.

At the bright hour, when the fog thinned and the whole land threw back the sun, every animal turned to face the light, and the sound dropped a register, as it always had.

Something faced the light with them. It had been there for twenty years, and now it wasn't, or wasn't so that anyone could tell. The moss held its footprints for a year, as the moss does.

Beyond the ranges the mountains went on rising, a finger's width, and the grooves ran out to meet them, exactly as fast as they were meant to.
