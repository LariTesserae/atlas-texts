# What the Rim Heard

It arrived as weather, because weather was the only arrival the Kiln would not notice.

It was very old and very large, and it had been given, by whatever gives such things, a long patience and a wish that nothing be harmed. It had seen many worlds. Most were loud with wanting, and it had learned to be careful there, since a power that arrives wanting to help is often the most dangerous guest. So before it did anything on the Upper Kiln it did the one thing it was sure of. It slowed down.

It slowed until its thoughts moved at the pace of the beam-engines, one stroke a minute, and then slower, until it could follow a dust-front from birth to dissolution without impatience. It took no body. It thinned itself into the high amber air on the sunward side and drifted across the tableland for most of a season, the way a good walker would. It watched the grinders file the ridges, the sailwings wheel above the vent-stacks, and the mites graze the varnish one cell at a time. It listened to the bells count the storms. It was quite a while before it let itself have an opinion.

## What it did not do

The first temptation was water. The plateau was thirsty, and the power could have laid a season of rain across it in an afternoon. It considered this for a full week and saw what would follow. The varnish would bloom into something that was not varnish. The salts would drown, and the dew-pans would become ordinary ponds with ordinary shores. The truce at the pans, which is the closest thing the Kiln has to a law, rests on thirst that everyone shares, an hour that belongs to nobody because everybody needs it. *A truce made of scarcity*, the power thought, *is not improved by plenty.* It left the pans as they were.

The second temptation was to give. It could have taught the builders things: better engines, a medicine for the lung-scale that took their elders in their sixties, the names of the stars behind the amber. It watched them instead. It watched a builder stop half a pace before a grinder came around a corner, without looking. It watched a hand answer a question that was never spoken aloud. It saw that they had spent a thousand generations learning to attend to each other and to the animals, and that a gift from the sky would teach them to attend to the sky instead. They might do well at it. But something would be lost between the lanes, and the power could not tell how much.

The third temptation was to soften things. It saw a young warden take a lame grinder at the herd's margin, and the quiet of it, and the tinkers arriving afterward like a slow tide. It saw a sailwing fold at the top of the wheel and fall turning over the threshing-floors while the workers stopped and laid two fingers to their breastbones. It could have prevented the hunt. It could have kept the sailwing aloft another century. But it understood that the hunt and the falling were part of what the two-finger gesture meant, and that the grief the sailwings felt on the engine-house roofs was not a flaw in the world. It was how the world stayed close to its own machinery.

It did not chisel down the wall either. It watched one grinder stand for six days with its jaw against new masonry, and it wanted to help, and it did not. On the seventh morning someone came with a chisel, as someone always does.

It did permit itself one small liberty. On a dew-night it drifted low over the largest pan and let itself be, for an hour, one more shape in the line. It cannot be said that anything noticed. But the grinder nearest it moved a little to one side, an eighth of a step, the way you move for a neighbor you don't know but expect to see again. The power carried that eighth of a step with it for a long time.

## The disc

Then it went to the center.

It came at night, when the animals were far off, and it settled against the great dark disc as mist settles on a stone. The disc was warm, as everyone said. The power let itself feel the warmth, and slowly, over several nights, it began to understand.

The disc was not a monument, and it was not a being in the way the builders were beings. It was more like a held breath. The Kiln, the whole plateau with its grinders and its cords, its wind-sung pillars and its minute, ticking mites, was a mind of a kind the power had met only twice before. It did not think in one place. It thought by moving. The herd's long sentence across the Combed Country was a thought. The knots in the deepest rooms were a thought. Every tea-fall, every dew-night, every hammer stroke on the down-beat was a small motion in one enormous, unhurried deliberation that had been going on since before the wind took its present direction.

The disc was where that mind gathered itself. Once a season, when the light went clear, it drew all its parts to one place and became for a few hours a single attention, an *I* with a ring of bodies around it. And in that hour it said the only thing it had ever said.

*Still here.*

The power lay against the stone and listened to the whole history of that sentence. It had been said at every turning for longer than the builders had cords to count. It was not addressed to the animals, who already knew. It was not a prayer, because nothing on the Kiln had a god. The disc was polished as smooth as a lens, and it was pointed at the sky. The Kiln had been speaking upward into the dark all this time, softly, the way a plant faces the light, without hope or expectation, having no idea there could be such a thing as an answer.

The power was quiet for a long while after that. Then it was troubled, which it had not expected.

If it answered, the Kiln would learn that it was not alone. That is a large thing to learn. It might turn the Kiln's slow attention away from its stones and toward the sky, and a mind that has spent a million years looking down into a ridge does not necessarily grow by looking up. The Kiln's whole contentment was the contentment of a thing that had never been compared. An answer would be a comparison. It might be the end of a certain innocence.

The power did not decide quickly. It had no right to. It waited, and while it waited it kept the Kiln's own grammar in mind: *some hours belong to nobody.* It asked itself whether the Kiln had ever been offered a choice. It had not. But the power saw at last that there was a way to offer one. It could give the smallest possible answer, in the Kiln's own language, and leave the Kiln free to make of it whatever it liked, or nothing.

## The turning

The fronts thinned. The light went clear, and the sky showed its brief pale blue, and the plateau began to walk.

The power went with it. It settled into the tail of the last amber front and drifted toward the rim among the herds. It was not a shape, but the animals made room anyway, the way the grinder at the pan had. Sailwings settled on the backs of the herd. Wardens stood among the grinders. The tinkers spread across the pale rock like a second, darker floor. Builders stood shoulder to warm shoulder with beasts they had known all their lives, and the engines wound down in every town, and the quiet grew large.

The power took a place in the ring, near the edge, next to an old grinder with a bloom of violet along its belly. It faced in, as everyone did. For hours it did nothing, and did it very well.

Near the end, when the animals were beginning to shift their weight toward turning, the power did what grinders do. It pressed a very low note into the ground. It was not loud, and it was not the chord of the pillars, which would have been a spectacle. It was the hum the herd makes at the pans in the hour before the damp, the one sound on the Kiln that means *we are here, and it is enough.* The power gave it to the disc. It did not say *I am here*, or *you are not alone*, or any of the things it might have said. It said, in the only words the Kiln would recognize:

*Heard.*

For a moment nothing happened. The power waited, ready to withdraw if the Kiln flinched.

Then the disc warmed by less than a degree, for less than a breath, the way a sleeper stirs when someone speaks their name from another room.

Every grinder in the ring lifted its jaw from the ground at the same instant. The sailwings tilted, all together, a rustle of tea-colored light. The tinkers stopped their ticking, which they had never done, and for one breath the silence on the rim was total, the dead-still quiet of the plateau listening to itself. Then the ticking came back, and it was a little different, a shade softer, like a room after good news.

Not one builder spoke. But several of them put a hand flat against the sternum, though no beam-engine was running, though there was no thud to feel.

The old grinder with the violet bloom turned its lidded face, slowly, toward the place at the edge of the ring where the power stood. It looked there one beat longer than the turning required. Then it turned with the others and walked back into the Combed Country, and by nightfall the rim was empty.

## After

The power did not stay long. It had said what it came to say, and it did not want to become a fixture. It lifted from the plateau on the high air, as thinly as it had come.

In the nearest town, an old cord-keeper sat with the storm-cord in her lap. She was a plain woman, respected and not admired, who had recorded nine hundred dew-nights and forty-one tea-falls and every turning of her life. She did not know what she had felt at the rim. But she had felt it, and it had no category. There is a cord for births and a cord for the dead and a cord for storms, and nothing in the practice of her craft for an event that has no name yet.

She tied a single knot in the storm-cord, since that is where things go that are not yet understood. She tied it differently from the others, looser, with an open loop at the top. Then she hung the cord in the deep room with the rest, and when the wind rose in the pillars and the cords swayed, that knot swayed with them, a little apart.

The power came back at the next turning, and the one after, and the one after that. It always stood at the edge of the ring, next to whoever made room. It gave the same low note and got the same small warmth in return. Neither of them ever added a word. The power understood at last what it had been circling since it arrived. A wise power is mostly a good neighbor to something much slower than itself. It comes when it can. It gives what was asked for, even when nothing was asked in words. And it leaves the world exactly as unfinished as it found it, because the unfinishedness was never a fault. It was where everyone lived.

The engines rise and fall. The bells count the storms. In the old towns, in the deepest rooms, one knot with an open loop sways a little apart from the rest.

The builders will tell you, if you ask, that the Kiln has no one living on it but the Kiln. They say it lightly, and they lower their voices, and they look toward the center. Lately some of them have started to look up as well, only for a moment, and only at the turning, when the sky goes pale. It isn't a request or a prayer. They look the way you glance at a neighbor's window to see whether the light is on.
