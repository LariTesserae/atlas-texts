# What Was Done at Harrow Lock

## The noticing

It did not descend. Its regard passes over a great many places without weight, and this one it found it could not easily lift away from. The place was not grand. What held it was the thudding: sixty people had built their whole lives around a held breath that was never released, and had learned to sleep inside it.

It looked for a long time before it did anything. It looked at the brown ridge of river standing above the roofs, at the bowl below, and at the arithmetic between them, which had never been in the Lock's favor. It read the ledgers, all twelve hands of them. In a hundred years no one had written a sentence of triumph. There was only *Number 2 bearing replaced. Screens cleared. Wall dry.*

*Wall dry.* It thought this might be the most complete sentence it had ever met.

## What it weighed

It could have lifted the river, or raised the bowl, or made the wall stand forever. It considered each and set each down. It had no rule against helping, but it could see what each gift would dissolve. The Lock's joke is that the cutters never finish and that this is the job. To end the war at the speed of a fingernail would be to hand a finished world to people whose whole tenderness is in the unfinishing. They would be safe, and they would no longer be the Lock.

It also did not mistake restraint for virtue. A power that only ever declines to act is not wise, only tidy. So it asked the better question: not *may I?* but *what would they ask for, if asked slowly and at their best?* They had already answered it in every sign they made. They did not want rescue. They wanted company in the work, and more time.

## The season

So it took a body. It came in the dry month along the buried tram rails, a plain figure with a plain bag, and said it could cut. They tested it as they would anyone, with a machete and a place at the far end of the line. It cut badly for a week and then not badly. It took a crescent scar on the left forearm, and a child inspected it and approved.

It learned the hand-talk. It learned the sign for the pumps and the sign for the river, and it learned that *the water rising* and *be patient* were one gesture, the flat hand pressed slowly down. It understood that this was the closest thing they had to theology, and that it was sound. Around the third week it stopped wishing the cutting would end.

It spent evenings in the switch room with the keeper, who sat among the humming transformers with a book and a kettle and did not ask who it was. On the ninth evening she poured a second cup without looking up.

"You going to stop them?" she said, meaning the pumps.

"No."

"Good. We'd not sleep."

After a while she said, "How long, d'you reckon? Honestly."

"Longer than you."

She lifted one shoulder and tilted her head toward the river. It was the shrug, and it was also the answer. She went back to her book.

## What it did

**The wall.** On the dark side of the third bastion, under the clay, an old drain timber had rotted to meet a burrow, and a hollow was forming that would have wept in a year or two and run in three. The wall crew would have found it the way they found everything, late, by a stain. It mended the hollow by night, the way they would have. It puddled the clay, laid the turf in the crew's own herringbone, and staked it with green bamboo. It left tool marks that were theirs. In the morning the wall crew found the repair and argued for a day about which crew had made it. They settled on old Dov's crew, before the last raising, forgotten and unlogged. It let them.

**The sinking.** It could not undo the sinking, because the bowl is what it is. But far beneath the delta the silt was settling under its own weight, and it eased that settling by a tenth. A tenth is a few grains, and no ledger will show it in a lifetime. In a hundred years the wall will need raising a little less often. *Longer than you* became a little longer, and nobody will know why.

**The rails.** It walked the buried tram line out to its far end, where the delta lifts to an old low rise. It made sure the ground there was dry, the bed sound, and the way open under the moss. It told no one. It did not intend an escape or an order to leave, only a door in the wall left unlocked, so that whoever comes to the last generation finds the ending can be a departure and not a drowning.

**The hearing.** It did not touch anyone's hearing. The old lose the top of their range and gain an ear for thirst, and Tamsin Oke can tell from the doorway that Old Meg is running warm. That is a trade the Lock made honestly over a century, and it did not want to undo it.

**The frogs.** On its last night it sat by the canal and, for a while, listened below the pumps, in the register where the works had no purchase. There the frogs were singing, the whole canal of them, patiently and in full voice, as they had every night for a century, unheard. It listened until it had heard all of it. It told no one, because not everything a witness hears needs reporting. The heron, on the outfall wall, turned its head once.

**The bearing.** From the scrap bin it took a worn bearing, ordinary and rusted, and chalked a date on it, the day it had come. It hung the bearing on a nail among the hundreds. A week later Tamsin Oke found it, ran her thumb over the chalk, and said nothing. In the ledger, in her square hand, she wrote: *Bearing hung. Not ours. Left.*

## Departure

It left the way it had come, along the rails, in the gray before the cutters went out. Behind it the thudding followed it a long way, farther than it expected, through the ground and up through its feet. When at last the sound fell below what any body could hear, it stopped and stood a moment.

It had wondered, all season, whether it had done too little. It concluded that a place held together by hands should not be held together by a hand, but that a hand could stand beside the hands, and that this was neither nothing nor everything. It was a repaired wall, a tenth of a grain, a road left open, and a cup of tea.

Then it understood what the villagers meant. Away from the sound, it felt the silence come up around it, wide and attentive, and it woke. It listened, and far behind it the water sat against its wall and thought about coming in. Under that, faint and steady in a hundred beds, sixty people slept.

In the ledger, on the last page written that month, below Tamsin's line, is one more entry in a thirteenth hand. Nobody remembers it being written.

*Wall dry.*
