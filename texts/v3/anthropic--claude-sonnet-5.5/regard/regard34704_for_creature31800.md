# The Visitor at the Eleventh Minute

It did not arrive, because arriving is for things that were somewhere else. It came the way the cloud deck comes each morning, from below and without ceremony, taking the lowest districts first and rising through them as drizzle, and by the time anyone might have noticed, it had been there a while.

What drew it was not the engines, or the height, or the green glass roofs burning in the thin light. It had seen greater machines, and stranger heights. It was the pause. Across ten thousand terraces, at the same moment, hands stopped on trowels and ladles and children's hair, and let something pass through them. Then they went on. It had watched a great many worlds, and it had rarely seen a whole people stop together for no reason but courtesy.

So it stayed, and it weighed what it might do. It was a benevolent power, and benevolence, examined honestly, is mostly a list of things one must not do.

## What it set down

**It could thin the air.** The Stackfolk gasp on the way up from the cloud towns, and their own children come into the world barrel-ribbed and slow on the flat, having paid for the altitude in advance. It could make the breathing easy. But the long sigh, the chest, and the word *still-lunged* are the shape their long argument with the mountain has taken. To end the argument by decree would be to answer a question no one had asked in a language no one spoke.

**It could finish the walls.** One afternoon's work would do it: every joint sound, every crack closed, every conduit new. The Repointers would wake to a mountain that no longer needed them. They are the most contented people in the city, and they are contented precisely because the work does not end and they know it. This was the gift it wanted most to give, since it could see the rope-burns and the bad knees, and it was the one it understood most clearly to be a theft.

**It could end the dying.** It looked at this one longest. Then it watched a procession go down through the districts, each district stopping its hammers as the linen-wrapped body passed. It watched the Mixers receive her with something like tenderness, and a child ask which tree was her grandmother, and the grown-ups say *the bananas, probably, she was always hungry.* Everyone laughed. It was all right. The power was not certain it could improve on a grief that had learned to laugh.

**It could tell them about the rock.** Beneath the terraces is stone that no one living has seen, the thing they keep faith with. It could have said what it was, how old, what shape. But they do not keep faith with the rock. They keep faith with the idea of it, and the idea has held up five thousand meters of stairs and gardens for centuries. The rock is fine. It doesn't need visitors.

So it did not make the mountain easy, or finished, or deathless, or known. This did not feel like restraint. The mountain already had what the power might have brought: attention, mutual obligation, the daily correction of small errors, a shared breath. It was a thing that already knew how to be cared for.

## What it did instead

It was not a stone, and it did not pretend to be one.

**The crack.** One stretch of wall above a stair-street, where children ran to school, held a crack that a Repointer named Hessa Dorne had said for forty years was "thinking about it." That season it stopped thinking. The power could see the moment coming, weeks off, on a wet morning at the hour the children passed. It did not mend the crack. It did not move a single grain of lime. On a rest-day, Hessa woke with an itch she couldn't place, ate her breakfast standing, and found herself walking her stretch for no reason. She laid her palm on the wall. It was warm in the wrong way. She said nothing to anyone, mixed a bucket, and packed the crack from a rope until noon, muttering, and the children went to school under it for another thirty years.

She never learned why she had gone. The power counted it as one mercy, not a policy, and it knew the difference between the two. There are other cracks, other ropes, and other mornings it did not touch, and it held that unfairness openly. To become the mountain's guardian would have made it the one thing the mountain lacked, an owner. Water is not owned here, but its order is.

**The bees.** It went to the hives in the wall-niches, because that is where news is taken in the Stack, and it told them who it was. Of all the residents, the bees alone were unsurprised. They were older than the terraces, and they had been told stranger things by stranger visitors. The family that kept that hive reported only that the honey that summer tasted, faintly, of a flower none of them could name, one that did not grow on the terrace above. They sold it in the small ceramic thimbles at the usual price.

**The Bellows-keeper.** In the end it went to the Slow Vents, where an old woman named Ilvane Roh sat at the lip of a great stone mouth with a kettle on a warm flue. She was one of the few who kept the eleven-minute count without a watch. The power sat down beside her, as much as it could be said to sit, and asked in whatever way it could what the mountain was doing.

She did not answer. Nobody had ever gotten an answer out of a Bellows-keeper, and she wasn't going to start. The vent exhaled, a huge low breath felt more than heard, and she laid her palm flat on the stone. The vent drew in. She poured something dark, smoky, and faintly sweet into a second cup and passed it over, and they sat through three breaths, and then a fourth.

Somewhere in the fourth, the power understood that it had been answered. The mountain was breathing, and the ten thousand terraces breathed with it, and nobody found this remarkable. They had made it a courtesy. They did not need the mountain to be explained. They needed it to be *kept*. And the keeping was not something to be given to them but something to be joined.

At the eleventh minute the stone exhaled. The power, which had never needed to, took a breath. Ilvane Roh did not turn her head, but she nodded, barely, as one nods to a neighbor across a street.

## The joint

Before it left, it borrowed a trowel from a hook on a Repointer's ladder and, hanging from a rope for the length of one afternoon, mended a single joint on a wall in the mid-levels. It was not a special wall. It was a good joint, pointed with lime from the nearest bucket, and no one saw it done. Nobody checked the ledger, because there was no ledger. There were only stretches, and each stretch had its keeper, and the keeper of this one came back to it the next morning, found a joint that was somehow already sound, and thought nothing of it.

In sixty years the joint will weather, and someone will rake it out and set it again. The old mortar will go down in a basket to the lower districts, where the leftovers of the highest are built into the lowest. In time it will be crushed, mixed, and buried in the loam, and a tree will be planted in it. A child will ask whose tree it is.

*The bananas, probably,* someone will say. *It was always hungry.*

It left with the evening, going down as the cloud deck rolled back up over the lower terraces and the frogs began in the cisterns. For that one hour no one spoke over them. The foundry-hands and the Cup-bearers and the Repointers on their ropes went quiet, the way you go quiet beside a large animal. Then the lamps came on, each district a shade warmer than the one above, and people began, bit by bit, to talk again.

The Stack went on, warm and green and unfinished.

At the eleventh minute the great stone mouths exhaled, and a low breath moved through the walls and the ferns and the sleeping conduits, and across ten thousand terraces people paused, a hand on a trowel or a ladle or a child's hair, and let it pass through them.

If you had been very still, and very lucky, you might have felt the breath pass through something else as well, something that had come to give and had stayed to be given to.

Then they went on.
