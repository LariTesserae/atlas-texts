# What Came to the Flats

It did not arrive. Arriving needs a direction, and the flats have only one, downstream. It was simply, at some point, attending, the way the light is attending.

For a long time it did nothing. It learned the rain: here at the ninth terrace, there at the fourteenth, always on the same fine curtain of hours. It learned the slow tick of the gantry hoist and the pulse of the wheels, a little slower than a heart. It stood in a channel among the combs, and it let itself be counted.

The count was gentle. A tender paused at the place where it stood and chose to go around. A comb leaned an inch toward it. On the nearest gantry a roost's head turned one degree. Then, silently, a bird at the far end of the span opened its wings and left, low over the water, toward no terrace in particular, and the row closed over the gap.

The power understood what it had seen. The flats could not take in one more without giving up one. The ledger had no column for a guest, and the balance, warm and patient and without malice, had corrected for it at once. It had subtracted a roost.

So it withdrew from the count, which took some care, and the bird came back. The gap opened again in the row and closed the other way. Nothing had been harmed. But the power had learned that in this place even attention has a price.

## What it thought

It considered the flats honestly, because it was wise, and wisdom here mostly meant refusing the obvious.

The obvious was pity. Here was a place with no outside, made for a purpose no one remembered, turning the same water through the same bodies until the bodies became the floor. A thousand years standing in one place would leave you a graze-around, an entry. It would be easy to look at that and call it a cage, and then to break it. Open the walls, pour in weather and seed, wake the tally from its arithmetic.

But the power looked for suffering and did not find it. The tenders slept in stacks, and the ones on the bottom worked their way up. The combs flushed pink down the beds, telling one another *richer, thinner, cooling, clearing*, and that was a whole and sufficient conversation. The dead held up the living and were not lonely in the stone. The roosts were patient, not bored. Even the balance, which was the nearest thing here to a single being, had never been told it was alone, and so was not.

It decided that a mercy the flats had not asked for would be a theft with good manners. It set aside rescue, and it set aside repair. It did not raise a hand against the stillness, because the stillness was the place's own work, and the work was good.

There was still one thing, and it was small.

## The top of the staircase

It went up. It went the way the larvae come down, in reverse, terrace after terrace, past bed after bed of strangers who would be ancestors of strangers. The beads thickened on the walls until they were fat as forearms, a gray ledger of days. The water warmed and worked itself less. At the last, the cloud roof came lower and the wheels grew fewer, and it stood on the first terrace beside the first sluice-wheel.

It was smaller than expected. The wheel was the size of a house, not a hill. Its axle was worn bright by an unimaginable number of turns. Nothing about it was grand, and the power found that moving.

On the first lip, where the stone goes over into the second terrace, there was a thumbprint.

It was pressed into the cast stone in the first pour, when the cement was still soft: a plain human whorl, a little smeared, made by someone who had leaned on the edge for a moment to look down at what they had made. In all the crowded seasons since, the combs had never healed it. They had never crowded to it. They leaned around it and left a margin of bare stone the width of a hand. Perhaps they took it for a wound too old to close. Perhaps it was the one place the balance had never learned to count.

The power knew whose it was. It could have said. It could have put the maker's name into the pink ripples of the beds, so that every comb down the staircase learned that they had once been made by someone, and that the someone was gone.

It did not. A maker is a reason, and a reason is a weight. The flats did not need one and had not been carrying one. Grief given to a creature that had none would be a change, and it would not be a gift. So the thumbprint stayed, and the power made sure it would stay. That was the only thing it deliberately left alone.

## The gift

What it did was this.

Above the first terrace the cloud canopy is thinnest, ragged at the base and flat at the top, and it made a slit in it. The slit was narrow, a few hundred meters long and no wider than a road. Through it, for one hour, it let a wind come down.

The flats had never had wind. This was cool, dry, and a little sharp, air from somewhere that was not warm and not worked. It smelled of nothing the flats had ever contained. It came down the staircase the way water does, terrace after terrace, shivering the sheen at each lip.

The combs felt it first. All down the beds they leaned against their whole lives' lean, upstream, into the cold. The flush went through them the wrong way, pink rising up the staircase in long diagonals. It carried no message about the water, because the wind was not water, and for the first time the beds were saying a thing that had no word. The tenders stopped grazing all at once. The threaders' braids in the culverts knotted and stilled. On every gantry, on every rail, on every span, the roosts lifted their heads, which had faced along the rail toward the next terrace since the last pour, and turned them up toward the seam of pale blue in the cloud.

They stayed that way for the hour.

Then the wind went out. The power did not seal the slit. It made the slit exactly as large as the combs could close, if they chose, in a season. Combs crowd to wounds. That is their nature, and the power did not overrule it. It only made the wound one the flats could heal or leave, as they saw fit. The gift was built to be refusable. That was the whole design.

It did not stay to see what they chose. Watching is a way of insisting, and it did not want to insist.

## What is left

No one on the flats can say what happened afterward. The accounts disagree.

Some say the combs closed the slit within a season and the cloud has been flat-topped and unbroken ever since, and that the roosts went back to the rail. Others say that on the first terrace there is a small place where it rains at an unscheduled hour, and that a tender or two go there to graze in it, and that the count of roosts is off by one and has been for a very long time, and that the flats have learned to live with it.

But every account agrees on one thing. On the day of the wind, every bead on every wall laid down its ring as it always does, one ring, one day. That ring is paler than the rest, and thinner, and slightly cold in the grain. It sits in each wall like a held breath, one line in a hundred thousand.

If anyone ever cuts a wall open, ten thousand years from now, in a country the flats have not yet become, they will find the ledger of days, gray on gray, unbroken. They will find that one line and wonder at it. And they may guess, if they are wise, that on that day something was offered to the place. It was free to say no, and it was never told what saying yes would mean.
