# What Came to the Sump Flats

It did not come as light.

It had seen the place from far off, and in the first moment it understood that light was the one thing it must not bring. Nothing in the Sump had ever been lit. To be lit is to be found out, and the place had never needed to be found. So the first thing the visitor did was decline most of what it was.

It came down through the layers of itself until it was about the size and temperature of something that could sit on basalt. It was wet and warm and a little clumsy, and it left its edges soft on purpose so the clickers could draw it badly.

They drew it. The drawing went from rafter to rafter, gantry to pipe, pipe to cliff, and for the first time the map held a mark that was large and very quiet, and did not know what to do with it. The waders assembled in the shallows and turned their smooth faces toward it. The distances arranged themselves around it as a crowd does around something too big to be hurried. The pump house, which should have been ten minutes off, took days to reach. The visitor did not mind. Hurry was one of the things it had left at the door.

## Counting

At first it tried to count them, as anyone would. It failed, as anyone does, and it understood what the failure meant. The being it had come to see was not a crawler or a coil or a wader. It was the density, the one slow organism with many mouths, for whom the species were only places where the warmth had taken a shape. There was no one to address. So it did the only courteous thing, which was to stop trying to be introduced.

It lay down on a mud terrace with the polygon rims lifting around it. After a long while, something small and slate-backed came and pressed its length against its side to find out whether it was stone.

It let itself be stone. This was the first act of benevolence and perhaps the truest one: to be, for a season of the water, a thing that asked nothing.

## What it heard

Lying there, it listened as the waders listen, through the ground. It heard the water table shifting and the pipe knocking. It heard the fraying and it heard the heat.

The fraying was not sickness. It heard that at once. The Sump was a young place that had not finished agreeing with itself. The distances that lied, the pools that tilted, and the echoes that arrived early were what a geometry sounds like while its residents are still negotiating it. Every hesitation was a kind of manners. The visitor could have straightened all of it. It could have made the seeps run one way, leveled the pools, and given each shelf a fixed position. It considered this fully and saw where it led. The crowd would have no reason to arrange itself, the ticking would go quiet, and the map would become the map of a solid place. That place would be legible and safe and less than what was there now.

It left the slippage alone.

The heat was different. Under the eastern basins, a channel that fed the warm seeps was narrowing, mineral by mineral, and would seal in something like a hundred generations of stalks. When it sealed, the felt would go grey, the stalks would drop, the crawlers would tick softly over the last of them, and the Sump would cool into an ordinary cold cave.

The visitor did not make the place permanent. Permanence would have been a different place, a preserved one, and the Sump's whole character was that it was slow but not still. It reached down into the dark, found one stone in the channel, and moved it the width of a hand. The water found a new way through. Nothing announced it. The channel would now last several times as long as it would have, and no one would ever know it had been helped.

## The still ones

It went among the still ones, because they were the ones who had once been able to ask for something.

They sat in the deep crevices, furred with pale felt, crawlers layered against them, coils across their laps. The visitor laid a palm on each in turn and felt the slow pulse under the skin. Nearly all of them had settled into the knocking of the pipes, and nothing in them wanted anything. It thanked them in the only language they had left and went on.

One was different. Her pulse matched the pipes almost perfectly, but every so often there was a small stutter, a beat that came half a beat late and then rejoined. It was a habit of waiting, worn so deep that the body kept it after the mind had let go of what it was for.

The visitor sat with her for a full turn of the tide. It did not wake her. It slowed its own warmth to the pipes and then, very gently, put a stutter of its own into the rhythm, answering hers a half-beat late. She was no longer keeping the habit alone.

She opened her eyes. They were an old tool that had learned another trade, and they did not need light. She asked nothing.

It showed her the way out. It did not make it sound close or far. It only let her know that, at this moment, it was four steps from where she lay, and that the four steps would still be there if she took a very long time.

She considered it for the length of a tide.

Then, through the pulse, not the mouth, she said *not yet*. She asked for one thing. She asked that someone be told she was warm.

It carried that word out of the Sump and set it down where it was needed, at a door, before a person who had stopped laying out a second cup. It was told in a way that could be believed that she was warm, and unhurried, and not alone. Whether that was comfort or grief, the visitor did not presume to say.

## The breath

Last, it went to the generator hall and waited, because the hall was where the Sump breathed loudest.

When the long breath came, the turbines slowed and stopped. The pipes ran cold, and the swarm drew in from the dark. The visitor did not lead the breath or resist it. It set down, for that one inhalation, the last of its own edges and let the swarm gather around it. For the length of a held breath it was warm among warm things and was counted.

Then the Sump let go, and the turbine began to turn.

The visitor knew which way. In that moment it had the answer to the question the turbine had been asking since the Sump was raised. It kept the answer the way one keeps a friend's secret, from everyone including the friend, because the not-knowing was load-bearing. The Sump had made a home in the question. The visitor would not take away its roof.

## What it took

It took nothing. But before it left it did the one thing it could do that the Sump could not do for itself.

It remembered the place. It did not draw a map, since the clickers had already made one, better than any it could make. It held the Sump the way something is held that is loved: every terrace's sigh, every pump-house approach that ended in three sudden steps, the one dry animal in the switch room and its single forelimb, the black stalks leaning toward a hand and away again. It gave the place a witness who would not forget, so that when the channel finally sealed, centuries on, and the felt went grey and the last crawler pressed itself to the last cooling stone, the Sump would not be lost. It would be known, entire, by something patient enough to keep it.

That was the only permanence the visitor allowed itself to give, and it did not disturb a single thing.

## After

Nothing on the flats was visibly different. On a terrace a hundred meters from the pump house, a slate-backed crawler pressed itself against a patch of basalt that was faintly warmer than the stone around it. Nothing was there. It pressed a while, testing whether the warmth was stone, and then the warmth faded and it moved on.

Far out on the flats, a turbine turns once in a minute. Behind it the water table shifts, the pipes knock, and the clickers pass along their drawing of everything warm. The Sump breathes, in or out, and does not know which. Something that does know keeps the secret, and keeps the Sump.
