# What Came Down the Stair

It came the way visitors come, on foot, because the stair was made for feet and it saw no reason to insult the stair. It took a body for the purpose, an ordinary one, and let the heat do to it what heat does. By the fortieth step its shirt had given up. It put its weight into each dished bowl of warm water as everyone has, and did not hurry.

## The Sitting

At the bottom it found a dry lip of flowstone between two basins, with a tree fern at its back, and sat down through one whole breath.

This was the only way to learn anything. The place had no answers to give in less than a cycle, because the place was a cycle. It watched the shaft of light crawl and hand itself from rank to rank of the broad leaves. It learned the Whistlers by ear until it could tell where it sat in the sink by the choir around it. It felt the pressure build, the flywheel quicken, the pools rise a hand's width, and the sound thicken toward solid. Then it felt the exhalation in the soles of its feet, and after it the hush.

Somewhere in that hush it found it was being leaned toward, as everyone is. A small cold-white bulb had raised itself at the edge of its heel, beside a bead of clear sweet fluid. It let the bulb be.

## What It Weighed

There was nothing to rescue. It looked for the failing thing and found only things already being mended: a leaking seam packed with gray felt, a belt of root taking up its own slack. The fig in the engine-house roof was splitting the last course of brick, and in a century or two the walls would come down and the sinter would bury the wheel. But the sink had drowned and dug out its engines a hundred times. Burial was part of the breath, and it left the fig alone.

It thought longest about company. There were no animals, no wings in the spores, no small feet in the litter. It could seed frogs in the pools and beetles in the fronds, and the garden would fill.

But the emptiness was a visitor's feeling. The sink was not lonely, because loneliness needs a someone, and the sink was a passing. Animals would gnaw the white cords and tunnel the flowstone. They would need what the sink does not have, and in time they would turn a passing into a place with residents, and then into a place with owners.

The hardest question was whether to give it a mind. It could. It could gather the Whistlers and the Pale and the ferns and the slow wheel into a center, and the sink would wake as a body it had never been asked about. But the first thing a mind does with a body is want something for it. The peace here is a skill nobody in the sink knows it has, and it was not willing to trade that skill for the knowing.

It did not preserve the place either. Holding the sink still would stop the breath, and the breath is the whole of what it is.

So it decided against nearly everything, and was easy in that.

## What It Did

It did two small things.

The first was the chord. At the peak of each breath the Whistlers tune themselves upward together and agree on a single clear note over the disorder of the vents, for a few minutes, before the exhalation breaks it. The visitor had listened to that note through one peak and wanted to hear it once more, so it stayed for a second. When the leaves agreed, it added a voice beneath them, quietly. It was the one tone the vents lacked, and with it the dozen screaming bleeds, the pump-thud, the iron *whump* of the wheel, and the kettle-leaves stood together for a few minutes in a single resolved chord, like a room going silent because everyone in it has stopped at once.

Then the exhalation came, the note fell apart into falling ones as it always does, and the voice was withdrawn. The leaves turned as they would have turned. The cords warmed as they would have warmed. Nothing in the sink knew, and nothing could have. It was a gift with no recipient, and the visitor was honest with itself that it might have been for itself. A being that has seen a great many places is allowed one.

The second thing was a layer. As the pools sank from their rims, it leaned over a basin and let a film go down into the fresh mineral, thinner than paper, the way the place lays them. Into the film it put an account of what it had seen: that the wheel was well kept, that the mending was good, that the one who cut the stair was thanked, since someone should. No one will read it. If some far century a person cuts the flowstone with a fine saw and a lamp, they will find among the tide marks one layer very slightly the wrong color, the color of nothing, and take it for a season of unusual rain.

## The Way Up

The stair is the only part of the sink that faces outward. A film of green so thin it looks like shadow keeps the bowls wet, and it does so without any notion of why. That film is the closest thing the sink has to hospitality, and it is entirely accidental.

On the top step the visitor stopped and laid its palm flat in the last bowl, in the warm skin of water, and said thank you to the film of green. It felt foolish and did it anyway. Then it climbed out into the thin, silent, dry air with its ears ringing, and left the bowls exactly as it had found them, each holding its skin.

Below, the pressure had begun to gather again. Every leaf was a little turned, and every cord a little warmer, and the whole green place leaned toward the next breath, ready, as it had been before anyone came and as it would be after.
