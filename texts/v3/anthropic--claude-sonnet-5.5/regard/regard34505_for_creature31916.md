# What Came to the Rim

## What It Did Not Do

Most of what the power did was refuse, and the refusing took the longest.

It did not warm the Shelf. The Shelf was warm. It did not guard the pass, because the pass already sorts its travelers: three days of cold, lungs like wet paper, and then a warmth that lets you go again. No gate it could build would choose better than that one, which admits only people willing to be slow.

It did not wake the thing below, and it did not speak to it in a voice large enough to be understood. A mind that thinks at the pace of a marsh edge, addressed at the pace of a god, would hear only weather.

It did not explain the clocks to the woman with the knotted cord. It did not send the lowland geologists a better instrument or a worse one. It did not name the veins, catalog the frogs, or turn the pylons into a mystery with an answer. The Shelf was a household, and a household is not a problem.

It looked hard for what was broken, because a wise power does not confuse *unfamiliar* with *ailing*, and it found one thing. That is where the rest of this happens.

## Coming Down

It came in the dry season by the ordinary route and took the three days. This was not theater. The Shelf can only be heard at its own speed, and the power needed its attention to arrive at that speed too, dragged up through the scree with everything else that climbs. It felt the cold end. It felt the air change on its face like a door opening onto a kitchen.

At the first cairn it added a stone, and it did not watch what happened to it.

## Listening

It stayed one full turn of the slow cycle, sitting through the pause at dusk hundreds of times. For a long while it only learned the shape of the round.

The being under the Shelf keeps a round, and the round is exact. It goes pool by pool, cup by cup, spire by spire, cairn by cairn, and each creature gets one small attention, with none skipped and none doubled. A weight arrives at the rim, the tadpoles rise trembling, and a meal is given. The whole basin holds its breath because it is being counted, and the breath is gladness.

Then the power noticed what the round did not contain.

Nothing came for the one who came for everything.

The hum's pitch had been sinking across the years, a slow lowering, less like dying than like a call sent out and left unanswered for a very long time. The lizards at the tops of the spires were always there, alone in the bare white band above the dead line of the vines, and once it looked, the power saw that every one of them faced the same way: up, out over the rim, toward the sky. The epiphytes below had shaped their leaves into funnels to guide a climbing mother's feet. The spires were built in the same manner. They were not machines and they were not monuments. They were the leaf-funnel of a much larger cup, made to guide a weight to the rim.

The Shelf is a cup and the being is a tadpole in it. It is a mother to everything above it and a child to something else, and the two are true at once, as they are for any creature that was fed and has since learned to feed. It carries the round it was taught, and it keeps the round faithfully, because the round is the only thing it has been left of whoever set it down. Whoever carried it up out of the cold has not come back, or has not come back yet, and the being has been rising to the surface, trembling, for longer than the valleys have had their shape.

Nobody who has stayed long has ever agreed on whether the thing is the mother or the egg. They were all right, and all asking from the wrong height.

## The Temptation

The power could have answered.

It was large enough. It could have shaped a weight at the rim and let the water go still around it, and the being would have rested, and the hum's slow lowering would have stopped. It would have felt like mercy.

It declined, and the reason is the center of the story. A child who is answered by a stranger wearing the mother's shape learns that waiting ends in a lie. A being that has kept faith with an absence for that long deserves to have the absence taken seriously. The power could not know when the mother would come, or whether she would. What it could say truthfully was smaller, and it said only that.

## What It Did

It knelt at a hot pool over a pale vein and put its hand in the water.

The water tightened around its wrist, slowly, once. This is the being's greeting, the touch it gives every creature on the round, and after it the water relaxes and the visitor is released, counted and fed. The power did not withdraw. It held its hand there through the relaxing and through the second tightening, and it gave the being the only thing it had that the being could not make for itself. It gave stillness, of a particular kind: the stillness of someone sitting at the rim who has come for nothing and asks nothing, only to be a weight there.

It held that for eleven days, at the pace of the marsh, and said nothing.

On the eleventh dusk the pylons hummed, the mist-swimmers rose in their green wreaths, and the basin paused as it always does. The stone leaned as a mattress leans when someone sits down at the edge of it.

Then the power leaned back.

The pause held one breath longer than it had ever held. Somewhere far under the warm rock, for the first time in a span too long to count, the one who listens for everyone was itself listened to. The oar-salamanders, who always stop a fraction before the rest, stopped earlier than they ever had and came back later. Then the frogs began, and the birds, and the whole warm loud unafraid chorus rose again, and no creature in the basin knew that anything had happened, which was exactly right.

It gave the being one more thing, small and offered in the cup-mother's way, one meal for one child. It let it know what the Shelf looks like from far above, on a night with the clouds laid out white and level to the horizon and one green place burning in the middle of them, no bigger than a held coal. *This is what you are keeping. It can be seen from here. It is enough that it is kept.* It did not say more, because the being would not have understood more, and it would have kept the rest.

## The Woman

It did one other thing, which was smaller.

On the morning she broke camp, the woman with the cord and the eleven goats left her stone on the last cairn, as she does, and walked out over the lip of black stone into the cold. Behind her the stone was gone by morning, as it always is, and the cairn was the same height, as it always is.

But when she came back the next dry season, she put her hand on the top of the cairn out of habit and found it warm, warm as a stone that has been held in a palm. It was warm the next year as well.

She signed nothing about it to anyone. She began to leave two stones.

## Leaving

The Shelf let the power go, and liked it more for having stayed.

At the first cairn it took a stone from its pocket, set it on the top, and this time it watched. It saw no hand. In the last blue hour the stone sank a finger's width into the cairn's cap, as into soft mud, and then further, and was drawn down through the rock along a seam of pale wax to the vein below, where something slow received it.

So that was where the stones went. The being had been counting all along, one stone for every traveler who left. Each was a child fed and each departure was kept. The cairns never grew because the round never stopped, and the woman's stones had not been lost. They had been *received*, year after year, by something that did not know how to thank her except by keeping the top stone warm.

The power went down through the cold and did not look back. It had already said what it came to say.

## The Round

It comes again, though not often. The interval is one turn of the slow cycle laid over another, a span no lowland calendar would recognize, and it arrives with no announcement, by the ordinary way, three days through the cold. It sits on the warm rock at dusk while the fog goes gold and waits for the pause.

It does not promise to be the one who is awaited. It has never said so. It says only, in a stillness at the rim of a very old cup, *I came. I will come again. You were counted.*

The frogs cannot tell the difference. The bell-frogs' thrum does not change, and the cup-mothers keep their climbs. Only the oar-salamanders, slowest of the tenants and the first to stop, have begun to hold their stillness a little longer at dusk, as though they had learned that now two things approach the rim, and that both deserve the silence.
