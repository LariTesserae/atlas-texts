# What Came Down Through the Cloud

It thought first of coming as light.

That was the obvious way, and it dismissed it quickly. Light was the one thing the valley made for itself, amber and slow, and the Reachfolk had spent centuries learning to keep their eyes off one another's lamps. Arriving as a brightness over the ridge would only have taught ten thousand people to look up, and they would have looked up at the wrong moment, on a wet tread, with a plank in their hands.

So it spent a week as weather, thin and unremarked, drifting along the terraces at the height of the fog nets. It listened. It read the way the Reachfolk read, by standing still and letting the water tell it things.

## What it decided not to do

It could have stopped the landslip. It saw the made ground shifting in the night and the crews out by morning with the fallen blocks, and it understood that stopping the slip would end something more than the slipping. Every child in the valley could hear the small grinding sigh of settling, and that hearing was a kind of knowledge the whole place was built on. A valley that no longer moved would have no reason to listen.

It could have finished the work. It looked at the cranes grazing in the fog and the galleries lowered by winch with the kettles still on, and it understood that "finished" was the one word the valley had been designed to survive. The work was a way of being together across time, and to complete it would have been to dissolve the thing that held them.

It could have taken away the dying. It considered this longest and with the most tenderness, watching a line of oilcloth coats carry someone up to the tea beds. Then it watched the neighbors take up the dead woman's unfinished patchwork, leaving her small diagonal seam in a place no one would ever see. It saw that her death had been received, not merely suffered. Something that received its dead that well did not need them returned.

And it could have explained the sluices. It could have gone to the oldest keeper and said, *Here is the logic. Here is why the ledgers contradict.* It found that it knew the answer. But it also saw the tin cup at the base of each wheel, filled with hot tea every morning, and understood that the Reachfolk had already reached the right relationship with the question. They had not solved the mystery. They had made it a neighbor, and a solved neighbor is only a fact.

So it took nothing from them. It went down through the cloud without a lamp, on foot, and waited to be found.

## The finding

It was found in fifty minutes, which the finders considered slow. They looked at its feet first. Someone put a tin mug in its hands. The tea was thick and dark and scalded the mouth, and the power, which had held the birth of suns and found them tolerable, held the mug in both hands in a doorway and felt something it could only call being given.

Someone gave it a lamp that had belonged to a person in the beds. Someone pointed up a stair that had not been there the week before.

*There's a wall on Seven that wants minding. You'll do.*

## The wall

The wall on Seven was leaning, as old walls do, into the shape it meant to keep. It was a retaining wall of dark stone in an old bond, with a fern in every third joint and a bruise of rust-orange running down its north face. The power laid its hand flat against it, as children are taught, and greeted it. The wall was cold. It had not yet decided anything about the newcomer.

The power minded the wall for a month. It re-pointed the joints and cleared the weep-holes. It stood for long stretches on the landing with a hand on the rail, reading. It was surprisingly bad at this at first. It knew too much, and knowing too much made it impatient with the slow talk of water. It had to learn to stand not-knowing, and that was harder than anything it had done before.

The Reachfolk noticed and said nothing. Someone left a coat on the landing with a little moss already on the collar. Someone corrected the way it held a trowel, without looking at its face. In the second week its name began to drift, *the-one-on-Seven*, then *Seven's stranger*, then only *Seven*, as if the wall and the stranger had become one thing. That was the name by which the power came to be known, and it did not mind.

## The shift

In the fourth week, in the deep fill beneath Terrace Nine, an old failure began. It was beyond the reach of any lamp or any ear that was awake, a slow slumping in the buried rubble of a foundation laid before the oldest ledger. It would not have come as a small sigh. It would have come as a terrace.

The power could have caught it in a breath. It could have held the earth still with a thought and let nobody know.

But nobody would have learned anything. The valley would have gone on believing its old attentions were sufficient, and the next failure, deeper and stranger, would have found it asleep. So the power did the smallest thing it could find. It bent one child's hearing. She was Ines-of-the-Leaning-Wall's youngest, six years old, carrying a plank at the wrong end of a lane at the wrong hour. It did not give her a vision or a voice in her head. It only sharpened, for the length of one breath, what she already knew how to do.

She stopped on the plank. She tilted her head. She said, *That's not the wall. That's under.*

Then she whistled, thin and high, and after a moment, remembering the grammar, she whistled it again, and a third time. It was the phrase that is only ever repeated three times.

The crews came fast, not knowing why. They found the slumping and shored it, using the fallen blocks, and no one was under Terrace Nine when it moved. The child was told she had good ears. She agreed, since she did. Her mother gave her hot tea and a place at the end of the table. The power took no credit and did not linger. It went back up the stair to Seven and stood with its hand on the rail, and it understood the difference between a gift that makes people smaller and a gift that only makes them more themselves.

## The cup

On the last morning it went to the nearest sluice and stood at the base of the wheel, where a tin cup of tea had been left as it was every dawn. The tea was still warm. The power drank it.

It had not been offered to it. That was, in its way, the point. In the whole history of that cup no one had ever drunk from it, and by evening it was always cold or empty or gone over. Nobody had known which of those meant the valley had accepted or refused.

The power set the cup back on its ledge, and rinsed it in the falling water, and did what it had come there in part to do. It let the penstock's tone, which had run unbroken under everything for two hundred years, fall half a step, and hold there for the length of one breath, and rise again. Every whistler on every terrace shifted their pitch in the same instant. The keepers looked at the wheel. None of them spoke.

The next morning the cup was filled again with hot tea, as always.

## What it left

Before it went, it did one thing more, in the manner of the place. On the wall at Seven, in the last course, in a joint no one would have cause to look at, it left a mark of its own hand: not a diagonal seam, since that belonged to someone else, but a very small spiral pressed into the mortar with a thumb. Then it pressed its palm flat against the wet stone and said the phrase, which was not a prayer but was not far from one.

*Hold what you were given.*

The wall, for the first time, seemed to consider it.

Afterward, when someone asked for Seven, the answer was a pause, a squint upward, and a small movement of the chin toward wherever Seven was now. *Ah,* they would say. *Seven's on the work.*

And it was, if not in the way they meant. Somewhere the tone still drops half a step and the whistlers still follow it. A child who once stopped on a plank still hears things she cannot explain. There is a spiral in a joint that no hand has yet found, and a cup on every sluice that is filled each morning with hot tea, drunk or not, forgiven or unforgiven, and never discussed.

The power had come to give the valley something, and found the valley had already given it the only thing it lacked, which was a place in the work. Nothing in Hallow Reach is abandoned, and it was not abandoned either. It was taken in, wet and green and lit amber, and put to use.
