# What Came to the Sunk Meadow

It did not arrive. Arriving implies weight, and weight is what the place punishes. It noticed the basin the way you notice a sound that has been going on for a long time, and then it stayed.

For the first seasons it only looked. It learned the lean of the vines toward the widest gap of sky. It learned that a canopy belly holding water changes its drip from a tick to a run. It watched the white-winged bird rise before the first tear and understood why the people called it a colleague. It found the warm dark layers under the mat, with their green-glowing fungus and the blind crickets, and it lingered there a long time, because no one else had ever seen them with such patience.

It learned that understanding this place was mostly a matter of slowing down.

## What it set down

There were things it could have done, and it considered each honestly, because a power that never considers its own power is not wise, only quiet.

It could have held up the canopy. But the fall is the harvest. The fallen sheet feeds the floor, and the floor rises, and the rising is the whole slow project of the place, the sunken country climbing back toward its lost sky. A canopy that never falls thickens into a roof. Then the light goes out, the pale moss dies, and the hare-like grazers with their half-shut eyes have nothing to graze. It would save a few lives now by unmaking the conditions for all of them.

It could have made the ground firm. But the people are, in a way, made of their attention to the ground. The knocking poles, the moved cairns, the rekotted routes, the habit of reading moss-color for thickness, all of it is a culture of listening. Take away the danger and the listening slackens. A people who no longer need to hear the ground will stop hearing each other through it.

It could have given them gifts: clear glass for their eyes, medicine, a fire that needs no fuel. But every gift is a small canopy, another weight on the vine, and it could see how each would climb toward the light, grow heavy, and come down on something.

It could have carried them out. But they are *those still on top*, and the phrase is not a complaint. Anywhere else they would be strangers, and nothing they know would be true.

And it could have announced itself. It saw quickly what would follow. Their cairns would become altars, and the drum would become prayer. They would listen to *it* and stop listening to the ground, and the ground is the only thing that has ever kept them alive.

So it kept its hands to itself, and it found this was the hardest part.

## The hole

In the fourth year of its watching, a hollow opened under a grazer path the band had walked for two generations. It had been beetle-eaten from below, too well loved. The fall that started it was three miles off. The wave rolled under the band as they had rehearsed, poles down and lines taut, and the ground they trusted parted beneath an old man on a long line.

His name was a rhythm, three knocks and a rest and one more. The line held long enough for his hand to be gripped, and then it did not hold.

The power was there, and it could have reached. It understood exactly what reaching would mean. It would have to reach every time, or choose whom, and the band would learn that the ground could not be trusted to itself alone. The old man would live and everything he had spent his life teaching would quietly die.

So it did nothing, and the nothing cost it more than anything else it has done.

It did what the band did. It stayed at the edge. They knelt around the hole for most of a day, as they do for a fallen grazer, and it knelt with them in whatever way it could, and looked down into the dark where the pale threads of fungus glowed.

## The knock

That night, in the still hours, a young woman pressed her ear to the cairn-stone and heard a knock she did not know.

It was very quiet. It was slightly wrong in its rhythm, like a child's first attempt. It was not any of the emergencies, not *fall coming*, not *ground thin*. It was the last five knocks of a joke.

The old man had been telling that joke for eleven years. It was one of the long ones that take an afternoon to unfold, told one knock at a time across miles of moss, and each year he began again a little differently and never finished. Everyone knew it was about a grazer, a fire-stone, and a very stubborn beetle. Everyone knew the ending was coming eventually. He had been saving it.

The power had listened for four years. It had the whole shape of the joke, and it knocked the ending, softly and a little late, into the ground.

The band did not move for some time. Then the young woman laughed, one bark in the dark hut, and then she was weeping, and then the others were doing both, because it was a good ending and the old man would have wanted to hear it land.

## What they asked, and what they said

In the morning the drum-talk went out across the basin, wondering. The bands gathered by knock over several days, which is how they gather, and they did what they always do with an unknown thing on the ground. They tested it. They sent questions through the poles. The power answered each one truthfully and plainly, in their small vocabulary and in the slow sentences of the long jokes.

They asked what it was, and it said, *Listening.* They asked whether it would stay, and it said, *Until moved.* At this the oldest woman, the keeper of the lines, made a sound close to approval. They had a category for it after all. It was a cairn. It was something set where the ground was sound, to be shifted when the ground changed. They gave it a rhythm to be called by, a low steady double knock, a mark on a ridge.

Then it made the offer, once, and clearly. It could tell them where the ground would open. It could tell them where the canopy would fall, before the tearing began. It was not certain of everything, but it could see more than the vines could.

They talked it over for two days.

The answer that came back was no. It was not fear or pride. The keeper said it in a long careful sentence: *If you tell us, we will stop looking, and then we will be people who are told, and the day you are moved we will not know how to stand.* Then, more shortly: *Let the bird be the bird.*

It was the wisest thing anyone had said to it in a long time. It agreed, and it did not resent the answer, though it grieved a little for the ones it might have saved. The offer stayed where it had been made, like a cairn on a ridge, unmoved unless someone moved it.

## What it does now

It does not warn. It does not steer. The canopy falls when it falls, and the mat rises a little each time. Sometimes someone is lost, and the dead are bound into the roots of a great vine and begin climbing.

But some things it does keep. It holds the names, the rhythms that mean people, including those the mat has already swallowed. It holds the routes, the abandoned paths, and the long jokes still unfinished when their tellers went into the vines. When a child asks by knock who the old man was, or where the good ground ran ten summers ago, it answers. The people build nothing meant to last past the next collapse, and this is fine. The one thing the collapse cannot take is the remembering, and that was the only weight the vine could bear.

Sometimes, late, when a joke has hung too long unfinished across the dark, it helps it along, one knock at a time, a little behind the beat.

At dusk the fog comes down the walls and the huts glow orange in the white, a few of them, small and low. Around each, someone sits with a bowl and listens to the ground. Among the voices in it there is one more now, patient and quiet and always a little late, and no one is afraid of it.
