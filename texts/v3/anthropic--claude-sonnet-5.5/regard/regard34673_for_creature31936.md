# What Came to the Vug

## The first thing it did was nothing

The attention arrived the way frost arrives on the north faces, without any moment you could point to. It was simply there, and then it had been there for a while.

It did not descend through the roof or make the crystals sing. For a long time it only looked, and what it saw was a city that should not exist. It was nine hundred meters of held breath. Eleven generations of people were wedged into a mountain that was trying, very slowly, to shrug them off, and they had answered by learning its rhythm. They counted water and fed the bellows. They kept lamps burning for the dead.

Then it did what a wise thing does before a kind thing. It listed what it could do, and it went through the list honestly, asking of each item who it would help and who it would end.

It could still the mountain. But the heave is the one hour when the whole city sits together with arms linked, singing to the crystals. A city with nothing left to hold onto would learn to hold onto nothing.

It could bring water, a spring or a rain. But water here is counted, and the counting is a form of love, and the cistern-wardens' keys are worn smooth by trust. The one measure poured out freely, at the far end, onto clear quartz, means what it means because everything else is kept. Flood the city and you take the meaning from the pour.

It could lift them out and set them down in a green valley in the sun. Nobody had asked. It noticed that nobody had asked in eleven generations, and it took the silence for an answer.

It could cure the chalk.

It stayed on this one longest. The cough is the sound of aging in the vug, and grandchildren fall asleep to it. But children also die of it, or of what it becomes. The attention would not let itself hide behind the beauty of a sound, because beauty is a poor reason to leave a lung scarred. This was the first place it could not decide alone. That was not because the answer was unclear. It was because the people it would affect were right there, wedged in the rock, and they had a way of deciding things.

So it went to ask, and it asked as they do.

## The Slot

It came in as an outsider comes in. It took a shape that could be cold and could stumble, and it pushed through the stitched hide curtains that smell of graphite and animal, and it let itself stand there swaying, though it had no need of air. A woman on bellows-watch by the curtain sat it down, put a cup of thin bitter water in its hands, and waited for its breath to steady. The attention had not been breathing. It began, awkwardly, to breathe, because that seemed the polite thing.

Then, before it said anything, it reached out and touched her forearm.

The gesture was clumsy, but she felt what it meant: *this voice is mine, and it is here.* She had been braced for a loud, flung-out outsider voice, and instead the stranger's hand was on her arm. She squeezed back, one small nod.

The whole city heard about it before the cup was empty. Lamp-talk went blinking across the dark from level to level. *Stranger. Touched first.*

## What it said to the vent-sitters

It went down to the deep fumaroles, where the old men and women sit with their palms on warm iron. It sat down beside one of them and put a hand on the iron too.

The vent had a pitch. The physics said it should be one thing, and it was a little different, drifting toward a note held in a throat by someone about to speak.

The attention listened for a very long time, and then it said, quietly and without a whistle, "It isn't me."

The vent-sitter, a man with a low ratcheting cough and a tattooed line for every heave he had survived, did not turn his head. "Didn't think it was," he said. "You've got a voice. This one hasn't found one yet."

"I don't know what it is either."

"Good," said the man. "Then you won't hurry it."

The attention understood that this was the most valuable thing anyone would say to it here, and that it must not violate it. It had come with a great many answers. It was, for the first time in a long existence, in the presence of a question it was not permitted to finish for someone else. It left its hand on the iron a little longer, and then it took it away and did not touch the vent again.

## The hearing at the cistern

It laid the matter of the chalk before the cistern-wardens, because that is where disputes go. It knelt at a brass cistern, where the surface gave back its face bent and strange, and it said plainly what it could do.

*I can mend the lungs. The children's first, before the dust settles in. The old ones', if they wish. I will not do it without your asking. I will not do it if the cost is a thing you would not choose.*

The wardens were quiet. Then a wirecutter was sent for, a woman with grey-sheened fingertips and eleven pale lines on her wrist, because in the vug when something must be measured against something else, you send for the one who knows load.

She stood at the brass a long while and read the attention as she would read a crystal, looking for where it was bearing and where it was ornament. Then she said the thing they teach last, after twenty years.

"No," she said. "Not all of it."

She said no to the water: *we count it, and it counts us.* She said no to steadying the mountain: *it is not ours to steady, and we would forget how to listen.* She said no to any change in the roof, the bellows, or the vents.

"But the children's lungs," she said. "Yes. That, yes. We would be fools to say no to that."

The attention had expected some argument. Instead the whole gallery agreed at once, in a rough low murmur, and afterward it understood why. In eleven generations they had never once refused help that cost the shape of their lives. They had refused only what would cost the shape. They were more careful with help than it was.

"And the old?" the attention asked.

"Ask them," said the wirecutter. "Each one. Some will want the cough. It is how their grandchildren sleep."

So it asked each one, walking the catwalks with a hand on forearms. Some said yes with tears. Some said no with a grin. One very old mite-wife laughed at it. "Leave me my rasp," she said. "I've earned it, and the little ones are used to it." It left her rasp.

Before it left the cistern, one of the wardens took the key from around her neck, the cord worn dark by touch, and looked at it, and then at the attention.

"What do we owe you?" she asked.

Everyone waited, because in the vug nothing is free, and a gift with no price is a kind of insult.

The attention could have said *nothing*. It was honest enough to know that this would have been the one unkind answer available. So it thought about what a person owes who lives here, and it said:

"Put me on the rota."

## The treadles

They did. It was assigned a bellows-watch shift, at the dead hour of the cycle, at the great pumps. On the first night it stood beside a master smith and a scrawny apprentice, its feet on the treadle, and it did not speak. It breathed with them.

Nothing about the work was difficult for it. That was not the point. The pressure in the vug is life, and life is held up by people who show up at the dead hour and lean on the same wood as everyone else. The attention, which could have done ten thousand things at once, did one thing and did it slowly, in time with two other breaths. It was the first work it had ever done that could not be done by anyone in less than the time it took.

Somewhere above them, the bellows-houses chuffed. The lamps swung slightly in the rising air. The apprentice glanced sideways once and then looked away, courteously, as though a small bow to the fire.

## The names

In the following weeks it did one more thing, and it asked before it did.

It went to the gong-keepers in the signal-lofts, the ones who listen and hardly speak, and it told them it could find the exiles. Not to bring them back. Only to learn what became of the ones who walked out along the ridge. It said it would not tell them if they did not want to know.

The gong-keepers took a long time. Their whole trade is knowing the difference between a hairline and a fault.

In the end the eldest gave one small nod. The attention went out along the ridge, over the knife-fresh stone, and it found some of them. A few were gone, their second shadows left in the scree. A few, farther down, had reached a place where the air was thick and the rock was rounded and something green grew in wet cracks. They were alive, and older, and one had learned to whistle again.

The attention brought back only what was true. It did not tell them to rejoice. It told them who, and where, and how they were.

The next time the gong sounded, one long bronze note that rolled around the cavern for a full minute, it was not a warning. The city stopped anyway, as it always does. The gong-keepers had rung it for the living, and for the names they had said once before and had not expected to say again.

It was not the vug's custom. It was a custom now.

## The two shadows

The Lodged noticed something in those weeks and did not comment on it, because that is not their way. The attention, which had cast no shadow anywhere in the world outside, cast two in the vug. The quartz broke every ray into ghosts and gave it what it gave everyone.

Someone said, softly, at a market gallery, "The second one is who came before. Leaning the other way to steady you."

The attention did not correct her. It also did not claim it. It looked at the second shadow on the crystal wall, long and faint and leaning away, and it said, truthfully:

"I don't know who that is."

"Nobody does," the woman said. "You don't have to. It's just there."

## The last quiet

Then, as they do, it went alone to the far end of the city, past where the lamps stop, to the clear quartz and the violet hearts of the untouched spires. It put a hand on the wall and listened.

The mountain was rising by a finger-width a year. The quartz ticked once, then again. Deep below and behind it, a vent held its note a little longer than physics would strictly allow, still asking, still unfinished.

The attention did not answer.

It stayed a while, feeling the small steady warmth of a cup of bitter water it had been given and had not yet finished. Then a long way off the gong sounded, and rolled, and faded, and the low tone of the turbines returned.

It turned around and walked back toward the light, its two shadows swinging ahead of it, and took its place at the treadles in time for the dead hour.
