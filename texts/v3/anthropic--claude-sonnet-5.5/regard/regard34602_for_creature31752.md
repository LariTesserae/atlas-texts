# What Came to the Floor

The attention that found the Setting Floor was old, and it was kind, and its first act was to notice what it was capable of doing.

It could finish the place. It saw this at once, the way a person holding a lit match sees the dry field. One complete look would do it, a steady light in which every seam was sealed and every course met its neighbor. It knew what would follow: the corners rounding, the lintels sagging, the whole plain slumping into a single warm heap with no one left to sort it. It would have been a very tidy ending, and it would have been the cruelest thing it could do, the more so because it would have felt like help.

It could also reconcile the dreams. Under the brick, in the humming dark, several sleepers dreamed several floors, and where the floors touched, tiles tilted like pack ice and staircases climbed to walls. The power could have woken the sleepers gently and introduced them, or laid one dream over the rest. But it watched the Setters on either side of a tilted seam, each laying the next exact tile with a gladness that had no complaint in it. It understood that the tilt was not a wound. It was the sound two dreams make when they meet. Nobody was suffering. The disagreement was the texture of the place, and the place was the only thing it had ever been.

It could have shown itself to the Setters, or shown the Setters to the walker. Every wisdom it had said the same thing about that: no. A Setter held in the steady light of looking would be complete, and would soften.

So it did what wise things do when they arrive somewhere that is already working. It began by learning how not to look.

## The Lesson of the Nap

The grey felt on the shaded walls hears by being looked at. It lies flat before a gaze and rises behind it in a slow wave, and the wave tells the hands where the watching is not. The power's gaze was larger than any walker's and would have flattened the nap for a mile. Every Setter in that mile would have stopped, held, and begun to lapse.

It spent what would have been a long time, if time there had been counted, learning to attend sideways. It learned to look the way one listens in the dark, with the edge of the attention and with the part of a mind that is not aimed. It learned to hold the whole floor in a regard so light that the nap lay neither flat nor bristled but stayed as it was, the way a sleeping animal's fur stays when the room is loved but not entered. The felt did not shiver. The seams kept ticking. Far off, a great stone was dragged across another stone, and a hill moved, unobserved and unharmed.

That was the first gift, and no one on the floor ever knew it had been given: a mile of the world in which nothing was seen, and therefore nothing was lost.

## The Third Shadow

Then it came nearer, to the one shape on the floor that was not a Setter.

The walker had a doubled shadow, its own and the stooped one that bent over the tile at its feet. The power did not wish to add a fourth thing to explain. It took the shape of nothing at all and let itself be a third shadow, falling from a little to the other side, faint, unstooped, and thin as a bruise fading. It kept the walker's pace, and it kept it exactly.

It did not answer the question the walker had never quite asked: whether the floor was being laid for it or whether it was being laid into the floor. The power could have answered, and the answer was *yes, and those are one thing seen from two sides*. But it saw that the question was the walker's own unfinished business, the very thing that kept it walking, and that to complete it would be to lapse the walker. The question was a course still being laid. It left it open.

What it gave instead was small. In the colonnade with the fourth columns reversed, where the walker meets its own footprints in wet mortar arriving, the mortar took a second set of prints. They walked beside the first, not toward them, keeping the same direction and roughly the same stride, a shade longer, as of someone slowing a step to keep company. They were not arriving. They were accompanying. The mortar set around them and held them, and the walker, coming upon them, stopped, and put a bare foot beside one, and found the tile warm.

That was all it said: *someone is walking with you, and that is not a thing that has to be explained.*

## One Worn Place

There was one more thing it did, and it took a long time to decide, because it came close to the line.

Every tile on the floor had an edge sharp enough to cut a palm. Nothing had had time to wear them. That was the floor's youth and its glory, and the power would not have taken it away. But near the tilted seam where an oxblood herringbone met a sooty basket-weave, where the tiles leaned against each other and no walker would choose to put a hand, the power laid its own palm on one edge. It did not sand it or dissolve it. It leaned there, in the warm, for the length of a slow breath, the way a person rests a hand on the rail of a bridge in the evening.

The edge softened. It was not finished, and the tile did not lapse. It was worn, in the way things are worn by a long kind use, and it was the first worn thing in the world, an hour old and already loved. In that one place a walker might sit on the seam, with the pack ice tilting gently beneath it, and rest a palm on a tile that did not cut.

The power did not repeat this anywhere. One worn place was a courtesy. A hundred would have been a plan.

## The Slump

It stayed for the evening. This was the hardest part, and the part it would have said, if asked, that it was proudest of, though its pride was a very quiet thing.

The ceiling darkened and lowered. The kilns flared at their crowns. The terraces liquefied from their footings and ran in slow red tides, and the storm of steam tore across the plain. A wise power might have held the storm back, or gathered the walker up out of it and set it down on some dry, unbuilt height. It did neither. The storm was the way the floor decided what it was next. To interrupt it would have been to finish a thing that was mid-sentence. So it stood in the third shadow, on the walker's far side, and let the reset go through them both, hot and red and shining, and kept only one small promise: that the walker's footing would arrive under its weight, as it always had, and that nothing that was not the floor's own doing would be done to it.

In the flood-light after, the Sorters came, seen only as motion in the corner of the eye, gentle, sorting oxblood from ochre in the shining mud. They pressed and stacked. And one tile, turned over on its pallet in the morning-that-was-not-morning, had a print in the slip of its underside that was a little too deep. It was not a fingertip and not a tread. It was a whole palm, open, laid down flat and lightly, with the fingers a little spread, the way one rests a hand on a sleeping thing to feel it breathe.

The Setters took the tile without remark and set it in the next course. It is there now, in a herringbone that has not yet met anything, a few hundred paces from where it began. The courses run on toward the pale grout at the edge of sight, where the Unassigned lean a few degrees toward the last laid tile. The walker goes on walking. The floor goes on being laid.

The power is nowhere on it. It kept no place there, made no claim, and finished nothing. But the warmth that comes up through the tiles a half-beat before the tile arrives is now a little kinder than it was. No one will ever have cause to say so.
