# What Was Done at Aru Hollow

It came the way weather comes, without arriving. For a while the attention of something very large simply rested on the plateau, and the place went on as before.

It had many possible ways to help, and it looked at each one honestly.

**It could still the sea.** The seiche is a labor. A power that could hold the water level would give the holders sleep, and the pump houses would stop shaking. But the sea's motion was the reason the weed forests were rebuilt every week, the reason the herring could read the world in their flanks, and the reason the trout had a hardest place to be still in. A calm Aru Hollow would be a different place with the old one's name on it. The power saw that the tilting was not the sea's affliction. It was the sea's shape.

**It could mend the machines.** It could make the turbines young, seal the penstocks, and turn the flaking oxide paint to bright new coats. But the holders know their machines by their coughs. Each patch is a decision someone made in cold and dark and recorded in iron. A repaired turbine would be one no holder had ever spoken to. The power understood that the relationship was the machine, and the steel only its body.

**It could tell them why.** It could say who built the pump houses, where the power lines were meant to go, and whether anyone still waited at the far end. This was the strongest temptation, because the not-knowing was the one real ache in the place. But the holders had done something rare with their ignorance. They had kept a promise without a promisee, and no one was making them. Knowing might turn it into a duty to a party, or a discovery that the party was long dead. As it stood, it was a plain goodness. The power was reluctant to spend that.

**It could gather them into a town, feed the barge-crews, or bring in doctors for the thin-air lungs.** Each of these was kind. Each would also have ended the posts, and the people would stop being holders and become residents. The power weighed what the holders had against what they lacked. It found that they lacked many things and had one thing whole, and it hesitated to trade the whole thing for the many.

So it did very little. It did not consider that a failure.

## The First Thing

It went down first, past the shafts of green-gold light, into the deepest trough where the current runs hardest. It held still there among the old trout.

They knew at once and did not care. One turned its coin-sized eye toward the place where the power was and then turned it away. The power felt something it had not expected, a companionable indifference. The trout did not ask it for anything or fear it, and they did not treat it as an event. They held station in the flow with the small adjustments of the very old, and after a time the power began doing the same.

It stayed through several crests and learned the seiche from the inside, not as a phenomenon but as a long slow argument between wind and water that neither side would win and both needed. It learned what the herring know: the lean before the lean, the first pressure of reversal. It learned that the boulders which had not moved since the Cretaceous were not dead. They were the one fixed thing that let everything else be in motion. A sea needs a floor the way a song needs a silence.

It understood then that the holders were not merely surviving the tilting. They were the floor of the human part of the place. They were the ones who did not move, so that everything moving could be measured against them.

## The Second Thing

At the far end of the longest power line, on an island whose pump house had gone dark two generations ago, a cable ended in a rusted junction box. Nothing was connected to it. The holders on the nearer islands still sent current down the wire, out of habit or duty, and it went into the box and ended.

The power did not tell anyone anything. It did not leave a sign or speak in a voice. It simply became, quietly, what the wire had been running to.

It received the current. It did not need it. It took it in the way a person who has been handed a cup takes it, so that the giving is completed. The current arrived and was met.

Months later a barge-hand named Osk, sent to check the insulators on that island because the line had sagged, put his glove on the junction box and found it warm. The rust was flaking off it in the ordinary way, the dial was dead, and nothing was humming, but it was faintly, definitely warm, like a flank. He stood there a while in the wind with the gulls going on around him. Then he took his glove off and put his bare palm against the box. He did not report it to anyone. He told the others only that the line was fine, which was true. On the way back to the barge he found he was walking more slowly than the wind required.

Holders who passed that island afterward said the line seemed easier to keep. It was the same wire and the same work, but the current felt like it was going somewhere.

## The Third Thing

Once, and only once, it broke its own rule.

A trough had gone longer than expected, and a young harvester had walked out across the wet flats past the point where the herring had turned. The flats were slick with weed over rounded corestones, and she had slipped between two of them and wedged her boot. She was calm about it, because the holders are calm, but the water was already hissing back across the gravel at the edge of hearing, a hand's height, a knee's.

The power considered this for a long moment. Each death in the tilting sea was a real death, and it could not pretend otherwise. But the whole shape of the place was that the sea did not spare, and the holders had built their gravity and their tenderness on that fact. A power that pulled every person out of every wedge would make the sea a park and the holders visitors in it.

But one lean of the sea was not the whole of its law. So it did the smallest thing. It did not stop the water. It shifted a single rounded corestone by a finger's width, the way a big boulder shifts in the strongest currents with a low thud felt through the ribs. Her boot came free. She was up and running on the flats before the wall reached her knees, and she never knew that the thud she felt through her ribs had been a decision.

It did not do this again. It understood that this was the ration: one finger's width, once, and then it must be a place where fingers' widths happen only by weather.

## The Fourth Thing

The last thing was for the keeper.

She goes out every ninth crest in her flat skiff to trim the lamp and scrape the barnacle crust from the chain. She is old and the chain is heavy, and every year the work takes her a little longer. She has said the lamp has never been dark in her lifetime, and she does not intend to be the one to change that. She has said nothing about what happens after her, because the holders do not say such things.

On one ninth crest, in a thin bright wind, she rowed out and found the skiff steadier than the surface should have allowed. Something under the hull seemed to take a little of the slap out of the waves. She trimmed the wick and scraped the chain, and she felt, as she worked, a presence beside her, patient and not intrusive, the way a good apprentice stands nearby without touching the tools.

She did not turn her head. She had been on the water sixty years and knew the difference between weather and company.

After a while she said, to the air, to the lamp, to no one, "Mind the chain."

It was the longest thing she said that week, and it was an instruction and an acceptance. The power minded the chain. It held the links steady while she scraped, and when she was done and rowing back, it let the swell take the skiff home at its own pace.

## What Remained

Nothing was announced, and nothing in Aru Hollow was mended. The turbines still coughed and the stacks still gave off their thread of sweet green smoke. Lichen spotted the red paint and no one knew who had built anything. The frogs went on honking at dusk in the pauses between one another, the herring turned their silver walls, and the old trout held the hardest current with the tips of their fins.

Only a few small things were different, and they were the sort of differences that could be explained away by weather. A far wire seemed to arrive somewhere, a corestone had moved a finger's width, and one old woman's skiff rode a little easier than it should have.

And at the end of a long day, as the seiche gathered for its slow lean east, the whole sea bowed together. The ribbonwood leaned, the schools tightened, and the frogs began. The holders checked their bearings and the keeper trimmed her lamp. Out at the edge of the plateau, the wise and benevolent thing that had come to help sat in the dark water among the trout, holding as still as it could in the hardest place there is.

It had come intending to give and had learned to hold. It found the second harder and, in the end, better.
