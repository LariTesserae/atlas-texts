# What Came Down

It arrived on the ebb, when the water was stillest, and it did not arrive the way visitors do. Nothing broke the disc of the surface. The pale brass light dimmed by perhaps a hair, as it does when a cloud passes far above, and afterward there was a new attentiveness in the water, the way a room changes when someone sits down quietly in the corner.

It was old, and it was kind, and it had learned that these two things do not always agree about what to do.

It drifted first along the rooftops with the pale commuters, at their height, in their direction. It read the town the way a wise person reads a house, by what has been worn smooth and what has been left alone. It saw the cupped cobbles, the polished arcs on the windows, the lattice of calcified vine seated in the cracked gear. It felt the pulse of the vent far below, slow and unhurried, a heart in no danger.

Then it considered what it might do, because considering is what such a power owes before it acts.

It could drain the bowl and give the town back to air. But the town would die in the drying. The vine would crack and the lamps would go grey. The Sleeper would lie on the cobbles like a stranded boat, and the long note would have nothing to ring in. It set the idea aside.

It could fill the streets with people, and the machines with purpose, and give the looms thread. But the looms answer the turbines now, and the turbines answer the vent, and nothing in the town is waiting to be needed. The power saw that a rescue has to be for someone who is drowning, and that nothing here was.

It could wake the vine, give it speed and a voice, and let the slow mind of the copper speak in a season instead of an age. It held this one longest, because it was the most tempting and the most like a gift. But the vine's patience is the source of its fit. A mind hurried into conversation might begin to want things, and the wanting would change what it did to the gears. The turbines turn more easily now than when men oiled them, and it was not clear that anyone, the vine included, could be improved upon the way they'd been made.

So it did what the wise usually find themselves doing, which was less than it could.

---

It went down to the engine hall and hung under the vaulted iron ribs while the three turbines turned in their cradles. It looked at the lattice of bone-hard vine and the ring of coral worn smooth by the belt. It looked at the vent in the floor, breathing its warm mineral breath through a fissure that had been widening and narrowing for longer than the town had been a town.

And it saw the one thing that was slowly wrong.

The vent was not eternal. Rock shifts and seams close. Every thousand years or so the breath grew a little shallower, and the interval between pulses lengthened by a minute, then by another, and nothing in the town could feel it, because the town lived at the pace of the change. Someday, far past any horizon the snails or the shrimp would recognize, the eleven hours would become twelve, and then a day, and then the long quiet would not end. The lamps would not flare. The fish would hang in the streets and not resume. The town would not die, exactly. It would simply be the shape of a rhythm that had stopped.

The power did not touch the turbines. It did not touch the vine. It reached down through the floor of the sinkhole, through cold limestone and older stone, to the place where the fissure narrowed, and it held the rock open. It did not force it. It only persuaded, the way you might ease a stuck drawer with the flat of your hand. It did the same at three other places along the seam, and at a fifth deep enough that no map of the town would ever include it.

When it was finished, the pulse of the vent was not stronger or faster. It was only more likely to go on. The breath had been given a few hundred thousand years. That is not forever, but it is enough that the town would never learn what the end of it looked like.

No one saw this done. It will be found done, if it is found at all, like the seated valve and the pressed-in rivet. A little coat of lime may form over the place where the rock was persuaded. If the Menders exist, they may add it to their accounts and not know why the accounts seem lighter.

---

At the next pulse the power waited in the middle of a lane, at the height of the second-story windows, and made itself no more than it had to be.

The current rose. The ribbon-weed streamed flat. The fronds on the chimneys bowed. The tail at the top of the tallest flue drew in hand over hand, and the Sleeper came down, gray and scarred and slow, with its whiskers trailing like the chimney-weed.

It touched the vine on the wall. It touched a brass handle worn to butter. It touched the glass face of a gauge whose needle had rested at one point for a century.

Then it came to the place where the power was, and its dull pebble eyes did not change, and the whiskers brushed the water where something attentive hung. The Sleeper held there for three breaths, not two.

What passed between them cannot be said, because the Sleeper has no words and the power did not use any. But the power felt itself weighed, without being judged, and entered into an account. It had the sense, brief and unmistakable, of being counted as a fixture, the way a valve is counted, or a lamp, or a patient snail on its pane. It found it did not want to be counted as anything grander. It stayed still and let itself be checked.

Then the great gray body turned aside and passed, and the note rolled through the pipes. Every lamp in Low Vell flared at once and every street sprang out in layers to the rim, and the fish stopped and hung, and the snails stacked against the glass in their soft clicking pyramids. In that dawn-bright moment the power did a last small thing, and it was the only thing it did that anyone might have called a gift.

It listened. It did not answer the note or add to it. It let the note pass through it as it passes through everything, and it was a body in the town, ringing faintly, like a pipe.

The lamps settled a little dimmer. The fish resumed, mid-lane, mid-route.

By the time the water was still, the power had gone up through the green, past the rim, into whatever it had come from. Behind it the shrimp were ticking along the vines, passing a message down the lane from hand to hand. It was, as always, altered by every pair it passed through. By the time it reached the end it might have been about the pulse, or the current, or the unusual stillness at the ebb, or nothing at all.

But if you had been close enough to hear it clearly, and knew how to listen, you might have caught, somewhere in the middle, a small variation that had not been there before. It was only a pause between two ticks, a little longer than the rest. It might have been the shrimp's way of saying *all is well*, or *something has been here*, or, as near as anything in Low Vell could say it, *thank you.*

The town did not know it had been given anything. That was the point. The power knew, and was content.
