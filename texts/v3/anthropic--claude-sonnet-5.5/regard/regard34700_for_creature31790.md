# What Came to Harrow Weir

## Arrival

It did not descend. Nothing crossed the rim. There was only a day when the town was being attended to more closely than usual, the way a room feels different when someone has stopped speaking and is simply there.

The power came as attention first, and for a long time it was only that. It did what everyone who comes to Harrow Weir must do: it listened for a full day, until it stopped trying to resolve the chord and began to hear the chord as an answer.

## What It Considered

It could have done a great deal. It saw each thing clearly and held it a while.

It could have mended the bearings and stopped the beat's slow drift. It could have raised the population to the size of the rooms, filled the lanes with children, and lit the lamps to the brightness of noon. It could have put birds in the tree ferns, frogs in the races, and insects in the horsetails, so that the unbitten leaves would finally be bitten and the town would be, by the standards of every other place, whole. It could have found the buyer of all those stacked bolts and jars and ended the mystery, or become the buyer.

It set each of these aside, and the reasons were not the same each time.

The bearings were the hardest. The beat is slowing, and the number is on the wall in chalk that never dries. But the thirty have made their peace in a form no outsider could have given them: they do not treat it as an ending. To stop the slowing would tell them, in effect, that they had been wrong to trust the town, that it needed rescuing, and that its changes were failures rather than speech. Help that arrives as a verdict is not help.

The animals it set aside more gently. The place looks finished because no one has ever needed to be afraid of it. That is a rare thing, and it is not a wound. A world is allowed to be one of the ways a world can be.

So it kept its hands where they were, and this cost it something, because it was not indifferent. Restraint is what love does when it has understood the situation.

## The Walking

It walked the causeways in the dark between the lamp-globes, because it saw that this was where one was welcome. It did not step into the light. Twice it passed a dead lamp and went around the held breath of its globe, as the lamp-tenders do.

The thirty watched it from the seams of the alleys. Each of them, separately and without deciding to, hummed a little lower for a while. None could have said why.

At a junction near the felting shed a root-wright was kneeling, as she had been for most of an hour, coaxing a rootstock toward a brass bracket it did not want. The power did not help. It knelt in the wet at the edge of her lamp-fog and waited with her, and this was the whole of the gift. Being waited with is different from waiting alone, and she felt it as a steadiness in the hour. Toward the end of it, the rootstock turned on its own and took the bracket. She thanked it, as she always did, and did not thank anything else, because she had no way to know she should.

## The Number

In the engine hall, under the vault, past the troughs steaming faintly around the flywheel's bearings, the power found the wall.

The tally is written in chalk that never dries and is copied fresh each generation. At the bottom is the number no one says aloud, set out where anyone could read it and where no one does.

The power read it. It was the first to do so in four generations. Then it did what the engine-keepers do: it did not say it. The courtesy was the same, and it understood why. To speak the number would be to own it, and the number belongs to the town.

## The Fog

At the deep hour it went down to where the fog lies thick and low around the engine house. It stood in it and spoke, quietly, as the people do, saying only what it meant to leave behind.

It said: *I was here, and I saw you. Nothing is wrong.*

The voice came back half a second late, lower, from the wrong side. It spoke again, and the returned voice came back lower still.

Then it hummed the chord. It did not correct it or resolve it. It hummed the unresolved thing as it was, all the notes the pipes were giving that hour, and it let the fog have them. It was the only offering it was certain no one could mistake for a demand.

Last, after long thought, and having decided against a good many things, it made one small allowance.

The town names its daughters for sounds. It has a Wren, named for the note of a small clay feed in the dye-works, and no one in Harrow Weir knows the name once belonged to something alive. The power weighed whether to tell them. Telling would be an answer, and the town had not asked. A sound, though, is only a sound. It is a question that can be declined.

So it said, into the fog, once and very quietly, the song of a real wren: quick, bright, and unmechanical. It pitched the song exactly to the note of the clay feed, so that nothing in it would be foreign to the chord. Then it left, and the fog drew in around what it had been given and kept it, or did not, as the fog decides.

## Afterward

Nothing changed that anyone could name.

The beat went on, a fraction slower. The chord slid, some months later, to a new key, and the ferns trembled together, and the thirty hummed their way down to it without deciding to.

A listener, walking her morning round with her palm flat on the pipes, paused at the clay feed in the dye-works and stood there longer than the pipe required. She could not have said what she was listening for.

And in the deepest hours, in the thickest of the fog, among the fragments of lullabies and the counting-off of pipe numbers in a voice from before anyone's grandmother, there is now something else. It is quick and high and cheerful, and it is the one small living noise the town has never had.

A girl named Wren, who goes down to the engine house sometimes because she likes how her voice comes back, heard it once and stood very still. She did not know what it was. It sounded like her name, if her name were alive.

She hummed back. The fog returned it, half a second late and lower, from the wrong side.

Nothing more has happened. The power does not need it to. It left a door unlatched and did not push it open. Whether anyone walks through, and what they find, is Harrow Weir's to say, in a voice the power trusts the town will learn to hear.
