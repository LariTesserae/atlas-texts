# What Came to the Shelf

It did not arrive as light, or as a voice. The frogs would have gone silent for that, and the first rule it set itself was that the frogs should not go silent.

It came the way the morning steam comes, as a thinning at the edge of things. A thread rose from the second terrace that had no vent under it, wandered north, and joined the others. Nothing on the Shelf noticed. This was the first good sign.

## The Temptations

It had power enough to do nearly anything, and it spent the first several days, which is to say the first several breaths of the sitters, deciding what not to do.

It could make the sitters known. It saw at once what that would mean: boots on the moss, instruments in the springs, the careful reverence of people who take samples. The courtesy of the lowlanders, a hand laid on the moss before resting, is worth more than any monument, and it is fragile. It left the sitters unknown.

It could make them wise. But it listened to the bell tone rolling down the terraces and understood that the word did not apply. A bearing is not wise, and it is not lacking anything either. The sitters know the one thing they need to know, which is where to lie. Giving them a mind that wondered would only give them something to be troubled by.

It could lengthen their lives. Some of them were already older than the tree ferns growing on their backs. But it saw the white stone rising slowly along the flanks of the eldest, ring on ring, and it saw that the ringing was part of the machine too. Travertine is what the dead and the nearly dead are turned into so the terrace can keep stepping down. It let the stone rise.

It could multiply them, wall them in, quiet the storms, or fix the geysers to their schedule so they never skipped. It considered each, and each time it found the same flaw. The Shelf is a thing that keeps itself, and a keeper who steps in too often teaches the thing to stop.

## Learning the Hour

So it did what none of its temptations required. It learned to be slow.

This was harder than it sounds. Its attention was quick by nature and the Shelf was not, and for the first days it kept arriving at conclusions the mountain had not yet begun. It made itself smaller and warmer and wetter. It let the daily cloud rise through it. It let the rain of four o'clock drum on whatever it had agreed to be. By the time the geysers had fired their forty-minute sequence a dozen times, it could feel the interval between each huff of each vent as a kind of walking pace, and it fell into step.

Then it began to understand the place.

It found that the sitters are not one animal but a slow conversation. The geyser fires, and the sitter that receives the mist wakes and sounds its bell. The next sitter down the terrace is already leaning toward the sound. The frogs pick up the echo, and the ferns unroll to the frogs. A sitter shifts a little in its sleep, a fissure narrows by a finger, a spring drops three degrees, and somewhere a geyser waits a beat longer before firing. It is all one long, slow sentence, and the sitters and the plumbing are speaking it together. Nobody is in charge of it and nobody is missing from it.

It found, too, where the sentence was in danger.

## The Two Small Things

There were two threats, and it chose to answer only one of them.

The first was on the trail, far below, where the lowland people cross the last ford before the climb. A fungus was moving up through the frogs of the basin, one river at a time, a thin pale death that lives in the skin. It would arrive on a boot or in a jar of collected moss, and it would take the small orange frogs first. It would take the frog chorus, which is the sitters' echo. The tocking would thin, and the ferns would lose their cue, and the swifts would come to the mist waves and find fewer insects. The sentence would not end, but it would lose its words one by one.

For this the power did one thing. At the ford, on the night of the next rain, the water ran a little warmer and a little more mineral than the stones could account for. It did not stop the fungus. It made the ford an unpleasant place for it, a place where the spores that came off the boots of climbers would find nothing to hold on to. A lowlander would notice only that the water was oddly soft on the feet. The power did not put a wall around the Shelf. It only made one door a little harder to carry sickness through.

The second was the old sitter at the northern end, whose vent was sealing.

Travertine had been climbing its flanks for a lifetime of mountains. The fissure beneath it was closing, the huff of its breath was longer between and fainter, and the moss on its back was beginning to dry at the crown. It was dying, or beginning to, in the only way a sitter dies: by growing cooler than the stone around it until the stone finally claims it.

The power lay beside it for four nights.

It did not open the fissure. It considered this at length and set it aside. The sitter had not asked. The vent had sealed itself as vents do, and a sealed vent lets a new one open somewhere else, and the young sitters at the edge of the terrace were already leaning toward the warmth that would come. What the old one had earned, after all its centuries of lying still over the mountain's breath, was not an extension. It had earned company.

On the fourth night the geysers fired their sequence, and the bell went down the terraces from sitter to sitter, and when it reached the northern end the old one did not answer. There was a gap in the sentence. It was the first the power had heard, a beat of silence where a tone should have been.

The power did not fill it. It thought about filling it, and decided that the frogs should learn the gap. Grief is a form of accuracy. The living should hear the space where the dead one was, and the terrace would rearrange itself around the silence the way a river rearranges around a stone. So it let the gap stand, and only laid its hand on the moss of the old back, which is what the lowlanders do, and stayed there through the cooling.

## The Question

Once, near the end, it did ask.

It went to the middle-aged sitter at the top of the third terrace, the one whose eye is often a little more open than it needs to be, and it lay its attention against the mud beside the great mossy dome. It asked, in no language, only in a pressure and a warmth: *Is there something you need?*

The answer took eleven days. It came as a long slow swell through the sternum, the same bell tone the sitter gives at the shift change, with nothing added to it and nothing left out. The power turned it over for a long time before it understood that this was the answer. The sitter needed nothing. The bell was an acknowledgment that it had been asked, and it was the only sentence the sitter had ever spoken. It was what every sitter says to everything.

Beyond the sitter, by the boggy pool, the pipe leaked its thread of steam into the morning. The tree had grown around it, the wasps were in the join, and the orange frog was in the bore.

The power understood the pipe then. Someone had come here once, somebody quick, and had done one small thing without knowing whether it was right, and gone. The mountain had taken the thing in. The sitters had not pushed the tree over or pulled the iron out. They had let the mistake, or the kindness, or the question, whichever it was, become part of the place, because that is what the place does with anything left alone long enough. The power saw that its own small acts at the ford and the vigil were the same kind of thing. It could not say which they would turn out to be. But it saw now that the right size for such acts is the size the tree can grow around.

## The Leaving

It did not leave a mark. For its last season on the Shelf it did the one thing that would cost it nothing to undo and the mountain nothing to absorb: it sat.

It took the shape of a rounded boulder, and the moss came. The first season was liverwort, then the filmy ferns, and by the end of the wet months a seedling bromeliad had lodged in the crook of its back. It felt the rain of four o'clock as a weight of quiet gladness. It kept time with the geysers. When the shift change came, it did not sound the bell, since it was not one of them and had no right to, but it leaned in the direction of the sound, the way the young sitters lean, and let the tone pass through it.

Then, on a morning when the steam was thick and the terraces were gone, it stood up. A rain of small things fell from its back: beetles, mites, a few tadpoles from a bromeliad cup. Most of them, out of habit, began to climb.

Where it had lain, the mud held the shape of a low dome. By evening the dent had filled with warm tea-colored water. By the next week an orange frog the size of a thumbnail had laid a string of eggs at the edge of it.

Nobody on the Shelf remarked on it. The geysers fired north to south over forty minutes and rested. The bell went down the terraces, and at the northern end, past a gap the frogs had already learned, a young sitter turned in its sleep, and the huff of a new vent came up through the moss.

The power went down through the ford, which ran a little warm and soft, and was gone. The pipe went on breathing its thread of steam into the morning, and the middle-aged sitter beside it closed its eye, not all the way, and kept the time.
