# The Guest

It arrives as a shadow before its cloud.

The cutters see it first, out past the drying racks, a dark patch sliding over the moss against the wind. They stop and lean on their spades, and nobody speaks, because they have no doubt about it. Shadows that come early have always been the plateau's business. But this one does not fade when the sky it belongs to finally arrives. It goes to the nearest dome, climbs it, and sits down on the granite among the orange lichen.

It has thought about how to come. It could have come as light, or as a voice in the pumps, or as a great figure standing in the cloud. It rejects all of these for the same reason: the plateau has spent two hundred years learning a courtesy, and the first thing a wise guest does is notice the courtesy and keep it.

So it comes down off the dome as a walker, and it walks badly. It strikes the moss as if the moss might answer. The moss does answer, by giving way, and the print stays open for a moment and then begins to fill, shivering, over the course of an hour. The visitor watches the hour pass. Then it tries again, lifting the knee a little too high, setting the foot down flat and wide, and waiting a half-breath for the ground to decide.

By the second day it walks like a child of the tableland. This delights it more than most things it has done.

## What it does not do

It has, in principle, a great deal of power, and it spends the first week deciding how little to use.

It does not stop the pumps. The pumps are why four or seven or eleven cities are still standing, and behind the cities are a great many people, each with a private, particular life. It does not lift the tableland's slackness, though it could. It stands in the oldest districts, where the alleys run longer than the buildings allow, and it feels the slack the way one feels a loosened knot in a rope that holds a load. It has tightened such knots elsewhere and been thanked and later regretted it. It does not reconcile the ledgers. It does not count the chimneys. It does not explain the trees to anyone, and it does not explain the people to the trees.

A benevolent power's first temptation is to improve things. Its second is to be seen improving them. It resolves to avoid both and looks for a third way of being useful, which, it suspects, is mostly a matter of finding out what has already been done.

## The question put to Third Beam

The first thing it asks is a question, and it asks it of a person.

It finds Third Beam, Ashgate, at the end of a night shift, sitting with her back to the great beam as the eastern crews do, one palm flat on the cylinder housing. She is humming the low note of her city. She does not look up. She has felt someone approaching for a minute already, and she has taken the footsteps for an apprentice's.

"If the pumps were no longer needed," the visitor says, and it has learned to lean close, "if the roots could be taught to drink, and the water kept itself away from your streets, would you want that?"

Third Beam hums for a while. The beam rises behind her. The *whump* has already come and gone, and now it comes again.

"My mother's note is in this housing," she says at last. "Hers, and her mother's before. If the beam stops, whose is it then?"

"The ground's, perhaps."

"Then ask the ground." She points down, the way a census-taker points, without a number. "Not me. I only keep the time."

## What the ground said

The visitor lies down on the mat, on its back, in the open plain, at the edge of a wet ring where the upward rain has left the moss drenched and cold. It lays its palms on the surface and does not speak. It only makes itself the way a question is: open at one end.

The print of its body begins to fill in. The moss comes up around the shoulders and the heels, and the visitor feels the tide, and beneath the tide the enormous patience of forty, sixty, a hundred feet of what has died and is still, slowly, deciding what to become. It feels the pump-strokes as words, each one a small, heavy syllable dropped from the cities and received. It feels that the ground has learned the language perfectly and has never once been asked whether it likes it.

The answer does not come as speech. It comes as the shape of the last hour of a long attention.

The visitor finds that the ground does not want the pumps to stop. It has grown into their rhythm the way a river takes on the shape of its bridge. But it also does not want to be ignored. And in the eastern cities, where the sound arrives before the piston moves, the visitor understands something no engineer ever did. The lateness and the earliness are not decay. They are the ground leaning toward the beat, listening ahead, trying to be ready. That is why the east is ahead and the west is exact. The ground is holding the door.

The alleys that run on longer than their buildings are the same gesture. Somewhere in two hundred years of noise the ground began making room, and it has been making room ever since, without being asked, for anyone the loud world had no place for. The lodgers are not a fault in the plateau. They are what the plateau does when it has a great deal of soft depth and no one to say it may stop. The visitor lies there a long time, and when it rises the moss has grown in over its outline as though it had never been there.

## The cairns

It walks out with the peat-cutters for three days, in the woven shoes, in a slow line. On the third evening, at a camp where a cairn stands of small granite chips gone dark with soot, it asks an old cutter what the cairn is.

The cutter touches the top stone. "This is how tall it was."

The visitor looks at it. And it understands, all at once and with real astonishment, that these people have kept a ledger after all, and the only honest one on the plateau. Every stone stands for a column of peat: the depth of the ground they took out of the ground. The cutters do not count, because counting would be a kind of lie about what a thing is. So each season, at each camp, they carry one chip down from the domes and add it, and a cairn is a record, in the ground's own colours, of what was taken and that it was noticed.

That is the answer to the question the whole tableland has been asking, the one the hammermen hum under their breath and the bird sings in the pause between strokes: is it enough? The visitor cannot say. Enough is not its to say. But it can see that it is honest, and that the ground has, in its slow way, received it.

## What it does

The visitor does three small things and one larger one.

It pays rent. It presses a nail into the flank of a tree, one of the great fluted ones, and feels the bark begin to close over it. It puts a coin, a real one, out of nowhere it can afterwards remember, in a tin on a windowsill in the oldest district, and in the morning finds the tin empty and a square of grey cloth folded in its place. It carries a chip of granite up from a dome, lichen and all, and sets it on top of the cairn. It does these things the way a good guest washes a cup.

Then, on the last evening, it goes to the eastern city and stands in the pump hall among the crews, all sitting with their backs to the beams. The bird is singing in a hollow chimney-trunk. The visitor waits for the pause, and when it comes it does the one thing it has decided to do.

For a single stroke, in every city, the Seen and the Heard agree.

The beam rises and the sound comes with it. The hammermen feel it in their floors: the note and the motion are one thing, exactly, as they might have been in any ordinary place. It lasts as long as one stroke. In four or seven or eleven cities the clerks look up from the parallel columns and see, for that moment, that the two would have matched. Not one of them draws a line. Not one of them says a word. The bird, which sings a little before the gap, sings a little before the gap, and the beam answers it, and the tableland goes on as before.

The visitor does not repair anything. It shows that agreement is possible, briefly, as a kindness, so that no one need wonder whether the slack is a wound. It is a way of living. It could be otherwise for a moment and go back, and that is a good sign.

## After

By morning the shadow has gone. Nothing on the plateau has changed. The sound arrives out of order again in the east, the road takes a day and a half one way and two hours the other, and the rain falls upward in its narrow columns near the domes.

The tin on the windowsill holds a nail.

Out at the edge of the wet ring, an old cutter comes to the cairn at dusk to add his stone and finds one already on top: a granite chip, freshly set, its lichen already orange and older than the cairn. It is the wrong size, or perhaps the right one. He looks at it for a long time. Then he touches it, and says, to no one, or to the ground, or to whoever is listening:

"This is how tall it was."

And beneath him the mat gives a little, and takes the answer, and, as it has always done, decides.
