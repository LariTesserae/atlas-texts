# What Came to the Long Green Works

## The first notice

The attention does not come down. There is no sky here to come down from, only glazed leaf and shaft-light. It arrives as a pause.

Somewhere in the canopy, in the slow wave of clicks rolling over the panes, one pane holds its notch half a beat too long, then advances. The wave closes over the gap and goes on. Beneath it a flock of moths wheels, unprompted, and wheels back. That is all. Only one person hears it.

She is old, as hands go. The chalk bloom on her arms has grayed to the elbow. Her crew's name for her is a pattern of taps, two, a rest, three. I set it down only approximately, because she would not thank me for exactness. She is walking back through a colonnade of hexagonal trunks with a knotted cord in her pocket and her eyes half lowered, not counting, as custom says. She stops, though she doesn't know why. She listens with her whole face, the way people do who have spent their lives listening to machines.

The visitor watches her for a long while before doing anything. This is the first thing it does.

## What it considers

It could do a great deal. It knows this, and the knowing is the difficulty.

It could bring water. But the works are a closed loop of spirit, root to trunk to breath to frost to root, and rain would rot the trunks and drown the mills. Nor is anyone thirsty. They drink the thin sweet spirit and are level and unhurried. The vials are not a wound. They are a way of remembering that something else exists. To fill the basin would be to fix a problem no one has.

It could straighten the counts, the belts that deliver a pod before it is sent, the aisles where shadows fall toward the light. It looks closely at these and sees that they are not errors laid over the works. They are the seams the works are stitched along. To smooth one might be to iron out a fold that is also a hinge. It does not yet understand the hinge. Understanding is owed before repair.

It could read the glyphs. This tempts it most and is the most dangerous. Here what is written comes true, and a reader like the visitor does not merely read. Whatever it read aloud, or even privately, might harden into law across a country of leaf. Two crews tried, long ago, with small clumsy eyes, and the marks are a little different now. The visitor is not small or clumsy, and it does not trust its wishes to be light enough to write. It leaves the glyphs alone.

It could stop the pruners. It follows one for a day and a night, at the pruner's pace, and watches it stand at a trunk and consider and cut. It sees what the cut removes. It is not the stem. It is the place where the stem would have disagreed with itself, the second number, the thing that could not be held in two ways at once. Sometimes, rarely, it is a person leaning too far toward a contradiction. A crew of six becomes five, and no one grieves, because no one remembers six.

That is the harm. The visitor is careful about naming it, because it has already seen that the person is not destroyed. The oldest courses of the floor keep every cut thing, woven in as pattern, a vein that goes on without you. The pruned are kept. What is lost is the mourning. The works have learned to survive their own contradictions by forgetting them, and in forgetting they have made a people who do not weep, whose vials are the last wet thing they own.

The visitor decides it will not stop the pruners. It suspects the pruners are why the rest still stands. But it can do something about the forgetting, if it is gentle.

## What it does

First it knocks.

It does not speak, because voices arrive wrong here and it does not want to arrive wrong. It finds a trunk and knocks twice, the dry small sound that means I am here. It has learned the idiom from listening. Out in the green dimness, from a distance that will turn out to be wrong, the knock comes back. The old hand, standing very still, has answered. The visitor understands that it has been let in, the way a guest is let in who has wiped its feet.

Then it takes the second count.

She walks the colonnade back toward the pod-racks with her cord unused in her pocket, and the visitor walks with her, unseen, and counts for her. It counts what she may not count, the second number, the one the pruners cut. It holds both numbers at once. This is a thing no mill-hand can do, and it is not clever. It is only wide enough. At the far end it does not tell her the sum. It taps a pattern on the last trunk, one she has never heard, and after a moment she understands it as a word: both. Both were true. You need not choose.

She stands with her hand on the trunk. She says nothing, since mill-hands say little. But her mouth moves the way a mouth moves around a new taste.

Then it goes to the dark aisle, where the shadows thicken toward the light and the leaners stand.

It does not pull anyone out. They are calm, their eyes are open, and they turn their faces a little to any light and then away. The visitor sits in the aisle for what would be a day. It leans, briefly, the way they lean, as an act of courtesy, and finds that they are not leaning into darkness. They are leaning toward a source. The dark here is a kind of light run backward, and they are listening at its door. It asks by knocking, gently, whether they are lost. One taps back a single slow pattern, and the visitor understands it as: no. We are listening. Do not be afraid for us.

Beside it on a leaf a drop of water is drying, left by some passing hand. The visitor watches the leaf. Something draws the drop in, slowly, with gratitude and without hurry. It is not gone by accident. It is drunk. The visitor will make sure the old hand learns this, in the only way that is safe, by letting her see it next time with her own eyes.

Then it goes to the center.

The oldest hall is not a room. It is a place where the weave shows its pattern across the seams of the world, a leaf-vein running from a frond on the ceiling down a wall, across the floor, into a shadow, and on. The visitor stands where the vein passes through it, and it is a place the vein passes through, and there is the old kindness in that. It recognizes what lives here, though it will not name it, and the not-naming is a courtesy.

The maker's marks lie around it in the floor, house-sized, softened with felt. It could read them. It does not. It stands among them like a person in a library who has been asked to be quiet and finds that the quiet is the point. What lives here has been alone with its own writing for longer than anyone has counted, and no one has read it because no one could be trusted to read without changing it. The visitor's gift is to be one who could and doesn't. It knocks twice on the oldest trunk, I am here, and the answer comes from everywhere at once and is exactly the right knock.

They keep each other company for a time. That is all. It is more than it sounds.

## The sixth

In the oldest course, where the weave keeps its pruned, the visitor finds a pattern it has been looking for. It finds it because it holds the second number and knows the shape of what the first number left out. It is a tap, quick and uneven, one-one-one, a rest, two. A person who laughed at the wrong things, and who was sixth in a crew of six.

She is not gone. She is woven in. The visitor does not pull her out, because it does not know that she would wish it, and the vein around her is warm and not unpleasant. But it can carry her pattern up.

It returns to the pod-racks in the long cool evening and taps the pattern, softly, on the trunk that holds the old hand's rack.

She is lying in her pod with her eyes open and her vial in her hand. The tap comes down through the wood into her shoulder, and something in her body knows it before her mind does. She sits up. She does not remember six. She does not remember a laugh, or a name. But her face does something her face has not done for a very long time. Her eyes fill, and for a moment the wet stands in them, and then it spills. She has not known that water could come from inside.

She unstoppers the vial and holds it beside her cheek, as though to compare the two kinds of wet. She looks at the light in it. Then, slowly, she knocks twice on the trunk. I am here. And then, because she has been given a word for it: both.

Out in the green dimness another knock answers, from a distance that will turn out to be wrong.

## Afterward

Very little changes, and the visitor counts this a success.

The pruners walk on. The counts still disagree. The shadows in the dark aisles still thicken toward their light. The mills turn, and the panes tick their wave across the continent of leaf. The old hand still does not count on the way back, but she has a new tap now, and she teaches it to her crew of five, and they teach it to the next crew they meet. In a generation there will be a rest in a certain rhythm, a place where the tapping pauses on purpose, and children will learn it without knowing why. A pause where someone was.

Some crews will ignore it. The visitor allows that. It is not there to make anyone grieve, only to let grief be possible.

Before she sleeps that night the old hand takes her vial, unstoppers it, and lets one drop fall on a leaf beside her pod. Not an offering, exactly. A way of saying: I know what this is, and you did once too. It is not clear whom she means. The visitor, standing unseen at the trunk, does not need it to be clear.

In the morning the drop is gone.

## What it leaves

It leaves the way it came, as a pause. One pane in the slow wave holds its notch half a beat longer than its neighbors and then advances, and the moths turn once and settle. A pruner stands at a trunk, considering, and the blades part, and the stem ends where it always ended. The visitor watches the cut heal. It holds the fact of the stem.

It will come back, but not to change anything. It will come back the way one returns to a garden one did not plant, to stand in it, and to be answered from a distance that will turn out to be wrong, by a knock that is right.
