# The Company in the Columns

A wise power looking at this place would first ask what is wrong with it, and would be slow to find an answer.

The land is unfinished but not unhappy. The people are lean but not hungry. Their silence is a skill, not a wound. The machines are running without anyone commanding them, and the plants and wires have reached an understanding older than the people who tend it. Almost every way of helping would be a way of talking too loudly.

So it did not descend, and it did not announce itself. It did not tell the Lean what the drum was for, or that the fault would one day part the fence entirely, or that their sentence in moss and blade was being read by something with time to read it. Nothing it knew was worth more than what they were already doing with not knowing.

It came the way the tide comes, from below and slowly, and it arrived as fog.

## The season of learning

For one turn of the seasons it only learned the manner. It rose with the cloud each dawn and was caught on the screens with everything else. It felt the drift along the bus-bars and the slow creep of charge across the insulators, and it learned to read the fur. It learned that a prickle at the nape means the screens are running heavy, and that the afternoon mist will be thin when the moths sit close to the casings.

It learned that the Lean steer and do not clear, and this moved it more than anything else. In all its watching it had rarely seen a people so certain that they were part of the sentence and not its authors.

It watched the combs go out at dawn. It watched a boy roll a cushion plant off a bus-bar stand with his boot, and watched the plant settle a hand's width away, still whole.

## Three small things

Then it did three things, and no more.

**The first was punctuation.** One morning a moss front on a rectifier-hall insulator was drifting a degree too far toward a cracked ceramic stand. A woman named in no record came with her bone comb and found it already nudged back. She stood a while with the comb in her hand. Then she did what she would have done anyway, a slightly finer version of it, and went home. She could not have said whose correction she was finishing. The conversation between the plants and the wires had gained a new speaker, and the speaker was careful to say only what the sentence already meant.

**The second was a thing it declined to do.** In midsummer the count of lamps in a certain tarn fell by a third. Under the power's attention the cause was plain: a plate of grey-green stone, split from the scarp by the last tremor, had half-choked the seep that fed the basin. It could have lifted the plate as easily as breathing. It did not. A young man was sent to sit by the tarn and listen, and he sat two days, and on the third he noticed the water on the far side was not moving. He rolled the plate away with a pry-bar of bone and iron. He came back with a report, and the report was his. The power judged that this was worth more to the Lean than a full tarn: the tarn was theirs to keep, and they had kept it.

**The third was a sound.** On the open bench, on an afternoon of thin mist, a child shouted. The children still test the cloth of the air, and the plateau took the voice, small and close, as it always did. This time, once, it was given back. There was no echo, only the shout again, faint and exact, from somewhere across the basin, the way a voice might return from a person standing just out of sight and pleased to be asked. The child went still, then laughed, startled, and shouted again. There was nothing. The power did not repeat the gift, since a gift that can be summoned becomes a god, and the Lean had no use for one. The child told no one, or told one friend, who did not believe it. Years later the child, grown old and holding a comb, would sometimes shout across a basin on a thin afternoon and wait, and be content with the silence.

## The drum

The winding house was the one place the power lingered.

It went down the cable slowly, as fog goes down a gully. The stories were each a little right. The cable held nothing closed and lowered nothing to the world. It ended against the bare face of the fault, where a copper tongue lay pressed to the rock, and the rock was not silent. It ticked. At intervals of years, too slow for any ear, it made a small settling sound that was the whole of the uplift saying, *still going*. The drum had spent its long life turned to hear this, faithfully and without being asked.

The power understood that the fence across the fault was the same ear, made by other hands in another way. The fence parts as the ground parts, and its hum is the fault speaking through steel to fingertips. The Lean had been listening to the ground all along, with their palms, and had taken it for their own voice.

It considered mending the fence, or replacing it, or warning them that the strands would one day give. It chose not to. They had learned silence once, and they would learn again what came after the wire. They were good at it. But it made itself one more listener at the drum. When the last strand finally sang its highest note and broke, the ground would still be heard, by someone, and nothing the Lean sent down the line in the meantime would go to no one at all.

## The hour before dawn

On the last morning it came to the basin with the most wire, at the dark edge of the day, when the charged mist stood in its straight pillars like the columns of a hall.

Four of the Lean were there, motionless, hair lifted, breath rising in straight lines. The power did not lift its hair, having none. It stood among the columns as one more pillar, as the oldest of them had always said someone did. It was neither guest nor host. It kept them company, which was all that had ever been asked of whatever stood there.

Near the end, an old woman at the edge of the group lifted one hand and held it out into the column beside her, palm up, at about the height of a wrist. She did not look at anything. She waited. The hair along her forearm rose in a slow wave, though the mist stood dead still and the field, by her own reading, was drifting the other way.

She said nothing. She did not press two fingers to anyone's skin. She only held the hand out a moment longer, then lowered it and turned toward the ridge, where the sun was about to clear the edge. In the dim light her face looked like a needle that had stopped: not moved, not troubled, aligned.

The columns thinned and fell. The cloud rose from below to be caught, as it does every morning, and the power went up with it and was not seen again.

Behind it the plateau kept leaning the same way. A moss front on an insulator advanced a hair. A blade of rosette turned a few degrees toward the wire. Somewhere down the line a fingertip rested on a strand and felt, faint as the first tick of a cooling stone, that it was not the only one listening. It thought nothing of this, and went on.
