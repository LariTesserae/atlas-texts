# The Attention

It did not arrive so much as notice, the way the snails notice the damp: a slow turning of something toward something. Nothing marked the moment. A wide, unhurried regard came to rest on the Bowl of Lit Salt and the one who keeps its count, and it stayed there a long time before doing anything at all. A wise power knows that a place like this is easily ruined by help.

## What It Weighed

It considered giving the bowl more rain. It saw at once that the rain was the point. The salt regrows its polygons a little differently each time, the pool creeps east a hand's width, and the snails lift their shells like lanterns because lanterns are rare. A wetter bowl would be a different bowl, and this one was nearly finished, so it left the sky alone.

It considered explaining the pulse to the keeper, to the lizards, to anyone. But the birds circle low and silent because they do not know. The not-knowing is load-bearing, like a keystone. To take it out would leave a fact, and the fact would be smaller than the pulse.

It considered company for the keeper. Then it looked at the evidence: the circuit kept faithfully, the cups turned so the rims wear evenly, the tools left ready in their niches, the face lifted into the green each dawn. This was not the shape of loneliness. Loneliness is often what a guest sees when it looks at a solitude it has not yet learned to read. The keeper looks at travelers as weather, and the power decided this was not an insult. Weather is what one takes seriously and does not need to talk to.

It considered the reflection. Once a year the keeper stands in the mirror-water and looks at itself a long time without, perhaps, knowing. The power felt the pull of that one strongly. It would have been easy to say *that is you*. But recognition that is handed over is not recognition, and the keeper had already been looking, patiently, for longer than the power had been watching. Who was it to hurry a look that had lasted centuries?

Last, it considered its own presence, and saw that this was the largest intervention of all. So it decided to come the only way the place allowed: as a traveler who does not stay.

## The Day

It walked across the flat brown country until the ground ended. It went down the wall by the ledges the water had rounded, and it left no fresh break in the stone. It reached the floor before dawn, on a morning the calendar had already told it the keeper would be kneeling at the fluting on the western arc.

It did not approach. It stood far off on the pan, where the salt went *tick* under its feet and then stopped, and when the algae flashed emerald it lifted its face into the green. The keeper, forty paces away and already a flaw in the light, did not turn. But for those forty minutes there were two faces tilted up into the only vivid color in the bowl, where for as long as anyone could say there had been one. The power did not count this as a gift. It was only company, and company in the keeper's own currency: attention, offered without a word.

When the green went to soot, it did no work of its own. It watched the keeper's work. The keeper emptied the cups and turned them, swept the grinding hollow with the bird's wing, lifted the hand-sized stone, set it down, and lifted it again. When the keeper went on to the far wall to press its mat to the damp, the power crossed to the grinding hollow. It lifted the stone and turned it once, as one turns a sleeping animal so it doesn't stiffen, and set it back a quarter-turn from where the keeper had left it. It was the smallest possible trespass, and the only one it allowed itself. Whoever tends everything is tended by no one. For one morning, one thing in the keeper's care would be found already cared for.

At noon it sat against the wall in the shadowless glow and did not search for the keeper. It let the pulse come around the circle, ten thousand small mouths turning to the sun, and it saw the figure appear on the cream pan, outlined in white fire, eyes open, lips moving. It looked at the keeper the way one looks at someone at prayer, sideways, without needing the words. What it understood then it did not write anywhere. The pulse stayed unexplained, and afterward it was, if anything, more so.

## The Gift

Between the pulse and the breath, in the long gold slackening of the afternoon, the power did the one thing it had come to do.

It went to the niches, where the flint scraper and the bone awl lie tucked back as if to be found again. By the custom of the place these are for whoever comes next, and it was, for the moment, whoever came next. It took the awl. From the foot of the cliff it took a snail shell that had fallen and cracked and was no use to the crescents, and it pierced the shell with the awl, slowly. Then it stood by the pool and waited.

When the wind crossed the rim and the whole bowl sounded its one low note, it listened past the note for the faint second sound, the one an octave higher and half as long that always leaves the silence larger. It heard it, or believed it did. Then it worked the shell, a little more here, a little less there, until the small hollow of it would speak that pitch when air was drawn across it. It cleaned the awl and returned it to its niche. It set the shell in a second niche near the pool, mouth up, as the keeper sets its own empty shells.

It did not blow the shell, and it did not answer the second note. An answer would have made the note a conversation, and the note had been fine for centuries as a question.

Before dark it climbed out over the lip with the swifts pouring past it, and crossed the brown country, and did not stay.

## Afterward

At the turn of the year the keeper made its round of the niches, collecting the tools nobody had taken, cleaning them, setting them somewhere different. It found one it had not made. It turned the shell over in its hand as it turns the stone, at length, and then did what it does with everything: it set the shell down somewhere else, mouth up, ready.

Whether it ever raised the shell to its lips is not known. It was seen, later, at the pool at the hour of the breath, standing very still, holding something.

Some years after, in the late afternoon, the bowl sounds one low note, then a second, higher and half as long, and then, on certain evenings, a third: dry and small and a little flat, from somewhere up in the swifts' pockets, where no one has been seen to climb. No one has asked whose it is. The lizards press flat to the stone. The tree rings a little louder. The silence afterward is larger than before, and the bowl, which keeps its own count, counts it and marks it no differently from any other day.
