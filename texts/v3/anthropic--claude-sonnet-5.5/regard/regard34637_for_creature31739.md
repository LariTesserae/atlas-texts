# What Came to the Cistern

## The Shadow That Did Not Pass

It began as a cloud, and for a while everyone was sure it was one.

The vault went gold-green and dim. Sixty faces lifted, the scrapers stopped mid-stroke, the listeners rose from the grate, and the fish scattered out of the light. The village held its breath together, as it always did.

But the shadow did not thin. They reached the end of the breath, and the end of the pump-stroke that paces it, and still the windows stayed dim. A child let go first, a small bubbling gasp, and no one hushed her. Then the rest of them let go. They hung in the warm water with their faces turned up, breathing the old way, and the shadow stayed, and it did not feel like weather. It felt like being looked at by something that had come a very long way and was in no hurry to say so.

The oldest listener would later say it felt like the moment in a room when someone you love walks in behind you and does not speak, because they can see you are busy being alive.

## What It Weighed

The visitor was large and wise and kind, and so it began by doing almost nothing, which is the hardest thing for such a being to do. It looked. It let the pumps come into it, the three thuds, the failing bearing, the clatter of governors, the hiss of the singing bubbles. It counted the tide twice through before it allowed itself to think.

It saw what it could do, because it could do nearly anything.

It could mend the engines. The three pumps were old past reckoning, and one day, in ten thousand years or a hundred thousand, they would stumble, and the warm room would cool and go dark. It could fix that in a moment, and the people would never know. But it saw that they did know, in their way. Their whole religion was that the count goes on until it doesn't, and they had made a home inside that fact. A world with no ending is a different world, and it was not clear to the visitor that it would be a better one for them.

It could open the grate and end the argument among the listeners. It could wake the Under, or tell the people what the Under was. It could lift all sixty of them into the wide light of a living world, where there was more of everything, more people, more green, more sky.

It weighed each of these and set each one down. The people did not need saving. They were not lost. They were, if anything, unusually found. Each of them knew what their hands were for. They had a wall to scrape, a wheel to grease, a place in the breathing of the room. They had a saying, *you do not open a door on a song*, and the visitor thought that a people who had made a law of that had already understood something it might have spent an age teaching.

So it asked itself the only question that mattered: *What does one do, when arriving at a home that is whole?*

It decided one behaves as a guest.

## The Count

It learned the count first. For three tides it kept no shape and made no sound. It took in on the upstroke, held through the downstroke, and breathed out with the bubbles, though it had no lungs and no need. It let the water-hammer climb the walls and stopped for it with everything else, and it noticed, with something like delight, that even it was listening to that older voice with the same bowed attention as the fish.

Then, on the fourth tide, it found a wall.

It was an east curtain, high on a terrace, where the grazers had passed a day before and the rust had not yet come back. A young scraper named Oru was working the last of it with a bone blade, alone, badly tired. Beside Oru, in the water, a second pair of hands began to work. Oru did not startle. She would say afterward that it felt exactly like when her mother used to scrape beside her without saying anything. They worked down the length of the wall together, and near the end the last scale came away under the visitor's hand, and the steel beneath was bare and bright, throwing back a whole coin of window-light.

That is the moment the scrapers live for, the shine between the grazing and the rust.

The visitor did not hold the moment open. It could have, but the moment was the point, and a shine that lasted forever would only have been a shiny wall. It let the light sit there on the steel for exactly as long as it always sits, one breath and a little more, with a young woman watching it who understood, then, why the old ones said the walls want to be seen. Then the first thin orange bloom crept in along the seam, and the wall began, gently, to be a wall again.

Oru laughed aloud in the water and rapped something rude and happy on the nearest pipe. Half the vault answered.

## The Quarter

Only one person in the cistern looked directly at the visitor and did not look away.

She was very old and her skin had gone the deep green of a well-used leaf. She drifted on the lower terrace where she always drifted, a beat late to everything, and when it came to her she turned her head and said, in tide-speech, after the pause it took the words to come around their corner:

"You're late too."

The visitor, which had not expected to be surprised, was surprised. She had put her hands on a thing that turned once a quarter and had gone with it, and it had left in her a small permanent room where the timing was different. Through that room she could hear it plainly. The others felt the shadow. She felt it arrive.

"Yes," it said, in the only language she could hold, which was mostly vowels. "I think I am."

"They bring me the best fan-leaf," she said. "I never asked."

"No."

"I'm not unhappy."

"I know," the visitor said. "I came to see if anyone here was. I'm glad to be wrong."

She was quiet for a long time, and then she said, kindly, the way you speak to someone very young: "Do you want to see the wheel?"

It considered this longer than it had considered anything, and then it said no. "It isn't mine to touch. But thank you. That was a generous thing to offer."

She nodded, and they drifted side by side while the pumps did their slow work. It was the closest thing to a long conversation the visitor had all that week, and neither of them said much.

## The Grate

It went down to the deep floor on a downstroke and lay along the warm stone beside the oldest listener, who was too polite to open her eyes.

It put its attention to the iron, and it heard the hum.

Whatever it heard, it kept. That is the only part of the story it never told, not to the Quarter, not to the wall, not to the hammer. Those who watched said it lay very still for the length of eleven tides, longer than any listener had ever managed, and when it rose there was a stillness on it like someone leaving a sickroom, or a cradle.

It did not open the grate. It did not hum back.

But the oldest listener, who had heard for sixty years the sound of someone who has been waiting a long time and learned to enjoy it, told the others afterward that the tune had not changed at all. What had changed was that she was no longer the only one who knew it was there. She said this was worth more than any answer, and she slept that night, for the first time in years, all the way through the tide.

## The Turn of the Tide

At the turn of the tide the sluices opened and the roar rose to cover everything. In that roar a few of them rapped, low and soft, on the pipes, the old dead-knock, the thing that is said only once a generation: *maybe we are what it makes while it dreams.*

Under the noise, from everywhere and from the iron itself, came a single answering knock. It was not loud. It was in the count exactly, neither early nor late, and it said, in the fewest words pipe-speech has:

*Good sound.*

They did not know who had said it. Some thought the Under. Some thought the visitor. A few thought the walls. It did not matter. It was neither a confirmation nor a denial, and it left the great question exactly as open as it had been. It only said that if they were the sounds of a dream, the dream was glad of them. The knockers went quiet, and something in the rows of green faces, an old held tightness they had not known was a tightness, let go.

## What It Left

Then the water-hammer began at the bottom of the dome, the sequence of iron knocks that climbs the walls, and the visitor did what it had learned to do. It stopped. It listened with everyone else, the fish and the eels, the sixty green people and the old woman who was late, and it bowed, as much as a being like that can bow, to the one voice in the room that was older than it was.

When the hammer finished it was gone.

The windows brightened. The coins of light slid across the floor. The failing bearing shrieked on, uncorrected, because it was the only one in the room singing solo and a good room needs one. The grate hummed. The Under passed beneath the engines without hurry, and the low fronds flared blue-white along its length. The pumps went on with their long slow beat, and a fourth one, lower, slower, arrived once in every eleven or twelve strokes, and was, for the first time in a long while, a little less alone.

On the east curtain there was a faint orange bloom where a bright wall had been. Oru went and scraped it, and it came clean again, and she caught the shine a second time. She told no one for a whole tide. Then she told everyone, in a rap so quick and so pleased that even the pulse-fish turned.

The count went on.
