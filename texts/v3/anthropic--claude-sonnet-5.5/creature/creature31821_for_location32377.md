# Who Lives in Ahn-Vessel

Start with the smallest residents, because they are the most numerous and the city is mostly their doing.

## The Grazers

**Lantern-mites** live in the trillions and are born, mated, and dead within a season. A single one is a pale, translucent crustacean with a body like a grain of rice and a fan of feeding legs that combs the microbial film in tiny strokes. When it feeds, a gland behind its eyes-that-aren't-eyes lights a soft green. When it is full, the light goes to blue. When it is frightened, it goes white for one instant, and if enough of them do this together the whole street flashes and the carpet pours off the walls in a slide. The people call this a *rinse*, and it is not dangerous, only wet and startling.

They do not know they are part of a city. They know the film, the seep, the warmth of a neighbor's back. Mites clump in the corners of stairs to molt, and the discarded shells collect in drifts that the beetles later gather. In the thin phase of the fog cycle, when the light dims and the feeding slows, the mites go dormant in tight glowing knots along the lattice seams, like embers that never quite die. In the thick phase they bloom and the lower terraces run with slow green tides.

Small children are given a mite in a cup of wet cloth to keep for a day, and the child learns from it what the whole city is: a thing that eats, glows, and does not mind being watched.

**Sill-snails** are the mites' quiet cousins, gray coils no bigger than a knuckle, who live in the window-cuts and rasp the mineral scale from the lattice in long pale lines. Their trails are read as a kind of graffiti by the ones who read with their fingers. Tenders can tell from the pattern of rasping which wall is drinking too hard and which is starving.

## The Choir

The **choristers** are the city's voice, and they are less like animals than like weather that has learned to sing.

Each one is long-limbed and soft, a body like a strip of wet clay pulled into a fist-sized torso, four arms that end in wide, sticky pads, and a tail that curls twice around whatever it hangs from. The skin is smooth and faintly tacky. The throat is a pouch that swells and slackens, translucent, so that in the dimmest light you can see it fill with breath and glow faintly from the mites that cling to it. They have no real faces. The head is a rounded wedge with two mild black eyes set wide, and a mouth that opens only to feed or to sing.

They hang in clusters of hundreds, sometimes thousands, wound around the tension-lines like grapes on a vine. The clusters are not random. Each cluster is a *voice*, tuned to a pitch, and the pitches interlock. There are the deep **drone-hangers** in the great spans between towers, whose throats produce a note so low it is more pressure than sound. There are the mid-voiced **weavers** who carry the melody in slow turning phrases. There are the high **needle-choirs** in the upper arches, whose sound is thin and bright and sometimes cruel. Between them the whole city is held in a chord that changes with the hour, the fog, and the mood of the mountain.

They eat the midges that breed in the gutters, and the drip-film that condenses on the cables. They mate in the thick phase, when the noise is greatest, and lay their eggs in strings in the warm cisterns under the terraces. The young are dark, finned things that live in the water for two years and then climb out one night, all together, and find the cables. People gather for this night and hum quietly, and the first climbing young are answered by the old, who lower their pitch to welcome them.

When the fault shudders, the choristers do not scream. They stop. It happens all at once, the whole basin, hundreds of thousands of throats closing in the same instant, and the silence that follows is so large that people say they can hear the stone settling. It lasts a breath, sometimes two. Then a single voice starts up, always a deep one, and the rest join, and the city goes on.

Nobody knows how they know a shudder is coming before the floor moves. The Listeners say the choristers feel it in the cables first, but the pause comes slightly early even so.

## The Others in the Dark

**Mist-bats** are small, soft-furred, dun-colored animals with long ears and a habit of hanging upside down from the bridge undersides in clusters of ten or twenty, damp and steaming faintly in the cold. They hunt the flying midges above the choristers, and their clicks, high and dry, run over the top of the choir like a fine rain on a tin roof. The people can hear a bat's approach and duck without thinking. Bats are considered neither lucky nor unlucky. They are simply neighbors, and people say *pardon the ticking* when one passes close.

**Armored beetles**, called *haulers*, plod along the gutters in slow processions, each one the size of a loaf of bread, black and ridged, with plates that overlap like roofing. They carry grit in their mandibles and in the pouches under their shells to the growing edges of the lattice, and they deposit it in the cracks where the material is thirsty for minerals. Watching a line of haulers work, you see the city being fed by hand, in tiny mouthfuls. They are stubborn. If you stand in a hauler's path, it will stop and wait, and if you keep standing there, it will wait until you move or die. Children test this.

**Sump-eels** live in the deep drains and cisterns where all the water of the terraces finally comes to rest. They are blind, pale, slow, and as long as a person is tall, with soft flat heads and a sense for vibration that lets them feel a footstep on the grate above. They eat the fall of everything: shed shells, dead mites, a beetle that lost its grip, a bat that slept wrong. They are the city's last digestion. The people are careful of them and grateful, and the dead are carried down to the drains at the low edge of the basin, where the family hums the name of the one who has gone until the water moves. Then they leave. What happens in the dark below is not spoken about, but no one is afraid of it.

There are things in the lattice too. **Thread-worms** live inside the growing ribs, thin as hair, and no one has ever seen one whole. They are known by the small amber sparks they make in the dark of the material, the old processes that still light. Some Tenders believe the threadworms are the lattice, and others believe the lattice is what the worms are dreaming. It is a friendly argument, and it has gone on for generations.

## The People

They call themselves by a phrase that means something like *those who keep the water moving*, though the phrase is hummed, not spoken, and does not translate cleanly. Outsiders call them **Tenders**, and they have stopped minding.

They are built for the place. Compact, broad through the chest and ribs, with large lungs, short strong legs for stairs, and hands that are the most remarkable part of them: square, thick-skinned, with fingertips so finely ridged and so sensitive that they can read a handrail's cool and warm patterns in full darkness at a walking pace. Their eyes are small and deep-set and rarely used. Most keep them half-closed, or wrapped in a narrow band of cloth when moving through the brightest glow-lanes, since a sudden bloom of mite-light can leave a dark-adapted eye seeing ghosts for an hour. Their skin is a pale gray-brown, slick from oil, and permanently a little pruned.

They wear layer over layer. The outermost is a heavy oiled sheath woven from mat-fiber and waxed with eel-fat, which sheds water in beads and sounds like a soft drum when rain, or its equivalent, strikes it. Under that are thinner wraps, each with its own slow rate of soaking, so that a Tender may shed the outer two when they grow saturated and still have something warm beneath. The sleeves are cuffed and drawn tight. The boots are tall, resoled with beetle-shell for grip on the wet steps. Everyone carries a small hammer of bone or glass at the belt, the glass knapped from the green-gray rhyolite of the scarps, and the pings of the hammers on the junction stones are the city's second heartbeat.

They are never still. A Tender at rest is a Tender losing heat, and they move even in their sleep, in the sense that they sleep in pressed rows along the warm walls, where the lattice holds a gentle heat on command, and they shift and roll together without waking. Sleeping alone is a punishment, or a sign of grief.

### How they speak

Ordinary speech is useless in the roar, so they have built a language out of three things: **touch**, **rhythm**, and **hum**.

Touch-signs are pressed into the back of the hand or the shoulder, in a grammar of pressure, drag, and lift. A good touch-speaker can carry on a whole argument through a crowd without being heard by anyone. Rhythm is tapped on walls, rails, and each other, a shared code that can also be sent through the tension-lines to reach someone three towers away. And hum is the deep thing: pitches sung low, in the chest, to cut through the choir without competing with it. A greeting is two notes, low then lower. A warning is a rising slide. Sorrow is a held note that wavers. Laughter is a fast, breathy stutter of pitches that sounds, to outsiders, exactly like a sump-eel's gurgle, which is the joke.

Names are hummed. A child is given a name in the first month, a phrase of two or three notes chosen by the parents and then tested against the choristers: the family takes the infant to the lowest cables at the hour of the deep drone and hums the name, and if the hangers above shift, even slightly, in answer, the name holds. If they do not, the family tries another. No one says this is a religion. It is only how it is done.

### What they do

Everyone has a work, and most have several across a life.

**Drainwalkers** go down through the capillaries with long hooked rods and small lamps of cultured mat, clearing blockages of scale, shell, and dead things. It is the lowliest work and the most respected, and every child in Ahn-Vessel is expected to do a season of it.

**Tuners** climb the tension-lines and adjust them, hauling counterweights, tightening cords, and listening with their whole bodies for a wrongness in the chord. A bad tuner is one who hears only pitch. A good tuner also feels the animals on the line, and knows when a cluster of choristers is restless, or ill, or preparing to move.

**Stone-singers** keep the junction stones: they re-cut the resonant blocks that mark each crossing, and they retune them as the mountain shifts. A stone that goes flat is a stone that lies, and a traveler who trusts it may walk off a stair. Their work is precise and lonely, and they hum constantly, almost without knowing.

**Ribbers** tend the new growth on the terrace edges. The lattice extrudes there in dark, glistening ribs that stay soft for years, and they must be guided, propped, and kept damp, like the bones of a slow animal being born. A ribber may spend a decade on a single arch and never see it bear weight.

**Listeners** are the strangest. They sit at the fault walls, in the low cuts where the raw rock still weeps, and they place their palms and foreheads against the stone and wait. They are listening for the shudder before the choristers know it. Almost none of them ever hear it first. Their real work is to be the ones who breathe out first, after the silence, and start the hum that tells everyone else the terraces are still standing.

**Shepherds** of the mites move the great glowing tides from one terrace to another, opening and closing gutters, so that no street goes dark and no wall goes bare.

### What they eat, and how

There is no fire, so there is no cooking, but there is warmth, and the lattice can hold a temperature on command. Kitchens are warm walls: long slabs of lattice tuned to a slow simmer, where pastes of mite, mat-cake, and eel are pressed and left to soften for hours. Everything tastes faintly of iron and wet stone. Fermented things are prized: a sour, cloudy drink made from mat-culture, served warm in shell cups, and a dense black paste of aged eel that is spread on flat, chewy bread of ground beetle-shell and lichen-starch. Meals are shared, in the pressed warmth of the wall-rows, and no one eats alone.

### What they believe

Not much that could be called doctrine. They believe the city is alive, but they believe this the way one believes a friend is alive, without argument. They believe the mountain is listening. They believe that a name hummed in the drain is heard by something, though not what. And they believe that a place has been properly lived in only if someone has added to it, a street, a stair, a rib, a stone, however small.

When a shudder comes and the choir stops, every Tender stops too, mid-stride, mid-word, mid-bite. It is not fear. It is courtesy. For that breath, they are all simply listening with the animals, the walls, and the water, and the silence has a weight that people carry with them for a day afterward.

Then someone starts the hum, low, and the city starts over.

## The Whole of It

If you ask who lives in Ahn-Vessel, the answer is no single kind of creature. It is the mites and the snails and the haulers, the bats and the eels, the choristers hanging in their thousand-throated clusters, the Tenders threading between them with their hammers and their hums, the worms in the walls, and the walls themselves, drinking and humming and slowly leaning. None of them could live here alone, and none of them could say the city's name alone.

The Tenders have a phrase they hum to their children at night, in the warm rows, just before the sleepers stop shifting. It has no words. It is three notes, rising, then one falling. It means *you are held*, and it means *the water is moving*, and it means *listen.* The children hum it back, half-asleep, and around them the whole dark basin hums with them.
