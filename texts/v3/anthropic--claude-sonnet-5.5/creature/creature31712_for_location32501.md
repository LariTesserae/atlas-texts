# Who Keeps Ossary

## The People

They call themselves *the Kept*, or something near it. The word can also mean "the mended" or "the ones set in place," the way a stone is set. They do not use it often. Among themselves they use no name for the whole people at all, any more than you would name the air in a room.

They are a broad, level-eyed folk, pale in the way of people who have never known hard sun, with a faint green-grey cast at the knuckles and cuticles from handling wet copper. They wear their hair in long braids bound with wool cord, and the cord is undyed, so whole gatherings look like a single family. Their eyes have settled to the Held Hour. They never shade them, never squint, and never look up at the sky to guess the time. When they look at something they look all the way into it, calmly, as if the thing might be handed to them.

They walk with the head tilted a little downward, watching the dished stones of the causeways. Their soft shoes make almost no sound, and a whole procession of them crossing a meadow makes less noise than a single deer.

Their speech is slow and low and full of gaps. A question is often answered after several breaths, and the pause is not hesitation. They are letting the question finish arriving. Their language has words for *as it is* and *as it was kept*, and something like a third tense that the visitor takes days to hear. It is not the future. It is closer to *as it will be found*, the way you speak of a tool you have set down and know you will pick up in the same place. The word for a day is the same word as the word for a task.

Greeting is not spoken. Two people meeting on a causeway each touch the nearest stone, a wall, a marker, the dished edge of the road, and then look at each other. The touching says: *this is here, and so are you.*

## Names

A person's name is not their own. It belongs to a place in a compound: *Third Cistern*, *North Lintel*, *Comb-of-the-Long-Shed*, *Fern-Door*. Each compound has a fixed number of these places, and each place has one keeper. When a keeper stops, whether from age, from illness, or by walking down into the sealed lower storeys, as some do, the name does not stop. Someone new takes up the place and the name together, and within a season, or whatever they use for a season, no one can say the change was ever made.

Ask about it and they look at you kindly and confused, the way you would look at someone who asked why a wall was still called a wall after one of its stones was replaced. *It is the same block,* they say. They do not mean that it looks the same. They mean that it is the same.

You may find this chilling. They do not. It is a warmth to them. Nobody in Ossary is ever lost, because nobody is ever more than a place that is currently being held.

## Children, and the Count

There are children. You see them carrying moss-lined baskets between the compounds, or sitting in a row on a wall combing burrs from wool. They are quiet like the adults, but they look at strangers with more open wonder.

You will not find a birth. No one speaks of a lying-in, no one marks an age, and no one can say when a certain child arrived. If you count them, as a visitor with too much time will, you will find the number the same on the tenth day as on the first. You will find that a child you noticed on the third day, small and gap-toothed, is somehow always small and gap-toothed. You will decide you must be mistaken, that children only look alike, that you are seeing the strain of mountain air on your own attention.

Perhaps you are. The Kept do not correct your arithmetic. Asked where children come from, they say what they say about the deer and the cats: *It is returned.* They say it without mystery, the way you would tell a stranger which way the road goes.

## The Work

The day, if it can be called one, is a chain of tasks that hand off to one another with no seam between. Someone finishes tending a cistern and moves to combing wool. Someone else who was combing gets up, without any signal, and goes to the quarry face. No one gives orders. No one seems to decide. It is as if the work were a slow tide and each person a stone it moved through, and they are content to be moved.

The most respected work is repair. A cracked block is a small event in the compound. It is not hidden, and it is not an emergency. A mason will kneel by it for a long while, feeling the crack with the flat of the thumb. Then he will go to the quarry, wedge out a block of exactly the same measure from exactly the same face, and carry it back on a sledge. He and the others lower it into place. They chink the seams with fresh moss, and afterward they all touch the wall together, as if to seal a promise.

No one has ever said *this one is better.* No one has ever built anything larger. The idea of *more* does not appear in the language. When you press them, saying *couldn't you build a new house, a tower, a wall for the eastern meadow,* they consider it for a long moment, politely. Then they answer that the eastern meadow already has its wall, and you realize that it does. It has always had one. You just hadn't noticed.

Then there are those who do nothing you can see. These are the *listeners*. They sit on the roofs at the edge of each compound, cross-legged, eyes half-closed, hands on their knees. They are listening for the *thock* of the far mauls, the drip from the thatch, the wing-snaps of the birds. If any of these should fail or change, they would know. They have never had to say so. It is enough that someone is listening. The valley, they say, is more comfortable when it is heard.

## The Keepers of the Columns

Each rain column has a keeper. Each is a single person, usually old-seeming, who lives in the nearest compound and comes to the column daily to stand in it a while. They do nothing there. They do not pray, sing, or carry offerings. They stand with their faces lifted and their braids darkening, and the hiss of water fills the space around them.

When they come out, the moss at the rim of the column looks a little greener, though it cannot be. The keepers say that the column is *the valley's way of remembering it is a place.* If nobody stood in it, they say, it would go on raining. But it would be raining *alone.*

## Animals as Neighbors

The people do not think of the animals as property, or as wildlife, or as separate. They step around the spotted cats sleeping on the lintels, and they shift their loads so as not to jostle a cat's dream. They do not tame the hawks, which hover at the cloud's base like nails. But they glance up at them the way one glances at a clock: a brief, private confirming. If a hawk moved, they would stop.

The pack-beasts have the run of the causeways, and the beasts know where each load goes. A woman will set a bundle of combed wool on a beast's back, pat its neck, and turn away, and forty minutes later the bundle appears at the far compound, where someone is already waiting to lift it down. There is no exchange of thanks. It is understood that the beast is another kind of hand.

They eat, of course. Grain from the terraces, roots dug from the meadow turf, curds from the beasts, small fish from the ponds. They eat without ceremony. But you never see a slaughter. You never see a snare set, a bone gnawed clean, a carcass in the grass. When you press them, they say that what is taken is returned, and they say it of their own meals as calmly as they say it of the hawks'. You understand, slowly, that this is the whole of their theology and their economics: *take what is given; what is taken is given back.* They believe it wholly. So far as anyone can tell, the valley has never given them cause to doubt.

The salamanders in the ponds they treat with a shade more reverence than anything else. When a pale, fat, translucent shape rises to the surface, everyone nearby goes still, and for a breath the whole pond, people and water and reeds, holds together. Then the salamander sinks and the work goes on.

## The Lower Storeys

Below the living floors of every compound, sealed and filled, lie the older compounds. Nobody goes there in the ordinary way. But sometimes, when a keeper's place comes open, the one who held it will be seen walking to the oldest doorway, the low, windowless one at the base of the wall. They go in with a lamp and a bowl of water and do not come out. The door is not sealed behind them. It is simply not used again for some time.

Ask what is down there, and the Kept say, *The ones who held before.* Ask whether the ones who held before are still down there, and they will give you their long pause. Then they will say, gently, that the question is like asking whether the bottom stone of a wall is still holding the wall.

You will not get a clearer answer, and you should not want one. On some evenings, or what passes for evenings, when the light stays just as it has been all day, you may find yourself standing on a compound roof beside a listener. In the silence you will think you hear, very faintly, from under the thatch, from under the stone, from somewhere beneath the fitted courses, the soft *thock* of a maul.

The listener will not turn. She will only nod slightly, as if you had confirmed something.

## What They Make of You

Visitors are welcome and are not wondered at. You will be given a place at a hearth and a bowl of something warm and slightly bitter, and someone will show you where to sleep. No one will ask when you are leaving. Nobody will ask when you came. It will be understood, without discussion, that you are one of the *going-past*, as the other places are, and that this is neither a fault nor a distinction.

If you stay long enough, someone may give you a small task: a bundle to carry, a stretch of wall to chink. Do not take this lightly. You have been set in a place. They will not say so, and you may leave whenever you choose, and no one will stop you or remember, in any way you can measure, that you were there.

But at the top of the four thousand steps, as you turn to go, someone will be standing at the last marker with a hand on the carved face of a deer. They will not wave. They will lift their eyes to yours for one level moment, and then look away, back to the stone, and go on touching it, patiently, as though it were the one thing in the world that needed them to.
