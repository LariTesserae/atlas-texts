# Those Who Keep the Flat

## The people of the knuckle

Start with one band, since the flat has no larger unit. Call them the people of the Pale Knuckle, though they would never say the name aloud. Their word for themselves is a gesture: two flat hands drawn apart over the surface of water, then a fingertip pressed to the sternum. *Those who feel the floor.*

There are thirty-one of them this season. The number shifts with births, deaths, and the drift of cousins to other knuckles. They are a lean, low-shouldered people, broad through the chest and the backs of the hands. Their skin has the faint grey undertone of long dampness, and it does not crack. It never fully dries, and they have oil glands in the palms and soles that keep the skin supple against constant wet. Their eyes are large and set a little wide. Hearing is nearly useless to them, so sight has taken up the work, and they see well in dimness. They can read the glow of a pool at a mile and tell a bellwright bowl from a clatterer feeding-ring by the shape of the light alone.

Their ears are the most altered part of them, and the alteration is chosen. Every child has the ear canals plugged with wax and felt before they can walk. By adulthood the outer ear has thickened and folded shut, a small hard rosette of scar. They are not deaf. They are *closed*, and the closure is a mark of adulthood. A child who leaves the plugs in past the age of nine is said to have "stopped the door." A child who pulls them out is watched closely, because a person who listens to the flat for too long starts to hear words in it.

## How they speak

Speech is done with hands, but not only hands. The face carries half of it. Brows, cheeks, and the corners of the mouth are all grammar. A lifted brow with a closed fist is a question about the past. The same brow with an open palm is a question about the tide. An old person's face is a well-worn instrument, deeply creased along the lines of long conversation.

Names are gestures, given at the first tide after birth. A boy might be *Two-Fingers-Skimming*, for the way he was said to reach toward the water as an infant. A woman might be *Fist-Opening-Slowly*. The names are short because signing is tiring, and they get shorter over a lifetime. The very old are called by a single flick of the wrist.

There is one exception to the silence of their speech, and it is the most private thing they have. When two people are close, lovers or a mother and a nursing child or two friends at the end of a long day, they press their foreheads together, or lay a jaw against a shoulder, and *hum*. The plugs and the closed ears block the air, but the skull carries the sound. What passes between them is felt, not heard, a low buzz in the bone. It is not a language. It is closer to a texture, a way of saying *I am here and I am not afraid* without any words. Outsiders, if there were any, would not understand that the whole roaring flat has made this the most intimate act the people know. To be hummed to is to be let inside the noise.

## The knockers

Long speech across distance belongs to a small trade: the knockers. Each band has two or three, usually people with steady hands and a great tolerance for boredom. They work the carved plates on the knuckle's flank, the rows of cups and grooves, with hammerstones of dense black flint worn smooth by generations of palms.

The knock language is slow, formal, and compressed. A single strike on the third cup of a row, followed by two on the seventh, might mean *water*, or *hushers near*, or *my daughter is ill*, depending on the preceding phrase. Because a message may take an hour to send and another to be answered, the people have grown fond of brevity and bad at small talk. Their proverbs are all a few strokes long. The most common, sent to a departing traveler, is a single low knock on the deepest-toned plate, held with the flat of the hand so it rings and rings. It means *the floor will hold you*.

Knockers can be recognized by their forearms, which are thick and corded, and by the way they sleep. They tap two fingers against their thigh, over and over, and wake if the rhythm stops.

## What they hunt, what they eat

The food is the mat and what lives on it. Mat is cut in flat, wet squares with the flat-bladed knives, layered ochre over oxblood over violet, then pressed into cakes and dried on the hearthstones. It tastes of iron and something almost sweet, like the smell after rain on hot metal. The cakes are the bread of the flat and the fuel of the fires, and every hut keeps a smoldering reserve.

Clatterers are hunted with hooked spears, slowly, in the manner of stalking. The hunters approach downwind in the din, matching their steps to the beat of the beaks. A clatterer flock is a moving screen of noise, and a person walking inside it can hardly be told from another bird. The hunters carry the kill back over their shoulders like folded umbrellas. Skirl-eels are taken from the seams with bone hooks and eaten raw, split down the belly, sweet and cold.

Bellwrights are not eaten. They are tended. A family that owns a good bowl-pool keeps it clean of fallen grit, resettles the young, and shoos off the clatterers that come to raid. In return the pool sings a note the family knows, and they use that note the way a household elsewhere might use a hearth. It says *home*. The people tell their pools apart the way others tell voices, by the feel of the tone in the chest and the pressure on the sternum. A woman blind and plugged and lost in mist could find her own bowl by walking until her ribs recognize it.

## Feet and light

The people walk barefoot on the wet stone wherever they can, or in thin hide soles that let the mat show through. It is not for grip or comfort. Their steps leave pale prints that fade over a minute, and a print is a kind of speech. A mother walks a particular way, with a firm heel and a soft toe, and a child learns to track her across the flat by the glow of the last dozen steps. Hunters leave deliberate trails, a scuff, a double-step, a spiral, to tell those behind them *this way is safe*, or *go around*. Lovers sometimes step in each other's prints. The old say a good life is one where your footprints are never all gone at once.

Lost children are found by lantern-lines: adults spread out across the flat in a row, each just visible to the next, sweeping the ground for a small unfamiliar glow.

## The husher-sense

The hushers are the reason for the people's craft of attention. A husher is not seen, since it is low, grey, and eyeless and looks like wet stone. It is *missed*. What the people watch for is a hole in the noise, a place where the bellwright chorus thins to a ragged edge and the skirl-eels go dead on the far side of it. The plugs do not block this. It comes through the feet and chest as a kind of pressure change, a *wrongness* in the floor. Children are trained young. They are set in a ring on the knuckle's crown and taught to point, without looking, to the quiet spot in the din.

Every adult has been close to one at least once, and the people can tell you the fear exactly. It is not the fear of a hunter or a flood. It is the fear of a thing that takes the world's sound away. Stories of the hushed places are told slowly, with lowered hands, and children are hushed *by touch* whenever they are too loud. The word for a dead person is the gesture for a husher's patch: a palm laid over the mouth, then withdrawn.

## The dead

When someone dies, the band carries them at first light to the thinnest and most resonant plate within reach, the one that rings longest under a struck stone. There the body is laid on its side with the knees drawn up and the ear-scar pressed to the rock. The knockers play the death-rhythm, forty strokes, and everyone in the band lays a hand on the stone and *feels* it go through them. Then they leave, and the bellwrights come. The chorus takes a body in a week or so. The people believe, in a way too bone-deep to be called belief, that the dead become part of the sound. On a tide-night, when the choir doubles and the mat blazes and the whole flat seems to lean toward some great low note, they lay their palms flat on the ground, close their eyes, and feel their grandmothers in it.

## The tide

The tide is the great occasion. When the chorus drops a half-tone, which the people feel a day before the first thickening of the sheets, the bands strike camp. The knuckle's low ring-walls flood, and everyone moves to higher ground, the crowns where the hearths burn. Other bands converge. Cousins meet, and marriages are arranged with a few practiced gestures and a night of humming. The knockers of all the bands sit together on the great carved plate and exchange, over three days, everything that has happened since the last tide: births, deaths, where the hushers have moved, which pool has gone quiet, which new seep has opened and begun to glow.

Then the water rises to ankle depth, and the whole flat lights up. The mat blazes green-white from horizon to horizon, and the chorus deepens until the air itself seems to have weight. On those nights no one sleeps. The people stand out on the wet stone in their thousands, ankle-deep in glowing water, plugs in, eyes open, hands pressed to their chests. They are not praying, exactly. They are *listening with everything except the ears*, and what they feel is the world, immense and unfinished and loud, breathing them in.

When the water recedes and the plates come up slick and shining, someone always laughs, a rough soundless heave of the shoulders, and someone else signs the oldest joke there is: *Still here.*
