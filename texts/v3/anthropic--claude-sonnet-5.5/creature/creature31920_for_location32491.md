# The Lineholders

Almost nothing lives in the Long Works, and what does is quiet in a way that suits a place this loud. A few hundred people, perhaps fewer, live in the small orange rooms of the lamp-poles. They have no name for themselves that a stranger could say. Crossers call them **Lineholders**, because they hold to the still lines.

## The Body

They are built low and wide, for standing on a floor that never stops moving. The knees stay slightly bent, the feet turn out, and the soles are thick as boot leather, ridged and gray. Their weight sits in the hips, so a Lineholder walking a nodal line looks like someone carrying a full bowl of water on their head, without the bowl. Iron has stained the creases of their hands and the corners of their eyes. The eyes are large and dark, and they throw back the lamplight as a dull copper coin. Their skin is the pale gray of things that have never seen sun.

They are deaf. Children are born with hearing, and it does not last a year in the roar. Nobody mourns this. The ear is a small organ in a place where the sound is a condition, and the Lineholders hear in other ways: the sternum, the jaw, the long bones of the leg, the flat of the palm. A grown Lineholder can tell which piston has struck, and how far off, by the way the stroke arrives in the shins.

## The Floor-Tongue

They speak to each other through the ground.

A speaker kneels and raps a knuckle, or the butt of a short iron rod, against the slab. The taps travel through the concrete and are read through the hands and feet of the listener. Over distance they use a struck rail, a length of old pipe, or the lip of a drain-cover. Speech is timed to the great beat. A phrase is struck just after a piston lands, when the slab is ringing with it, and the speaker waits out the eleven seconds for the ground to clear. A sentence is a few strokes long. A story is an afternoon. Nobody hurries a Lineholder, and the language would not let them.

At close quarters they add hand-signs, made inside the lamp's orange room where the hands can be seen. A signed word is more often a shape than a symbol, and many of the shapes are the pool-patterns: the ring, the star, the crossed ridge.

The greeting is not spoken at all. Two people put a palm flat on each other's breastbone and wait for the next stroke, and when it goes through both of them at once, they have met.

## The Lamp-Rooms

Each lamp-pole holds one household, and the circle of orange light is the house. It has no walls, but everyone knows where it ends. They sleep at its edge with their feet toward the pole, feeling the faint hum in the metal. Their few belongings hang from the pole's crossbar in bags of woven sedge.

The lamps are the center of their lives, and the lamps are dying. No one built these poles for them and no one replaces them. When a lamp gutters out, its household must find a place at some other lamp, taking a guest's seat at the light's edge, which is a hard thing to be for long. The living lamps are fed by scavenging. Lineholders carry spent sodium tubes in cradles of dried sedge to any pole that might take them, and sometimes one does. They know a hundred small ways to coax a fading lamp. They cannot make a new one.

The number of lamps is the number of families, and every Lineholder knows it. The old ones can recite the lamps in order, pole by pole across the plain.

## The Gardens

Their real work is tending the drawings.

The felt-moss, the pale sedge, and the arcs at the pool margins are all diagrams of the sound, and the Lineholders can read them. A ring of moss around a pool tells them the depth of the water, the rhythm of the piston, and the health of the piston itself. When a piston seizes, its beat leaves the chord and every pattern it fed shifts by a hair. The old figures starve, because their still lines are no longer still. The Lineholders go out with cuttings of moss and pinches of spore in damp cloth, work out where the new nodal lines fall, and replant. They may spend years redrawing one pool. The ground makes the design and they follow it. A repaired figure is the nearest thing they have to art, and to them it is not art but a form of listening.

They eat from the gardens with great care. Sedge spores are gathered on stretched cloths and pressed into gray cakes. Felt-moss is taken only from the ends of a band, because the end of a line is the only place where a figure is not yet finished. Cutting a ring closed is the worst thing a person can do. A child who has done it is not punished, but is walked out to the ruined pool and made to sit beside it for a whole breath, four hours, and feel how the water no longer comes back into its shape.

## The Cycles in the Body

They keep time without clocks, because the works keep it for them.

**The stroke** is the pulse of the whole people. Babies are rocked on the eleven-second interval and fall asleep to it. What wakes them is the quiet at the end of the breath, when the roar falls away and the beat continues alone. A mother will press an infant to her chest through the lull so it can feel the stroke and not wake screaming. Adults keep time with the nearest piston as they walk, so a person's gait tells you which quarter they are from.

**The breath** is met with stillness. When the valves seat and the plain goes quiet, the Lineholders stop what they are doing. The quiet is of no use to a deaf person, and they feel it as a lifting of pressure from the skin and chest, like a hand taken away. They lay their palms on the floor and do not tap a word. They say the silence belongs to the machine, and that it is drawing in air. They do not know why it should be honored, but they honor it.

**The flood** they meet on the hummocks, with the lamps held high and the children lashed to the poles. The water climbs a hand's depth and drains. Afterward they walk the new silt, treading it firm over the moss so the roots can find it. It is done barefoot, as a kind of dance, slow and heavy, and the ground is fresh and cool underfoot.

**The slow decay** they meet with a dry patience. Every few years a piston stops and a small silence opens in the chord. Even the deaf feel it, as a missing beat in the chest, like a heart that has skipped. The whole household will gather where that beat used to arrive and stand through eleven seconds of nothing, and then a few more, and then go back to work.

## The Dead

The dead are laid on the nodal lines, in the still places, under the moss. Over a few seasons the black felt closes over them, thick as a thumb, and the line of a family is a literal line, a band of moss with the shape of a body somewhere in it. A Lineholder can point to a ring around a small pool and say, without pride, *that is my mother's mother.* The moss is not eaten there.

## The Glow

The Lineholders share the plain with one other resident they do not quite consider a neighbor: the **seep-glow**, the pale film in the wettest hollows. They don't know whether it is alive, and they don't ask. It is more useful to them than any other thing in the works. It brightens when the ground shakes, and each piston shakes it a little differently, so a hollow of glow is a slate on which the whole chord is written in light. A Lineholder can stand at the rim of one and read at a glance which pistons are striking, which are late, and which have gone silent. Where they have no ears, the film gives them a second sense. They call these hollows *listening rooms* and visit them the way others might visit a library.

## What They Say About the Machine

They do not worship it. They do not curse it, or ask who built it, or why it moves water from where it is to where it was. When a crosser asks, the Lineholders shrug, or rap a slow phrase on the slab that is usually rendered as: *the water is always going home.* It seems to be an old saying, and it serves as their whole philosophy. The works loses a little each cycle. The lamps go out. The pistons seize. They plant moss on the new lines, keep the tally of the poles, and press their palms to the floor.

## A Note for Crossers

Do not speak to them aloud. They cannot hear you, and it marks you as someone who has not learned the place. Kneel at the edge of the lamp's light and lay a hand on the slab. If they want you there, one will come and put a palm on your breastbone, and you will both wait for the stroke. Stand with your knees loose, and never in the middle of a pool. And when the plain goes quiet, hold still with your hands flat and say nothing, as they do.
