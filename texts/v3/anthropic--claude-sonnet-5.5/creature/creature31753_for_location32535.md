# Those Who Go Under

They have no name that can be spoken. The one they use for themselves is a gesture: both palms laid flat and level, then lowered slowly together, the way fog lowers itself onto the domes. Travelers from the lowlands, who have met them only at the rim, render it as *the ones who go under*. It means the fog, and it also means something like *the ones who go quiet on purpose.*

## The Body

They are built for thin air and constant wind. They are short and deep-chested, with heavy thighs and wide, spatulate feet that grip wet stone through hide soles. Their skin is dark and slightly oily, and it holds the warmth the rock gives back. Their hands are the most striking thing about them: long-fingered, scarred across the knuckles, and never still. Even asleep the fingers twitch through fragments of speech.

The face is where they invest. Because a face must be read across thirty paces of blowing mist, they mark the places where expression happens. Fine lines of soot and ochre are pricked into the skin at the corners of the mouth, along the brow, and beneath the eyes. A smile becomes a bright parenthesis and a frown a dark bar. The tattooing is done in childhood, with a thorn and a patience that only makes sense to people who talk with their whole heads. Their eyebrows are expressive, and their eyelids are quick and eloquent. A conversation between two of them on a windy dome looks like an argument between two very small stage-players. It is entirely silent, and it is entirely clear.

Their ears are the sad part, and they do not consider it sad. They begin wearing plugs of wax and plant-down as toddlers. By middle age most have lost the high range of hearing, and the old have lost nearly all of it. The elders are the best travelers. They read the plateau through the soles of their feet and the bones of their skulls. An old woman standing still on the rock, eyes half closed, will raise one flat hand a full minute before the gust arrives, because she has felt the pitch swell beneath her heels. They say the deaf ones *have gone under early*, and they say it with respect.

## The Speech

The hand-language is the center of their lives. It is a full and fast grammar, not a set of alarms. It has tenses, jokes, obscenities, and a courtly register. Meaning is carried by the shape of the hands, the height at which they are held, the angle of the forearm, and the movement of the face and shoulders. Distance changes the language. Across a dish or a dome the signs grow huge and slow, arms wide, shoulders rolling, almost like a dance. Close in, at a fire or on a sleeping shelf, they shrink to a flicker at the chest, and at the very smallest they are drawn with a fingertip in another person's palm.

Rain changes it too. Wet fingers slur, so rain-sign is coarser and blunter, and there is a gentle contempt for people who cannot be understood in the wet.

For the longest distances there is cloth: strips dyed with lichen in a handful of colors, hung from poles or flicked from the arm. Yellow-green means *water*. Oxblood means *stop*. The teal, the electric teal that only one lichen on the plateau produces, means *come to me*, and it is the most precious of the dyes. Girls are taught to gather it, and it is given at marriages and at deaths.

Voice is kept for a few things. A warning shout is permitted, and so is a laugh, which the wind takes anyway. Otherwise the voice is saved for the fog, and there it becomes something else.

## The Fog

When the cloud comes down and the roar goes out of the world, the people take their plugs from their ears.

Anyone who has watched it does not forget. A party crossing the stone, hands flying, faces working, is overtaken by the wall of white. Within a few minutes the tones have gone thin. Someone at the front holds up a fist. Then hands go to ears, the wax comes out, and the party stands quite still, listening to the drip from the leaf-tips and the tick of beetles in the moss and each other's breath. Some of them weep, quietly and without embarrassment. It is the sound of everything they are for.

They speak then. It is a low, unused, husky speech, since the throat has had a whole dry season of rest, and there is a shyness to it, like the first day after a long illness. They tell each other small things: what they saw, who is sick, what a child said. They sing, and the songs are private in the strict sense. A song belongs to a person and is sung into cupped hands, or into the hollow of another's collarbone, so that the listener receives it through the bone as much as the ear. When someone dies, their songs are not sung again by anyone else.

Fog is when people are conceived, and when they are married, and when the arguments that could not be signed are at last spoken and forgiven. Fog is when the sick are sat up in the huts and told everything. The dry loud months are for work. The fog is for what the work is for.

## The Loud Season

In the dry weeks, when the wind blows all day and the pipes do not stop, they mostly go quiet in a different way. They stay in the windbreak huts, backs to the door, plugs in, faces lit by hearth-coals, mending. The hands do two things at once, sewing and speaking. The huts fill with a busy hush of moving fingers. Children learn their grammar in these months, sitting between the knees of the adults and watching hands the way other children watch fire.

Only the hunters go out, and the water-carriers, and the ones who tend the cairns. Each cairn-bone must be checked every year, since a cracked bone changes its note and a changed note can lead someone off a cliff. The tenders walk the routes by ear, one hand on each stack, listening for the wrong sound in the right place.

## The Herds, the Sleeper, the Birds

The people do not own animals, but they keep company with three.

The **blind grazers** are the first. They are thick-coated, deer-sized, and whiskered, and they move in fog-herds along the safe ground, the way water finds a channel. The people follow them. A herd knows the edge of a slot canyon through its whiskers long before the eye would, and the old hunters say a herd has never once walked into a well. When they take one, the whole party stays still for a moment afterward. Nothing has been said, but it is a form of apology.

The **sleeper** is the second. It is the heavy scaled thing in the slots, and the people leave it food: a handful of seed-pods, a bowl of tank-water, once in a great while a whole grazer. They do not do this out of fear. It is deaf as stone and has lived through more wind than anyone alive, and to them that makes it a kind of elder. Children are brought to a slot at the edge of a camp to *sign hello.* The sleeper does not answer, and it does not need to.

The **watching birds** are the third. At dusk they hang high above the plateau on the updrafts, wings fixed, motionless in the turning sky. The people read them the way sailors read a barometer. If the birds drop low, weather is coming. If the birds go up and up until they are lost in the last light, the fog will settle by dawn, and the party will sleep easy. Feathers found on the rock are worn in the hair, and they are never plucked.

## The Dead

The dead are given to the wells. A body is carried to a deep vertical shaft chosen for it, sometimes years earlier by the person themselves, and lowered on cord into the dark. The plateau's wind does the rest. Every well has a note, and the people say that the note of a well is the note of whoever is in it. When a great gust bends the whole chord downward, you can see a family standing on the rock together, faces lifted, listening for one tone among ten thousand. They pick it out. To an outsider it is an impossible task, but they can, and they will nod, and one of them may sign *there she is.*

If you ask them whether the plateau plays for the dead, they will shrug, which in their language is a complicated and eloquent gesture. If you ask them whether the dead play the plateau, they will not shrug.

## The Struck

There are, rarely, people among them who cannot go under.

They are called the **struck**, and there is one or two in a generation. Each of them, at some point in youth, slept near one of the old piers, by dare or by folly or by loss, on a night when the particular wind came. In the morning they woke with the note still in their heads, a single high, cutting, unbending tone that does not fade in fog or in stillness or in sleep. They cannot take the plugs out, because the plugs do not help. They live at the edge of camps, in huts of their own, and are fed and honored and never asked to sit close.

The struck do not speak in the fog. They have never once heard it. What they have instead, the people say, is knowledge, though no one will say what of. When a struck person raises a hand and stares at one of the piers, the whole camp goes to its huts. They do not talk about it, in signs or in song. The struck walk out onto the open stone to stand near the old pier, in the loudest wind, with their faces lifted and their mouths slightly open, and the note goes through them.

Nobody has ever seen one of them come back afraid.

## What Remains

Seen from the rim, on an evening when the cloud is coming down, the people are almost invisible: a few small figures on the dark dome, dwarfed by the tin-leaf giants with their raised gray hands. The wind is at its worst. The organ is bellowing, the pods are clacking, and the drape-vines are straight out sideways over the slots.

Then the fog arrives. The roar thins. One by one, the small figures stop, and lift their hands to their ears, and stand still.

For a long moment the plateau is silent, and the only sound on the whole Table is somewhere very close, very low, coming from between two people: a voice that has been saved all year, singing into another's collarbone.
