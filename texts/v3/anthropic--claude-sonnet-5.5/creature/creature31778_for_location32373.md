# Those Who Mend the Well

Watch the wall long enough and you will see the first of them. It is a hand, then an arm, then a whole person, grey with dried slip to the elbows, moving along a ledge you'd have sworn was too narrow. They don't look down. They walk the terraces the way you walk your own floor in the dark, by the memory of the feet.

There are about a hundred and fifty of them, and they think of themselves as one thing only loosely. They are people of the well, and beyond that they are families, and a family is a way of laying brick.

## The Hands

Each family has a hand, and the hand is its name. The Long-Course lay bricks in unbroken rows, tedious and strong, and their sections stand straightest. The Thumb-Press mark every brick with a dimple before it dries, so their stretches of wall look faintly pocked, like bread. The Wet-Corner round every angle with a wad of plaster until the wall seems to have grown there. The Sevens set seven pots in a rosette wherever they can and will argue for hours about where one might fit. A child of ten can walk the third ring and say, "Long-Course, two grandmothers back, mended by Thumb-Press after the split," and be right.

This is how they know each other and their dead. No one has to be remembered by a name. The wall says who was here, what they were good at, and where they got lazy. A famous mason of the Wet-Corners is remembered for one corner that has not cracked in ninety years, and people bring their children to put a palm against it.

They do have names, given short and soft, often after a bird sound or a drip. Nobody minds if you are called Tik, or Wheeoo, or Slow-Third-Gutter.

## The Body

They are lean and sun-dark below the eyes, where the noon glare off the brick catches them, and paler where the terrace shade keeps them. Their palms are tough, nearly hoofed. Their feet are broad and long-toed, the soles cracked like dry riverbeds, and they grip a ledge the way a lizard does. The old have curved backs from generations of leaning into walls. Many have a slightly cocked head, one ear turned up, because they are always listening to the pots.

They dress in undyed wool and woven grass, and every one of them wears feathers. A pigeon feather in the hair is a maker's signature, and a young mason wears the feather of the first bird that nested in a pot they made. Some elders have a dozen, worn grey and bent, tied in a knot at the neck. When someone dies, the feathers go with them into the wall.

## What They Do

The days have a shape and it is the shape of the light.

At dawn the dew-tenders go out, usually the old and the very young. The wall is beaded and the gutters have begun to tick. They walk the channels with a flat reed scraper and a small bowl, clearing lint, feathers, and the first green film from the runnels. A blocked gutter loses a hand of water for the whole terrace below it. They speak to the water as they work, low and irritated and affectionate, the way you speak to a goat. When the cistern lid lifts and a bucket goes down, everyone within earshot stops, just for a breath, to hear how far down it has to go. The sound of the bucket hitting is the day's first news.

By midmorning the treaders are in the clay pits on the floor, stamping slip in shallow basins, singing the counting songs that keep their feet in time. The potters sit on the sunny ledges with wheels, and beside them, more often than not, are the wheelless, who coil and pinch and smooth with a wet thumb. The kiln-keepers are the quietest and most peculiar. They sleep little, smell of smoke, and can tell a firing's temperature by the color of the dark in the kiln's mouth. A pot that comes out plum-colored, overfired and brittle and beautiful, is not thrown away. It is set high in the wall where it will never be leaned on.

At noon, when the floor blazes, everyone goes into the pot-rooms behind the brick. These are low, cool chambers, and the people lie in them in rows, half asleep, sharing water from a single dipper. The murmur of the pigeons comes through the walls, and so does the sound of the pots, if the wind is up. It is their oldest lullaby, and they sleep better with it than without.

In the afternoon the masons work. Their craft is the hardest to describe, because it is not one skill. A mason must know clay, and how bricks will bear each other. They must know birds, and which nooks will be taken by owls or swifts, and how wide a lip must be to hold a pigeon without the pigeon fouling the gutter beneath. They must know pitch. A mason who cannot hear cannot build here, however strong their arm.

## The Tuning

Of all their arts, this is the one they love. Every pot large enough is mouth to a note. The masons carry the chord of the whole pit in their heads as we carry a familiar song, and they know when it is wrong without being able to say why.

A young mason's first great task is to make a pot that adds a note and does not clash. They work for weeks. They make the neck a finger longer, a thumb narrower, then fire it, then stand at the rim on a windy evening with the whole family watching from the ledges. The wind comes, the pot speaks, and the pit either accepts it or winces. There is a particular silence after a wince, and the pot-maker stands in it. Then the old ones nod, or don't. No one is cruel. A pot that clashes is taken back and either broken for grog or handed to the birds, who fill it with grass until it goes quiet.

When a pot is accepted, the young mason is given a note. It is only for a while called the pot's note. Soon it is the mason's.

## The Dead

The dead are put in wide-bellied jars, curled, with their feathers, and sealed with clay in the deep terraces near the bats, where the air is cool and the wall is thick. Above each jar the family sets a small singing pot, tuned to the note of the person who lies below. On a windy night the chord of the wall includes them all: the makers of the oldest sections, the grandmothers, the dead child of last spring, each sounding softly among the rest. When someone in the well is troubled, they put an ear to the brick near their own dead and listen.

The pit wants for nothing in the way of ancestors. It is nearly made of them.

## The Others

The birds are not pets and not property, though the people eat their eggs and burn their dung. They are neighbors who owe rent. A warden of the pigeons knows every flock by its habits and its gossip, knows which hens are brooding, which cocks are bullies, which lofts are crowded, and will spend a whole afternoon coaxing one sick bird back with a mash of seeds. The small owls are treated with something nearer respect. An owl in the mouth of a pot at dusk is not disturbed, and anyone who passes it drops their eyes and their voice.

Children run the terraces with gourd rattles, shrieking, and for their first years this is their entire work: keeping the sky safe. They know the shadow of a hawk before they can name it, and when it comes they raise a noise that turns the whole pit into a storm. The birds already know. But the children have to be seen to know.

The people do not startle at sound, and never have. A dropped pot, a shouted quarrel, a cat's plunge, all of it dissolves into the general noise. What startles them is silence. If the pigeons go quiet without reason, every person in the well stops where they stand, head cocked, hand on the nearest wall, and waits. It happens rarely. They do not talk about what they've felt in those moments, but they all know.

Visitors from the hardpan come now and then, herders with wool and salt and lumps of copper, and they stand at the brick ring with their hands over their ears. The people of the well descend to them with baskets of pots and eggs and guano, and bargain in a quiet, rapid, sing-song speech pitched to cut under the murmur. Often they speak with their hands, a language of flicks and taps that carries across sixty paces of noise. The herders leave gladly. The people of the well watch them go with polite bewilderment, the way you might watch someone walk away from a warm house into weather.

## What They Believe

They will tell you, if they trust you, that the well is not a place but an animal. It is slow. It eats mud and water and time. It breathes at dawn, when the wall sweats, and speaks at dusk, when the swifts go in. They are its hands, or perhaps its cells, or perhaps its parasites, and they don't much mind which. They tend it because it is failing all the time, and to tend a failing thing is very close to loving it.

The elders say the well wants to close. That in a thousand years the terraces will have crept inward until the sky is a coin, and then a dot, and then a warm brick hill full of sealed rooms and singing pots, with the birds inside, and the people, and the dead, and the water still ticking down through the dark. They say this without sadness. They say it the way you might describe a child growing up.

At dusk, when the swifts drop in their spiral and the noise rises to its great wet roar, you may see one of them standing at a ledge with a palm laid flat on the brick, eyes shut, not moving. They are not praying. They are checking, the way a physician lays a hand on a chest. The wall is warm. The water is ticking. Somewhere in it, faintly, a new pot has begun to sing.

Then the roar falls to the murmur, and the hand drops, and the mason goes to supper.
