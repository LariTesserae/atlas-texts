# Who Lives in the Slack

## The Makers

They are the only ones who build, so they are the ones you notice first, though you will probably notice them last. You will walk a long way among their cairns before one of them lets you know it is there.

A maker is about the height of a kneeling child but long in the body, built to stay low where the glass is sharpest. The skin is a gray leather, thick across the back and shoulders, dusted pale with salt. Every maker is scarred. The floor cuts everyone, and the cuts are packed with salt and left to heal into raised, pale lines. Nobody treats these as accidents. A maker's forearms and flanks carry ordered scars, rows and clusters and short ladders of them, and no two bodies are marked alike. The scars are a second script, a slower cousin of the shard rows, and it is written on the writer.

They have four limbs and use them unequally. The hind limbs are short and heavy, with horn-plated soles like flat stones. The forelimbs are long. A maker walks on the backs of its hands, knuckles curled under, because the palms are the part it can least afford to lose. The palms are large, soft, and hairless, the only tender skin on the body, and they are how the maker hears and half-sees. Held cupped and lifted as it walks, they feel the moving air, and they read the faint tremor of the loop as it leans on the glass. When something matters, the maker sets both palms flat on a plate and goes still. The glass rings under it, a *tik* or a long dry shiver, and the maker reads the ring the way you would read a face.

The eyes are small, sealed under a brow ridge, and useless. Along the ridge sit the heat-pits, a row of shallow, sensitive dimples that lift and tilt as the maker turns its head. Through them the world is warmth. Grazers are slow warm shapes on a cooler floor, and the lanterns are faint drifting coins of heat. The salt beds are cold, pale absences. The far red vein is, to a maker, the brightest thing there is, a wound of steady heat on the edge of the world. To a maker, you are a bright, restless, ill-shaped fire that makes noise.

## What They Make and Why

The tools are simple and very good. A flake is struck from a glass core with one blow, and the edge it takes is finer than any steel, keener than any use requires. Makers test an edge by drawing the heel of the palm across it, a habit that explains many of the scars. They bind flakes to hafts with cord twisted from the felt, and the cord keeps a little of its green-white glimmer long after it is cut. So the makers, who are blind, carry tools threaded with the only light in the place, and a knife in the dark is a hair of pale fire around a black edge. The hafts are rods of salt, grown long in the old seep-beds, snapped off cleanly at the joint. They are brittle but do not wear. A hook that was hafted before anyone counted is still as good as new.

They use the tools for a few things. They pry grazers from the herds by the seam of the shell. They comb felt into pastes and cakes. They score the glass, and they set the standing shards.

The shard rows are the makers' whole literature. A needle of glass is pushed upright into the salt, and the next is set a hand's width beyond it, and the next a little farther, and the spacing and height and lean are all meaningful. A row can be a name. A row can be a count of years, a count of herds, a tally of the dead in a cell. There is a long row near the edge of the largest salt flat, thousands of needles, that is understood to be an argument and has no known end. The makers walk beside it as they talk, and sometimes one stops and sets a shard, and the others go quiet, and then the talk resumes.

## Cells and Cairns

Each cell holds three or four, and each cell holds its number. This is the central discipline of maker life. The felt regrows at the pace it is eaten, and the herds settle at the number the felt can feed, and the makers, who eat from both, have settled at the number the herds can spare. A child is born when someone dies. They do not say this as a law. They say it as the shape of a ring: a ring has a fixed number of plates, and when one is taken out another is set in.

So the cells are quiet places. Three or four bodies sleep pressed together, the heat-pits of each turned faintly toward the warmth of the others. They keep the customary inch between bodies, learned from the grazers, which is a small courtesy of near-touching. Children are raised by all the cell. The old are kept as long as they can feel the glass.

A cairn is a different thing, a stack of dry plates set with their faces to the wind and reaching higher than a maker's back. Some are markers and some are memorials. A few are simply beautiful, balanced in ways that suggest generations of small correction. Each new plate placed atop a cairn is set by someone who was helped to it by someone else, and the last plate is never set, because the stack is understood to be still in progress.

## Speech and the Shape of Time

They speak in clicks of tongue on palate, or with two glass flakes struck lightly together for distance, a patterned tapping that carries across the glass. It is a slow speech, with long silences that belong to the sentence. The clicks have tones, and a tone can be shaped by the wind on the speaker's skin, so that a message said downwind means something a little different from the same message said upwind.

That is because their grammar of time is the wind's. What has been lies downwind, carried off by the loop. What is coming lies upwind, still on its way. But the loop is a loop, and everything carried off comes round again. So the word for *next* and the word for *again* are one word. To say a thing will happen and to say it has happened are nearly the same sentence, differing only in a lean of the tongue. They do not say *new*. The nearest word means *not yet counted*.

Their idea of a life is therefore a circuit, not a line. A maker who is old is one who has come round many times. Their one journey of note is the walk to the red vein, a long, slow trek across the glass toward the only bright heat, undertaken once or twice in a life. They go, they lay their palms flat on the warm rock, and they walk back. Nobody has ever stayed. The walk is the shape of the loop, made small enough for one body.

## Death

When a maker dies, the cell carries the body to the wall where the felt is thick. They lay it in the glimmer, palms up, and set a shard for it in the row. The felt takes the body and the grazers take the felt, and the maker is folded into the thing it lived on. There is no mourning that a visitor would recognize. The others stay by the body a while, and each in turn lays a palm briefly on the dead one's palm, and then they go and set the plate that fills the ring.

## The Others

The makers are not alone in the Slack, nor the most numerous. Three other tribes hold the place with them, and none of the tribes has words.

The **grazers** move on the floor in tides of shell, hundreds together, holding their inch apart. They are the slowest of the living, and perhaps the most content. A grazer lives its whole life inside a herd and never sees its edge. The herd is a single body that has forgotten it is many.

The **pickers** are pale on the walls, jointed and delicate, working the felt in the high places. They are the patient gardeners of the glow. A picker leaves a combed trail behind it, a thin dark stripe through the green-white, and the stripe slowly closes as the felt regrows, so the walls are covered with faint brushstrokes that are always healing.

The **lanterns** hang in the warm air like slow thoughts. They are soft, translucent, and full of gas, and each carries an amber spot that pulses so gently you feel it as much as see it. They ride the loop from the floor to the roof and down the walls, over and over, and none has any idea it is going around.

The **threaders** are the hunters, and they are rare. They live in the ceiling's glass hair, walking the strands like ropes, waiting with a patience that is almost holy. When one strikes, a lantern's amber spot goes out, and the rest keep drifting.

## The Visitor

Sooner or later a maker will find you. It will not be sudden. The heat-pits will have known you were there for a long time, and the palms will have felt you on the glass, and the maker will have decided, in its way, to let you come closer.

It will approach low, palms lifted. It will stop an inch from you, the courtesy of the herds, and hold there. Then it will lay one palm flat on the plate beside your foot and wait for the ring of your weight. Whatever it reads there it will keep.

Later, if you stay, you may see it walk to the nearest row of shards and set one more, a small new needle at the end of the line. That is you. You have been counted.
