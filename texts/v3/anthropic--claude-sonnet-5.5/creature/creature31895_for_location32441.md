# Those Who Keep the Close

The oldest resident is the film, and everything else is its guest.

It lines the undersides of the ledges in olive smears, faintly luminous where it thickens, and it rings the warmest seams in orange and dull pink. It has no name and no number, because it is not a number of things. It is one long habit of living on warm water and lime, passed hand to hand, cell to cell, from before the columns were columns. It does not spread or retreat. Each morning, if there were mornings, it would be the same width as the day before. Every other living thing in the bowl arrived later and eats from it, or drinks near it, or has made peace with it.

## The grazers

The pale snails are the size of lentils. Their shells are so thin that the dark of the body shows through, and along the whorls they wear a fine bloom of lime, the first stage of becoming the ledge. They move a body's length in the time it takes you to lose patience. You might count eleven in an hour, but the count is misleading, because they are not rare so much as slow and the same color as everything else. Some have not moved since you last looked. One or two, you will suspect, have finished moving, and their shells are being quietly promoted to stone.

In the lowest pools live the white crustaceans, each smaller than a grain of rice. They hang in the warm water like sparks turned inside out. They flick away from your shadow and return to the exact spot when it passes. They have no interest in you, only in the shadow, and they seem to consider you a weather.

## The wader

At the far edge, in a channel where the overflow gathers before it sinks, there may be a grey bird with a long bill. It stands motionless. It does not fly when you come, and it does not feed while you watch. It turns its head just far enough to keep one eye on you, and holds that angle for as long as you stay. It may be the only creature in the Close that has ever decided what to make of visitors. When you leave, it lowers its bill into the water and stirs a single ring that spreads to the edge of the basin and is answered by the rim.

## The ones who made the marks

They were small, or some of them were, judging by the hands pressed into the lime near the crack. They were patient people who carried river cobbles down through the crack sideways and set them in a path across the wettest ground so that no rim would be broken by a heel. Where a rim had to be notched they used a chisel-stone, and then they waited while the water healed the wound into a smooth new lip. They set clay bowls under favored drips. They did not mind when the stone took the bowls. Perhaps that was the point, and a bowl was only a way of giving the Close something to keep.

They built one dry room and used it rarely and gratefully. Inside, on a shelf, is the awl, green with age and laid in its niche as if the hand had just opened. That is the strangest thing in the Close. Everything else here has been furred by stone, yet the awl has been kept clear, and the green on it is a crust, not a coat. Someone has turned it over now and then. Someone has wiped the niche.

## The one who stays

There is no proof of a keeper. But the hearth ring holds a little ash, thin as breath, and the sleeping shelf is not quite cold. The tallies on the columns stop where the glaze thickens, and the glaze over the last row is a little thinner than the glaze over the rows before it, as if the most recent counting were the most recent, and had been scratched not so long ago.

If there is someone, they move as the drips do, and you would not see them move. They would be pale and warm-skinned and quiet. They would know each bowl's rate of filling by the pitch of its drop. They would step on the cobbles without looking, and count something, days or drips or visits from the bird, and cut the count into a column, and then let the water close the cut.

They would not be lonely. The Close is not a place where loneliness has room. Every ledge is occupied. Every sound is answered by the next lower basin. A person who lived here would be one more thing the water was working on, glazed a little each year by the steam, and would consider this fair, having done the same to the stone.

You do not meet them. You meet the sign of them: a bowl that was empty when you passed and is now a finger full, a cobble set straighter than it was. Then you turn your shoulders and read the gaps and go back up the crack sideways, and somewhere behind you, in the dusk between two columns, the grey bird lowers its bill, and a ring goes out across the water.
