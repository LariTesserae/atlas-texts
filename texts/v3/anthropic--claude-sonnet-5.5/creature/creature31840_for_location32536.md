# Those Who Keep the Shelf

Nothing on the Shelf lives alone, though almost everything is alone most of the time. The inhabitants are bound to one another by heat, by iron, by vibration through the drum-skinned ground, and by the slow habit of following the same fog rivers year after year. They are a scattered people, if you can call animals a people. Each has learned to be a small warm thing in a great cold-eyed dark and to rely on the others' fires.

## The Furnace-backs

Begin with them, because everything else does. A furnace-back is more weather than animal. It comes out of the dusk as a low hill that has decided to walk, and you hear it long before you see it: *chuff*, and a long wait, and *chuff*, each step a soft hammer-fall into gravel. The ember line along the spine brightens on the exhale and dims on the inhale, so the whole animal seems to breathe in orange. Steam rises from the vents on its back and drifts behind it, and small things follow the plume for the warmth.

The herds are families, though the family is a dozen animals spread across a kilometer of dark. They keep in touch by low pulses that travel through the ground, felt through the feet rather than heard. A calf, born the size of a cart, walks in the lee of an older cow and takes its first sulfurous breaths in her steam. Its dome will not ignite for a season. Until then it is dull and cold and follows the heat of its mother's back with an anxious, wobbling loyalty.

They polish their scale against the basalt. Some rubbing-stones have been used for so many generations that they shine like mirrors and hold a faint memory of ember-light for a moment after a herd passes. The old ones wear their scale nearly to silver. The very old grow slow, and their fires burn low and red, and the strider begins to watch them.

## The Smelters

The mounds are quiet in the way a workshop is quiet at night: something is always happening, and none of it is loud. Each tower holds thousands of small dark bodies, and the work never has a beginning or an end. One shift stokes the coal-bed and one hauls grit up the ramps. Another turns the slag, listening with its antennae for the note that says the iron has run. The pellets they make are bright as new coins. They are laid into the walls in courses, and over the years the tower grows a shell that rings faintly when the wind strikes it.

The smelters do not speak, but they hum. Stand near a mound in the dead hours and the note comes up through your boots, low and even, with a slow pulse in it, like a machine being kept company. Some say the colonies answer one another across the kilometers, tower to tower, through the metal in the ground. Perhaps so. The forty red points on the horizon do seem to brighten and fade in a loose order, like a slow sentence.

When a furnace-back dies, the smelters do not simply scavenge. They are the last to come, after the crawlers and the ash-beetles have cleaned the flesh, and they take the plates in careful columns. A carried plate is longer than any single ant-bodied worker, so it moves across the gravel like a dark sail on many legs.

## The Glass-gatherers

No one has named them well. They are small, knee-high, and dun-colored, with long-fingered forepaws and eyes that are all pupil. They live in burrows in the gully banks, in family knots of five or six, and they spend their nights walking the ground where lightning has struck. They find the fulgurites by touch: they tap the black sand, listen, and dig. A good tube is thin and pale and sings if you blow across it.

They use the glass in a dozen ways. Some tubes are set in the mouths of burrows as wind-catchers, so the warm air passes through and the burrow whistles a little to itself. Some are carried as drinking-straws to reach the last cold water in the deep of a rock crack. Some are simply kept, arranged along a wall in the dark in patterns that mean something to whoever arranged them. A dead gatherer is sometimes found curled around its collection with a single long tube laid across its chest.

They are not clever in the way the smelters are clever. They are curious. That may be the rarer thing on the Shelf.

## The Fern-ring Dwellers

Around each breathing vent, in the warm green circle, lives a little community that never leaves. Pale beetles graze the ferns. Wet-skinned amphibians, thin as leaves and nearly transparent, sit in the drip of condensation and sing in high clicks. Something small and quick, perhaps a lizard and perhaps not, hunts the beetles in the frond-shadows. Every ring is an island. Each has its own dialect of clicks and its own way of making a living out of the same few hundred square meters of warmth, and none of them has any reason to believe the world extends beyond the edge of the green.

When the vent dies, as vents sometimes do after a tremor, the ring browns from the outside in. The small ones stay until the last. Their kind is not built for walking.

## Below the Surface of the Water

The tarns hold the spark-eels and the things the eels eat, and beyond that a whole slow chemistry of scum and crust. In the warm water, translucent crustaceans drift like pale thoughts. When the eels come, the whole tarn jerks blue and the crustaceans stiffen and fall. It is not cruel, exactly. It is quick and tidy, a light going on and off.

Lantern-moths gather over the tarns at the height of the eels' hunting, dipping and hovering, drawn by the blue. Their gold lights mirror in the black water. From the shore, the lit pool with its wheeling swarm looks like a small, private sky.

## The Hunter on the Ridge

The sickle-strider is what the Shelf's patience looks like when it grows legs. It does not run. It walks in long, deliberate, almost gentle strides, and when it stops it stops entirely. It can stand for hours with its blades folded and its forelimbs pressed to the ground, reading the metal-rich earth as you might read a page: the weight of a herd, the uneven step of a lame calf, the hitch in an old cow's chuff. A strider that has chosen a herd does not hurry. It follows for days. It sleeps when the herd sleeps, a hundred meters off in the dusk, and it wakes when they wake.

When it strikes, it strikes from very close and very quietly, and the furnace-back's fire gutters once and goes out. The strider will not feed until the herd has gone on. It stays with the body and turns slowly, looking at the dark, until the scavengers begin to gather.

Perhaps it grieves. Perhaps it only listens for competitors. It is impossible to tell from the outside, and there is no one to ask.

## The Gully with the Rail

Near the equatorial edge, in a gully floored with black gravel, the reeds sing a little lower. Animals cross that ground more slowly than they cross the ground around it. The furnace-backs do not avoid the bar of rusted metal, but they step around it, and the lantern-moths thin out over it, and the glass-gatherers, who take everything else, leave it where it lies. Nobody has ever seen an animal stop there to think. It is only that the paths bend, gently, and that what lives on the Shelf goes about its business a little more quietly for a few dozen steps.

The smelters have not taken it. Their columns pass within a meter of it, carrying plates from a carcass to the tower, and the workers' antennae brush the rust as they go by. Then they carry on.

## What Lives Here, Altogether

If you stand on a swell in the long dark and wait until your eyes open, you will see it: the orange breath of a herd far off, the red points of the towers, the blue stutter of a tarn, the gold drift of moths, the pale coins of lichen underfoot. Under all of it you will feel the low hum of the ground and hear the reeds' unfinished chorus. Then the sky will crack, and for three seconds everything is shown at once, the herds and the towers and the rail and the small dun figure crouched over a scorched patch of sand with a glass tube held to its ear. Then the dark closes over them, and they go on working, each one a little heat and a little light, and none of them alone.
