# The Tuners

You will not see them at first, and they are not hiding. They are standing in the drum-cane groves, in ranks, as still as the plants, and the eye takes them for more cane. Only when the inhale comes and the fronds click shut does the difference show. The canes stay where they are. Some of the stalks turn, slowly, toward the sound of the straws.

They have no name for themselves that would survive being written down. What they call themselves is a chord, three notes stacked, different for each of them and shared by families. Anyone who has to name them by what they do would call them Tuners.

## The Body

A Tuner stands a little over twice a person's height and weighs almost nothing. The trunk is a jointed column, hollow, black-brown and waxed, the same glossy skin as the leaves, with the same bronze flash in the coin-light. It narrows at the waist into a joint like a cane node, then swells again into a chest shaped like a long urn. That is the resonating chamber, and it is most of what the creature is.

Four arms fold against the trunk when it rests, thin as stems, with long knuckled fingers that end in soft pads. Two of them do the delicate work. The others carry and brace. There are no legs. The trunk ends in a broad, flat, muscular skirt of a foot, and the foot secretes a thin lacquer of tarry sap that it lays down as it moves. The Tuners glide. They do not tread. That is why the dust holds no footprints, and why one route across the flagstones has been polished to a dark mirror by ten thousand years of slow, oiled passage.

The head is a smaller urn set on the trunk with almost no neck. It has two eyes like polished seeds, heavy-lidded and mostly shut, because there is little to see and the coin-lights are the only real brightness they know. Above the eyes is a crest of small rolled fronds. In the exhale the crests unroll and stiffen and lean toward the vents. In the inhale they curl shut with a faint ratcheting rattle. Watching a whole ring of them do it together, you can see the Hollow's breath pass through the crowd like wind through a field.

Their real sense is not sight. Along both flanks run bands of pale, taut membrane, like the caps of the drum-canes, and these hear. Or they feel pressure, which in the Hollow is the same thing. A Tuner standing in the plaza in full exhale is being read by its whole body, every horn and stack and cane speaking against its skin at once, and it can tell which house has a cracked flue by the wrongness in one thread of the roar.

## What They Eat, and How They Rest

They drink. A Tuner carries a long hollow straw folded inside its chest, like the selenite straws overhead, and at the seeps it unfolds the straw and sips the black tarry sap from the rock. The sap is their food and their blood and most of the lacquer on their feet. They also breathe the vent gas deeply, the flint-and-resin exhale, and it seems to feed them as much as it fuels them. They are creatures of the heat. In the inhale they slow. In the long exhale they are brisk.

They sleep upright against warm chimneys, in the ring of houses around the plaza. A sleeping Tuner stands against a whistle-stack with its arms tucked, its crest unrolled, and its chest sounding a single low note as the vent-gas passes through it. In the evening the houses hum faintly with sleepers, each in its own pitch, like a hive.

There are perhaps two hundred of them. The houses could hold thousands. Most doors open on empty rooms with clean floors, swept by someone who does not sleep there.

## The Young

The blinking patches on the floor are not the light.

When a pod bursts and the spores spray, most fall to be leaf-mold and green. Some land in the coin-lights, and those are the Tuners' young. They begin as lantern-mats: flat, dark, slick, no bigger than a hand, and then no bigger than a door. They creep across the flagstones a hand's breadth a year, following the sliding coins, flashing bronze as they turn their skins to catch the sun. The Tuners tend them. They carry them into the light when the coins are near and out of it when they are not, and sweep the stones ahead of them. If you step on the floor of the Hollow, you should step carefully.

After decades, a mat gathers itself and stands up. It stiffens at the center and thrusts a stalk upward, hollow and jointed. This is the cane stage, long and patient. The young Tuner stands rooted in a grove, fixed by a mat-foot that is slowly losing its spread, and learns the breath. It drums with each exhale and learns the pitch of the town. It cannot move or speak. It can only listen and answer. Elders stand among the canes and speak to them through the flues, teaching them the language before they can say anything back.

At last a cane pulls its foot free of the stone, and a Tuner walks out of the grove, awkward, hollow-chested, and silent, to be given its first reed-tongue.

## The Language

They do not sing. The chorus is not music, though it sounds like music. It is speech.

A Tuner has one reed in its chest and can sound it only on the exhale, and only in a single register. The town gives it the rest. A Tuner who wants to say something goes to the base of a wall, puts a pad to a flue-mouth, and pushes its note down into the clay. The note travels under the streets, and the whistle-stacks and horns and cane-organs take it up and shape it with their own pitches. What arrives at the far side of the plaza is not the Tuner's note but a phrase in the town's voice, built from the Tuner's note and the shape of every pipe it passed through. A Tuner at the far side hears it through its flanks and understands. It answers the same way.

At the peak of the exhale, when the whole Hollow roars, two hundred of them are speaking at once. This is not a hymn. It is business, gossip, and argument. Someone is warning of a cracked brick in the third stack. Someone is calling for help with a horn. Someone is telling a child in the cane rows to keep its stem straight. The roar is layered, breathy, and flat, and beneath it the drum-canes thump out the answering pulse of the ones who cannot yet speak.

In the inhale, when the pitch sags and the town sighs, they fall silent. Every Tuner stops. All work halts, and every one turns toward the high thin hum of the straws. In that quiet, the Hollow is listening, and this is the only time they are certain to be still and to look, if you know where.

## The Work

The work is tuning. The town was built for a certain warmth and a certain pressure, and neither holds still. Bricks crack. Reeds go flat. Copper corrodes and shifts the horn's voice by a hair. Every morning, in the first cool of the inhale, the Tuners move out along the streets with their small tools: scrapers of bone, files of pumice, twists of resinous cord. They scour a whistle-brick until it sings true. They clear the flues of the fine dark spores. They rivet new tongues into bronze. They kneel at the wall-bases, with their long arms folded, and listen with their whole bodies to a pipe that is a quarter tone wrong.

The newest organ-rank stands on the eastern roof, lashed with green cane that was cut this season, its bindings still supple. Its pipes are shorter and thinner than any in the town, pitched higher than the Hollow has ever needed, and pointed up at the thinning crust.

## The Path

When a Tuner's voice begins to fall, its note dropping a step, then another, over years, it knows. The lowest register is the last one. At the end there is only the deepest tone the chest can make, and the Tuner walks.

It goes to the western stair and descends into the plaza, and there it glides along the polished stones, the brightest ones, across the wide floor, past the horns and the silent houses, out through the last foundations and up onto the bare shelf of rock to the low door in the east. It does not hurry. Others stop their work as it passes and sound a single note down the nearest flue. The note goes through the whole town like a hand laid on a shoulder.

The door is open. Inside is hotter, darker, and louder. The Tuner goes in, and none has come out.

What is in there is something the young are not told and the old do not say aloud. If you could follow, you would find that the door opens onto the vent-chamber, the deep throat from which the Hollow's breath rises. You would find that the great flue is not entirely stone. It is lined, wall over wall, with hollow, waxed, black-brown columns, rooted in the heat and fused to the rock, each one still humming its last low note as the gas passes through it. Ten thousand years of them. Their bodies are the pipe, and its length is what sets the forty-minute breath. The Hollow's cycle is the slow phrase of the dead, and the living speak in the room their dead have made.

That is why the flues are kept clear, and why the town is kept in tune. What the Tuners tend is not a city. It is a voice, and it is theirs.

## What Is Coming

In the east, the roof is thinning. Over the years the coin of light has grown larger, and the Tuners have felt it on their skins and crests, a warmth different from the vents, drier and higher, bright as nothing they have a word for. The young gather under it. They lean toward it like ferns. The old stand at the edge of it and do not step in.

When it opens, the sun will fall on the plaza for the first time in a hundred thousand years. The air will change. Wind will come in from a sky none of them has seen and sound in pipes that have only ever sounded the breath from below. The chorus will change. Some of the Tuners believe this is the ending, that the light will crack the walls and dry the sap and stop the breath. Others believe it is the answer to the question their whole town has been asking, with its pipes pitched high and its horns turned up toward the crust: whether anyone is listening on the other side.

So the newest rank stands ready, its bindings green. In the silence of the inhale, a young Tuner with a fresh reed in its chest stands at the eastern wall, arms folded, crest curled, head tilted up toward the amber light. It waits for the exhale, and then it speaks its note into the pipe, upward, toward the roof.
