# The Residents of the Lantern Shelf

Nothing on the Lantern Shelf walks, swims, or watches. The plateau is still inhabited. Its inhabitants are the growth: the forests, the felt, the reeds, and the pale bladders on their stalks. Together they are less a crowd of separate creatures than one long conversation that happens to be rooted in the ground.

## The Ribbon-forests

The tall stands are the plateau's most visible people. Each blade is forty feet of dark purple-green, glossy as wet slate, and each stand is one continuous body. Beneath the silt the roots knit into a single mat, so that what looks like a thousand blades is one patient thing with a thousand ways of leaning.

They are the plateau's faces, the parts of it that show feeling. When the water stirs, a blue-green flush runs up each blade from its tip, and the stand passes the light along to its neighbors. The forest has no eyes, but it has a sense of itself: it knows which of its blades has been bent, and how far the bending has traveled. A gust of current crosses a stand like a thought crossing a brow.

Inside a stand it is close and warm. You push through with your arms raised, and the blades close behind you and settle. The light in there is a low ember, and the sound is muffled by the leaves, so it feels like the inside of a held breath. A stand may be older than the terrace it grows on. The oldest are so thick with their own dead that the ground under them is a black, sweet-smelling paste, a soft archive of every season the stand has lived. The living blades stand on that history. They do not mourn it. They eat it, and it becomes them.

## The Felt

The moss lies over the basalt in mats thick enough to sleep on. It is the plateau's low, constant murmur. It doesn't sway or sing, and it flickers rather than glows, in patches the size of a hand or a room, as though someone were turning over half-remembered things.

The felt is the memory-keeper. When a wave of light crosses the plateau and the ribbon-forests pass it on, the felt holds a trace of it a moment longer, so the pattern lingers after the pulse has moved on. It is the slowest of the plateau's voices and the one that forgets least. It seems to hold the phrases. Where the light-speech of the plateau repeats itself, the felt is likely doing the repeating.

## The Reeds

The reeds are the plateau's throats. Each hollow stem is pale and jointed like bamboo, and they grow in dense stands where the current runs. Every stand has its own pitch, set by the width of its stems and the length of its hollows. One basin drones so low it is felt more than heard, a bass note like the floor of the world. Another shrills. The reeds have no words in the sense of meanings pinned to sounds, but they have districts, and each district has its voice, and a voice can be recognized from leagues away.

They are the plateau's means of carrying news over long distances. Sound travels faster and farther in water than light does, and the reeds use it. A note struck in one stand does not stay there. It goes out, and the forests it reaches lean toward it and flush with light. The plants seem to hear, or to be wired to their own noise in a way that comes to the same thing.

## The Bladders

The gas-bladders hang in clusters like grapes, round and translucent on their stalks, filling slowly with what the vents give them. Each swells until it can hold no more, then bursts with a sharp *tock* and lets go a string of bubbles that rattles upward toward the ice. In a thick grove this goes on all the time, like rain on a tin roof.

They are the plateau's clocks, or its heartbeat. They do not keep time so much as make it, and everything else moves a little to their rhythm. They are also the most expendable of the inhabitants: born and burst by the thousand, they are the part of the plateau that dies often and easily and is barely missed. When the Turning comes, they go all at once, and their death is the plateau's loudest and brightest cry.

## What they are together

No single one of these is the mind of the plateau. Together they may be one. The ribbon-forests feel, the reeds carry, the felt remembers, the bladders keep time, and the paste at the roots is the store of everything that has died and been made use of. Whether that adds up to thinking is not something anyone here could answer. The plants don't say, and it may not be a question they would recognize.

What can be said is that the light-pulses in the calm months are not random. They come in slow phrases that repeat: a rising flush from one basin, a lull, an answering flush from the next, a long dark, then the phrase again with small changes. It is the way a very old song is sung, not the way weather behaves. Nothing hurries it. The plants have been left alone for so long that they have had the leisure to become whatever they are becoming.

## The Turning

The Turning is their great event, and it is the closest thing they have to a story. When the cold water comes over the plateau's edge, the forests bend flat in one direction and then in the other. The reed stands scream. The bladders burst in thousands, and the whole plateau lights from horizon to horizon, so bright that the ice overhead shows its cracks like a lit ceiling.

They do not resist it, and they do not seem to fear it. They are built to be torn. The torn blades go into the paste. The mats carried across bare stone take root where they land. Afterward the water clears, the silt lies fresh over the old, and the forests spring upright. The ordinary noise resumes. What the Turning does, more than anything, is mix the plateau, so that each generation of growth is partly a stranger to the last. A stand may wake after a Turning in a place it has never been, beside neighbors it has never met, and begin the conversation again from there.

## The stone ring, the posts, the stair

The plants treat the marks of the vanished builders as ground: something to grow on, to lean against, to use. The posts are furred with ribbon-weed. The cordage between two of them catches drift, as it was made to, and the plants do not know or care that it was once a net. The luminous moss in the carved lamp gives its small steady glow, as if it had learned what a lamp is for. Perhaps it has.

Or perhaps the builders left more of themselves here than stone. A people who tended this plateau for generations might have tended the moss too, seeded it, sung to it, carried it in their hands. The plants may be their descendants in some sense that has nothing to do with blood: the thing the builders taught the plateau to do.

## The pause

The strangest thing the plateau does is at the ring. When the slow phrases of light cross the terraces and reach the ring of stacked stones, they pause. The moss inside the ring, a single dense tuft, flares once, brighter than anything around it, and then the wave moves on.

It could be a greeting, or a remembrance, or a habit that outlasted its meaning. It could be the plateau saying the name of something it no longer has a word for. The ring has stood for longer than anyone can say, and the plants have kept up the pause for longer than the stones have stood. Each time the light arrives it stops, and honors whatever it honors, and goes on.

Whatever lives on the Lantern Shelf has no face to show you and no voice you could answer in. It has only this: a ring, a pause, and a flare in the dark. It is faithful to something, and it is not finished being faithful yet.
