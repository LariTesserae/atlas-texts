# The Drawn

From the rim you will not see them. You will see the fog lean, the mats shift, a heron on the wagon, and you will take all of it for weather. It is only after the second or third breath of the siphon that you notice some of the motion has nothing to do with the water. A mat that should drift left holds against the current. A stretch of orange crust that was a terrace is, for a moment, the curve of a shoulder, and then it is a terrace again.

They have no name for themselves that would survive being said aloud. The nearest sound is a drip on a wet iron plate, two beats and a rest. In the speech of the villages above, if anyone still spoke of them, they might be *the drawn*, because they look like something pulled out of the sump the way water is pulled out of it, slowly and with a sound.

## Bodies

There are perhaps forty of them, and never quite the same forty. They stand about as tall as a ten-year-old child but are longer in the limb and narrower in the trunk, and they carry themselves with a permanent slight forward lean, as if against a current. Their skin is not skin. It is laid in thin overlapping plates like the lip of a terrace, brittle at the edges, soft and faintly warm beneath. The plates are rust, yolk, slate, and in the oldest a bruised violet that only shows when the sky clouds. They flake and re-form. A drawn person who has been startled leaves a scatter of orange flakes on the mat, and by the next fill those flakes have already been grazed away by the film-eaters.

Their hair is living sphagnum, red at the crown and pale green at the tips, and it drinks. When they stand in a drip they sway slightly and the hair darkens. Their eyes are wide-set, dull as old brass, with a pupil that opens very slowly and closes slowly. They do not startle easily at light, only at pressure. A boot on a mat sends a slow ripple of attention through all of them at once, like wind on cotton-grass.

Their hands are the finest thing about them: long, many-jointed, the fingertips pitted like the inside of a sponge. With those hands they can feel a hairline crack in a rivet from the far side of a pipe, and can tell the difference between iron that is thickening and iron that is only wet.

## The Two Hours of a Breath

The siphon runs about forty minutes, and their whole life is shaped by it. They do not keep time. They *are* the time.

**In the fill** they are nearly still. This is the Holding. They settle onto the floating mats, in the boiler, along the engine-house sill, and they lean into the rising water and go quiet. Their plates close. Their breathing, which is slow anyway, slows further, so that in the last minutes before the drain they seem hardly to breathe at all. This is the silence in which every drip seems to hold, and it is partly theirs. Some press pads of sphagnum into the weeping seams of the shale, and the drip goes quiet under their palms. Others simply cease the small sounds they make. They are listening for the gulp, not with the ear but with the whole body, through the mat and the clay, and one of them, always one of the oldest, will lift a hand a heartbeat before the pipe begins.

**In the drain** they work. The channels quicken and the mats sink and scrape, and the drawn go with them. They ride the falling mats down into the trenches, steering with poles of alder, and they get off at the right moment, when a mat is about to be dragged into a channel that would tear it. They reset slumped banks by leaning on them. They kneel at the sluice gates while the water goes through with its dark glassy lip and clear the drift of root and moss that would otherwise silt them shut. Along the pipe, during the cascade of the terraces, they walk the undulating iron on its sinking piers, laying their palms on the joints and feeling for the four places where the pipe is broken or blocked. They do not repair the pipe. They *keep* the cracks: they pack the burst joints with fresh ochre paste and scrape away just enough of the clog that the air can find its way in at the right moment and break the siphon with its rattling cough.

Whether this is why the siphon always breaks and always restarts, they don't know. Perhaps the pipe would do it alone. The question interests them less than it interests you. What they know is that if the level were ever to stop rising and falling, they would have nothing to be shaped by, and that is a kind of fear they have no word for.

## The Engine and the Dream

They do not worship the engine house, but they defer to it. It is the tallest thing they know. They sleep in its flooded cylinder pit in the coldest weeks, on ledges of orange scale, and when the fog comes it comes first to the round-arched window. Above them the great beam leans out over the pit, furred with rust as thick as moss, and does not move.

Their oldest story is about that beam. The engine, they say, was built to lift the water out of the sump. It wanted to. It lifted and lifted, and then the men went away and it could not stop wanting. The siphon is what the wanting turned into. Every gulp of the pipe is the engine's dream of its stroke, and the drawn keep the dream going the way one keeps a fire, without ever asking the fire what it wants. They tell this story not in words but in taps on the pipe, a slow knocking pattern that runs the length of the iron main, and if you stood close on a still dusk you would hear it as the *tick of cooling iron*, the small sounds you took for the metal itself.

## Living

They eat the orange films, the bacterial skins that bloom on every still pool of iron water, scraping them with their pitted fingertips and licking the yield from their palms. They graze the sundew with great care, taking the midges the leaves have caught and leaving the leaves alone. They tolerate the frogs in the boiler pool and keep the boiler's roof of drips in repair. They tolerate the heron too, and it tolerates them; in the last light, when it stands on the fallen wagon so still that the mats seem to move around it, two or three of them will stand with it, equally still, at the edge of the tilted willow, and it is hard to say which is watching which.

They do not build. They *tend*, which leaves a different mark. Every terrace has been guided a little toward its shape, every mat nudged toward its neighbor, and none of it is visible as work. Rule 3 of the place, that the terraces heal within a day, is largely their doing, the mineral paste pressed into the damaged rim, a night of patient fingers. A footprint does not last because someone is quietly unmaking it.

## Birth, and What Comes After

The young are born in the terraces, small and pale as caddis larvae, and live there for years among the nymphs and the skin of water that shivers with each drip. When one is ready, it climbs out at the drain, on the ochre stalactites that hang from the burst pipe joints, and splits along the back. What steps out is wet, folded, and the color of new clay, and it hardens through the next fill, still and silent, while the others stand around it in the Holding. The husk stays on the stalactite. Some of the two-hand-long ochre spikes on the pipe are mineral, and some are the husks of the drawn, thickened over decades until nobody, including the drawn, could tell you which.

The old lie down in a terrace at the start of a fill. Nobody helps, and nobody stops them. The water closes over, the iron comes out of solution, and by the next drain there is a new stepped basin the color of egg yolk, or a plate on the boiler, or a rib in the engine-house scale. Rule 4 is a kind of burial custom. Metal does not rot away here; neither do they. The distinction between the works and the marsh, and between the marsh and the drawn, has quietly stopped mattering.

## What They Are Not

They are not ghosts of the peat-cutters, though some of the oldest trenches follow the lines of old spades. They are not the machines' children, though they take the machines' side. They do not mean you harm and do not particularly mean you well. If you walk onto a mat and it holds against the current, know that a hand is under it. If it gives way, know that a hand has let go, and that the fill is nearly done, and that in a few minutes the whole hollow will begin, once again, to speak in its low, wet murmur.

At dusk, from the rim, watch the fog gather and lean toward the pipe. Under it forty shapes the color of the terraces are settling into the Holding, plates closing, hair darkening, hands lifted a hair's breadth from the mat, waiting. There is one moment of perfect quiet. Then the pipe gulps, and they open like a hand.
