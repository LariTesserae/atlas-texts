# Those Who Are Held

Everything in the basin is slow, but not equally slow, and this is the first thing you learn about who lives there. The frogs on the shelf-growths seem motionless until you look away and back and find one has moved a hand's width. The bark-colored insects take minutes between steps. The lizard-sloth chews once every several seconds. You take these for the inhabitants, and you are wrong in an instructive way. They are the fast ones. They are the flicker at the edge of the place, the small quick weather of a community whose real life is conducted much more deliberately, at a tempo your senses were not built to catch.

## The Quick Ones

The frogs are the size of a thumbnail, translucent green, with organs faintly visible as darker threads, like veins in a leaf. They sit in the palm-sized pools on the shelves, and each pool seems to belong to one frog the way a house belongs to a family. They do not croak. Under the hum, if you hold your breath, you can sometimes hear a tick, a tiny wet click made by a throat swelling and releasing, perhaps once an hour. The clicks from neighboring pools do not coincide, and yet over a long stretch of listening they seem to be taking turns, passing a single sentence from shelf to shelf up the height of a trunk.

The insects graze the film on the curtain-vines, the fine furred algae and the beads of water themselves. They are jointed like bamboo and carry their legs folded before they extend them, as though each step required a decision. Their bodies are the exact brown of wet bark, the only brown in the basin, and it unsettles you to see the color on something alive. They are the nearest thing the place has to rot, and they eat nothing that is dying.

The eyeless fish hang in the lake in their hundreds, white as candle wax, each about the length of a forearm. They face the same direction, slightly angled, like compass needles that have agreed on something. They feed by opening their mouths and letting the warm water pass through. Now and then one drifts a little to the left, and the whole school adjusts, a wave of correction that takes minutes to cross the lake and never quite arrives at a new arrangement. They seem to be listening for something under the water, and they don't need eyes for it.

The lizard-sloth in its column-trunk is the largest animal you are likely to notice. It is long-armed, grey-pale, with skin that hangs a little loose, and moss grows in the folds of it, the same moss that grows everywhere. It has almost stopped being an animal and begun being a feature of the trunk. When it blinks, the slow lateral lid takes a full breath to cross the eye. It is not sleepy. If you watch its face for long enough, you see that it is paying attention to something, and the something is not you.

## The Ones You Walk Past

Then there are the others, which you will not see for a long time, and when you do you will realize you have been looking at them for hours.

They stand among the column-trunks, and at first they are trunks. They are the same pale green, the same smooth unbranched shaft, though somewhat thinner, and a little shorter, perhaps four meters, perhaps five. It is only when the light shifts on one, a change so gradual you feel it in your eyes before you see it, that you notice the shaft is not a column. It is a body. The surface is not bark but a tight, satiny skin, and near the top it divides into a narrower neck and a head like a closed bud, bowed slightly forward, as if it were listening to the floor.

There are hundreds. They stand in loose groups a few body-widths apart, the way the trunks do, and between them the curtain-vines hang undisturbed, because nothing has parted them in years. Their arms, if these are arms, are long, and fold against the flanks in a way that mimics the ridges of the trunk-bark so exactly that you have to put your palm against one to be sure. It is warm, warmer than the air, and beneath the skin something moves, a deep slow pulse like a tide in a pipe. You count and lose count. One beat in perhaps the time it takes to walk to the lake and back.

They are the people of the basin, or what remains of the idea of people here. They have no name for themselves that anyone has heard. It would be wrong to call them plants, since they mourn, or seem to, and it would be wrong to call them animals, since they root. They are something the basin arrived at. Their feet, when you are brave enough to part the moss around one, are not feet but a spreading of pale rhizome that runs down into the floor-sponge and joins it. They drink through it. They think through it, perhaps, if the sponge is a mind. You do not know where a person ends and the floor begins, and neither, likely, do they.

## How They Live

They live by leaning. That is the nearest word. A person who wishes to speak or to be near another does not walk, or not so that you would recognize it. It tilts, by a degree a season, until its crown lies against a neighbor's crown, and the two stand joined, sharing the slow pulse. The doorways in the trunks are these pairings. They are not entrances to anything. They are conversations, and the arch of two bodies leaning together is a sentence held open, sometimes for decades. The chambers behind them, the vine-walled rooms with the hollowed moss floors, are where the bodies lie down at the end of a leaning, or at the start of one, to rest in the warm hollow the last resting shaped. The hollow is worn not by feet but by the long pressing of a body that does not move, and whether it was left by one sleeper or by a hundred in turn, no one can say.

They make the cairns as they make everything: by attention. A person whose season has come stands over a stack of shelf-growth plates, and the plates begin to lean toward each other, and the ring is formed. In the center a pod swells on the moss. That is the child, or the seed, or the memory of the person who made it, and the distinction may not matter to them. The pod sits and grows heavy. Eventually it opens, or it does not. The ones that do not stay in their rings as small pale weights, and are cared for as much as the ones that do. Nothing here is discarded. Even failure has a place to sit.

The grooves in the rock walls are their reckoning. At shoulder height, where a standing body's hand would fall, a person once laid a finger against the stone and drew it slowly down, one stroke to a year, or to a death, or to a note. The groove contains nothing because what it holds is duration. The roots avoid the grooves because roots know a thing that is still being written when they meet one. You will find a few that are shallower on one end than on the other, and if you look closely at the shallow end, the stone there is faintly polished, warm to the palm, and the mark is very slightly newer than the surrounding rock. Someone is still making it.

## Their Speech

Their speaking is the note. You will hear it once, high and thin and pure, from somewhere up in the crowns or out along the far wall, and then the silence. They do not sound often. The note is not a call, or not one that expects a reply within your lifetime. It is one syllable of a sentence that began before you were born and may take a generation to finish. The silence after it is the listening, and the next syllable is the answer, and it will come from another body, on another side of the basin, when its own slow pulse has carried the thought across to it. The whole place is a single conversation with a very long pause between each word.

You may wonder what they talk about. The answer, if anyone could give it, would probably be the basin. They describe it to itself. They tell the walls that they are raw and the water that it is warm and the light that it is even. They tell the moss it is deep. And the basin, hearing, holds. This is what "held" means. The stillness is not simply a fact of the place. It is a thing being maintained, continuously, by people who lean and hum and mend, and who must say every day, at their pace, that nothing has changed. The wound that seals in a day, the leaf that does not fall, the drop that never quite drips: these are their doing, the way a held note is the singer's doing. If they stopped, the light would shift, the walls would begin to weather, the first brown would spread from a leaf, and the pause would end. They know this, and it is not a burden to them, any more than breathing is.

## What They Make of You

When you enter, they know. The floor carries your weight to them through the rhizome-net, each step arriving as a small dull knock in the long pulse, and the knocks continue for hours because you are still walking. To them, you are a storm. You move so fast that you cannot be seen properly, only inferred, a streak of heat and noise crossing a picture that ought to be still. Some of the bowed heads, as you pass, turn. It takes them the length of your visit. By the time a face has finished lifting toward where you were, you are on the stair, and the face will hold that angle for a season, looking at the place where a flicker happened, and then slowly, slowly it will lower again.

They do not fear you, for the same reason the lizard-sloth does not. Fear needs a rate of change, and nothing that has ever come into the basin has come faster than the basin could absorb. You will leave, and your footprints will fill and drain, and the moss will spring back, and within a day the print will be gone. Nobody will have been hurt. Nobody will have been changed, except perhaps you.

If you stand still long enough, and you can do that in the center of the floor with the light pouring in from every side, you may feel the pulse of one of them a few meters off begin to line up with your own. The beat of your blood will slow, or seem to. The drip will catch your breath. For a moment you are almost one of them, a body standing in warm green light, a syllable of the long sentence. Then the moment goes, because you are quick, and it is not yours to keep, and you climb the stair-shaped ledges toward the white oval of the sky, and behind you, somewhere very high, a single clear note sounds and is answered by the silence, and you cannot tell whether it was meant for you.
