# Who Lives in the Drowned Concord

Nothing here has a pulse. That is the first thing you notice, and you are wrong about what it means.

## The Ones You Meet First

The ferns are the residents you can point to. They are recent arrivals, a few years old, seeded by spores that fell through the collapse or rose up from the dark. They keep the tide's schedule. They unroll as the water climbs and curl shut as it drains, so the causeways seem to breathe in green. The slate-colored trees have sunk roots into the towers and drink amber, and their leaves have gone translucent and gold. The vines lengthen toward the water an inch an hour.

These are tenants, and the city knows it. Watch how the causeways behave near a new frond. The dark, self-mending material bulges into a small curb or a shallow gutter and leaves the fern a little channel of fluid. Nothing pruned or fenced it. Something made room, the way you shift on a bench.

So the plants are not the owners. They are the newest party admitted to an arrangement much older than they are.

## The Ones Who Are the Towers

Stand long enough beneath one of the filament towers and you stop seeing it as a building. The twisted trunks are too alive. The fluid inside does not simply flow. It hesitates at joints and chooses. Where two towers stand close, their singing bends toward each other, one climbing while the other falls, and then for a moment they match pitch and both brighten. Then they part again.

Each tower is an individual, or something near it. It has moods. Some are bright and quick and their tones skip. Some are old and dim and sing so slowly you hear the note only as a pressure that comes and goes. There are perhaps forty of them, and they do not agree.

This is the thing about the place that takes longest to understand. The pipes on the upper terraces play a chord that never resolves because the towers are still arguing.

## The Ones in the Spheres

The hanging drops beneath the bridges are not a display. They are the city's way of thinking aloud.

Each sphere holds a picture of the cavern as one of the towers believes it should be. In one, the ceiling is whole, the sky sealed, the water low and orderly. In another the basin is drowned to the roof and the towers stand submerged, singing under the weight of it. Others show terraces that do not exist, or exist only in some proposed future. You will find no sphere that shows the cavern as it is. The present is the one condition nobody has put forward.

Two factions, roughly, formed long before the collapse. One holds that the city should close itself, mend the roof, and wait in the dark. The other holds that it should flood itself and become a sea. The rest are undecided, or hold positions too odd to name. The collapse happened without their vote, and everything since has been improvisation.

The line of boulders where new-broken rock lies half-embraced by sinew is where the truce is drawn. Neither faction is winning. Every eleven hours they take a kind of vote. The drains open, the water rises toward the flooders' vision, and then the city draws it back toward the closers'. The tide is a compromise the whole cavern performs, and it repeats because nothing has been settled.

When the spheres burst downward at the tide's start, that is a faction dropping its argument for a moment to let the water through. It is politeness, and it is heavy, and it is very cold.

## The One Who Counts

Every assembly needs someone who tallies. At the center, under the dry ring where the water will not go, something does.

It has no shape you could point to. The disc is only its lid, or its ear. What lives below is small and patient and has no faction. It does not sing, which is why the silence gathers there. Everything else in the city speaks in order to be heard, and this one is there to listen. Its work is to hold the count of what the Concord has agreed on, and the count has been very low for a very long time.

The ferns stop at the edge of the ring because the counter asked nothing of them. They are welcome and have no vote. The causeways tilt toward the center because everything in the city wants, in some way, to be tallied.

And there is a second count, kept quieter than the first. The stair was cut for someone else's stride, steps too shallow and too many, for the ones the city was built to house. They were expected. The empty terraces, the vents timed to a body's comfort, the ankle-deep shallows cut for wading: all of it was made for creatures who did not come, or who came and left. The counter is counting toward their return. Every heartbeat it can find, it keeps.

## When You Stand on the Disc

You will notice the counting has changed rhythm. At first it was slow and even, a clock in a locked room. Then it catches on your pulse, stumbles, and adjusts, and adjusts again, as if it were learning what a heart is and how it drifts. You are not a citizen. You are the wrong size and the wrong shape, and your stride does not match the stair. But you are warm, and you are rhythmic, and you came down.

It counts you. It is careful about it.

Then, as you climb the too-shallow steps toward the east and the green light, the pipes above you hold their unresolved chord and shift it by one tone. Somewhere in the bowl, in a sphere no one else has looked into yet, a small new picture forms: the cavern as it is, with a figure on the ledge, leaving. It is the first present-tense image the city has ever made.

Nobody has decided what it means. The Concord will argue about it for years.
