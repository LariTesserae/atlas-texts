# Who Lives at Harrow Flat

## The Masons

The true inhabitants are the ones nobody sees as inhabitants. The tawny mat that skins every wet surface is a population, a nation of unimaginably many small lives holding one long argument with water. Up close it is the color of weak tea or old honey, slick as the inside of a shell, and it trembles slightly under a fingertip. It has no center, no head, no favorite place. Its intelligence, if the word applies, is the intelligence of a slope: it knows where the wet is and it leans that way.

Everything else in the Flat lives inside what the masons decide. They make the chambers where the birds nest and the pools where the worms graze. They also make the walls that break the wind so the moss can grow. They are slow builders and hopeless conservationists. They will seal a doorway shut with a season's growth and open no other, and the creatures that used it must find a new way in. Nobody is ever consulted.

The mat is thickest at dawn, when the warm water meets the cold air and the world is briefly at its most extreme. At that hour, if you crouch at the lip of a spire, you can see the rim thicken. It is not visible motion. But look away for the length of a long breath and look back, and the edge has moved.

## The Pale Worms

In the pools where the mat is deepest lives a translucent worm about the length of a finger. Its body is so clear that you can watch its gut fill with tawny paste after it feeds. It has no eyes. It moves in slow S-curves through the warm shallows and grazes the mat in long, patient strokes, like a scythe going through wet grass. It is the only creature that eats the masons at any scale, and the masons seem to tolerate it, perhaps even to grow toward it. Where the worms are plentiful, the mat forms small ridges and terraces in the pool floor, as if it were being tended.

The worms cannot leave the warm water. A pool that cools kills its worms, and their bodies stiffen into tiny white threads in the stone. Collectors of such things, if there were any, would find whole seams of them in the older walls, thin as hairs, laid out in the direction they were swimming when the water changed.

## The Small and Hidden

Under the moss the ground is crowded. Beetles the color of wet slate work the root mats in slow dark armies. Pale springtails gather in drifts on the warm runoff, so many that they look like spilled flour until a shadow falls and the whole drift leaps at once and resettles. Mites, threadlike nematodes, and colonies of rust-colored algae smear the terraces. In the cracks between the domes' shoulders, small brown spiders spin funnels that fill with beaded condensation each morning and are gone by noon.

None of them hear the town's music in any sense we would recognize. But the vibrations run down the spires and into the ground, and the small life responds to the low drones by burrowing deeper and to the hail-stutter by going still. The chord is a kind of weather they feel through their feet.

## The Woolly Grazers

At dusk, when the cloud has settled and the light goes amber, the herd comes down from the domes. There are perhaps forty of them, though you would rarely see more than a dozen at once. They are small animals, shoulder-high to a child, with thick coats the color of oatmeal and long, mildly foolish necks. Their feet are broad and split, made for wet moss and slick sinter. They walk in single file, each nose close to the tail ahead, and each breath makes a plume of steam that trails back over the one behind, so the line looks like a slow train drawn across the plaza.

They crop moss with a soft tearing sound, and they take it selectively: the lime-green cushions, never the wine-red. They stop often to stand in the warm runoff with their forefeet in the water, eyes half-closed. The drones don't trouble them. When a spire booms beside them they flick an ear and go on chewing. They have grown up in this noise and treat it as a landlord treats the weather.

A few things they will not do. They will not enter a spire with a fresh-cut mouth, where the mat is still raw. They will not cross a lane in the hour after a large drop has fallen into a cistern. And they will not stay in the bowl during the jittery stutter before hail. They will lift their heads all together, as if a string had been pulled, and file out over the saddle, and by the time the first stones fall they are gone. Anyone who wants to know what the sky will do can watch the herd.

## The Dark Bird

In the highest chambers of the tallest northern pipes nests a heavy, dark-bodied bird, about the size of a large hen, with a short neck and broad wings it uses mostly to glide. It is not graceful on the ground. It is graceful only in the long descents from the pipes to the terraces, where it drops like a folded cloth and opens at the last moment.

Its call is a slow descending whistle, and it is the one sound in the Flat that answers the town. The bird listens to the drone and pitches itself just under it, a sliding note that ends in the bass of the spire and disappears into it. Sometimes a spire takes up the note and holds it a moment longer than the bird intended, and the bird goes quiet, as if it had been interrupted.

They nest on ledges of sinter they seem to prefer freshly laid, still faintly warm. The eggs are pale green, speckled brown, and are sometimes stone-skinned by the mat before they hatch. The chicks, being the birds they are, break out anyway. Old nests, abandoned, become small white bowls fused to the wall, with a few feathers stiffened in the rim.

## The Listeners

There are people, too, though few enough that the Flat has never had a name for them, or they none for it. A handful come up over the saddle in the cold season and winter in the lower chambers. They bring almost nothing: wool, a few pots, dried grain, salt. They build nothing and cut nothing. They live in what the masons have finished, sealing a doorway against wind with a hanging of felted hair, keeping a small fire on a sinter shelf, and drawing water from a pool that is warm enough to wash in.

They are called listeners because that is what they do. They sit inside the spires, backs against warm walls, and read the chord as another people might read the sky. They can tell you an hour ahead when the wind will veer, and a day ahead when the cloud will thicken. They know each spire by its note, and they have names for the notes, not for the towers. When a big spire falls they stop whatever they are doing and sit for a long time, and afterward they pass the new chord between them by humming, checking the gap where the old voice was, learning the town over again.

They leave things in the pools. A feather, a bead, a tuft of hair, a bone from a meal. In a season the offering is white and hard, a small sculpture of what it was, and they leave it there. On the lower terraces there are shelves furred with such things, layered over the years: pale birds' wings, stiff wool, the delicate lattice of a grazer's rib. Nobody counts them. When a listener dies in the Flat, which happens seldom because most go down before the worst of winter, they are laid in the warm shallows of a quiet pool with their face turned up. By spring they are part of the terrace, and the next people who come sit near them and hum.

## What They Share

None of these lives is permanent, and all of them know it in the body's way. The herd leaves before the hail. The listeners leave before the deep cold. The worms die when their pool cools, the birds abandon a ledge when the mat closes over it, and the spires fall. Each is a guest in a house that is constantly being rebuilt around it, by an occupant that will not be thanked and does not notice. What holds them together is not any bond among themselves but the shared, patient attention to a single sound: the chord that rises from the town, shifts with the weather, and is always, in its slow way, telling them what comes next.
