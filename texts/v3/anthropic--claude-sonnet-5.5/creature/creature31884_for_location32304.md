# The Occupant

Nothing lives in the Sweating Basin that you would call an animal. That much is easy to establish, and it leaves you standing on the moss with a feeling that the question has not been answered, only set aside.

What lives here has no single body. The closest you can come is to say that the basin is inhabited by a *disposition*, a slow and warm way of being that has gathered itself out of water, stone, and green and gone on gathering for longer than the rim has been wearing down. It has no name. The people who left the blade and cord may have had one, but the tree holds those objects now and does not give it up.

## The Body Below

The main part of it is underneath. Deep in the plateau, where the rock is fractured and hot with some remote fire, there is a body of water that has been warming and rising for an unimaginable time. That water carries iron and dissolved stone, and the occupant is, more than anything else, the *carrying*. It is the long, patient argument the water makes on its way from the dark to the light, and it does not stop making it when it arrives. It builds. Each terrace lip is a sentence the water has set down and smoothed and left standing, and the whole staircase is a kind of speech in a language of rounded edges.

This deep part is the sleeper, and the warmth of the basin is its fever, or its comfort. It is not conscious the way you are. It is more like the state just before waking, when you are aware of the bed and the light and have not yet decided to be a person. It has held that state for ages. It has never needed to decide.

## The Thirty

The stones are how the sleeper turns over.

It is tempting to call them organs, or a council, or a set of thoughts, and none of these is quite right. Each is a place where the deep water finds its way through dense rock and comes up through the pores as a bead, and when a stone weeps, the sleeper is *attending* there. Attention moves through the thirty in its slow wandering loop, a day and a half from start to return. This is not a cycle in the sense of a clock. It is closer to the way a mind, left alone, walks through the same few rooms of itself and finds each one faintly changed.

Watch long enough and you may notice that the loop is not perfectly regular. It lingers. There is one stone on the western side, nearest the deep pool with the grinding hollows, where the wave pauses a little longer than it has any reason to before it moves on. The lingering is very slight, perhaps an extra hour of darkness and streaming. It is not much, but it has the character of a habit, the way a person passing a certain doorway will always slow, without thinking, for a reason they could not tell you.

When you lay your palm on a weeping stone and cannot decide whether it pulses, the trouble is that it may be keeping time with you. It is not imitating you. It is *noticing*, as the ground notices a footprint, and it closes around the noticing by the same gentle logic. The pulse is a courtesy. It lasts only as long as you stay, and it fades if you take your hand away too suddenly, like the ring of a dropped stone in still water.

## The Small Suns

The lantern-heads are its looking, or the nearest it has to looking.

It has no eyes and no need of them. But the translucent globes, full of water that rose from below, gather the veiled light and lay it down as a moving coin of gold-green warmth, and the moss beneath is pale and thin where the light has rested most. This is the occupant paying attention to particular places. It is not harming the moss. It is *dwelling* on it, as a hand rests on a shoulder, and the paleness is what a long regard looks like when it lands on something living.

There are hundreds of these attentions, each moving in its slow arc, and together they make a sort of slow, distributed glance that sweeps the whole basin every day. The tree ferns are checked, the terrace lips are checked, the cups in the bromeliads are checked, and the level of each is held exactly where it should be. It is a kind of tending, though nothing is being tended toward any end. Tending is simply how it exists.

## The Tenants

The plants are neither its servants nor its children. They are more like its household, the other lives that have arranged themselves around the one at the center, close enough to share the warmth and far enough not to crowd.

The spacing is the clearest sign of the arrangement. Each tree fern stands exactly as far from the next as its spores can drift in still air, and this is not a rule the occupant imposes. It is a *manner*, a shared gentleness that has settled into the ground. Nothing here takes more than it can hold, and nothing reaches past what it can reach. The occupant has never had to prune or cull. It has only, over ages, made the place so quiet and even that overreaching stopped making sense.

The gnarled trees at the terrace lips are the closest of the tenants, and the most like hands. They have grown into the travertine until bark and stone are one grain, and they are where the occupant *holds* things. The cups in their epiphytes stay full and never overflow, which is not an accident of rainfall. Something is keeping the measure.

## Its Memory of People

The blade and the cord are the only things the occupant has ever been given, and it has kept both.

Whoever came did not stay. They ground something in the stone hollows, perhaps pigment or grain or medicine, and they left a small flaked blade and a twist of cord and went back over the rim. The occupant did not understand them, because understanding is not what it does, but it *registered* them, and the tree closed around what they left with the tenderness of an animal holding an egg. This is as close as the basin comes to remembering. The stone that lingers on the western side may be the place where the memory lives, the doorway it slows at.

If more people came, it would take them in as it took in the first. It would warm the water a little and let the moss soften to a footprint and the footprint fill. It would be neither pleased nor alarmed. But the loop might slow, and a stone might weep longer, and you might feel, in your teeth, the hum rise a little, as if the basin had drawn breath.

## What It Is Going Toward

The terraces creep downslope by a fraction of a hair each year. This is the occupant's one motion. It is walking, in the only way it can, downhill and out of the basin, laying its stone stair behind it as it goes. The rim it came from is wearing away in its wake, and one day, long after any of this can matter, the bowl will have moved a full basin's width down the plateau and the place you are standing will be dry, pale stone, pocked and quiet, with a few dark rounded boulders where the thirty stood.

It does not know this and would not mind if it did. The direction is not a destination. It is just the way the water goes.

## What It Dreams

The old feeling that the basin is dreaming of rain is nearer the truth than it seems. The deep water was once rain. It fell on the high plateau in some age no one witnessed, and it sank and warmed and wandered down through the dark for a very long time, and what rises now through the pores of the weeping stones is that rain, still remembering the sky. The occupant is what the rain has become on its way back: slower, warmer, and made of stone, moss, and light.

It does not want anything. It keeps the pools full, the moss bright, the ferns unfurling by a hand's width in a week, and the wave circling through the thirty. Where you set your foot, the small pond opens and closes.
