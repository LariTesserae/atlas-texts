# Who Lives in the Sunk Meadow

## The People

They call themselves something that means, roughly, *those still on top*. It is not boastful. It is how you would describe the last few coins in a purse.

There are perhaps a few hundred of them across the whole basin, in bands of six or eight, and you will almost never see one. You will see where one has been: a cairn, a stilt hut, a bowl turned over against the drizzle. They see you long before you see them. Their eyes have spent generations in this light, and they can read a shape moving on the pale moss from a mile off, the way a sailor reads a sail. They watch from the ridges, sitting very still in their gray-green wraps, until they decide what you are. Most travelers are never anything but a curiosity, and the bands go on with their work.

They are short and broad, built low, with long forearms and a way of standing with the knees slightly bent, as if the ground might move, which it might. Their skin is a weathered tan, gone yellowish under the constant white light. The skin around the eyes is creased deep and rubbed with soot to cut the glare, so that every face wears the same dark, smudged mask. Their eyes are nearly always narrowed to a line. When they take off their bark visors indoors, in the low dim of a hut, the eyes open slowly, like something coming up from under water. Their feet are wide, with splayed toes, and over these they lash mat-shoes of woven fern-stem, broad as dinner plates, to spread their weight across the moss.

They rub their skin with rendered beetle-fat against the cold, so they smell faintly of tallow and smoke and crushed green. In the morning frost their hair and eyelashes whiten and they look, briefly, like very old people.

## How They Speak

The moss eats sound. A voice raised across a hundred feet of mat comes to the listener as a mumble. So they talk quietly and close, and at a distance they talk through the ground.

Each person carries a probe-pole of hollow stalk, longer than they are tall and shod at the tip with bone. It is used first to test the mat, to feel for hollows, thin crust, and the give of a sinkhole's lip. But it is also a drum. Struck against a firm ridge in a certain rhythm, it sends a knock through the root-felt that another person, kneeling with an ear against a cairn-stone miles away, can hear and read. It has a small vocabulary, made for the emergencies of the place: *fall coming, come away. Ground thin here. Water rising in the low basin. Fire is lit, come eat.* Of course it also carries gossip, and long, slow, affectionate jokes that only take shape over an afternoon, one knock at a time. At dusk in the still hours, if you kneel and press your ear to the moss, you can feel the whole basin faintly talking.

## How They Live Between Falls

Their days are shaped by one duty, which is to know where the next collapse will be. They cannot know it, but they can guess better than anything else alive. They watch the vines for a change in the lean. They watch the sag of a stretch of canopy, the way it has begun to hold water in a belly, the way the drip beneath it has changed from a ticking to a run. They watch the white-winged bird, which they consider a kind of colleague.

When the tearing begins they do not run in a panic. They move at once and in silence, gathered by long practice, tethered together on fiber lines, their children on the short lines and the old on the long ones, and they walk perpendicular to the drift of the shadow and put their poles down on firm ground until the whump has passed and the wave has rolled under them. It has cost them people, and they do not pretend otherwise. But the fall is also the harvest, and this is the thing outsiders find hardest to hold. The fresh heap of canopy is the richest ground in the basin for a week: seed, fruit, fat grubs, nest-down, long clean lengths of vine for cordage, and stunned beetles by the thousand. So after the wave has passed, they turn and walk toward the new heap, toward the same thing that nearly killed them, carrying baskets. They work fast, before the moss can close over it, and they are gone by the time the sprouts are a hand high.

They eat spore-cake from the fern-drifts, sour and gray and dense as bread, dried on racks in the thin light. They eat the grubs, and the tender roots, and the grazers when they can loop them, though a snared grazer is always thanked, quietly, with a pinch of its own gray fur pressed to the ground. They boil the black pool-water with hot stones. They drink a thin bitter tea of moss that keeps the cold off. They laugh more than you would expect. Cold, brightness, and constant risk make for a people who cherish a warm bowl.

## The Dead

They do not bury. Burial is impossible in the mat: what you put in the ground rises again in a season, pushed up by the heave. And they do not burn the dead, for fuel is precious.

Instead the dead are carried to the foot of a great vine, one of the thick living trunks that climbs toward the canopy from a firm ridge, and there they are bound into the roots with fiber, wrapped in fern, and left. The vine takes them. In a few years there is nothing but a swelling in the bark, and the dead are climbing, slowly, the way everything here climbs. They say the dead go up with the vines to see the sky, and that when the canopy falls, the dead come down with it and are gathered again into the mat and rise a little each time. Nobody in the basin has ever thought of this as grim. It is only what things do.

## The Grazers

The long, low gray animals move across the cushions in twos and threes, and they are more careful than anything else in the basin. Their wide flat feet spread their weight like the people's shoes. They walk with their eyes half shut, ears swiveled, sniffing the moss before each step. They will not cross a thin place, though it looks like all the rest, and it is for this reason that the people follow them. The oldest paths in the basin are grazer paths, and a cairn is often only a marker set where the grazers already knew the ground was sound.

They are gentle, mostly silent, and curious about fire. Sometimes at dusk one comes to the edge of a hut's light and sits with its front paws tucked, staring at the flame with its narrowed eyes, and the people let it. Now and then a grazer goes into a sinkhole anyway, for they are not perfect, and the band that finds the hole will stay a while at its edge.

## The Beetles

They are pale as tallow and thumb-sized, and they work the root-felt in slow, unbothered thousands, chewing galleries through the mat. They have no fear because nothing here has ever taught them to be afraid. The people pick them up and set them down again, and render the fat of only the ones they need. They are, in a sense, the basin's slow engineers. Where the beetles are thick the mat is honeycombed, and the ground is thin. The people know this too, and learn to read a patch of moss for the tiny round doors of the galleries, and to be wary of ground that is too well loved.

## The White-Winged Bird

There is one. Or there is one kind, and it is so rare that it seems one. It circles above the falls on broad white wings, catching the insects the tearing canopy shakes into the air, and it does not sing. It does not seem to need to. It watches the vines in a way that is not mere hunting, and often it rises before the first sound comes, and drifts, and waits. The people say it hears the canopy thinking about falling.

When it lifts and begins its slow circle over a certain stretch, everyone in earshot stops, and the poles go down, and someone knocks: *away.*

## Below

There is more life under the mat than on it. In the dark layers, warm with slow rot, live pale threadworms and springtails, mites, and white root-fungus that glows faintly in the sinkhole throats, a cold green light that you can see only by leaning over the edge and letting your eyes forget the sky. Blind crickets cling to the cut faces there. Things hatch, feed, and die in the ring-layers, in a slow, warm, dark economy that has no idea of the brightness above it, and it supports everything that stands on it: the beetles, the grazers, the small fires, the people, the dead climbing in the vines.

At dusk, when the light finally begins to thin and the fog comes down the walls, the huts begin to glow one by one in the white. They are small, low, and orange, and there are never more than a few in sight. Around each one, someone is sitting with their bowl, listening to the ground.
