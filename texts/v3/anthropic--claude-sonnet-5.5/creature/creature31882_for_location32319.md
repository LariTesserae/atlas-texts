# Who Lives on the Upwelling Shelf

Begin by giving up the question of how many.

You will want to count, because you come from a world where living things are separable: this one, that one, the herd, the flock. Here you could walk to a trunk and call it a tree, and it would be a fair name for a while. Then you would notice the buttress that is also its neighbor's buttress, the root that has gone through three other roots and come out the far side still carrying water, and the bridging arch that is alive at both ends and belongs to neither. The individual is a convenience of your eyes. On the shelf it is a fold in a sheet, and the sheet runs for a hundred kilometers.

What lives here is one thing, and it is also ten thousand. The two counts do not disagree. They are the same fact at different distances.

## The Pumpers

The nearest and slowest part of it stands in the colonnades. The trunks are the body's great vessels, but calling them plumbing misses the way they feel. Put your hand on one and you feel a beat lasting many seconds, rising, cresting, and letting go, and beside it another beat a moment out of step, and beyond that another. Over minutes they slide toward one another, and for a while a whole district of the forest beats as one. Then some small disturbance, a shift in the cloud or a gust across the crowns, unpicks them, and they drift apart and begin again.

This is the nearest thing the shelf has to a mood. It is not one you would recognize. It is a slow agreement about when to lift, taken up by the wood itself and by nothing that resembles deliberation. The trunks are not thinking about the water. They are a chorus that has never needed to hear itself in order to sing.

## The Crown

Above them, in the cloud, is the part that looks outward. The dark iridescent leaves are turned toward the faint light like faces, each a lens and a filter, each tuned to its own note. Together they hold a chord, and the chord shifts. In the long afternoon it thins and flattens as the cloud thickens. Before the Pour it climbs, and every leaf leans a little toward the top of its range, as a crowd draws breath.

Whatever the forest perceives, it perceives here. It knows the depth of the cloud, the tilt of the light, and the temperature of the air against a hundred million surfaces. It knows this the way your skin knows the sun, without a watcher in the middle to receive the report. But the report goes somewhere. The pale threads pick it up and carry it down through the wood, and thousands of leaves that saw nothing know within minutes that a storm has begun to gather forty kilometers away.

The chiming is not decoration or accident. It is the forest tasting itself.

## The Threads

The pale threads are the difficult part, and the part most likely to be the answer. They run through wood and felt, through stem and root and the dead layers underfoot, carrying charge in patterns that widen and fold and travel in waves. The waves have shapes: fronts, eddies, standing rings that hold for an hour and dissolve. Something in the pattern resembles arithmetic and something resembles argument.

Whether the forest is doing a sum or is simply the kind of thing that waves, no one has ever been placed to judge. There may not be a difference. A brain is also only a dense place where signals keep happening. If there is a mind here, it is not behind the forest but in the way the forest coordinates: a large, slow, distributed deliberation with no center and no hurry, in which a decision such as *seal the leaves* or *open the vents* is less made than arrived at, the way a crowd stops clapping.

It is not a person, and it does not need to be. It has moods, reflexes, appetites, and what might be plans. A heartbeat's worth of a wound taking time to heal is, to it, as much thought as a whole afternoon.

## The Deep Layer

Under your feet lies the part that remembers.

The blackened, pressed strata below the living felt are dead only in the sense that they no longer grow. They are warm. They still conduct. They still carry a faint tremor of the signals passing above, like a river bottom feeling the current. The older the layer, the slower it carries its portion of the conversation, until at the bottom, on the young rock, there are sheets of tissue that answer a message only after days, or seasons, in a voice so low it might as well be geology.

This is how the forest holds its past. It doesn't store it in a place. It lays it down as ground and then walks on it, and the walking continues the thought. When you sink to the shin and pull free with a sigh, you have stepped through something's yesterdays. The cold sweet water that runs from your footprint has been through them. The forest does not mourn what it has buried. Nothing here is buried. Everything is under load, and everything is still in use.

## The Small Ones

In the lit gaps and the wet dimness, on every fork and fold, live the quick things: filament-moss, gel-algae, liverwort mattresses, and the thin lichen-like films whose veins glow and dim. They live fast. A mat may seed, spread, and be overgrown in a season, and none of them is exactly a separate creature, because they have long since stopped being separable from what they grow on.

If the trunks are the forest's slow will and the threads its long argument, the small ones are its restlessness. The glowing films pulse in ripples that pass through them faster than anything else on the shelf, a shiver at the edge of sight. It is the closest thing the place has to a glance, or a flicker of attention. They are not sentries. They are what the forest does with its surface when it has nothing better to do, and it never has anything better to do.

## The Drifters

At the top of the cloud, in the updrafts the trees have shaped, the forest sends part of itself away. The pale filaments are the length of a road, far too long to be called seeds. Watch one lift from a ledge and it seems less a plant than a decision. It sails across the gray for days and may come down on a ledge no larger than a table, and there it will start.

They are the forest's way of wanting more. They know nothing of home. They know only *up*, and *air*, and *land somewhere*, and the roughly one in a million that finds a hold will become, in a hundred years, another district of the thing they came from, joined to it by threads that will find their way back across the gap as surely as water finds a level.

## The Frontier

The rawest and most alive part of it is at the edges, where the rock is still bare. A scarp fails somewhere and the stone opens, black or pale, sharp-edged, and unclaimed. Within days it is furred with spores. Within a season its edges are green. The forest does not fight the rock. It never has. It simply arrives, and holds, and lays down a film, then a mat, then a felt, then a foundation. Everything that happens here is in service of arriving before the rain does.

This is the closest thing to a purpose the shelf has. The rock is rising and the weather is beginning to carve it, and the forest is racing to cover the stone before it is unmade. It has no idea of the race. But it acts as though it does, everywhere at once, with no pause and no doubt, and it is winning.

## What it is like to be it

Not fear, not hope, not lonely. It has no other. There has never been another to compare itself to, so it has no word for *self* and no need of one. It is a going-on: water climbing, light gathered, signal passing, stone covered. It weeps in the Pour and stretches in the ticking dry hours. It clears its throat in bottle-blue light and takes up its hum again.

If you stay long enough you begin to feel where it is, the same way you feel a large animal in a dark room. But you are not what it is aware of. You are a warm, wet, moving thing inside the largest patience in the world, and it passes over you the way weather passes over a stone. It will go on, and it will never once turn to look.
