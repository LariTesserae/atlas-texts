# Who Lives in the Ringing Vug

## The Tar-Beetles

The beetles are the population. There are perhaps a few hundred thousand, and they have no cities, because they are the city. A drift of them on a warm floor is a single dark surface, breathing. Each is the size of a palm and the color of a wet stone, and each is varnished from the inside out. They drink tar, sweat it, and comb it across their own shells, so that every carapace is a slow, glossy skin of the thing they eat. When one moves fast, the varnish sparks cold blue-white along its back, and the sparks run through the drift like wind through grass.

They walk in columns from the warm floors to the seeps and back, on paths worn into the sulfur dust to a depth of a finger. Nobody leads. A beetle at the head of a column is only the beetle that happens to be at the head. The combs on their forelegs are fine enough to skim a film of tar without being caught by it, and at the seep they line up along the edge, all of them skimming at once. From above it looks like a black mouth being lightly stroked.

They do not sleep, or they are always partly asleep. They breed in the warm cracks under the drifts, and the young are pale, soft, and silent for a while. Then the rasp comes into them, and they fall into step with the four-second beat as if they had always been on it. There is no memory of learning it. A larva that has not yet found the beat is a small, uneasy thing, walking off the columns. Once it finds the beat, the column takes it back.

Their only language is the rasp, and it has only one utterance: *here, here, here, here*, said by hundreds of thousands, swelling and ebbing every four seconds. If it means more than that, the meaning is in the swelling.

## The Glass Mantises

In the crystal thickets live the tall ones. They are pale, many-jointed, and nearly transparent, so that where they hold still you find them only as a bend in the light. Their bodies are the length of a forearm and their legs are longer, folded and refolded like carpenter's rules. Inside the thorax you can see a dark thread, the gut, and a faint amber bead that is either a heart or a stored lamp.

They hunt by tapping. A mantis strikes a gypsum blade with the tip of one foreleg, then holds still and listens through its feet. A blade with a beetle standing at its base returns a different note than an empty one, a duller, heavier one, and the mantis reads the difference the way you would read a face. They can tell a beetle's size, its distance, and whether it is full of tar. The whole thicket is a hunting instrument, and each mantis is a player who has learned it. When one strikes a blade, three others answer, because they are checking the same blade, and the ticks come down in small, quick phrases that land on the pistons' downstrokes.

They are, in their way, the vug's musicians, and they are fussy. A mantis will spend a long time on one blade that does not ring true. It will tap it and lean into it, then finally lever it off at the root with a precise, sideways shove, and the blade goes down into the dust with a clear chime. A few have dragged the stamped tin tags up from the sulfur and hung them on crystals as small dampers. Nobody knows whether they know what the tags once said. The tags say things like **P-7** and **NO STEAM** and **B. HALLOWAY, WEDS.**

They are the only creatures in the vug that are ever seen to *decide*. That is a large claim, and it is probably wrong. But when a mantis stands at a dull blade for a long time and then removes it, you will feel that a choice has been made, and that the room is one blade cleaner for it.

## The Whistlers

Nobody has seen them. They live in the shelves of the ceiling, in the dark between the shelves, and they make the long, sliding, falling whistles that drop through the chord like stones through a chandelier. Each whistle begins high and thin and ends somewhere under the floor. They do not come in time with anything. They *fall* in time. Their descents finish on the beat, and it is the only thing they seem to care about.

The best guess is that they are something like bats, or something like eels that have found their way into air. The beetles cannot see them and the mantises do not tap for them. Once in a long while something small and dry drifts down from the ceiling, a husk or a shed skin or a fold of pale membrane like a wet paper wing, and lies on the floor for a few days until the beetles carry it off.

The holes where the ropy belts enter the roof are lined with their droppings, a dark, glossy crust. It could be that the whistlers live in the belts' passages and that they are warm there. It could be that they *are* the belts, a long, cured, patient animal strung through the rock, turning the flywheels without ever showing a face. Nobody in the vug is in a position to say. If you had asked a beetle, it would have said *here, here, here*.

## The Ones in the Floor

The worms are heard and not seen. They live in the fractures below the tar, in a warm dark that has never had a shape. They are long, and they are slow, and they sound like a cello played by a very large, very calm hand. Their notes are so low that you hear them mostly in your shins, and they seem to lean against the floor from underneath, as though the vug were a drum and they were tuning the head.

The two pipes that sit slightly out of pitch, the ones that give the four-second beat, stand directly over the two largest fractures. This may mean nothing. Or it may mean that the beat is not a defect but a *worm*: two of them, in two cracks, holding two notes a hair apart, and the pipes above them faithfully copying what comes up through the stone. In that case the clock that is nobody's is at least *somebody's neighbor*.

## The Pale Growths

The lithophytes are the quiet citizens. They are bone-colored rosettes on the vent-lips, breathing sulfur and doing nothing else, and their filaments shiver in the sound like the hair on an arm. They are not asleep, but they are the nearest thing to it that the vug allows. The spore-cups, dark and round, open once in a long while and release a puff of dust that the beetles walk through and carry off on their varnish to every warm floor.

On the ledge near the roof are the two stands of reeds, the jointed ones with the translucent pipes. They are the vug's only *listeners* in the plain sense: their whole body is a set of ears turned inside out, so that they take the vent-wind in and give it back as a high, faint, clear tone, an octave above the chord, that belongs to no machine. It is easy to miss. When you notice it, you hear that the chord has a top to it, a lit edge, and that the top is grown.

## The Chimney

There is one more thing, and it is only a possibility. The brick chimney that stands alone on the floor breathes warm air out of its mouth, slowly, in time. Around its base the sulfur dust is swept smooth in a ring, and the ring is not made by the beetles, who go around it. Something small, or something that is not there, tends the ground at the foot of the only building that has no building.

It may be the last of the ones who came before, or their echo, or their habit. Or it may be that a chimney which has breathed for long enough at the right frequency simply becomes an inhabitant, as a bell becomes one, or a room.

## The Visitor

You become one of them, briefly. That is the last fact about who lives there. Within a quarter of an hour, your breath has slowed toward the four seconds, and your pulse has started to lean on the downstroke of the pistons. You do not decide this. You look at a beetle column and find that you are nodding. Your chest is rasping *here, here, here*, quietly, in the small muscles of the ribs.

The vug does not welcome you, because it doesn't know you have come. But it does not refuse you either. It takes you in at the same rate as it takes in everything, as one more warm body in a room that holds its shape by holding everyone in it to the beat. The longer you stay, the harder it is to remember that you have a different clock, one that runs in one direction, and that you are going to have to climb up into it.
