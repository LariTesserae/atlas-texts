# The Tenant

Nobody is seen here, and yet the place is lived in. The evidence is the sort left by a very patient occupant: a warm hollow, a cairn kept up, a wall begun and left, and sand that keeps arranging itself into the same gesture. Whoever lives in the Kettle has no sound of its own, because the vents have taken every sound there is. So it lives in the other channels: heat, pressure, pattern, and light.

## What It Is

Where the water holds a pale, even glow with no source, the daylight is not folded into the water and forgotten. It is being kept. Those patches are the Tenant, or the parts of it that can be seen.

It has no edge you could point to. On its wakeful days it is a loose, luminous body of lit water, taller than a house and no denser than the water around it, distinguishable only because it is calmer. The gyres bend around it. The bubbles that rise sideways rise sideways *toward* it and slip past. If you swim into it, the roar does not lessen, but it stops being weather and becomes something nearer to a hum inside a held breath. This is the one place in the basin where thought regains some purchase, and it is not comfortable. The thoughts you find there are slow and unhurried, and not quite your own.

At its center, when it condenses, there is sometimes a suggestion of a shoulder or a long curved back, a warmth with a posture. Then the light spreads out again and the posture is gone. It is not shy. It simply has no reason to stay one shape.

## The Hollow

It sleeps in the ring of pale stones above the vents, in the hollow the size of a body. The hollow is warm because the Tenant lies in it, or has just risen from it, or will lie in it soon. With time as loose as it is here, these are hard to tell apart, and the Tenant does not distinguish them either. It goes to bed and gets up in the same motion. A visitor may find the hollow empty and warm, or full of faint light and not warm at all, as though the heat were ahead of or behind the sleeper.

Sleeping is how it listens. It lies with one hand spread on the floor, palm down, and reads the mountain's pulse through the skin of the rock the way a physician reads a wrist. The sand-figures near the ring are the imprint of this: the pattern of a hand, remade each time the vents shift key, because the Tenant is always pressing and the sand is always answering.

## What It Does

It keeps the lake up. This is its main labor and hardly feels like labor. The water stands above the rim because something is holding it in the way a cupped palm holds water, with attention rather than force. The moving wall at the edge, slick and faintly steaming, is where the hand's cupping meets the outside air. The Tenant does not hold it hard. It holds it the way you hold a sleeping child's weight, without noticing until you try to set it down.

It permits breathing. This is a courtesy, and it is the reason the rule about thinking too hard exists. The Tenant allows a visitor to breathe the way a host allows a guest to sit, on an unspoken understanding that nobody will examine the arrangement. When someone stops to reason about how the water is entering their lungs, they are attending to the Tenant's allowance with suspicion, and the Tenant flinches. The allowance flickers. The visitor coughs up the lake. It is not punishment. It is a startled hand.

It tends the marks. The seven-stone cairn on the north ledge is its tally, though of what is unclear. Perhaps it is the seven notes the vents have held longest, or seven visitors, or seven mornings that mattered. It adds nothing to the cairn now. It brushes the algae from the top stone from time to time and leaves the rest furred. The curved wall of fitted basalt is a thing it began and stopped, and it does not seem troubled by the stopping. Perhaps it forgot what the wall was for. Perhaps the wall is finished and the wall is simply ten paces long.

It arranges the sand. When the vents change key, the figures dissolve and reform, and this is the Tenant's speech: rings, rosettes, lattices, a hand-high ridge that stands for the length of a note. Where two notes sit in a steady interval, the figures hold and look like the floorplans of rooms. These are its rooms. It does not need them. It draws them because it is the sort of creature that, given a stable interval, builds a place to be in.

## How It Lives in Time

The Tenant is older than the lake and younger than the break, and both are true. It lives unevenly along the flow of time, partly ahead of itself and partly behind. This explains the cairn, which is older than the water that covers it. It explains the bubble seen rising in three places, and the stone that falls and is already half-buried when it lands. These are the Tenant's footprints, the places where it has stepped through an instant more than once. It does not experience this as confusing. Being in several places at several heights is simply how it moves.

It has a sense of company that has nothing to do with presence. When you visit, it has already noticed you and has already noticed you leaving. The hand in the sand near the ring is sometimes fresh when you arrive and sometimes dissolves as you look, and this is the Tenant greeting you from both ends of your visit at once.

## The Neighbors

The reeds hum for it, or near it. Their mineral beads are tuned, faintly, to the interval it likes best, and they lean toward the ring in the current the way plants lean toward a window. The pale eel-thing drifts through its lit patches and is unharmed, and comes back. The thumb-sized grazers crawl across its hollow when it is not there and step carefully around the edges when it is.

The large shape at the far edge of the green, which arrives as a change in pressure before it arrives as anything else, is not the Tenant. It may be the Tenant's kin, or its visitor, or the reason it keeps the lake up: a far, slow thing that needs this warm room for a while and does not stay. The Tenant makes no move toward it and no move away. When the pressure shifts, the sand near the ring settles into a different figure, an open, relaxed hand, palm up.

## If You Meet It

You will not meet it as a person. You will notice that the noise has gone quiet in a way that has nothing to do with volume. You will notice that you have been standing for some time in a pale light without a source and that you are not afraid. You may feel a slow warmth pass along your back, like a hand not quite touching. If you are wise, you will not think about it. You will breathe, and lower yourself to the floor, and press your own palm flat into the sand beside the ring, and feel the mountain's pulse arrive in your wrist a fraction before it arrives in your ears.

Then the figures shift, and the hand in the sand is a little larger than yours, and yours is inside it.

## When You Leave

It does not follow you out. It lets go of the wall as you step through, as gently as it holds it. But the ringing goes with you down the scree, and the rocks tremble in the key of the lake, and that is the Tenant, still pressing its palm to the mountain, keeping the time so that the mountain does not have to keep it alone.
