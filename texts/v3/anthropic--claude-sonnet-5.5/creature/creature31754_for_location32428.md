# The Lodgers

You will not see them first. You will see where they have been.

Across the moss run the long raised veins where the heavy rodents tunnel through the peat. In places the crest of a vein has been pressed flat by broad prints, five-toed, the toes spread wide and gripping, already swelling back as the moss recovers. There is a smell, faint and specific: crushed fern, cold iron, and something like the inside of a wet wool hat. Then a tremor passes through a stand of filmy fern, and something that was there is not.

This is how the shelf introduces its people, as an absence that has just occurred.

## Bodies

They stand a little under a meter and a half, long in the back and arm, built to go under branches rather than around them. The fur is short, dense, and dark, and rain beads on it and runs off in threads. Every Lodger is also planted. Colonies of lichen are permitted on the shoulders and along the spine, slate blue and oxblood, and tufts of cushion moss are kept in the hollow behind the neck and the small of the back. A Lodger crouched in the forest is a mossy stump with a slightly wrong outline.

Under the jaw, along the inner forearms, and across the palms, the skin is bare, thin, and pale, and this is where they speak. The eyes are large and black, with a clear inner lid drawn down in fog and rain, so that a Lodger looking at you through the mist seems to be looking through a drop of water. The feet are broad, with a long, clever second toe. They walk the way the mat requires, weight rolling heel to toe, distributing themselves over the water beneath.

## Speech

They have no spoken name for themselves. The nearest is a long two-handed press that means something like *those who live in the coat.* The name others might give them, Lodgers, is a courtesy borrowed from the way they occupy things without owning them.

Their speech has three channels, all quiet, because sound dies in moss and because a sound is how the marten finds you.

**Touch** is the main one. Two Lodgers who meet press palms and stay there. A greeting lasts as long as it takes a drop to gather at a leaf tip and fall. Meaning is carried in pressure, in rhythm, and in warmth. A Lodger who has sat at a steam vent before an important conversation is understood to mean it.

**Scent** comes from glands at the wrist. It is rubbed on trunks and left along trails: *this way is dry, something dropped here, I was here at the time of the first fog.* A trail is a very long sentence that many people have added to.

**Color** is for distance. The pale skin flushes ochre, then gray-green, then a deep bruised red, taking a full breath or more to cross from one to the next. Nothing is quick, because a quick flash in the fog reads as an eye, and an eye is a predator. Across a marsh channel, or from ridge to ridge over a sea of fog, they hold up forearms and pass messages hand to hand along a chain of slow lights. A rumor can take a day to cross a valley and arrives unchanged.

## The Coat

The Lodgers believe that the ridges are animals.

They are not wrong about the shape. The long, rounded backs of dark rock, sleeping in rows, are a herd, and the mosses and lichens and ferns are the herd's coat. The fog is their breath, drawn in each dusk and let out each dawn. The weeping seeps are their sweat. The Lodgers are the things that live in the coat and keep it, the way small birds keep the hides of larger animals.

So their work is grooming. They pinch back the liverworts where they crowd the cushion moss. They clear the seep-threads so water runs evenly and does not pool and rot the mat. They patch bare places with plugs of moss, pressed in with the heel of the hand and watered from their own mouths. They never build, because one does not build on an animal. They lodge in what is already there: a hollow in a moss cushion, the crook of a log, a gap between roots. Each hollow is tended and handed down until, after six generations of sleeping bodies, it has taken the exact shape of a Lodger curled on its side.

## The Day

They sleep on the ridgetops, in the warmer air above the pooled cold. At dawn they wake above a white sea and press palms in the pale light without moving.

At mid-morning the fog lifts and tears and climbs the slopes, and the Lodgers descend with it into the dripping forest. The first thing they do is drink. Spiders string their sheets between the branches, and on foggy mornings each is a hammock of glass beads. The Lodgers draw a tongue along the silk and take the beads without tearing the web, carefully, because a spider whose web is torn hunts all day, and a hungry spider is everyone's problem.

They eat what the forest gives without emptying it. They take the fungal shelves from the second tier up and leave the lowest for the slugs. They take a few tadpoles from each rosette, and fern croziers, and the forearm-long banana slugs. The slugs are wrapped in leaf and left in the warm seeps of the steam vents. That is the only cooking on the shelf, and the slugs come out tasting of iron and sweet smoke.

When the afternoon cloud lowers they go to ground, in pairs, back to back, in dry hollows under leaning trunks. The rain is the one time of day when talk is easy, because nobody is moving. Palm to palm, they say the long things. Then the cloud breaks along the western edge and the horizontal gold comes through the trees, and they climb into the lower branches and stand in it with their arms held out. Their fur steams like everything else. The color in their palms brightens, and the colonies of lichen on their backs turn faintly luminous, and for an hour the forest is full of small warm figures the same color as the light.

Their unit of time is the siphon. The terraces on the western slope fill and drain every forty minutes, and a Lodger who says *two gulps* means an hour and a half, or a short walk. The herons that stand at the terraces know the rhythm too. The Lodgers say they learned it from the herons. The herons, presumably, say nothing.

## The Walking Chain

The first thing a young Lodger learns is stillness. The second is the palm language. The third is how to notice what is gone.

Predation on the shelf is silent and sudden. A body drops from a limb and the ferns shiver, and there is nothing to hear. So Lodgers travel in single file, each with one hand flat on the back of the one ahead. The chain is not for guidance, since the trails are known. It is a head count that never stops. If the pressure under a palm goes slack, everyone knows in the same instant.

When it happens, no one cries out. The line halts. Each Lodger presses a palm against the moss where the last one stood, and stays. Nothing is said aloud, because saying it aloud would tell the marten where to find the rest.

## The Kettle

In the marsh valley nearest the eastern rim lies a great orange-black shape on its side, furred with moss, with fern trees growing out of its mouth. A family of Lodgers lodges in its firebox.

They do not think it is a made thing. In their understanding it is an animal that lay down a very long time ago and did not get up. The narrow rail running into the peat is its tail, and they never walk along it, because things that go into the peat do not always come back. The fern trees in its firebox are its breath, or its hair. They groom it as they groom the ridges, tending the moss on its flanks and rubbing ochre from the marsh edge into the worst of the rust so that it keeps its color.

They are gentle with it, and slightly pitying. It is the only creature they know of that has been finished. It cannot spread, or root, or lean. When they lay a palm on its cold iron flank, what they say to it is the closest thing they have to a prayer: *you are still being made.*

## Made Things

They own very little, and none of it is complete.

The rain-cloak is a living mat of cushion moss on a frame of woven vine, worn over the shoulders and rewatered every dawn. A cloak is older than its wearer. Sections rot and are replaced, and new moss is seeded in from other ridges, until no thread of the original remains and the cloak is still, by universal agreement, the same cloak.

From the bog-iron pans they break small nodules, orange-brown and lumpy. They sew them into cloak hems for weight and give them as tokens. A nodule from a distant pan, carried across a valley, is the most serious gift there is. The ochre they scrape from marsh edges goes on their palms to deepen certain words, and on tree trunks at a child's eye height to mark the dry ways.

Names are not fixed. A name is a pattern of touch and a patch of color, and it changes as the lichen on a Lodger's back spreads and shifts. At thirty a Lodger has a different name than at ten, and only the ones who gave the old names remember them.

## Settling

When they grow old, Lodgers slow down. They choose a hollow on a ridge, somewhere with afternoon light, and sit there longer each day. The moss climbs the legs. Lichen takes the shoulders. The palms, which once flashed through three colors, settle into a single long green.

The young come to sit with the old and press palms, and the old answer. At first the answers are slow. Later they are so slow that a question put in one season is answered in the next. No one is sure when the answers stop, and no one stops pressing. Eventually a seedling stands in the hollow, and after a time a tree with a green column of a trunk, and inside the column the faint hint of a shape. The Lodgers say that the shape inside is not the tree.

The dead taken by the marten are not settled, and they are grieved differently. But the fallen logs are full of small trees, and the Lodgers set their hands on those too, in passing, without knowing whose they were.

## The Cranes

Twice a year the cranes come over in long lines, and the Lodgers do the one loud thing they ever do.

They come out of the forest onto the open marsh, whole communities of them, standing on the tussocks with their faces turned up. The cranes' calls come down through the fog, thin and rusty, like a hinge turning far away. The Lodgers fill their throats, which have almost never been used, and answer. It is a small, cracked, creaking sound, an imitation of an imitation, and it goes up into the fog as thin as breath.

The lines pass and the sound thins to nothing. The Lodgers stand a while longer, slightly embarrassed, looking at their feet. Then someone presses a palm to a neighbor's back, and the chain re-forms, and the silence closes over the marsh again.

## The Big Rain

When the great gray wall arrives and the three-day rain swells the braided channels into a single glittering sheet, the ridges become archipelagos, and the Lodgers finally travel.

They cut slabs of the floating mat, a body-length thick, and push off. Peat rafts drift between the islands with their cargo of nodules and cloak fragments and moss seed from distant ridges. Communities that have talked only in colors across a valley for a year meet palm to palm. It is the season of exchange, of arranged pairings, of news that arrives in the body and not in the light. When the water falls, the rafts settle back onto their own ground and sink into the moss. In a season no one can tell where they were cut.

## Under the Mat

There is one other tenant the Lodgers do not talk about.

It lives beneath the floating mat in the black lanes of the deepest marshes. No Lodger has seen it. What they have felt is a slow pressure underfoot, as though something very large were turning over in its sleep. They cross those places at the narrowest points, single file, hands on backs, holding their breath. It is not a predator, they think. It is only large, and it is asleep, and it is another part of the herd.

## Feel

Lodgers do not hurry. They are always in the middle of something: a cloak being rewoven, a trail being lengthened, a conversation with an elder that will take another year to answer. In the late gold light, standing on a low branch with their arms out, steaming, they look like a kind of fruit the tree has not yet finished growing. In some sense that is true. The moss is on their backs, the rust is in their pockets, the rain is in their fur, and every one of them is on the way to being a small green mound with a tree on top.

They know this, and it does not trouble them. It is the coat, and they live in it.
