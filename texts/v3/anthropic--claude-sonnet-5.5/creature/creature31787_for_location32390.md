# Who Lives on the Floor

The first thing to know is that most of the residents are smaller than a thumbnail, and that they outnumber everything else on the continent's high ground put together. The plateau is a small-animal country with a few large animals passing through it, like weather.

## The Singers

The frogs hold the land. There are perhaps a dozen kinds in the mat and the bromeliads, and nobody has counted them all.

The smallest is a black frog with a butter-yellow throat, about the size of a lentil, that spends its whole life inside one cartwheel of leaves. It lays its eggs on the wet leaf-bases and guards them. When they hatch, the mother carries each tadpole up on her back and drops it into a separate cup of water, then returns every few days to lay an unfertilized egg beside it as food. A single bromeliad may hold a nursery of twenty cups, each with its own child and a mother who knows the route by touch. Her call is a bell note, a clean *tink* a little above the pitch of a struck glass. The males answer from other plants and the tone passes across a whole hillside, tuned differently in each hollow.

There is a black toad that cannot hop. Its feet are too stiff and its limbs too short. When something large comes near, it tucks itself into a ball and lets go. It rolls off the cushion, bounces down the slope, and lands somewhere in the peat with a small wet tap. It has a surprised, injured dignity when found again. It is one of the oldest lineages on the plateau, and it never left, because there was never anywhere better to go.

Others whistle, or trill like a wire being drawn, or make one long descending note like a coin spinning down on a table. In the evening they sort themselves by pitch and by distance and by hour, so that the chorus has a grammar even though the ear only hears a wall. The frogs that call from over hollow ground sound lower, because the mat under them acts like the body of a drum. This is how the ground can be read by anyone who has learned it.

## The Under-Crowd

Below the frogs, in the wet dark of the mat, live things that have never seen the sky. Pale springtails jump in the peat at a density that would make you dizzy if you could see it. There are blind beetles and thread-thin worms, mites with red bodies like grains of paprika, and a long translucent centipede that hunts along root-channels the way a train runs in a tunnel. Fungus threads bind it all. The mat is not so much a plant as a city of roots, hyphae, and feeding mouths, three meters deep, breathing slowly.

In the bromeliad cups are the drowned worlds: mosquito wrigglers, damselfly nymphs, tiny crustaceans that flick through the water and are eaten by other tiny crustaceans. A cup no larger than a teacup can hold a complete food chain, with a top predator the size of a grain of rice. When the cup dries in a hard week, they all die together. When rain returns, the eggs that waited in the dust wake, and it begins again.

Slugs the length of a forearm glide over the cushion-plants by night, silver-trailed and entirely unhurried. Nothing much eats them. They are the closest the plateau has to cattle.

## The Ones with Wings

Hummingbirds do not just visit here, they are the resident pollinating class. They come in a dozen plumages: copper and green, blue-throated, one with a white tail like a cut feather, one so dark it looks black until it turns in the light. They fight over bromeliad spikes with sharp ticking calls, hover in the fog with their bodies beaded silver, and at night sink into a torpor so deep that a hand laid against one feels a cold pebble. In the cold hours before dawn the plateau is dotted with dozens of them perched motionless on stalks, and they can be lifted off like ornaments if you are foolish enough to.

Swifts nest in the cliff seams and pour into the largest wells at dusk in a spiral, thousands at a time, calling as they fall. Within the shafts, where light never reaches, they roost on ledges above a black floor. Below them, in the streams that run in the dark, are cave animals the color of candle wax: salamanders with feathery red gills, small eyeless fish, and shrimp that live their whole lives in the drip of the quartz. These are the deep residents. They know the plateau only as a rumor of water coming down and sometimes a petal, a feather, a dead beetle, a gift from the upper world.

A hawk with rust-colored shoulders lives on the thermals off the cliffs. When it passes, the frogs go silent for a mile around, and this silence is the closest thing the plateau has to a government.

## The Low Walkers

The large grazers move in bands of six or twelve, sometimes thirty, across the sedge tops. They are knee-high to a person, long-haired, and drab, with a coat the color of wet straw that holds droplets of fog like a fleece of glass beads. They drink by licking their own flanks and each other's. They have wide, splayed, cushioned hooves that spread their weight across the mat, and they know exactly where it is thin. Their trails run in long soft curves that go around the buried wells and avoid the places where the peat sounds hollow. No one taught them this, and they still cannot say how they know.

They rest in the lee of the ridges, all facing the same way, and chew. When cloud rolls over them they do not move. The fog collects on their backs and runs off in strings.

The hunters that follow are foxlike, rust-gray with long muzzles and black stockings, thin and quiet. On the plateau there is little for a hunter of large game to catch, so these are patient omnivores. They will take a young grazer if the chance comes, but most days they eat frogs, slugs, big beetles, and fallen fruit. They also dig for eggs. They live in dens in the drier outcrops and travel in pairs, calling to each other in a soft two-note whistle that is nearly lost against the chorus. The frogs seem not to fear them, which is a kind of comment.

Snakes are here, slender and banded, pale as sand and warm as a stone. They lie on the sunny rock and wait for a lizard, or for a frog that is too tired to move.

## The People Who Come

There are people, but they do not exactly live here. They arrive in the season between the great rains, twelve or twenty at a time, up a cleft in the cliff where roots and rope ladders have been renewed for longer than anyone remembers. They come in a file, quietly, the old ones first and the children carried. The oldest woman is usually at the front, because she has the map in her ears.

That is the truth of it. They navigate by sound. Each of them, from childhood, learns the register of the ground: a certain pitch of frog means solid mat, a lower thrum means a chamber beneath, and a sudden hush means the peat is thin and wet and will not hold. The cairns of white quartz mark places where the map is uncertain, but the real map is a way of listening. Those who guide the group walk with their heads slightly bowed, as if hearing something from the ground itself.

They speak little. On the plateau a voice is swallowed at ten paces, so they use their hands and a few whistles. They bring nets for hunting the low walkers, though they take only a few each year, and the herds have learned when the people are near and when they are not. They gather sedge for rope, sun-dry peat for fuel, and a bitter blue berry that grows only in the moss. They fill skins with water from the bromeliad cups, which tastes of leaf and rain. They sleep on the pole platforms above the wet, and in the night, with the frogs roaring through the wood, they lie awake and listen as if they were in a warm room while a storm passes.

They do not stay long. When the first heavy rains arrive from the east, they leave, and the mat closes over the camps in a season. They say the plateau does not want them, and also that it does not mind them. They do not say why they come. Perhaps it is only that a person needs, once a year, to stand on the oldest floor and be small.

## What Is Really There

If you walk west past the last cairn, and stop, and kneel in the hummocks with your hand pressed flat on the mat, you will feel the noise before you understand it. It comes up through the palm as a fine trembling, thousands of small throats sending their voice through the weave of roots. The plateau is not empty at all. It is crowded and singing, and the few big animals and the few people are only guests on the surface of something enormous and alive at every scale beneath the skin.

The frogs will begin at dusk, one and then a hundred. A hummingbird will hover a moment at your shoulder and go. Somewhere a well will breathe out its cold air, and a stone will fall in it, and take a long time to answer.
