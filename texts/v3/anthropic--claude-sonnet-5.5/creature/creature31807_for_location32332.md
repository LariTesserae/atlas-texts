# Those Who Live in the Chord Room

Most of what lives here does not know it is living. It has only gone on.

## The Haloes

The crust is the first inhabitant you notice, and only because it draws rings. Wherever a tube mouth catches a ray, a pale green-gold film has spread outward from the bright opening in a slow concentric bloom, thickest at the lip, thinning to nothing about a hand's width away. Up close it is barely a color, more the suggestion that the glass has been breathed on by something warm.

It is a colony of single cells lying edge to edge in a layer thinner than a coat of paint. Each one is a small green lens holding a little sunlight. They do not compete or hurry. A cell at the center of a halo has the best light and the worst view of everything else, and a cell at the rim has the dimmest light and the most room. Neither seems to mind. When one dies, and they do, one at a time, so slowly that no eye could catch it, the light it held passes to the neighbor who slides or swells into the gap. The halo keeps its exact diameter. It has kept it, ring by ring and tube by tube, since before the last brine left.

The crust cannot hear. But it sits on glass that sings, and every cell lies on a surface trembling at the same frequency for its whole existence, generation after generation. If the crust knows anything, it knows the chord as *ground*: the thing that is under you and always was. A halo has no word for sound because it has never met silence. It might say instead that the world is a thing that holds you, faintly, all the time.

## The Tuft-Keepers

In the wider tubes, in the shaded throats where the light arrives already softened, the grey moss has packed itself in like wadding in a bell. Tufts, springy and dry, each stem a hair-fine column of dead-looking cells with a living skin. Press one and it gives, then returns, and the tube's note wavers slightly under your finger and steadies again.

They live on the trace moisture the gas carries up from below, a dampness so slight it is more a rumor than a wetness. A tuft may take a long life to gather a single bead of water. It does not grow toward anything. It has no direction, no season, no reason to reach. It holds the water in its stems and gives a little to the air, and the two amounts are equal.

You come to think of them as the elders of the room, though they have done nothing to earn it except stay. They sit in the deep bass tubes, the ones that make the sternum-rumble, and they are shaken more than anything else alive. Whatever they are, they are steady. A tuft has been vibrated at one pitch for more years than a mountain has been worn down, and it is still perfectly green-grey and perfectly dry and perfectly there.

## The Threads Below

The open rings in the floor drop into darkness, and there is life down there too, though no one has seen it and it would not look like much. In the fine grey silt at the bottom of the deep tubes, where the gas first enters, lives a colony of something more thread than animal, a pale weave of filaments feeding on the mineral breath itself. It needs no light. It has never seen a ray or a halo. It knows warmth, faintly, and the taste of the rising gas, and the fact that the world above it is loud.

If the crust knows the chord as ground, the threads know it as *weather*. They are closer to its source. The vibration reaches them as a slow pulse rather than a tone, a heaviness that comes up through the gas and goes through them and keeps going. They would say, if they could say anything, that the world is a long, low pressing from above and a long, low pushing from below, and that they are the small place where the two meet.

Nothing connects the threads to the crust. Light never goes down and gas never carries a message up. Yet they live in the same instrument, at opposite ends of the same tubes, and each is part of what keeps the other's world exactly what it is.

## The Absent Builders

The ones who made all this are not gone, exactly. Somewhere in the deepest bundles, in tubes so narrow that only water once passed through them, there may still be the last cells of the original mat-colonies, dried to a pinch of dust and waiting. No one has looked and no one could. They laid down glass for a span with no useful number, and then the brine left, and they stopped, and that is all. Perhaps they are dead. Perhaps they are only very patient, and they would bud again if the water returned.

Either way they are the room's oldest presence. Every tube is a sentence they wrote by living. The chord is their handwriting, read aloud by wind.

## The One Who Visits

And there is you, or whoever comes. The room has no resident animal, but it takes visitors seriously in the only way it can, by leaving something in them.

You enter sideways, out of the quiet basin, into the sound. For a while you are just a body being played. Then you learn the hushes and the aching places, and you stand in a cancellation pocket with the chord pressing at your ribs and you close your eyes. After long enough the loudness stops being noise. You are, briefly, another creature of the room: still, warmed by borrowed light, vibrating at a pitch not your own, living on nothing but what the place lets through.

When you leave, the chord leaves with you. In the hush of the basin you carry it faintly, a single sustained sound behind your eyes that dies away slowly. In those moments the reef has a resident that walks. It goes out over the pale rubble humming, an odd small instrument in the world's great quiet, carrying a note that will fade long before it ever reaches anyone else.

The crust doesn't see you leave, and the moss doesn't notice. The threads below never knew you came. But the room registers you as it registers everything. Not a single tube lip is broken, and the chord holds, exactly the same.
