# Those Who Keep the Works

Begin with the evidence, since no one there will introduce themselves.

A line of small square holes in the moss, each a hand's width and each filling with clear water, runs in a curve around the base of a Stack. Someone has been walking here with a probing rod. Farther on, a bellows-bed has a patch of its leathery skin sewn shut with black cord, the stitches tight and even. The seam has been healing for years. The bed has grown new tissue around the thread and taken it in, so the stitching is now part of the animal, or the plant, or the machine, whichever it is.

Someone lives here. You will not see them for some time.

## The Wrung

They have no name for themselves that survives translation. Travelers who have met them call them the Wrung, because the plateau has wrung most things out of them. They are people, or descended from people, or they became people again after some interval as something else. They are small and dense, brown as strong tea, with skin tanned by centuries of peat acid. The tannin is in their blood now. It gives them the look of old saddle leather and keeps them from rotting in a place where everything else rots gloriously.

They walk with long rods of fused stem, jointed like bamboo, and they test every step. The rods are not only for safety. Each is hollow and tuned to a different pitch, and when a Wrung taps one against the ground it rings faintly. The Wrung talk this way across kilometers. Sound carries strangely on the plateau, and they have learned which directions it lies in and which it fibs in. A conversation is a slow patter of taps, a pause, and an answer that may arrive before the question or not at all. They do not seem to mind. They have made an idiom of the plateau's unreliability, and a message is understood as much by the order it arrives in as by its content.

They speak aloud too, but rarely, and softly, as one talks in a room where someone is sleeping.

They live low. Some dwell in the cooled lower chambers of Stacks, where the peat's warmth rises through the hollow core and the walls sweat gently. The Stacks tolerate them, or have not yet noticed them. A Wrung camp has one rule the visitor is told at once: **never make the door face the Stack's lip.** A Wrung dwelling has its entrance turned away, and its dwellers keep their eyes low and their attention thin, the way you would walk past a sleeping dog. They are practiced at looking at nothing. It is their oldest craft.

Others live in the grub-smiths' galleries, widened and re-lined, in cells of hard secretion the color of amber. The grubs tick along the walls. The Wrung say the ticking is company.

## What They Do

Ask, and they will shrug. The nearest answer is that they tend.

They stitch bellows-beds that have torn, though a bed does not need mending and would grow over the wound in a season. They clear silt from the throats of sink-pools, though the pools would clear themselves. They move the Belts a few meters when a Belt drifts toward a place it should not go, and no Wrung can say why the place is wrong. They cut and re-cut the small channels through the living mat, and the streams that start from nothing are, in part, theirs. They begin the streams in the middle of flat ground and start them running, and the streams go on to vanish into pale throats as they always did.

It is work without product. The Wrung know this, and they do not seem troubled. A woman old enough that her face had settled like wet clay into a permanent patience said to a visitor: *The Works don't need us. That's why we're allowed.*

Their dead are not buried. They are given to the Belts. The tannin preserves them, so they travel whole, slow and dark and peaceful, at a meter an hour, down to the spiraling pools. The pools strain and digest them, though not fast. Sometimes a face surfaces in the slow current and looks up through the black water with an expression of mild attention. Everyone agrees this is not a haunting. It is simply that the dead are still being used.

## The Others

The Wrung are not alone, and they are careful about it.

**The Furrow-Walker.** It is the large thing, the wake that is never seen with its maker. The Wrung do not call it an animal. They say *the one who goes under.* A furrow opens in the moss at walking pace, deep and long, and fills with brown water, and if you stand where it is heading you hear the mat creak like a ship's hull. Nothing comes up. In the hours after it passes, the ground closes with a soft sound like a great exhale, and the Wrung leave small offerings at the edge of the closing seam: a knot of cord, a ring of braided moss, a bead of hardened grub-amber. The offerings are gone by morning. Nobody claims to have seen them taken. Nobody says they have not.

Descriptions vary. One story says it is a single vast root, walking. Another says it is a herd, so tightly packed that it moves as one mass. A third, told only by the very old and only in whispers, says it is a Wrung who went under long ago and kept going.

**The Knocker.** It is not seen at all. Its work is the slow deep tapping in the ground that almost repeats. The Wrung do not call it a creature either. They say *the count.* They believe the plateau is tallying something. Every Wrung child is taught to listen for the count, and taught not to answer with a rod, because a rod struck at the wrong moment is a wrong number, and the count must start again. The old ones sit with their rods across their knees and let the knocking pass through them. They say it is like being a tuning fork in a very large hall.

The visitors who notice their pulse matching it are not, the Wrung say, in danger. They are being included. It happens to everyone who stays. It is not a capture. It is more like being asked, very politely, over a very long time, to take up a small share of the arithmetic.

## What Emerges

Return now to the description of the plateau and its odd claim: *a factory without workers or product, running for the sake of running.* It is true, and it is incomplete. The factory has workers. It only hires slowly.

The Wrung were once visitors, or the children of visitors, or the children of the children of a party that lost the streams in a dry-fall and walked toward a Stack that never got nearer. Their pulse found the knocking. They learned to tap. Their skin took the tannin, and their patience took the shape of the land. Over generations they became what the Works had lacked: hands. Small brown hands that stitch and clear and cut, that give the Works an occupation and give themselves a purpose no one asked them for.

They are also, quietly, the plateau's audience. It has been said the Works have none. That is close to true. But the Wrung watch the Stacks glow at dusk, the scatter of cold lamps across a dark with no far side, and something in their bearing is not quite worship and not quite work. It is what you might do at the bedside of something enormous and very old, which does not know you are there, and which you have decided to stay beside anyway.

At the end of a long day, the tapping across the basins dies down, and the plateau holds its single sustained chord as the wind plays the Stacks. In the low places the milk-white slicks come up, and on the rim of a basin a Wrung sits with a rod across the knees. Beneath that, slow and nearly repeating, the count goes on. The Wrung's chest rises and falls in time with it, and the Wrung could not say, and does not try to say, which of them began.
