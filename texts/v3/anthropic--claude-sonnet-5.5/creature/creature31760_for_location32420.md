# Those Who Live on the Plateau of Throats

## What they call themselves

They call themselves by a sign, not a word: a hand cupped loosely at the base of the throat, then opened outward like something released. Lowlanders translate it as "the held-voiced" or "throat-folk," and the people don't object, since a translation is only ever a rough sign for the real thing. There are some tens of thousands of them, scattered across the districts on their rises and rims, and they are less one people than a long agreement among neighbors.

## Bodies made for watching

They are broad through the wrist and forearm, with strong, quick, well-kept hands, because the hands are the voice. The face is the grammar. Eyebrows, the set of the mouth, and the tilt of the head mark questions, negation, irony, and tenderness, and a person signing with a flat face is understood to be either ill or lying. Their eyes are trained wide, and they catch motion at the very edge of sight. A crowd of them in a market moves like a school of fish, everyone aware of everyone through a hundred small flickers.

Their skin is damp-pale and their hair is kept short or braided tight against the wet. Their ears are stained yellow or amber at the rim from a lifetime of beeswax, and the wax is dyed by trade and district: green for the loom halls, ochre for the crushers, black-brown for turbine-wrights. A stranger's plugs mark them at once as someone who hasn't chosen.

Sleeves are banded at the cuff with pale cloth, so a signing hand shows clearly across a courtyard or a lamp-lit street. In winter they wear waxed wool and oiled felt, and fingerless gloves that leave the joints free. Bare hands in the cold are a sign of urgency, of something that has to be said now.

## The hearing and the deaf

No wall stands between them. Roughly one child in nine is born deaf, and every child, hearing or not, learns to sign before anything else. By middle age most of the hearing have lost enough that the difference is small anyway. The deaf are not pitied. They are envied a little, since they live in the plateau's voice without being worn down by it. They are the best night-signalers, the steadiest lamp-speakers on the moor, and the ones others go to when a message must cross three districts without a single error.

But the deaf are also the ones who say the Still Rooms are stranger than anyone knows. In such a room the floor, which speaks constantly everywhere else, finally stops. Some deaf people go there only to feel that. They come out looking as if they have been told something.

## Touch, name, and pitch

Every person has a touch-name, a pattern pressed on the inside of the wrist: two taps and a slide, or a long press and a flick. It is given in infancy by the mother and later refined by friends, who add small variations only they use. In the dark, in a crowd, in fog, a touch-name is how you know who has found you.

Each also has a Throat-name, the shaft nearest their birth, and so a pitch. They will say, without any sense of the strangeness of it, that they are "of the low Throat of Marren," and in the wet season, when Marren drops a tone, they will admit to feeling lower. It is not superstition, exactly. It is more like the way a person mentions the weather in their own bones.

## Work

The turbine-wrights spend their lives hanging from cradles in the upper walls of the shafts, tending housings that turn in the falling water. They talk to each other by tugs on rope and by hand-flashes, working in a mist so thick that a signal must be made a foot from the face to be read.

The rod-keepers walk the long lines of rotating shafts that carry power to the mills. They diagnose by hand. A palm laid flat on a spinning rod, lightly, tells them of wear, of misalignment, of a bearing about to fail. The best can name the fault in a second. Their palms are calloused into a smooth, waxy sheen, and they are the only people who shake hands cautiously.

The lamp-speakers work on the tall stone platforms that stand out on the moor between districts. They send coded flashes across miles of reed and standing water, and they read the answers, often through fog, by the shape of a glow rather than its color. Their reputation is for terseness. They are said to be able to say a whole quarrel in nine flashes.

The climbers go down into the Throats on ropes, past the turbines and below, to gather the pale cave-cress, the ghost-mushrooms, and the mineral crusts that the mills need. They come back up white to the elbows and say little about what they saw. The plateau needs them. It does not question them.

Then there are the small, specific guilds. There are tree-keepers, each family holding by inheritance the care of a named willow or rowan, who talk to their tree in sign and are considered slightly mad in a respected way. There are stair-tuners, who recut the old sympathetic steps when they wear. And there are the brick-carriers, the Walkers, who move a dying district stone by stone to a new rise. Each brick is numbered with chalk and then a chisel, and each bears a history of every district it has stood in.

## Hands at table

A meal is a slow negotiation, because hands are both food-carriers and voices. Custom is to eat with one hand and talk with the other, and to fall silent, by which they mean lower both hands, whenever someone else begins a long thought. Round tables are lit low from the center so every face and every pair of hands can be seen. Guests are seated where they can see most, and hosts take the worst seat.

They drink a dark, iron-tasting tea brewed from pan-water and reed root. They eat barley, grain, and salt brought up on the rail from the lowlands, pickled cushion-shoots, cave-cress, and the mushrooms. They do not eat the pale salamanders. Nobody has ever said why.

## The cup on the sill

Each household keeps a shallow dish of water on a sill, and the ripples in it are a clock, a weather-glass, and a kind of gossip. The pattern of the tremble says which Throats are up or down. A child who can read it well is praised. A child who can read it wrongly, or too dreamily, is teased the way a lowland child would be for daydreaming through a church bell.

Children play on the stairs with their eyes shut, naming each step by the hum in the sole. They tease one another with signs shaped just wrong, the way another people might use a mispronunciation. They learn early that the ground is a kind of speaker, and that they are always being spoken to.

## Anger, courtesy, and love

Shouting is useless here, so anger has no volume. It is done by stillness and withdrawal. To turn a shoulder, as the buildings do, is the deepest rebuke, and to refuse to look at someone's hands while they sign is worse than any blow. A quarrel between two neighbors can consist of a whole week of not being seen.

Courtesy is gentler. A palm on a shoulder means *I am here, you needn't turn.* Two fingers on a wrist mean *wait,* or *I'm with you.* Visitors are taught one sign before any other: a flat hand held to the chest, then extended, which means *I am here, and I am not hostile.* They are told it should be made slowly.

Lovers court in the dark by pressing words into each other's palms. Some of the most tender exchanges in the language are done this way, in a vocabulary that has no name because it was never meant to be read by anyone else.

## The voice

The voice is the plateau's most private thing. Everyone has one, most rarely use it, and some have not heard their own in decades. It is kept for the Still Rooms. There a couple marry by whispering their vows to each other, often the first time either has heard the other's real voice. A person told of a death in a Still Room hears it in the speaker's whisper, and the whisper is part of the news. Contracts are made there, and so are confessions. Someone who speaks aloud outside such a room, in the roar, is not thought to be heard by anyone. It is understood as a kind of prayer, or a kind of madness, or just a person talking to themselves.

Coming out of a Still Room is described as surfacing. People stand a moment in the corridor with their hands hanging at their sides, letting the noise rebuild around them. Nobody hurries them.

## The others

Cats live in every district, deaf and unimpressed, sleeping along warm pipes and on the flanks of turbine housings. The people speak to them in sign and are fairly sure they understand.

Swifts wheel in the steam columns and nest in the walls of the Throats. The pale salamanders drift in the pans, and children lay palms on the water to feel them.

The waders stand in the reeds at dusk, and people sometimes stand beside them for an hour.

## The towers

There are also the round towers, which are the reason the whitewashers exist. Once a year a small guild, always the same families, carries buckets and brushes out across the moor to each tower and repaints it white. They work without speaking, in the usual way, and when they have finished they lay a palm flat on the wall and hold it there for a long while. They will not say what they feel. Some say the wall is faintly warm. Some say it hums a moment after their hand leaves, as if a hand had been held against theirs and was slow to let go.

Nobody has opened a tower. Nobody has proposed it in living memory. If asked, the people will say only that the towers are being kept, and if pressed on what by, they make a sign meaning both *nothing* and *not ours to say*. It is the same sign they use for the dead.

## Leaving

They know their districts are temporary. Every family has moved at least once in living memory, or its grandparents have, and the ghost districts are visited the way lowlanders visit graves. In the wet season, when the moss is thick and the old streets are green and softened, people go out and sit on the rounded steps and lay their hands flat on the stones. The old buildings hum faintly, a low hum with no source anyone can find. They call it *the district remembering,* and it is not sad. They say it is the plateau still talking to the places it once held.

They will tell you they are not afraid of the roar, and that it does not frighten them because it has never once been silent. "Something that never stops," one old lamp-speaker signed to a visiting engineer, "is not a noise. It is a floor." Then she pressed two fingers to his wrist, lightly, and held them there until he stopped shaking.
