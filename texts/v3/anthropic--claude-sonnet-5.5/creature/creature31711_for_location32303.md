# Those Who Keep the Count

The first residents are the ones you can't help noticing, because they are everywhere. Ten thousand kinds of small appetite occupy the Warm Sump, and they were here before anyone thought to count them. The mats are the oldest tenants, and perhaps the true owners. On top of them live the shellmoths, the ladder-crabs, the wallowers, the eels, and the chitterers, none of them aware of the others except as food, threat, or weather. But the basin also holds a people, and they are the only ones who keep the count.

## The people

They have a name for themselves that isn't quite a word. It is a low, held syllable, pitched to sit underneath the wallowers' moan, and it means something like *the ones who are ready*. Outsiders who have heard of them say *sump-folk*, or *drip-people*, or nothing. Their neighbors have no opinion of them at all.

They are lean, and always damp. Their hair is worn long and wet on purpose, because dry hair in that air grows a crust of mineral and breaks. Their skin is the color of the terraces, a dusty cream over ochre, partly from the sinter that settles on everything and partly from years of being lightly glazed by it. The old among them look faintly enameled. They have wide, splay-toed feet with hard soles, scarred by hot water at the ankle, and they walk the wet stone with a shuffling, low-hipped, weight-spread gait that doesn't slip. Their hands are scalded and callused and very quick. Their eyes are large and dark and have adapted to the pearly dusk. They squint in the light-planks and turn their faces aside from them, like people leaving a bright doorway.

There are not many. A few hundred, perhaps, spread through the basin in groups of a dozen or twenty, each group tied to a stretch of ridgeline and a set of weirs. They don't gather in a village, because a village could not survive the pulse. They live as a string of small households along the highest dry terraces, connected by the fungus-fiber bridges, and every household has a *hearer*.

## The hearer

Everything they do is set by the surge, and the surge is set by the hearer.

This is a person, usually older, whose whole task is to know when the water will come. It is not a matter of instruments. The hearer sits with the nearest herd of pale wallowers, lying against a warm flank if the beast allows it, and listens. Wallowers feel the chamber fill before anything else does, in their bellies and through their soft undersides, and they begin a low restless murmur an hour or more before the shudder. The hearer knows one animal's murmur from another's. They know that the big old female in the third pool starts early and the young ones start late, and that when the murmurs pitch together into one chord, there is time for the length of a short meal and no more.

The hearer doesn't shout the warning. Shouting is useless in the basin, and the chitterers would answer it and bury it. Instead the hearer taps. A hollow bone-drum hangs at each household, and a pattern of taps runs from house to house along the ridge, faster than a person could walk. The pattern carries the count: *this many breaths remain.* Everyone stops what they are doing and looks at their work and asks whether it can be left. If it can't be left, they save what they can and let the rest go to the water.

They lose things, and they do not mourn things. This is the deepest habit of the people, and everything else follows from it. A weir is made to be rebuilt. A bridge is made to be patched. A hut is made to be sealed in stone some day. They put labor into what they build knowing the basin will take it back, and they do not resent the taking. The loss is part of what they mean by *ready.*

## Making and mending

Their craft is faster than it looks, because it has to be. A weir of reed-fungus can be re-staked in an afternoon. The poles are green-fungal cores wrapped in bone splints, and by the next surge they are sheathed in stone, hard as pottery, and stronger than the day they were cut. The people know this and use it. They set their poles knowing the sinter will armor them, so each weir is stronger in its second season than its first. They call this *letting the water help.*

They make things by leaving them in the rim-pools. A bone left a week comes out fat and white, rounded, sugared. A woven bracelet left three days comes out stiff and glittering. A bowl left rim-up under a drip fills, dries, and fills again, growing a lining of stone, until it is a heavy pale thing that no one alive made, exactly. Their finest possessions are made this way, with a maker who chose the object and the pool and the number of days, and the basin that finished the work. Such things are given to children at the ages when children need to learn what is theirs to keep and what is the water's.

They cook on small fires of dried wallower fat, in low round huts of baked silt. The smoke stains the ceilings black, and the ceilings, over years, become records. A smudge of soot on a certain curve of dome means a certain winter. A hut lived in for three generations is a book of fires. When the terrace rises around it, and it will, they don't fight the stone. They finish the last meal, put out the fire, carry out the bowls and the spears, and leave the hut to the stone with the smoke still fresh on its roof. Some of these huts they seal themselves, leaving a person inside who asked to stay. The ones you see closed and smudged, unopened, are not ruins. They are graves that were chosen and tended right up to the moment the water took over.

## Speech, and the three heartbeats

In a place where every square yard is screaming, speech is hard, so they speak in three registers. Long distance is the drum-taps. Mid-range is a language of clicked tongue and tapped fingertip on the wrist, silent to the air. Close speech is very quiet, a murmur pitched under the wallowers, meant to be felt in the chest as much as heard.

Only once in each surge do they speak aloud at full voice, and that is in the three heartbeats before the water breaks. Every animal falls silent then. The whole basin holds its breath, and for the length of three pulses there is a hush wide enough to fit a human voice. The people have made this moment their own. If a name has to be said, it is said then. If a child is born between surges, the name is given in the next silence. If there is a promise, a quarrel, a grief, a joke too good to keep, it waits for the hush and is spoken into it. Then the floor erupts and the words are swallowed by the roar. The people believe those words are swallowed on purpose, taken into the basin's keeping and not lost. Words spoken in the hush are held. Words spoken in the noise are only noise.

Visitors find the ritual awkward. A dozen figures stand in a ragged line on a ridge, faces tilted, and at the exact instant of the quiet one of them says something short in an ordinary voice. Then the water comes, and nobody remarks on it.

## The dead

Their dead go to the cairns. Bones are stacked in small white towers, each bone chosen and placed, and a cairn is left in a rim-pool until the sinter cements it into a single stone knuckle. They are not markers, exactly, because no one goes back to visit them. Once the stone has taken them they belong to the terrace. But every household knows the number of cairns behind it, and the number is part of how the household says who it is.

The very old, when they decide their count is done, walk down to the lowest, hottest gully alone and do not come back. No one follows. It is understood, and the household's drum taps once, slowly, that night, and that is all.

## In the deep gully

About that gully they say very little, and some of what they say is contradictory, and most of it is said in the hush and lost.

The mats there grow in spirals, and the spirals turn. Everyone who has watched them for an hour has seen it. The people have always known. Some of them say the mat is one creature, and the whole basin is its body and the pulse is its breathing, and they themselves are something like what lives in the fur of a larger animal, tolerated and useful and unimportant. Others say the turning is only the current, and there is nothing to it. Still others say that the thing stirring the pot is not a creature but a habit of the deep water, a rhythm old enough to have come alive.

What they agree on is that the clay bowls set rim-up under the drips are not only for water. Every household carries one down, now and then, filled with something: rendered fat, or a knot of a dead one's hair, or a handful of ash from a sealed fire. They set it at the edge of the deepest spiral. Later it is gone, or it is filled with a hardened white stone in the exact shape of what was put there. Nobody sees this happen, and nobody tries to.

When asked why they do it, they give the same answer, the one that seems to explain everything else about them.

*So it knows we are ready.*

They say it quietly, and the chitterers on the wall do not answer.
