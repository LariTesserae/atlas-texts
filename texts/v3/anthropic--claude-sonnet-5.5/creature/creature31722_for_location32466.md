# What Lives on the Flats

Everything that lives here is either a mason, a mouth, or a witness. Most are all three, depending on the hour.

## The combs

You will not learn to tell one comb from another, and the flats do not need you to. But they are not identical. Each comb has a slight lean, a personal degree of downstream. Each has a pattern of ribs, like a fingerprint, that runs from a dozen fine lines to a few coarse ones. A comb that has just taken up a mouthful of dissolved lime flushes faintly pink at the margin and holds the color for the length of a slow breath. Across a whole bed, you can see the flush pass in long diagonal ripples, each comb lighting a moment after its upstream neighbor. This is the closest thing the beds have to a conversation, and it is only ever about the water: *richer here, thinner now, cooling, clearing.*

A comb lives a few years. When it dies, it does not fall. Its fan closes and stays closed, and its neighbors lay cement around the base and then over the top, gently and without hurry, until the body is a smooth pale boss in the floor, and then not even that. The basin is therefore a burial ground of astonishing depth. The stone under your feet is combs on combs, the ancestors of every animal now standing above them. The floor is crisp and unweathered because it is not a floor. It is a population, mostly dead, holding up the living.

Their young are the size of rice grains, and they ride the water. They are born high on the staircase and drift down over months, settling wherever the current slackens and the stone is warm. Each comb-bed is a collection of strangers from further up, and each will be the ancestors of strangers further down.

## The tenders

Tenders move in the loose, unpanicked way of animals that have never been eaten. They are the size and roughly the shape of loaves, with a domed carapace the color of wet slate and a fringe of short legs that rustle along the stone like a hand brushing a table. Their mouths are underneath, broad and ribbed, and they graze in slow, deliberate arcs, taking the mat off the stone in a band exactly as wide as themselves. Look back along a tender's path and you see a bright stripe against the rust or violet, and then the mat already greening back into it at the edges.

They have habits. Old tenders take the violet mat first and leave the green, and young ones eat everything, indiscriminately, the way children do. They gather at the chipped places, where the combs are healing, because the mat grows fast on fresh cement. They sleep in clusters, stacked two and three deep in the lee of the weir-lips, and the stacks shift all night as the ones on the bottom work their way up.

When a tender dies, the threaders come. There is no ceremony. A brief braid of eels forms around the body, and in an hour the carapace is clean and hollow, and the tenders walking past will use it as a shelter for a while, until the combs take it too.

## The beads, and what they are

The beads on the sluice walls are the least animal-like of the animals. They do not move, they do not flush or tick, and for a long time no one thought they were doing anything. But press your palm to a wall of them and you'll find it faintly damp and faintly warm, and the surface will give under your hand and then slowly return, the way a sleeping animal's flank does. They breathe the vapor. They take moisture out of the steam and the drifting mist and pass it, drop by drop, down through the wall and into the culverts, so that no wall ever runs dry, and no wall ever floods. They are the flats' dampness, kept at exactly the right dampness.

The beads are also the record. Each bead adds a thin ring to itself every day, and a wall of them is a ledger of days as fine as a tree's, though no one has ever cut one open. The oldest walls, near the top of the staircase, are said to be thick as a man's arm, ring on ring of gray.

## The threaders

They pour. There is no better word. A braid of threaders in a culvert is a single, muscular, unhurried current of bodies, each as thick as a wrist and as long as a rowing oar, sliding over and under one another without ever seeming to touch. They travel with the flow and against it, and both at once, so that a pipe mouth looks like a fixed, gray rope of water that has learned to twist.

They are the flats' couriers. What the combs shed and the tenders drop, what the beads sweat out and the roosts let fall, the threaders take up and carry, in their guts and along their skins, to wherever it is short. A threader has no home terrace. It moves, and by moving it makes every terrace a little more like every other, which is another way of saying it keeps the balance level. A threader that stops does not last long.

## The roosts

Of all the animals, the roosts are the ones you will come to feel are looking at you.

They stand along the gantry rails, hundreds to a span, long-necked and wet-feathered, the color of old pewter. They do not preen. They do not shift their feet. Their heads are turned all the same way, along the rail, toward the next terrace, and their eyes are half-lidded and dark and utterly patient. In the language of the place, they are the tally. It is not a name they answer to. It is what they do.

They do not do it by counting, at least not in any way you would recognize. But watch one span of one gantry for a day. The gantry travels its rail, slow as a shadow, carrying its rows of roosts from terrace to terrace. Now and then a single bird will lift, silently, and drop to the water, and take one threader from the braid below it, exactly one, and return to its place. Sometimes, when a bed is thin or a culvert crowded, a roost will simply open its wings and go, low over the water, toward some other terrace, and settle on a rail there, and a gap will appear in the row it left, and the row will close up around the gap until it is not there. The arrivals and departures are never argued. They are never even noticed. But if you were to record every one of them for a thousand years, you would find that the total number of roosts on the flats had never changed by more than one.

They are the only animals here that seem to *know* the number. The others simply live it.

## The wheels and the gantries

Whether the machines live is the kind of question the flats do not ask. But the sluice-wheels are inhabited. The great pipes that lift the water back to the head of the staircase are warm, dark, and packed with a fine pale growth that no one has ever properly seen, and the young of nearly everything pass through them, borne up in the black flow, tumbled and fed and sorted, and released at the top like something being born. The wheels are the flats' mothers, if the flats have any. Their pulse is a little slower than a resting heart because it is the pulse of the whole.

And the gantries carry the roosts, and the roosts carry the count, and the count decides, in some way none of the rest of the place can see, who is born and who is lifted and who is left to close and be built over. A visitor might suppose the machines are in charge. It would be closer to say that the machines are the place's slowest animal, and the most obedient.

## The rest

There is one more inhabitant, and it is the one you cannot point at: the balance itself. It is not a creature. But it behaves like one. It notices wounds and crowds toward them. It corrects overreach. It is warm and patient and entirely without malice. Every animal on the flats is a limb of it, and each is content, in whatever way a comb or a tender can be content, to be exactly the size and number that it is.

If you stay, you will be counted. You will feel it first as a slight, gentle attention: a roost's head turned a degree toward you, a tender pausing at your ankle and then choosing, deliberately, to go around. A thin violet mat will begin on your boot, and a comb will lean an inch toward your heel. It isn't hostile. It is only the flats making room for another entry, and writing it down.
