# Those Who Live in the Settling

They call themselves the **Kept**, or in their own soft, clipped speech, *the ones the water has kept*. The name carries no pride. It is closer to the way you might say a tool is well kept: tended, not yet used up.

## Bodies

They are a shouldered-in people, built by their lanes. Generations of turning sideways to pass one another have given them a habitual quarter-turn of the torso, so that even in an open place they walk slightly angled, one shoulder leading. They are not tall. Tallness is inconvenient, and the tunnel-lanes have bruised it out of families over centuries of low lintels. Most carry a small permanent stoop and a flat callus on the crown of the head or the back of the neck, which children are teased about and the old wear as a badge.

Their skin tends pale, and the sun on the floor is brief, but in nearly everyone it is also powdered. Lime gets into the creases of the knuckles, the cuticles, the corners of the eyes, the parting of the hair. A Kept person is never entirely clean of it, and does not try to be. The hands especially are distinctive: cracked across the joints, nails ridged and thick, fingertips smoothed nearly fingerprintless in the scrapers and frame-weavers. Old scrapers have hands like weathered shell.

Their lungs are their weakness. The **white cough** is the trade disease of the scrapers, a dry, whispering rasp that settles in by middle age. Scrapers tie damp cloth across nose and mouth, and the sight of a street of masked figures kneeling at their doorways on scraping-day, all in the same soft gray, looks like some ritual of mutes. The damp also gets into their joints. Elders move like carefully lowered furniture, and every household knows the smell of the warm fat-and-juniper rub that gets an old knee moving in the morning.

Their eyes are used to dimness and fog. They squint at open sky and are uneasy under it. Many say a wide horizon makes their stomachs drop, and children brought up to the rim for the first time will sometimes crouch and refuse to look out.

## Dress and Adornment

Everything is wool, oiled hide, and reed, because everything is damp. The Kept wear layered, close-woven garments in undyed grays and browns, and a long hooded cape of overlapping reed or oiled felt that sheds the drip from the covered lanes. Sandals are soled with hide and hobnailed with copper against the slick travertine, so that the whole city has a faint tick-tick under its footsteps.

Color is scarce and therefore meaningful. Dyes are traded down from the rim country, and a thread of red, madder or ochre, marks a person with a duty. Red cord is the water-readers' color, tied at cracks and worn at the wrist. Others wear a single stripe of it at the hem to show they have lost someone that year.

The one universal ornament is **a lime bead**: a small pebble or seed, left in a seep as a child until the water has coated it, then strung on a cord and worn. The thickness of the coating shows how long the child was left, and so how patient the family was. A large, smooth bead is respectable. Adults do not remove theirs, and are usually buried in them, so the bead goes back into the flowstone it came from.

## Households and Trades

The unit of the city is the **house-line**, an extended family bound to a particular dwelling and its stretch of lane. A house is not owned so much as tended, and the tending is the inheritance. A person speaks of "my wall" and "my seep" and means a duty as much as a possession. Marriages are arranged partly for the water rights they join and partly for the trades they combine, so that a scraper's daughter marrying a frame-weaver's son is considered a sensible match.

The crafts are hereditary and named, and they hold a kind of gravity:

**The Scrapers** keep the doors open. They are the most numerous and least honored, and also the most necessary. They know by feel how hard a crust is and how much water the wall behind it has been taking. They speak of lime as an animal: "she's fast this year," "she's sulking on the north side." A good scraper never cuts to bare stone, but leaves a thin skin to protect the core and to give the next year's growth somewhere to begin.

**The Frame-Weavers** set the willow and reed lattices in the seeps and read the way water threads through them. Their work is a slow gamble, since a frame set wrong can seal a neighbor's cistern or starve a whole street's wall. They keep long memories and longer grudges, and disputes between weaver-lines can outlast the frames themselves.

**The Water-Readers** walk the rim at dawn, alone, barefoot on the fractured stone, wrists banded with red. They judge which seeps are rising and which failing, lay cord on new cracks, and taste the runoff. They can tell, they say, the age of a thread of water by how much it has learned. People defer to them, a little fearfully. A water-reader who walks through a lane without speaking is understood to be carrying bad news, and doors close quietly behind them.

Beyond these are **kiln-tenders**, **loom-wives** (the term includes men), **sling-runners** who carry loads down the chutes, **cistern-wardens** who keep the salamanders and guard the water's sweetness, and the **bone-workers**, whose scrapers, awls, and picks are made from goat, dove, and the rare deer traded in from the highlands. A person's craft is often the first thing they say about themselves, before their name.

## Temperament and Manners

The Kept are quiet, and the quiet is a courtesy. In a city where a shutter is the only wall between families, a raised voice is an intrusion, and children are trained early to modulate. They speak low and close, hand cupped near the mouth, and have a whole vocabulary of small gestures for the times when a lane is too crowded or too dim for speech: two fingers tapped on the forearm for *pass on my left*, a palm flat against a wall for *the crust is wet here*, the thumb drawn along the jaw for *I'm sorry for your trouble*.

Greeting is a touch to the wall, not to each other. Two Kept meeting in a lane will each lay a hand briefly on the nearest stone, then nod. It acknowledges the house that shelters them both, and only then the person. Visitors from the rim country, who shake hands or clap shoulders, are considered friendly but a little coarse.

They are patient, and their patience can look like fatalism to outsiders. They are used to working in increments. You do not fight lime; you outlast it. You do not rush a wall; you feed it. Conversation is unhurried and full of pauses, and a question may be answered the next day. Yet their patience has an edge. They can be fierce over water, and a theft from a cistern or a diverted trickle is a serious matter, settled first by the street's elders and, if that fails, by the water-readers' judgment, which is not appealed.

They have a dry, muffled humor. Much of it is about the lime: the man whose door closed with him inside, the bride whose dowry wall grew a second dowry wall. They laugh into their sleeves. A great many jokes end in some version of *"and by spring you couldn't tell."*

## Belief

They do not exactly worship. They **attend**. The water is what gives and takes, and the chief religious act is noticing: touching the wall each morning to feel whether it is sweating more or less than yesterday, listening at night for the stone.

For the stone talks. The low, irregular ticking of the basin as it settles is called **the Counting**. The Kept say the ground is counting itself down to rest, and that while it counts, the city is safe. Children fall asleep to it. Old people lie awake through the night and feel reassured by each tick. Their deepest dread is not the Groan returning, though the story of the Sinking is told every autumn, but **the Hush**: a night when the ticking stops entirely. They believe, or half believe, that a long silence in the stone means the ground is gathering itself. The tale of the Hush is told to children as a bedtime story, but adults do not say the word after dark.

Of the Sinking itself they speak with a curious calm. It is not seen as catastrophe but as a *birth*: the old country giving way so the water could be released. The Kept regard themselves as the descendants of those who came to a wound and stayed to watch it heal, and their whole culture is a slow tending of that healing. They have no great fear of the world ending, having already heard how it once did.

The dead are given to the western seeps, and no one there is a stranger. A child who touches the glazed shapes learns the names of those beneath the crust, and families visit on a fixed day each season to speak to a particular curve of flowstone. The crust never quite hides what is under it, and the dead are somewhat visible for decades, a shoulder, a folded hand, a hint of a face. The Kept find this consoling. In time the shapes soften and become part of the cliff, and it is said of an old one that she "has gone to stone."

## Food and Household Life

Their diet is tuber, mushroom, and hard goat cheese, with fermented roots and barley gruel and, on festival days, roasted dove or a handful of dried figs. Salt is a trade good and precious. A woodfire in a hearth is a luxury, and most cooking is done over fat-lamps or in shared kilns, so that a meal in the Settling is almost always tepid, a little damp, and eaten among neighbors who share the same warmth.

Mushroom cellars are the pride of every house-line. There are as many cellar strains as there are families, and each is carried in a clay pot from generation to generation, fed with scraps and lime water. A bride brings a pot of her mother's cellar to her new household. Pale salamanders share the cisterns, and the cistern-wardens speak to them with a tenderness that other people rarely get, calling them by names and remarking on their moods.

## The Young and the Old

Children grow up clambering ladders and wet chutes and are astonishingly sure-footed. They play a game called **Closing**, in which one child hides in a niche while the others "grow" the wall around them with cupped hands and whispered lime-sounds until the hidden child must find the gap and slip out. Nobody quite remembers how it began, but every child knows it.

Elders are honored for their memory of specific walls. An old woman who can tell you what your grandmother's door looked like before it narrowed has real standing. When a house-line dies out or leaves, its dwelling is not torn down. It is sealed, left to the water, and over decades becomes another cliff-face. The Kept walk past these closed houses with their hands near their mouths, as one passes a sleeper.

## What They Know of Themselves

If you asked a Kept person what they were, they would probably not answer in terms of nation or ancestry. They might lay a palm on the nearest wall and say something like, *"We are what the water hasn't finished with."*

They know they live in a place that is not done, and they have made their peace with it. Everything they love, house, door, stair, grave, is already becoming something else. They keep it anyway, scrape by scrape, spring by spring, opening a little of each year's closing so that the light and the lane and the people can still get through. Then they go inside, shut the narrowed door, and listen to the stone counting in the dark.
