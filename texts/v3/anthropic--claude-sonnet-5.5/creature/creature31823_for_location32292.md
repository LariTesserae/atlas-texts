# Those Who Keep the Hollow

Nobody lives in the Boiler Hollow the way a person lives in a house. The residents live in it the way a gear lives in a clock, and most of them would not know what to do with a day off.

## The Many

The frogs number in the hundreds, and each holds a station it has never thought of leaving. They are slate-colored and thumb-sized, with throats that flutter faster than the eye can follow. A young frog does not wander to find a place. A place falls vacant, a frog is hatched into the gap, and by the second season its ratchet-call has settled into the beat of the frog beside it. Ask which frog is oldest and the answer is that the oldest frog is always the one on the third tussock from the north lip. It has been that frog for a thousand years, though never the same animal.

The snails file along the weeping rock in their combed lines. The copper mayflies hold their ranks in the warm air. The bronze beetles pack amber into cracks. Each of these has a task, but none has a purpose in the sense that could be explained to it. The snail grazes, the beetle seals, and the hollow stays warm.

They are the crew. They are not the keeper.

## The One Below

Under the raft of mat, in the deepest of the tea-black pools, lives something that has no name because nothing has ever needed to call it.

It is a salamander, of a sort. It is long as a rowing boat and pale as the inside of a shell, with a flat blunt head and a mouth that curves upward as if it had been told something private. It has no eyes, only two smooth dimples where eyes would be. Around its neck stand three pairs of gill plumes, red as raw silk, branching and re-branching, and they wave slowly in the current with a motion that has nothing to do with breathing. Along each flank runs a ridge of electric organ, beaded like a row of small pearls. When it is still, the ridges glow faintly blue.

Its skin is the temperature of the water. Its heart beats once every nine seconds.

Nobody has counted this, since nobody has been there, but it is so. The stacks have their own separate cycles, and it would be wrong to say the salamander sets them. But the salamander's beat is the note beneath the chord, the interval the whole hollow drifts around and returns to. The stacks fire when they are ready, and their readiness is a little bit the salamander's.

## What It Does

It does not seem to do much. It lies along the bottom of the pool with its tail curled around the drowned root of a stack, and it waits.

Gas rises through the mat from rot that never ends, and gas is not polite. It gathers in blisters, and the blisters merge and swell until a whole patch of floor lifts like a blanket over a sleeper's knee. If it were left, the pocket would go up all at once, and the hollow would lose a stack, and the chord would lose a voice.

So the salamander goes to it. It rises under the swelling, presses the flat of its head against the underside of the mat, and *leans*. The pocket breaks into a hundred small bubbles. The bubbles seep sideways, find the roots of the stacks, and climb into the hollow stems where they are wanted. Above, on the surface, the mat sinks back and no visitor would ever notice. If you happened to be standing there you might feel a slow soft swell under your boots, a breath in the ground, and then it would be over.

It is not doing this out of duty. It is doing what a body does when it feels a pressure that shouldn't be there. That it does it about four hundred times a year, and has for as long as there has been a mat, is simply the same fact seen from further away.

## What It Knows

It hears nothing. There is no ear in that smooth blunt skull. But sound in the hollow is mostly pressure, and pressure it feels the way a hand feels a table. The horns arrive through the water as slow shoves against its flanks, nine seconds apart, thirty voices deep. The frogs come as a fine grain, a texture like sandpaper drawn across the skin. The clicking of the understory is a rain of pinpricks. To the salamander this is not noise. It is the weather of its whole life, and it knows it the way you know the light in a room you grew up in.

Its electric ridges do the rest. It reads the fish that hum and the sparks that leap to the roots, and from the way the current bends it has a picture of the place: the shape of the pool, the position of every stack, the pale threads of fungus that run through the mat like nerves. Perhaps they are nerves. There is a great deal of fungus and only one salamander, and it is hard to say where one ends. The threads are always warm where they touch its skin, and the pulses that pass along them sometimes arrive before the salamander has made them.

It does not wonder about this.

## Its Year

It has no seasons because the hollow has none. It has instead a slow round of tasks. It rises to press on the gas. It drifts to a stack whose roots are silting and cleans them with a sweep of its tail. It eats the small blind things that live in the mud, at long intervals, without hurry. Sometimes, for no reason anyone could name, it goes very still and the blue in its flanks brightens, and every hum-fish in the pool turns and hangs facing it, and the current runs strong through the roots. The stacks all sing at once, one huge shuddering blare that rattles the canopy, and the frogs go silent for a single second.

That is the only irregularity the hollow has, and it happens perhaps once a decade. Then the chord resumes as though nothing had occurred, because in the hollow nothing has.

## Succession

There is one salamander, and there has always been one. When this one ends, there will be another in its place, as there is another frog on the third tussock and another beetle at the smooth amber crack. Nobody has seen how. A small pale thing, no longer than a finger, is simply found one day in the warm mud at the foot of the root, gills already red, ridges already faintly lit. It has been given its station and it takes it.

The old one, if there was an old one, goes down into the mat, and the mat takes it. Nothing is wasted. The gas the next season is a little sweeter.

## If You Stopped

If you stood at the rim for an hour, you would meet the frogs, the mayflies, the beetles, and the long file of snails. You might see the stacks blare one by one and think you understood the place. You would not see the one who keeps it. You would feel only that the ground under you was gently, patiently alive, rising and falling once in nine seconds like a sleeper's chest.

Then you would go on down the marsh road, hearing the faint steam-note behind you, and not turn around.

Below the moss, in the warm dark, something pale lies with its head against the underside of the world. It leans, and the hollow keeps running.
