# Those Who Live in the Stack

Start with the hands, because everyone in Ouraveil has good ones. They are broad and warm and permanently a little stained: indigo in the creases, lye-cracked at the knuckle, or green-black under the nails from loam. Most people up here could tell you what district they were born in by smell alone, and they don't treat this as a talent. It is simply what a person is, the way a person has a name.

## The Stackfolk

They are a compact, deep-chested people, and the chests are not an accident. Five thousand meters is a hard altitude, and a few generations of it have made them what they are: barrel-ribbed, slow-walking on the flat and quick on stairs, with a habit of breathing in through the nose and out in a long unhurried sigh. Visitors from the cloud-deck towns arrive at the upper terraces gray-lipped and dizzy and are handed sweet tea and left alone, since there is nothing to do about it but wait. The Stackfolk find this touching. They call the newly arrived *still-lunged*.

Their skin runs warm. It is not fever, only a lifetime spent leaning on heated walls. They sleep against conduits the way other people sleep beside hearths, and a bed is properly measured by which pipe it backs onto: *a two-pipe bed*, *a good clay-side*. A child's first real gift is often a place on a warm wall, with the whole family shifting over to make room.

They dress for water rather than cold. Waxed cotton, oiled paper hoods, felted wool worn wet and steaming, and wooden-soled sandals against the perpetual glaze on stone. Above the cloud line the colors are pale, undyed linen and sun-bleached blue. Below it people wear whatever the dye works upstream have let down: rust, mustard, a bruised violet that is the signature of the lower districts. You can read a person's altitude in their sleeves.

## The Guilds and What They Carry

Nobody here is only a person. Everyone is also a tool they carry.

**The Cup-bearers** wear small brass tasting-cups on their belts and are the nearest thing to law the Stack acknowledges. They are quiet, dry-humored people who wander the water-chain dipping and sipping, tasting for tannin, tallow, and the flat mineral note of a foundry that has cheated. They can tell a tannery's discharge from a dyer's by a single mouthful, and they are almost never wrong. When they are, they say so publicly and buy a round. It is considered the mark of a serious Cup-bearer to have once tasted something terrible and lived. A pale scar at the corner of the mouth is worn with pride.

**The Repointers** work the walls. There are thousands of them, each assigned a stretch and expected to keep it for life, and often their children keep it after them. They hang from ropes with trowels and small buckets of lime, working joint by joint, and they know their stretch of wall the way a shepherd knows a flock. They will tell you that one stone sweats in spring, that another is patient, that a certain crack has been "thinking about it" for forty years. They are the most contented people in the city, and the reason is that the work never ends and they know it. They will die with a wall half-mended and think nothing of it.

**The Combers** tend the catch-works at the ridge, in damp wind and pearly light, sweeping the fine mesh screens with long soft brushes so the fog runs clean into the gutters. They are lean and silent and sing constantly under their breath, a habit born of the fact that on a bad day they cannot see the person on the next frame. The songs are wordless, low, and overlapping. Sometimes they lose the tune and pick it up again as though it had been passed to them.

**The Mixers** work the loam sheds at the mid-levels and are the most cheerfully filthy people alive. Tannery sludge, spent malt, fish silt, and slag pass through their forks and boots, and they have a large and specific vocabulary for the state of a heap: *sweet*, *sulking*, *hot in the belly*, *ready to be buried in*. Their sheds steam. Their children are considered lucky, and mothers from the upper levels send babies down to be held by them for a morning, and are not embarrassed to.

**The Bellows-keepers** are fewer, older, and more solemn. They attend the great vents on the ridge and the Slow Vents especially, and they are the ones who, when asked, say *it's the boilers* and lay a palm flat on the stone. They keep the eleven-minute count without watches. Some of them nod, barely, at the exhale, as one nods to a neighbor across a street. If you ask them what the mountain is doing, they won't answer. If you sit with them through three or four breaths, they'll pass you a cup of something and say nothing more, and that seems to be the answer.

## The Others

The Stackfolk do not think of themselves as the only residents, and any account that says otherwise would offend them.

The **macaques** hold the middle levels by long treaty. They comb the fruit trees for grubs and drive off the rats, and in return they are permitted to steal a certain amount, which everyone agrees is fair. Individual troops are known by name: the Cistern Gang, the Widow Ladder family. A macaque who takes too much is scolded by a grandmother and a macaque who is scolded too often gets a reputation. They ride the trellises like small tired dignitaries.

The **bee families** are a category of their own. The stingless hives in the wall-niches are older than the terraces around them, and the families who keep them are less owners than in-laws. A hive is spoken to at births and deaths. When a keeper dies, someone must go and tell the bees, and if this is neglected the hive is said to sulk and go sour. The honey is dark, sharp, and faintly resinous, sold in tiny ceramic thimbles, and it tastes of whatever is flowering on the terrace directly above.

**Swifts** are the mountain's real population, in the tens of thousands, and they scream around the towers at dusk in loops that seem to have rules. Children keep count of them and lose the count and start again. The old belief is that a swift never lands, and the old joke is that this is why the Stack never quite finishes: something must always be in the air.

And at dusk the **frogs**, who own the cisterns. For that one hour no one bothers to speak over them. The whole Stack, foundry-hands and Cup-bearers and Repointers on their ropes, goes quiet in the way you go quiet beside a large animal. Then the lamps are lit and people begin, bit by bit, to talk again.

## How They Live With Each Other

Nothing is owned outright, and everything is spoken for. That is the closest thing to a creed. A person does not have a house so much as a *place in the chain*: a room that receives water from above and gives it on below, a workshop that takes in someone's scrap and sends out someone else's raw material. Manners are built on this. It is rude to hoard, ruder to waste, and rudest of all to cut off the person beneath you. The worst curse in the Stack is *may your water go back dirty*.

Being high is not prestige. It is closer to a kind of loneliness. The highest terraces are the most brilliant and the least populated, kept by Combers and goatherds and a few stubborn hermit-gardeners, and the lower districts, warm and drizzled and built from everyone's leftovers, are where the laughter is. People say the low districts are where the mountain is *kind*. A well-off family might keep a room above the cloud line for a month each summer for the light, and go home to the damp gladly.

The dead go down too. Nothing is discarded, only lowered. There is no gloom in it: a body is washed, wrapped in linen, and carried in slow procession down through the districts, each of which stops work as it passes, and delivered at last to the Mixers' sheds. The loam that results is fed to the terraces, and someone plants a tree in it. Children ask which tree is their grandmother. The grown-ups say the bananas, probably, she was always hungry, and everybody laughs, and it is all right.

## What the Mountain Is to Them

They do not say they live *on* the mountain, nor *in* it. There is a word, *keeping*, that comes closest: they keep the mountain, as one keeps a promise, or a fire, or a household through a hard winter. They keep it warm and watered and half-mended, faithful to a rock that none of them has seen in living memory. Nobody sees the city change, and yet they are changing it every day, one plank and one joint at a time, with a patience so complete it has stopped feeling like patience and turned into simply being alive.

At the eleventh minute the great stone mouths exhale, and a low breath moves through the walls and the ferns and the sleeping conduits, and across ten thousand terraces people pause, a hand on a trowel or a ladle or a child's hair, and let it pass through them.

Then they go on.
