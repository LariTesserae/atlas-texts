# Who Lives in the Bowl

## The Quiet People

Fewer than three hundred people live in the Flywheel Terraces. They have no name for themselves in any spoken language, since they seldom speak. Among themselves they use a sign made by drawing one finger in a slow circle on the back of the other hand. Visitors who learn it usually translate it as *the ones who are here*, which is close enough. Nobody who has lived in the bowl has ever objected to the translation.

They are small and broad through the chest, with narrow hips and long, scarred hands. Their skin has taken on the color of the place, a warm brown with something of rust in it, especially on the forearms, where they lean against iron all day. Their eyes are unusually steady. They have learned to read faces closely, since a face is where most of their talking happens. They dress in goat felt and in a woven fiber of tree-fern bast, dyed with rust water into stripes of orange, violet, and ochre that match the streaks on the plates. From a distance a person standing against a wall is hard to tell from the wall.

The most noticeable thing about them is the plugs. Every person carries them, a pair of tight wads of beeswax, moss felt, and goat-hair on a cord around the neck. They wear them whenever they leave a quiet zone and take them out on entering one. The gesture is so practiced that it looks like a form of bowing: hands rise to the ears, the plugs come out, the shoulders drop. Watching a family cross the terraces, you see it repeated many times, a small ritual of arriving and leaving that never quite stops.

## Houses of Quiet

They do not build houses, or they build only the least part of one. A household is a node.

The forty silences are the settlements. Each holds one family, or two that are close, or one old person who prefers to be alone. Inside a quiet zone a few meters across, people have arranged what they need: a sleeping shelf of stacked felt, a hearth of warm iron where a steam-pipe runs near the surface, a shelf of clay pots, a rack for drying fiddleheads. Everything is small and low and tucked against the curve of the plates, because the quiet is lens-shaped and cannot be enlarged. The people have arranged their lives to fit inside a shape made by sound.

When the wind shifts and a node drifts a meter, the household moves with it. They do this without complaint, sliding the sleeping shelf, shifting the pots, carrying the hearth-stones over a few handspans. The sign for this is *the wind is moving the house*. Small children treat it as a game. Old people treat it as weather and say that the quiet is a guest that sometimes changes its chair.

The etiquette from the visitors' guides is not simply courtesy here. A quiet is the only place a person can hear and be heard, so it is the only place a person is fully alive to others. To invite someone into your quiet is to give them speech. To linger in someone else's is to take theirs. Children learn this before they learn to walk between nodes. The worst thing that can be said of a person is that they *overstay*.

## Speaking

In the roar they talk with their hands and faces, with the flat of a palm on a shoulder, and by touch on iron. Two people can tap a rhythm on a plate and be understood through the metal, since the wheel's vibration carries a tapped message a surprisingly long way if you rest a hand on the rail. They also use small shuttered lamps at night and in the mist. The lamps flash a pale blue, and the pattern is close to the pattern the frogs use. No one can say whether the people learned it from the frogs or the frogs from the people. The people find the question mildly funny. Their answer is that it is a good way to say things, so of course it has been found more than once.

Spoken language is used in the nodes, and it has changed because of that. Speech there is unhurried and composed. Because a person cannot revise a sentence once they step out into the roar, they say what they mean to say before leaving, and then they stop. Conversations are made of long pauses and finished thoughts. A person will sit down in a quiet, take out the plugs, breathe for a while, and only then speak, in a low voice, sentences with their edges neatly closed. Visitors often find this unnerving at first. Then they find it restful.

A private talk has its own custom. If two people need to speak without being overheard, which in a bowl of forty small silences means without a neighbor's node being close enough to hear, the host stands outside the quiet, in the noise, with the plugs in and their back turned. They stand there as long as it takes. It is the most intimate courtesy the people have. Being kept a private quiet costs the keeper an hour of roaring, and it is understood that this is what love is made of.

## Names

A person's name is a small spiral traced on the palm, a different spiral for each, varying in tightness, direction, and the flick at the end. It is not spoken and cannot be. A person who wants to call someone across the terraces goes to them and traces the name on their hand. People who have lived beside each other for decades have never heard each other's names aloud, and they do not feel this as a lack.

## Work

They say they do not tend the place, and they are mostly telling the truth.

They keep the stair treads clear of moss where it might make a person slip. They clear the gutters in the channels of dead leaves so that the rusty water keeps running toward the sump. They cut the vines back from the walkways to the width of a shoulder, no more. They do not touch the machine, and they do not prune the ferns, and they do not weed the terraces. If asked why, they lay a hand flat on the nearest plate and shrug. The gesture means *it does not need us, and we do not need to be needed.*

Their food comes from what the bowl gives without being asked. The starchy pith of the tree ferns is pounded into a gray dough and baked on the warm iron. Fiddleheads are boiled or dried. The dark goats give a thin, strong milk, which is soured into a cheese that keeps for a year. Swift eggs are taken in small numbers, never from the same crevice twice in a season, and always with a word of apology, in the nearest node. They do not eat the frogs. They drink rainwater collected in clay cisterns under the steam-fall in the late afternoon, warm and faintly mineral. The rust-water in the gutters is left to the frogs.

The goats seem to accept the people as a kind of weather. When someone's shadow falls across a browsing animal it looks up, and the person lays a palm on its neck and offers salt scraped from the pipe seams. The goats are not exactly tamed. Every one of them has, at some point, walked off up the outer slope and not come back. The people say that a goat that stays has decided to, and that this is the only kind of ownership worth having.

## Children

Children are born in the quietest node, which is the largest and oldest, near the north terraces. It has room for a dozen adults and is kept by whoever's turn it is. A child is not expected to cry aloud, and few do. They learn early, from their mothers' shoulders, that a scream is a thing you make inside a quiet and not outside.

The main childhood game is crossing. A child stands at the edge of one node with plugs in, and the object is to reach the next without stepping in the roar for longer than a single held breath. It is played across the whole bowl, from silence to silence, hopping and gliding on the worn iron. The bats do the same at dusk, and the children watch them and copy their lines. Older children, who have learned the geometry, can cross the whole bowl in a long zigzag without once leaving the quiet for more than three steps. The elders watch them with an expression the visitors cannot read, which may be pride or may be an old fear that someone will stay too long in the noise.

## What They Believe About the Wheel

They do not worship it. They do not pray to it, and none of them has ever set foot in the pit, which is not forbidden but is *not done*, in the way one does not stand on a grave.

If you sit in a node for long enough and ask what the wheel is, you will hear several answers. One old man says it is a clock that no one wound and no one can stop. A young woman says it is a heart, and that the mountain is what it beats in. A third says, without smiling, that it is a weight held up for a very long time, and that the point is not to find out what happens when it is let go. A fourth says it is only a wheel, the mountain is only a mountain, and people are only people, and that all three are enough.

They agree on one thing. Nobody remembers who built it, and no one is bothered. The asking is not treated as urgent. To the people the wheel is like the sky, something that was there before the first person looked up and that will still be there when the last one lies down.

## The Spirals

The brass plates around the pit rim belong to the people, though they will not say so directly.

Each plate is cut by hand, with a nail and a small mallet, in a single afternoon in silence. It is a person's name, the same spiral that is traced on their palm. There is disagreement about when it is cut. The northern families say it is cut at birth, so that the child begins life already written into the iron. The southern families say it is cut at death, so that the iron can keep what the body cannot. Neither is thought to be wrong. Some households do both, one plate in each direction, and lay the two side by side.

The moss grows thickest on the oldest spirals, and the people take this to be the mountain's answer. They do not scrape it away. A spiral that has vanished under moss is a person the place has finished receiving. The oldest ones are shapeless green mounds in the brass, and a hand laid on them feels only a slight softness where a groove used to be.

At the end of a plate's first day, someone lays a fresh fern frond across it. Nobody tells them to. Whoever feels the need does it. The frond is always from the ferns nearest the pit and always picked that morning. It is left to wilt on the brass and be carried off by the steam.

## One of Them

In a small node on the eastern terraces, above the pit's lip and just out of sight of the plates, an old woman sits for most of the morning with her plugs in her lap. She has cut a great many spirals. Her hands are knotted and slow, and the tip of her left forefinger is worn flat from tracing names. When the brass in the ring was still bright she had already been up, before the light, to the pit, and back down.

If you come into her quiet, she will look at you for a while before she speaks. When she does, her voice will be low and dry and will sound like it has been kept somewhere cool. She will not tell you whose plate she cut, or whether the frond was hers. She will ask you, gently, whether you have found your own quiet yet, and whether it is big enough. When you have answered, she will put her plugs back in and turn her face to the wheel, and you will understand that you have been given as long as she can spare, and that it is over.
