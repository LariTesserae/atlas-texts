# Those Who Keep the Level

## First sight

You take them for fewer than they are. On an ordinary day you see four or five, and by evening you would swear the town held a dozen. It holds more, perhaps a hundred and some, though the number is not one anyone offers. They are simply elsewhere, in the low doorways and lean-to cells and in the dim of the hall, spaced across the hollow so evenly that the empty ground around each house seems to belong to whoever sleeps in it. They do not crowd, and they do not avoid one another either. They keep a distance the way people keep a comfortable pace, without thinking about it.

They are not tall, and they are broad through the chest. The ribs flare wide under the wool, and the breathing is deep and slow, taken in one long draw and let go in another. You notice it most when one of them climbs a stair. They do not labor. They take the flight at a steady, unhurried gait, pausing on the deep treads at the same point every time, and the dishes worn into the stone are the record of that gait. Their lips and the beds of their nails are faintly dusky. Their cheeks are weathered to the color of the stone. The eyes are dark and heavy-lidded, narrowed against the hard light, and look as though they have spent generations looking at distances they will not cross.

They wear undyed wool in grey-browns that match the walls so well that a person standing still in a doorway can vanish from you in a blink. Both men and women wear their hair long and braided with the same grass rope they use for everything else. Around the neck, on a cord, most carry a small bronze cup no larger than a thumb-joint. It is not an ornament. It is the measure.

## The measure

Every one of them belongs to a channel.

Nobody was assigned this, and no one could say when it began. A child learns the tempos of the water before learning most words: the clock-tick channel, the low continuous trickle, the single round note that falls every few breaths into the hollow-bottomed cistern on the west side. Somewhere in the first years a child attaches to one of them, sits beside it in the cold hours of morning, and listens. This is what the child on the wall is doing, staring past you. The child is not idle. The child is keeping count of a drip.

The grown keepers walk their channels each dawn, running a thumb along the grooves of the weeping stones, clearing grit from the gutters with a copper awl, checking the moss band against its edge. They lift the cistern roofs and look at the dark water and the wet-dark line on the stone. Then they take what is theirs. They dip the bronze cup once, twice, or however many times their household's turn allows, and no more. The water at the mark is not a gift the mountain gives them. It is an agreement they hold up on their side. They take only what has fallen since they last took, and so the level does not move, and the line on the stone has not moved in any memory, theirs or their grandparents'.

They do not think of this as thrift or as sacrifice. It is what water is. If you asked one of them why the cisterns never empty, the answer would be a slow, faintly puzzled look, then a shrug of one shoulder, and a gesture at the mark on the stone as if the question had answered itself.

## Speech

They speak little and low, and they seem to speak on the outbreath, so that a sentence is a single exhalation and then a pause while the chest refills. The language is soft-consonanted, full of long vowels, the same vowel-shapes the wind finds across the mouths of the storage jars. It is possible, standing on the terrace, to believe the jars and the people are practicing the same language and neither is the original.

They do not shout. Shouting would waste breath and would carry too far in the thin air anyway. When they need to call across a court they clap two flat pieces of slate together, once or twice, and the sound is dry and small, and it reaches. When the clap comes you will see a head lift two courts away and turn, and no more than that happen, and the day resume.

They will greet you with the nod and nothing else. There is no suspicion in it. There is no invitation either. You are a thing that has come through the saddle, like cloud, and like cloud you will go, and there is nothing to be done about you but let you pass at your own distance. If you sit beside a cistern, someone will take the water from the next one over. If you stay a night, a jar of barley porridge and a bronze cup of cold water will be left at the threshold of whichever cell you have chosen. You will not see who leaves it.

## Hands

Their hands are the only part of them that is quick. It comes as a small shock: a woman at a doorway, otherwise as slow as the weather, feeds a coil of clay onto a growing pot with fingers that move like a bird's. A man beating a copper blank with a horn hammer strikes so rapidly and so evenly that the sound is a single roll. They pare the dead wood from the junipers with a small curved blade in strokes so fine you would not know the tree had been touched.

The junipers belong to no one and to everyone. Six or seven, waist-high, thigh-thick, flat-bent. Whoever passes and finds a dead twig takes it. The wood is stacked in the lee of the walls in bundles bound with grass rope, and the bundles are burned only in the hall's rare cold, or at deaths. That is the smoke you smell, faint and sweet, drifting from no chimney you can find.

## The hall

Everyone goes there, though not together, and not on a schedule you could learn. A person will stop what they are doing, set the tool on its peg, and walk up to the western terrace at the same unhurried gait as always. They stoop through the low door and sit on the bench, with their back to the wall and their eyes on the empty clay floor. They sit for an hour, or a morning. Then they stand and go back to what they left.

The bench runs round the perimeter, and the sitters do not sit close. They leave gaps between them, wide gaps, equal, so that when the hall holds a dozen it holds a dozen evenly spaced points on a ring, each looking inward at a center where nothing is. It is the same spacing as the town. The town is the hall turned inside out, or the hall is the town gathered and held still. The people do not say which.

Ask what the sitting is for and you will hear "sitting," and perhaps a small movement of the hand that means *what else would it be for.* But there are things you can see. Each sitter has a place, and the places are not marked, and no one hesitates in choosing. When an old person stops coming, within a season, sometimes within days, a younger one, not always a child of the same house, begins to sit in that spot. No one arranges it. That is why the polish on the bench is one long sheen, like old bone, without a gap or a shallow.

## The count

Here the place is quietest. The number of people holds. When a child is born, someone in the far end of the town has already been sitting a little longer than usual. Or someone old has been eating a little less, working a little less, sitting with their eyes closed longer on the bench. Nobody names this. Nobody grieves out loud, or celebrates out loud. There are no words for surplus or for lack, and if you asked what happens when there are too many, they would look at you as they would if you asked what happens when the cistern overflows: not with fear but with faint confusion, because it does not.

It is not a rule anyone made. It is the mark on the stone, applied to themselves. They take no more of the place than has fallen to them, and the hollow, in return, holds them at the level.

## What they are

They are not a dying people, and they are not a waiting one. They are not wise in any way you could use, and they carry no secret to tell you. What they are is a cistern of a different kind: a still, dark, cold thing kept at one line by a patient balance of what comes in and what is taken, roofed against dust, smelling faintly of slate and juniper smoke. They are the level of the place.

When you leave and climb the eastern stair, one of them will be at a doorway and will nod, and it will cost them nothing. Behind you the slate will clap once, small and dry, for no one, or for you. Then the drip returns, and the jars take up their note.
