# Those Who Live in the Sillworks

## The Builders

They are smaller than rim-people would expect, and paler, with a doughy fineness to the skin from a life without weather. Their eyes are wide and dark to the edge. In the green fungus-light they seem to be all pupil, and they look past you at the lamp behind your shoulder rather than at your face, since a face is hard to read here anyway. Their hands are the largest thing about them. The palms are broad and scarred with old burns, the fingertips are hard as horn, and the knuckles of the right hand are swollen from a lifetime of speaking to pipes.

They call themselves by the work of their hands. There are brickers, who cut the silt-blocks and stack them in the drying combs. There are kiln-tenders, who go grey-lashed and half-deaf in the dry heat and are envied and pitied for it. There are lampwrights, who render the fat and trim the wicks and know the smell of every rank. There are sheet-beaters, who hammer copper thin enough to sing, and gut-drapers, who tend the great membranes overhead and climb the wet roofs with pots of gum. Each trade has a smell that stays in the clothes and a rhythm that stays in the wrists. A stranger would find them hard to tell apart. They tell each other apart at once, by gait, by the sound of a step on brick, by the pattern a knuckle makes on a pipe. A person's name is a rhythm before it is anything else. A mother taps it into the bronze rail of a cradle-shelf until the child turns its head.

## How They Speak

They have voices and use them rarely, in small rooms, in the kiln-warm places where the din drops to a mutter. Otherwise they talk through the structure. A knuckle, a knife-pommel, or a small bone mallet on a pipe sends a message down the whole run of clay and bronze, and someone three districts off answers. It is a language of length and spacing, and it carries more than words. It carries mood, urgency, and who is asking. A grieving message is slow and evenly spaced. A joke has a stumble in it, a deliberate false step that makes a listener smile in the dark a quarter-mile away. Children learn to eavesdrop on the pipes long before they can speak, and the pipes gossip all night. Privacy means a wall with no metal in it, and those rooms are prized and a little feared.

They also have gestures, done at arm's length in the lamp-pools, big and slow and a bit theatrical: a flat palm turned over for *the water is late*, two fingers drawn down the cheek for *I am tired of the noise*. But that is the language of the lit places. Between the lights, they touch a sleeve and tap on a shoulder.

## The Keepers

The keepers are the part of the people that the rest of the people are slightly afraid of. They are not a class so much as a habit of mind that some are born with. They walk the causeways at all hours with their mallets, pitch pots, and coiled braided-hair rope, and they walk unevenly, stopping mid-stride with a hand up and their heads tilted. They are listening for the hole in the sound.

Every keeper carries a map of the racket in the head, the tipping rate of ten thousand buckets, and knows without thinking which one is late. They can say, "The third trough behind the copperworks has slipped a beat," and be right. When they find a jam they climb, wedge, hammer, and re-hang the bucket, and the channel takes up its rhythm again. A keeper who has just fixed a silence stands a moment with eyes closed, as though listening to a heartbeat resume.

They also tend the black felt in the gutters, scraping it in long strokes and boiling it into the grey gruel the whole basin eats. Their fingernails are stained the color of ink. Their food tastes of mineral and smoke and something like old rain, and nobody living remembers another.

Above all, the keepers are the ones who touch the cracks. When a hairline opens in a sealed wall, one of them will kneel, lay a palm to it, and stay there a long time. What they feel is not quite fear. It is the reverence of someone who has been told a story all their life and is now, for the first time, being shown that it might be true. The rim-tales say that buildings die slowly, and the keepers half believe it now, and no one has told the others.

## Rites and Beliefs

They do not think of the Laying as a god. It is nearer to a landlord, or a tide, or the weather of their own bodies: a thing that is owed attention. When a flood comes they gather at the terrace edges in the ash-colored light and make the slow, even tapping called *the settling*, which is meant to help the silt lie flat. They do not believe it works. They do it because it would be strange not to.

Their dead go into the wall. A body is wrapped in gut and fired-clay slip, and a course of brick is laid over it, so the dead rise with the city. Families know which course holds which grandparent, though the floods bury the courses under newer ones, and they keep up a custom of tapping the name-rhythm on the nearest pipe on certain nights, in case the buried can still hear through the clay.

The sealed vaults trouble them more. Down under the grey clay are doors packed shut and lamps that perhaps still burn. Some say no one tends them and the flames simply outlast their makers. Others insist that someone always has, some low patient people who came before and never left and answer the pipes only rarely, so faintly it might be a trick of the water. Children are told to tap once on a sealed door for luck and then leave at once, without looking back. Nobody says what the luck is for.

## The Grazers

The pale, blind, hoofed animals are not owned exactly, but they are kept. They move in twos and threes along the lower terraces, their hooves ringing on the brick in a clopping that is one more voice in the din, and they have learned the causeways as well as the people have. Their eyes are sealed skin over nothing. Their muzzles are wet and constantly working, and they navigate by echo and by the drag of their own breath against the walls. Drovers, who are mostly the old and the very young, walk with them and tap encouragement on the brick. The grazers keep the fungus mats cropped and the gutters clean, and when one dies its hide goes to the gut-drapers and its horn to the lashers, and the sound of its hooves is remembered by pitch.

They are gentle beasts and easily startled by silence. A grazer that stops dead, head up, nostrils flared at a stretch of quiet, is a warning the keepers respect. Often the animal knows before the keeper does.

## The Small Lives

The ash-colored moths are the nearest thing to a sky the basin has. They turn around the lamp-rows in slow spirals, thousands to a rank, and their wings drop a fine grey dust that lies on every sill and gets into every meal. Children catch them in cupped hands, whisper into them, and let them go, which is a game with rules nobody can explain.

Something in the walls eats the moths. No one has seen it whole. It is a movement in the gap between brick and brick, a wet clicking too fine for the din, a moth that is there and then not. The people leave small dishes of fat in the wall-gaps for it, without being sure why.

And in the deepest channels, where the flow is fastest and the tuning strictest, the long translucent eel-worms rise and slide upstream. They are as long as a person and thin as an arm, and they seem to be made of lit water. They do not scatter at the sound of the buckets. They come toward it. A keeper who sees one goes still and does not speak. It is said that they only surface where the tuning is good, and no one has ever caught one, and no one has tried.

## The Quiet

Beneath all these lives is the thing they do not name but all of them know: a listening. The whole basin, people and grazers and moths and the thing in the walls, is arranged around a noise that keeps the mud moving. They are small and few, they are guests of the machinery, and every one of them is trained from the cradle to hear the hole in the sound. When it comes, when a patch of true quiet spreads across a district like a stain, everyone stops. The hammers hang. The children hush. The grazers lift their blind heads. For a moment the entire population stands in the dark, waiting to learn where the water has stopped.

Then, somewhere, a keeper's mallet strikes bronze, and the sound rushes back in.
