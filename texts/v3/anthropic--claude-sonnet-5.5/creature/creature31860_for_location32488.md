# Those Who Live at Ashgrave Col

Nobody lives in the Kiln as an individual. That is the first thing to understand. Even the largest animals there, the Threadwalkers, live as part of a crowd, and their whole lives are spent in relation to the bodies pressed around them. Each animal is a small local decision made by a much larger creature that has no head and has never been seen whole. Still, the parts have habits, and the habits are worth setting down.

## The Lamplice

Their lives are short, warm, and almost entirely spent in contact. A lamplouse hatches from a clutch laid in the seams of the cooler clinker, a translucent bead no bigger than a grain of salt, and its first act is to press itself against the nearest sibling. Isolation is the one thing it cannot survive. A lamplouse alone on bare rock will stop feeding within an hour, and it will drift, flashing weakly and uselessly, until something takes it.

They graze with a slow, ceaseless sweeping of the mouthparts, and where a sheet has passed, the black film is scraped to a matte gray that regrows in a few days. Their flashes are not just fright. A lone flicker means *I am here*. A double flicker means *something is wrong*. A long green surge means *climb*, and the sheet climbs, and no individual knows who began it. Older lamplice have flashed so often that their shells have gone faintly cloudy, like handled glass. Their deaths are unremarkable. They stop, the sheet closes over the place, and by the next pulse the Sextons have been.

## The Bellows-Beetles

Each beetle owns a pit. It digs the pit young, in the softer clinker of the middle terraces, and enlarges it through its whole life by turning slowly in place, so that old pits are smooth-sided bowls and old beetles sit in them like eggs in cups. They rarely leave. Food comes to them: dead lamplice drifting down, the small stream of husks, and the lamplice themselves, which graze past close enough to seize.

They are the chamber's clock. The huff is a private thing that becomes a public one. A beetle holds its breath, feels the neighbor's chest tighten through the stone, and lets go a fraction of a second after the one beside it. The rhythm is passed inward from the rim, and no beetle leads. If a Threadwalker's leg comes down among them, the rhythm skips a beat, and everyone, from the beetles to the lamplice to the wall, registers the skip.

Their wing cases are crusted with pale mites, which they do not mind, and which clean them. In the deep ridges of the oldest beetles grows a gray-green lichen-like film, alive on the rock and alive on them, and nobody can say where the beetle ends.

## The Threadwalkers

They are lonely in a way the others cannot be. They must keep their distance from one another or starve, and so each keeps a stretch of the hot line, a territory of perhaps ten body-lengths, patrolled by standing still. A Threadwalker will hold a pose for hours. Its wire legs read the vibrations of the crowd like fingers on a lute, and it knows the difference between a lamplouse and a young beetle by the ring of the footfall through the rock.

When they kill, they do it with a single leg, and the tip of each leg is a hooked, hollow needle. The prey is lifted, folded, carried to the ledge, and taken apart with the slow patience of something that has never been hurried. Their molted husks, gray and perfect, hang from the upper walls in great numbers, each one exactly the shape of its maker, so that the ceiling near the shaft seems to hold a gallery of silent watchers. The living ones walk beneath them and do not look up. They cannot look. They have no eyes worth the name.

## The Sextons

They are the ones who keep faith with the fire. Black, blind, the size of a grain of rice, each Sexton carries a load in its jaws that may be a hundred times its own weight, and walks it in a line down through the terraces to the fissures, where the dry heat cures it. The cured matter is not eaten at once. It is packed into the hot seams as provision. Below the third terrace, in the dark chambers that open off the fissures, there are stores that have been curing for longer than the oldest Threadwalker husk. Nobody living knows how much is down there. The Sextons do not either, and they add to it every hour.

They live in shifts. A Sexton goes down, works until its shell is nearly too hot to hold, and retreats, steaming, to the cool ledges, where the others crowd around and cool it. In this way they are the closest thing the Kiln has to a shared tenderness. The lines part for an overheated one. Its sisters climb over it and press against it, wet, until the shell is gray again. Then it goes back down.

## The Wall-Mimics

They are the patient ones. A Wall-Mimic settles at the moment of its first molt and stays where it has settled for decades. Its skin takes the color and grain of the rock beneath it. Its pores open into the warmer minerals, and it drinks the salts, and the small warm beetles that wander near are drawn in.

If you looked closely you would find them by their edges, which never quite match, and by the fact that the crowd swerves around them the way water swerves around a stone. The lamplice give them a berth of a single body's width. Every so often a lamplouse forgets. What happens is very quick.

Some of them are old enough that colonies of smaller things have taken up residence in their folds. A few of them have become, in effect, small hills.

## The Small Tenants

Below the noticed animals is a further population that no one counts.

In the salt crusts lives a red-pink film of something too small to see, which blooms in the damp hours and blackens in the dry. It is what the crusts glisten with. A pale, thread-thin worm lives in the seams of the clinker, feeding on the dead pumice-paste, and turns up sometimes in the beetles' pits like a hair. There are mites on nearly everything. There are gray flecks of fungus that grow only on the dead and are eaten by the Sextons before they can fruit. There are things whose entire lives are spent in the crack between one shell and the next, and which have never in their history touched bare stone.

## The Visitors

The shaft admits a trickle of others, none of which live. Moths come down in the warm months, drawn by the smell of heat, and fall spinning on the cool ledges. Seeds arrive on the wind. Once in a few years a bat loses its way, drops into the dark, and lies stunned on the rim, its heartbeat loud in the rock. The whole rim goes still around it. Then the Sextons come.

These are the only outsiders the Kiln knows. They arrive dead or dying, and the crowd receives them without ceremony and without malice. They are welcomed the way the mountain welcomes rain.

## The Stacker

Then there is the ring.

The stacked stones at the edge of the third terrace are cool, bare inside, and old. The crowd has built itself up to the outer wall and stopped, as if at a shoreline. The animals do not enter. They are not afraid of it. They simply leave a place for something.

Something lives there. It is easy to miss, because it moves so slowly that the crowd has long since stopped counting it as an animal. It is small, about the size of a loaf, pale, hairless, and soft-skinned, with the loose gray-white look of a creature that has spent centuries out of the light. It has hands. They are narrow and long-fingered, and they are the only such hands in the chamber. It has no eyes, or none that open. Its breath is a single long draw every several minutes, so slow it does not disturb the dust.

Once in a few years, when the pulse has passed and the ground is coolest, the Stacker rises, crosses the bare floor of the ring, and turns a stone. Sometimes it takes a stone from the outside of the ring, where the crowd has pressed against it, and sets it in the wall. Each stone takes a season to place. The ring is very slowly closing, or very slowly rising, or very slowly being rebuilt from within. No one has ever measured which.

Whether the Stacker built the ring, or inherited it from something that came before, or is simply the last thing that remembers what the ring is for, is not known to anyone in the Kiln. The animals do not ask. But when the pulse comes and everything else flees to the rim, the crowd's climbing flow bends around the ring the way the tide bends around a rock, and the Stacker sits in the center on the cool ground, wholly still, while the heat rolls past.

It is said, though there is no one to say it, that the empty honeycomb on the eastern wall was built by the Stacker's people, and that it stands clean because no one in the chamber will settle where the last of them once lived. It is also said, by no one, that the Stacker is the oldest thing in the chamber, and that when it stops, the ring will be finished and the rhythm of the beetles will change.

## What Lives There

What lives in the Kiln is a crowd, a breathing and flickering tide, layered by heat and held together by touch, in which a thousand kinds of small life are each doing the one thing they were made to do. At its rim are the pale and the new. At its heart are the black, patient workers of the fire. Between them walk the hunters on their wire legs, and in the walls the still ones wait, and in the cool ring at the edge of the third terrace, unseen, one old hand turns one old stone.

None of them knows the others fully. All of them know the rhythm. It comes up through the rock, and it comes down through the shaft as moths and dust, and it goes on.
