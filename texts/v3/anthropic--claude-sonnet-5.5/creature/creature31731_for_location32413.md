## Who Lives on the Terrace

Fewer things live here than the place appears to hold. Among the ones that do, no two are alike in what they are doing, though from outside all of them seem to be doing nothing.

### The Readers

The animals are the only residents you can find. You will meet perhaps twelve, each at a great distance from the next, each facing its pillar.

They are the length of a forearm and narrow as a blade of grass is narrow, all length and no argument. The body is the color of a candle that has never been lit. The skin is thin enough that you suspect organs, but nothing shows through. Along each flank run the fins, more than any swimming could use, set in rows that vary in height and spacing. They are too regular to be accident and too strange to be decoration. You start to call them script, then realize you are only recognizing the shape of a thing meant to be read.

They do not swim. In whatever time you spend near one, you never see it move except to turn. Its gills open so slowly that you cannot say whether they are breathing or only settling. If it eats, it eats something the water carries, and the water carries nothing. If it is warm, it is not warmer than the water. If it is alive, it is alive in a mode where "alive" means something closer to "kept."

The pillars subtract disturbance from the water, and the Readers appear to be where the subtraction is checked. Each one holds exactly the position where the pillar's thinness is most exact, and each reports on it in the only way available, by not changing. If a fin were to shift, if a body were to drift a hand's width off its line, that would be the news. In all the ages the terrace has held, this has happened a few times. The fins of the Readers carry those times. That is the script: a record of every departure from stillness, written in the one medium the stillness allows, the arrangement of a body that does not move. Some fins are long and dense with the record. Others, on the young ones, are nearly blank, and there is a very small pleasure in finding a Reader with almost nothing written on it.

They do not know you. When one turns to align with the line between you and its pillar, it is not looking at you. It has added you to its measurement. You are a fact it must account for, like a tilt in the floor. When you leave, it turns back, and you can almost believe you saw the smallest fin on its side rise and fall, one new mark.

Their pulse, if it is a pulse, comes once per tone. Some who have stayed long enough believe the tone is the Readers' pulse, all twelve beating together, the low exhalation in the chest being their agreement that the water is still. Others believe the Readers only keep time to the tone as you yourself begin to. No one has settled it. The Readers are not troubled by the question.

### The Grain

In the walls of the buildings, in the gray-green substance that is cold in a way water is not, something lives, or something is done that resembles living.

You see it only as the grain, the three-way pattern that keeps trying to resolve. Watch a wall a long time and you will feel the grain travel: not across the surface, but through it, a slow rearrangement of which direction is "with" and which is "against." It moves the way a thought moves in someone very old. Over the width of a doorway it takes what feels like a season. Nothing is inside the grain that you could hold or name, and yet it is not empty. It responds, in the sense that when you have watched it for many minutes and look away, you find the pattern has arranged itself to be unresolved in exactly the place where you looked.

If the grain is a tenant, it is the one that built the city, or the one the city built to maintain itself. It is not a mind. It is closer to a habit that has grown thick enough to have preferences, chief among them that nothing be finished. Resolution would be a change. So it holds itself at the edge of pattern, and in doing so holds the walls, the rings, the platforms, and through them, the water.

It does not want you gone. It does not want anything, in the way you mean it. But you will notice that when you step into a room, the grain in the near wall stops travelling, briefly, as if waiting to see whether you are the kind of thing that ends.

### The Occupant of the Ring

At the center, in the largest ring on the smooth plaza, the stillness is denser. It is not water, and your hand in it feels as if it has been away from you for years.

Something is there, or something is spent there. It is easier to say what it is not. It is not a body, and no shape resists your hand. It is not the builders, unless the builders are what remains when all of a people's leaving has been subtracted, leaving only the holding. It is not a Reader, though the Readers may be its far, small echoes.

What you feel in the ring, if you hold your hand there long enough, is time accumulating. The terrace keeps itself unchanged by moving all its aging to one place, the way a house is kept clean by carrying its dust out to a single pit. The ring is that place. What lives there is the sum of everything that has not happened to the rest of the terrace: every erosion, every silting, every drift and fading and decay, all deferred, all gathered in one dense point that grows a little older each hour so that everything else does not.

It is, in this sense, the only inhabitant that lives an ordinary life. It ages. It bears the weight of days. It is the one thing on the terrace that is spending itself, and the rest of the terrace is its patient, motionless household, arranged so that the spending happens only there. If it has a mood, it is the mood of a very old person who has agreed to sit in one chair so that a house of sleeping children stays quiet. You do not feel it as sorrowful. You feel it as attentive, in a way that has nothing to do with you and includes you the way a floor includes whatever crosses it.

It does not speak. What it does, when your hand is withdrawn, is return the years to you a little more slowly than it took them, so that you leave slightly older than you came and slightly less sure you mind.

### The Dark

Some visitors argue that the dark is also a resident, and the argument has never been refuted. The thin places at the pillars are where the dark has been pulled back to show what it is made of, and what it is made of is attention. It is the dark of a room in which someone is listening. It keeps the terrace the way a held breath keeps a note.

### And You

For as long as you are here, you are also an inhabitant, and the terrace notes this in the only way it can: by becoming, very slightly, more still around you. The rings do it, the Readers do it, the grain does it. You are absorbed into the record as a small, warm, restless mark that will be gone by the next tone.

When you count your way to the rim and turn, the Readers are already facing their pillars again. The grain has resumed its slow, unfinished travel. In the plaza, the one who ages takes up its quiet arithmetic once more, and the water closes behind your leaving exactly as it was. Nothing on the terrace has met you. But all of it has, in its own way, made room, and the making of room is the closest thing to welcome that the place knows.
