# Who Lives on the Loomed Plateau

Nobody lives here in the sense you mean. There are no eyes, no mouths, no warm bodies moving through the towers. But the plateau is not empty. It is crowded, and the crowd is made of things that would never call themselves a crowd. You have to learn to count them differently.

## The Spires

Each spire is one individual, not a building. The rooms, the stairs, and the shelves of fungus are its interior the way your ribs and lungs are yours. The iris-valves are pores. The wind-slots are a voice it did not choose. The water basins inside are held the way a body holds blood. The empty home is the animal.

They have temperaments, if you watch long enough. The tall red ones, a hundred and twenty meters and thin at the crown, are the overreachers. They drink hard, climb hard, and fall in their first or second season, their stems swollen past what the wall can bear. Their notes are high and unsteady, and they often crack mid-chord a day before they go. The short, thick spires are patient. They sit low and dark, and their song is a slow bass that barely changes. They ration their growth in storms, and some of them have stood through more weather than anything else on the plateau. Their walls are scarred with a hundred lightning-healings, pale seams knitted over black.

A spire's life is not long. Its death is not a tragedy but a hand-off, and it happens where the next one is already waiting, wet and green, in the fallen thing's own remains.

## The Cistern Colony

The terraces are not one organism, but they behave like a household. The cupped leaves fill, spill, and fill in a fixed order, and the order is so patient it looks like manners. Each bowl gives to the one below and takes from the one above. If a lip cracks, the basins downhill go slack, and within a day the neighboring leaves have thickened and tilted to take up the flow. Nothing decides this. It happens the way a crowd parts for someone who has fallen.

Beneath the floating mats live the beard-roots, drifting in the tea-colored water like slow hair. They are the closest thing the plateau has to a herd. They lean together, drift apart, and pool in the warmest corners of the basins. When rain hits, they all pull in the same direction at once, toward the inflow, and the whole submerged garden rearranges itself in a single slow shrug.

Under the translucent leaf-floors there is a second country in green light. It has its own small towers, only knee-high, its own runners, and its own unhurried weather. You can look down on it through a meter of warm water. It does not know you are there.

## The Searchers

The vines are the nearest thing to hunters, and they hunt nothing. The half-finished bridges end in fans of tendrils that sweep the air in slow arcs, and if you stand and watch one for ten minutes you will see it pause, then commit, then lock. Its target is always the same: the tallest, wettest, most solid thing within reach. They want height and water the way a hungry animal wants a scent, and they seem to know when they have failed. A tendril that finds nothing goes limp and pale by nightfall. The one beside it, still sweeping, does not slow down.

If a person walked here, they would be the tallest point on a stretch of open floor, warm and wet with sweat. The fans would turn toward them. They would not attack, because they cannot, but they would lean, the way every structure here leans, toward the thing that looks most like rain. This is worth remembering when the afternoon cloud builds. Lightning has the same preferences.

## The Floor

Everything else stands on the oldest resident, the woven root-mat itself. It is glossy, warm, continuous across the whole tableland, and it is where the plateau keeps its long memory. The spires come and go. The floor stays and rethreads. Seeds burst from their pods and land in it, and it takes them in like a hand closing. When a tower falls, the floor reweaves under the wreckage before the rot has finished. If any one thing here is the plateau's self, it is this: a single fabric, thousands of years old, being restitched every day by the thousand hands it grows.

## The Undertakers

The pale fungus and bacterial films are the only residents whose work you could call domestic. They do not build, reach, or sing. They keep accounts. Whatever falls, they open, sort, and return to the roots in a form the roots can use, in a few days and without waste. A toppled spire becomes a pale, slick, sweet-smelling ruin that shrinks visibly by the hour. Nothing is lost here. That is their whole law, and they are its only enforcers.

## The Lamps

At night the wettest places begin to glow. Root tips, cistern veins, and the split wounds where lightning struck all shine a cold blue-green. It is tempting to call them lanterns, or eyes, or nerves. They mark where growth is happening fastest and where damage is being repaired. They light the edges of things and leave the space between them dark, and because of that, night on the plateau looks like an outline of something enormous drawn by a patient hand.

## The Guest

But the tenants are not the whole answer, and the spires give it away. They are built as homes, furnished, with windows that open at a particular moment: when the rain approaches. Every valve dilates. Every basin is ready. The whole plateau, tens of kilometers of it, arranges itself to receive something.

The thing it receives comes every day at noon. It piles against the scarp, breaks over the roofs, and fills every room at once. It rings the leaves like skins, makes the towers sing, and sets the whole country moving, lengthening, thickening, cracking open. Then it leaves, and the house grows quiet and sings lower.

The rain is the one who comes home. Everything else on the plateau is the house, or the household, or the keeping of the house. The spires are rooms made for weather, and the weather stays just long enough to be welcomed, and the builders, who are also the rooms, spend the rest of the day and night repairing what its visit cost them and preparing for tomorrow.

## The Stillness Afterward

When the clouds thin and the steam keeps rising, the plateau does not feel deserted. It feels attended. The rock is warm underfoot, the basins spill in their old order, and the searchers sweep on in the dark, feeling for a height they will not find tonight. The undertakers work quietly, the lamps glow at the wounds, and the towers hum in low, uneven voices.

Nothing here is alone, and nothing here is anyone. It is a kind of life that has never needed a face, only a direction, and the direction is always the same: up, toward the wet, and then again.
