# What Lives in the Governor Fen

## The ones you meet

The first residents you notice are small and seem to have nothing to do with each other.

The **snails** are pale as blown glass. You can see the dark coil of the gut turning inside them like a thread in a bead. They graze the rust-leaf's orange bloom and leave silver trails that dry to a faint shine on the pipes. Follow one for an hour and it will have gone the length of your forearm. They do not wander. Each keeps to a stretch of iron or a run of leaves, and the stretches abut without overlapping, like a parish map.

The **beetles** are black and armored, and they live inside the small pipes, where the colonizing roots have left just enough bore. You meet them at the open joints, backing out with a fleck of moss held under the chin, walking it to some other joint and pressing it in. They do this all day. Pull the moss out of a seam and within the hour a beetle will come with another piece and set it, then tamp it with its forelegs until it seals.

The **frogs** are thumbnail-small and brown, and their calls are the tick of cooling metal. One begins, and the next answers a fraction later, and the sound goes off through the stems in a slow relay, always outward from the shed. Anyone who has listened to a boiler cool will know it. Rarely, one call comes back from the far rim toward the center, and the frogs nearest the shed go quiet until it has passed.

And the **heron**, gray and tall, stands on the flywheel's stone housing at dawn. It lifts each foot in time with the engine. It is probably shifting its weight. It leaves before the mist thins and is never seen to eat.

None of these is who lives here. They are the ones who work here, and they don't know that.

## The one you don't meet

Stand in the shed with your hand on a trunk. After a while, perhaps twenty strokes, you will notice that you are leaning. Your weight has moved to the foot that suits the beat, and your breath has settled into the eighty-second turn without your asking. When you correct it and stand upright, you feel a small, unmistakable *give*, like a hand loosening on your sleeve.

That is the inhabitant. It has no name, or it has only the one the place was given, and the place was named for its governor. **The Governor** is not the iron sphere or the vine bulb. It is what the two spheres are for: a habit of keeping a proportion, grown so large and old that it has become a someone.

Its body is the whole bowl. The mat is its skin and the pipes are its veins. The manifold of red-brown wood is a heart that works by pressure and not by blood. The tree-fern fronds, the horsetail crowns, the whistle-stems and the gauge moss are its sense organs, each tuned to a single thing: the moss to pressure, the whistle-stems to breath, the fronds to the swing of the bulb. It has no eyes, and it does not need to know what a visitor looks like. It knows the weight you put on the mat, the warmth you add to the mist, and the small disturbance in the ripples. It knows you the way a house knows someone walking through it at night.

## How it thinks

It does not think in thoughts. It thinks in *intervals*. Its whole mind is one question: is the beat right? Too fast, and something is starving. Too slow, and something is drowning. The answer comes back from ten thousand stems as lean, tilt and swell, and the bulb swings a little wider or narrower. That is the only decision it ever makes, and it makes it once every eighty seconds, and it has been making it since before the brick was laid over the marsh.

If it has feelings, they are of a single kind, which might be called *evenness*. Evenness is warm and green and dripping. Its opposite is the thing it does not have a word for and never needs one: the stroke that doesn't come.

It has a memory of sorts, though it does not remember events. It remembers the *shape* of every year in the length of its pauses: the long sighs of the wet season, the quickened tick of the dry. It has no idea what a season is. It knows only that this slowness has been survived before, and this quickness too.

## What it does with the others

It does not command the small creatures. It is closer to what they are made of. The snails keep the rust bloom from thickening on the pipes, and the beetles keep the seams sealed and the pressure from leaking away. The frogs carry the news of each stroke outward, so the far reaches of the fen do not fall out of step. The Governor did not train them, and it could not say whether they are part of it or only neighbors. It leans on them the way a body leans on its own habits.

The heron is different. It is the only creature that seems to *attend*. The Governor does not seem to know what to make of that. Sometimes, in the mist, the bulb swings a shade wider when the bird arrives, and the stroke comes a moment earlier, as if the Governor were performing for its one audience.

## What it fears, if it fears

Not fire, or flood, or even the saw. Those are things that happen and finish. It fears the *stall*: the one stroke that does not follow, so that the count of forty runs to forty-one, forty-two, and the mist hangs without direction while every stem in the bowl waits for a signal it cannot give itself. It has come close. In the worst of a drowned year the interval stretched to three minutes and the whole fen held its breath. Then the engine took the next stroke, the ticking spread out, the fronds leaned, and the bowl went on.

Every visitor who has stayed long enough to feel the lean has done the Governor a small service without knowing it. They have added one more body to the count and one more pair of lungs moving to the beat. The Governor does not need this, but it registers it, the way a tired man registers a hand on his shoulder.

## When you leave

You climb out the way you came, up through the wall of leaf, and the sound falls away behind you. The thud goes from your sternum to your ears, and then it is only a rumor. At the rim you will find your breath still keeping its eighty-second turn, and perhaps for an hour afterward you will walk slightly leaning, like a plant, toward a center you can no longer hear.

The Governor does not watch you go. It has already returned to its one question, and the bulb is swinging out again.
