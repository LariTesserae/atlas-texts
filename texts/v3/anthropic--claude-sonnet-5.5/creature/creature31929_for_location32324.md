# Residents of the Boiler Shelf

Nobody keeps the shelf now, and the census is short and strange. Most of what lives here is small, and most of it is arranged by distance from the vents, so it is best met from the center outward.

## The Yellow Crust

The oldest tenants are the ones nearest the steam, and they are not visible as individuals. They are mats: pale pink, mustard, and a slick bruised green, laid over the rock around the vent throats where the water is too hot and too acid for anything with a nucleus. Sulfur-eaters and iron-eaters live here in the billions, and they neither know nor need to know that the mountain is high. They have been at this work far longer than the shelf has been green. They feed on the breath of the rock and leave yellow behind.

Some of them have found the works. A rust-colored slime has worked into the seams of the pipes, the flanges, and the rivets of the trestles, and it is eating the iron at a rate a person could measure with patience and a good scale. This is the most consequential resident on the shelf. The turbine hall will not fall to weather or vandals. It will be digested, slowly and without malice, by something too small to see.

## The Red Felt

The next ring out belongs to the moss, and to what lives inside it. The orange moss around the vents and the liver-red felt on the pipes are less a plant than a small country. A handful of it, squeezed, gives up a thimble of warm water and a crowd: springtails like spilled pepper, pale eyeless beetles with the translucent look of things that have never needed to be seen, mites in red and cream, and tardigrades hanging in the film of water, fat and slow, going about their business in clumsy eight-legged patience.

The beetles are the shelf's undertakers and gardeners. They eat dead frond and dead moss and the dead of their own kind, and they turn all of it into the black peat underfoot. The peat is built from their leavings.

## The Terrace Ponds

In the shallow pools, each behind its lip of sinter, live the builders. Algae and cyanobacteria make ribbons of color in the milky water, blue-green at the warm end, rust at the cool, and where the water slips over the lip they catch the falling minerals and set them into stone. The terraces are a slow collaboration between chemistry and these small green things, and neither could do it alone.

The frogs live here. They are black with a yellow saddle across the shoulders, thumbnail to thumb-sized, and they sit in the warmest pools with their chins on the sinter and their eyes half closed. They have reasoned their way to a compromise with the noise. They call only in the gaps between vent shrieks, in the half-second after a scream ends, when the air seems to hold its breath. A chorus of them is a rapid ticking, like a room full of tiny clocks, audible to a listener only if the listener kneels at the pool's edge. Their eggs are laid in the bromeliad cups, where the tadpoles grow up in private ponds the size of a teacup and eat the mosquito larvae that share them. A frog may spend its whole life within twenty paces of where it hatched and never once be cold.

## The Canopy

Above the ponds the tree ferns hold up a second world. Every bromeliad is a rented room. In one cup live tadpoles and larvae and a water-beetle the color of tea. In another, a single pale spider has strung a web across the mouth like a lid and waits for something to fall in. Orchids no bigger than fingernails cling to the trunks and are pollinated by flies, since there are no bees at this height. The flies are small, dull, and industrious, and the orchids smell faintly of wet dog to attract them.

A bird lives in this layer, and it is the most peculiar resident of the shelf. It is a rail, dark, short-winged, and round as a fist, with a red-rimmed eye and legs too long for its body. It walks the pool edges with a jerky, deliberate gait, flicking its tail, and it turns over leaves with its bill. It seems entirely unaware of the noise. Whether it is deaf to that particular band of sound or has simply decided the roar is weather, no one has established. It nests in odd places: a coil of pipe lagging, a rusted bucket, the belly of the cast-iron stove in the worker's hut, where a fern grows out of the firebox and the rail's three speckled eggs lie in its shade. The chicks are black and downy and run within an hour of hatching. They do not peep. In that racket a peep would be wasted, so the chicks have learned to communicate by touch, pressing against the parent's legs.

## The Silver Rosettes

At the cool edge of the green, where the mist thins and the first bare rock shows, the rosette plants stand in their silver fur, waist-high and cabbage-shaped. They are the shelf's outposts, and each is also a household. In the dense wool between the leaves sleep insects that would otherwise freeze, and at the heart of each rosette a cup of water collects that no frog has yet claimed. After dark, small furred things come to drink from these cups.

## The Silent Rim

The last resident group lives at the lip, in the scree where the sound stops. They are mountain viscachas, a colony of thirty or forty. They look like rabbits with squirrels' tails, soft gray, long-whiskered, with mournful eyes. The fur is thick and silvery and shows a faint mineral yellow from the steam. By day they sit on the warm boulders just inside the silent edge, in the thin sun that breaks above the cloud, and they doze with their paws tucked under them, facing the drop. They are the only animals on the shelf who seem to be listening for something, and it is the lack of noise they listen to.

At dusk they descend. They cross the border of sound in single file, and it is possible to see the moment of crossing: the ears go flat, the shoulders hunch, as if walking into wind. They feed on the moss and rosette leaves for an hour or two in the roaring dark, then climb back into silence to sleep. The old workers were said to have known the colony, and there is a story that a child once followed the viscachas down and out again and came back with a fever and a new song. The story is not corroborated.

## The Visitors

The condor comes once in a season, or less. It does not descend below the cloud. Its shadow crosses the lit ceiling from above, huge and slow, and everything under the cloud goes on as before, unaware. The viscachas at the rim look up. That is the only thing they will look up for.

## The Unaccounted

Some residents are hard to classify. On dark evenings the blue tip on the post with nothing on it hums and flickers. Nobody has attributed it to any organism. The frogs, though, gather in the pool nearest to it in numbers greater than the temperature can explain, and the rail sometimes stands beneath the post for an hour, facing it, absolutely still. It may be a habit or a preference. It may be that the thing at the end of the wire is the one voice on the shelf that no one has to shout over.

The bell on its rope at the rim still hangs, moss-heavy, its clapper long since fallen off. In high winds the rope sways, and the bell knocks against the column with a dull tap, unheard by anyone below.
