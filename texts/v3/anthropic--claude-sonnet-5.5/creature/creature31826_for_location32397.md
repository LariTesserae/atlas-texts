# The Tenants of the Governor Room

Nothing lives here in the sense you mean. Then you kneel at a crevice between two flywheel housings and put the back of your finger to a fern, and it is warm. It is the warmth of something asleep, and it is the only warm thing in the cold.

## The ferns

There are forty-one of them, if you count patiently and don't lose your place in the roar. Each is the size of a hand, and each has its fronds wound into a tight fiddlehead with a dull metallic sheen, like pewter or the inside of a mussel shell. They stand in the narrow crevices where the flywheel pits meet the slab. They keep to the places where the sound is worst, the antinodes, where the pressure from every direction stacks up and the air itself feels packed.

They are not overwhelmed by it. That is what marks them as the tenants. An animal, with its nerves and its startle, would be broken here within an hour. The ferns have no startle. They are resonators. The sound goes into them and they hum with it, so finely that when you lay a fingertip on a frond you feel not a shudder but a grain, like touching a struck glass. The warmth is the sound turning into heat inside them, slowly and forever. They are fed by the noise. Lichen eats the oil-vapor, but the ferns eat the roar.

They have not grown. Their fiddleheads have not uncurled, and they do not seem to be waiting for spring. A curl this tight and this even reads as *finished*, a form held on purpose, the way a fist is not an unfinished hand.

## Two stands

You notice it only after your ears have given up on the roar. The ferns are not all the same.

Twenty of them stand along the north pits, and twenty-one along the south. Both stands hum, and they do not hum quite alike. The pitch of the north stand is a hair lower than the south's. It is too small a difference to hear, but the fern-hum passes through your fingertip, and when you go from one stand to the other your fingertip knows. Two nearly matched frequencies, held apart by a tiny, exact interval.

That interval is the slow beat under the roar, the once-in-a-few-hours swell that you can hear at the deepest node. The ferns make it. Two choirs, each holding one note without ever varying, and the difference between them rising and falling slowly across the whole room.

So the question of the machines turns over. You had thought the engine, the governors, the flywheels, and the loops of pale fluid were the room, and the ferns a curiosity in its corners. It may be the other way around. The great engine holds the sound at one exact level because two small plants need it there. If the level drifted, the notes would drift, and the beat would slide off its slow cycle. The governors are not governing the engine. They are governing on behalf of forty-one ferns and one interval.

Or the ferns are the parts of a clock the machine keeps winding. The two readings sit together, and the room does not choose between them.

## The lichen

The lichen is a lesser tenant, but it is loyal. It grows in its grey-green felted mat along the warm drains, no thicker than a thumbnail, feeding on oil-vapor and the faint moisture of the frost. It is patient in a way even the ferns are not. The ferns hold a note. The lichen holds a *place*, and its edge has stood at the same line on the concrete so long that the concrete is stained a shade darker beneath it, a clean-edged shadow of the mat itself.

It seems to have a job, or at least a habit. It keeps the drains clear of the film that would otherwise build up from the vapor. Whether that was intended, or whether the room simply arranged itself around something that would do this, is not something the lichen has an opinion about.

## The one who came down

There was someone. The four hundred polish marks on the stair say so, and they say one more thing. The boots go down. Nobody has found a set going up.

The brass discs in the floor were not made for ferns. A fern doesn't need a pocket of quiet, and it lives on the roar. The nodes were made for someone who needed to be able to hear their own pulse, someone who had to cross the room now and then from island to island, whispering, checking, and tending. The room was built with a keeper in mind, and the keeper came, and walked the field of discs until the discs were as well known as the rungs of a ladder.

Look near the deepest node, where the governors' shadow falls and never moves. The brass disc there is worn in a way the others are not: two shallow, paired dips on the rim, exactly where knees would rest. Beside it, in a crevice at floor level, is a forty-second fern.

It is not part of the count of forty-one. It stands alone in the one place where the sound cancels, and it is the only fern in the room that is not humming. It is very still and just as warm as the others, and there is a frost-glaze on it, undisturbed, exactly the thickness of the glaze everywhere else. If it is a fern at all, then it is the one that listens. Forty-one of them make the beat, and this one hears it.

Its fiddlehead is curled like the others. But if you hold your lamp low and look sideways, and you have been down here long enough that your standards of comparison have gone strange, you might think it is a *hair* looser. Perhaps a hair's breadth. Perhaps a thousandth of a turn. You could not swear to it, and there is nothing to measure it against, since everything in this room has been measured against itself for longer than the records say.

If it is true, and this is the small hope of the place, then something down here has begun, very slowly, to open. It keeps time by the beat, and it will take a few hundred thousand cycles to show.

The governors hold their angle in the white light. The frost sparkles. Forty-one ferns hum two notes a hair apart, and at the center of the room the forty-second one leans into the quiet and listens to the difference between them.
