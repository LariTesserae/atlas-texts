# Those Who Live on the Stair

## The first one you see

You will not meet them at once. You walk a long way over pale roofs with your ears full of water, and see no one. Then, on a terrace a quarter mile off, a figure crouches with one palm flat on the stone, entirely still. They are not resting or praying. They are listening through their hand to a fall that has started to sound wrong. After a while they take a copper mallet from their belt and tap the rim twice, listen again, and tap once more, slightly to the left.

This is the first thing to understand about the people of the Stair. They hear with their whole bodies, and most of what they do is a conversation with water.

## The people

There are about sixty of them, and they are not many for so large a town. They have the look of people who have walked on wet stone all their lives. They are lean in the calf and wide in the foot, with a low, sliding gait that never lifts the heel fully. They keep their weight forward and their knees soft, the way you walk on a wet floor. Children run in a curious flat-footed skitter. Strangers fall constantly for the first week and are not laughed at, or are laughed at only in knocks.

Their feet are the mark of them. Every threshold has its washing-basin, and so the soles are hard and pale and faintly ridged, the skin of people who spend half their lives in cool alkaline water. Their hands are worse. Lime works into the creases of the knuckles, and the older people have hands the color of candle wax, cracked in fine white lines like glaze. They rub tallow into them at night, the same tallow they use on the saw blades, and a whole household smells faintly of mutton fat and wet limestone.

Their hair is stiff. Mist and spray leave a bloom of mineral in it, and by middle age most are gray-white at the temples whatever their years, so that it is hard for an outsider to tell a woman of forty from one of sixty. They tie it back with strips of goat-hide. Their clothes are felted wool, oiled against the wet, in undyed cream and brown and a dull madder red. Everyone wears a cord at the wrist or throat with a small bell knotted to it. It is the household's bell-name in miniature, cast in bronze no bigger than a thumbnail. No one is ever without it, and the small ringing at the edge of the roar is how they find each other in fog.

## How they speak

They do speak, but not much, and not in the day. Speech is kept for the evening, when the falls are damped by the closing of the sluices, and for the interiors of houses, where the walls of thick tufa swallow the noise. Even then they talk quietly and look at each other's mouths. Lip-reading is a universal skill. Their faces are expressive in a way that unsettles outsiders, with wide, slow, deliberate eyebrows and a great deal happening around the mouth. A child learns to enunciate soundlessly before she learns to talk aloud.

The real language of the day is the knock. It is a grammar of rhythms and pitches, rapped on hollow stones set into walls and plucked out of the tensioned reeds along the channels. Different stones have different voices. The people can tell whose hand made a knock, as we recognize a voice, and there are jokes and insults in it, and something like tenderness. An old man can knock a rebuke across two terraces that lands like a slap. A woman can send a message to her husband on the far side of town that says nothing but *I have seen you, and you are late.*

Everyone has two names. The speech-name is used rarely, mostly by lovers and in the evening. The bell-name is the household's rhythm of three or four strokes, and it is the name that matters. A person is *of* their bell. Marrying into a household means learning its rhythm until it lives in your pulse, and the moment when a newcomer first answers the knock without thinking is treated as a kind of second birth. Visitors who stay three days are lent a bell-name of their own, usually a plain one, two strokes and a rest. It is meant kindly, and it means *this one is counted, for now.*

## What they do

There are no rulers. The law of the footprint holds the town together: build where the old house stood, keep the hearth in its place, break stone only from the dry. Beyond that, people do the work that suits them, and it has settled into old trades.

**The tuners** are the most honored. They walk the falls with mallets and a bag of wet clay, cutting a hollow here, choking a channel there, and every year the town's voice is adjusted a little. They talk about a fall the way shepherds talk about a difficult ewe. A tuner works by ear and palm and by a feeling they cannot explain, and they say the water tells them what it wants to be. Some tuners go deaf in middle age from the constant noise. The people do not pity this. A deaf tuner works by touch alone and is said to hear "from the inside," and the best of them are deaf.

**The sawyers** cut tufa, working in pairs with long toothed copper blades, one pulling and one pushing, singing a wordless count in their throats so the strokes stay even. They are always dusty and white to the elbow, and always thirsty. They cut only from dry terraces, and before beginning a cut they lay a hand on the block and wait. Nobody has ever explained what they are waiting for.

**The channel-wrights** keep the split-log conduits and the hollow reeds, and are half plumbers, half musicians. A blocked channel is an emergency because it silences a part of the town, and a silent stretch of the Stair is a place where a person could fall unheard.

**The counters** ring the dawn and dusk peals. It is a coveted, rotating duty, and the great bell in the old plaza is taken up by whoever's turn it is. In practice a handful of the older women do most of it, and they are formidable. A counter must know, within a few breaths, whether each household has answered, and if one has not, she sends a runner. In living memory this has saved six lives and lost two.

Most people are also farmers, in a small way: barley on the drier terraces, sedge-root, cress from the warm channels, a little cheese from the goats. There is a pale, sour beer made from the barley and drunk warm. They are not a hungry people but they are a plain-eating one, and a feast is a dish of goat meat with wild garlic and all of sixty people crowded onto one roof.

## What they are like

They are patient, and the patience is not passive. It has an edge of attention, like a cat's. Everything about the Stair is slow, and they have learned to hold their impatience in the wrists and shoulders and let it out only in work.

They are courteous, almost to a fault. A guest is fed before a word is exchanged. A washing-basin is set out and the host's own hands wash the guest's feet, quietly, and no one comments. It is not servility. It is a way of saying *you are in the house, and the house is a continuation of every house before it, and you are now part of that continuity.*

They are dry-humored, in the way of people who have had to say a lot with very little. The knock-language rewards economy, and their jokes come in two or three taps. A famous one, told to every child, is a single soft rap that means *the stone is listening*, said whenever someone is being foolish.

They are superstitious, though they would not use that word. They speak of the growing stone as one might speak of an aged and touchy relative, someone to be deferred to, someone whose moods matter. Nothing is taken from it. Nothing is thrown into it. Nobody spits in a pool. Some avoid whistling near the deep basins at night. They will tell you frankly that they do not know whether the stone hears, but that it would be a poor bet to assume it does not.

And they are afraid of silence. Not in the way of a fear of the dark, which is sharp and fast, but in the way you might fear a long illness. The dry ear, the quiet of the upper pass, is where the town's few exiles have gone, and where the very old sometimes walk out to die. A child who wanders there and comes back pale and silent is watched for days. It is said that in the dry ear you begin to hear your own blood, and that after enough of it you cannot stop.

## The dead

The dead are not gone. They are below.

Each house sits on the roof of the last, in the same footprint, with the hearth in the same place. So when a family gathers at its fire, it gathers directly above the fires of its grandmothers and their grandmothers, sunk in white stone, in chambers left open and dry. At the counting, families lay a knuckle on the hearthstone and knock the household rhythm down through it. Nobody says why. Some say it is so the dead may know they too have been counted. Others say it is simply good manners.

The buried chambers are visited often, and without fear. A grown woman might go down through the hatch cut in an old roof to sit an hour with her mother, who lies in a niche on a bed of dry moss, wrapped in wool, gradually browning and stiffening as the seep does its slow work. The chamber's air is cool and smells of mineral and old wood. There is a sound down there, a low, broad hush, as of a large room filled with sleepers, and it is the sound of the water working its way through the walls. The people find it comforting. It is the only place in the town where you can hear yourself think without strain, and they think it a fair reward for the dead.

Nobody seals the chambers. The seep does that in its own time, over a generation or two, closing the door with a sheet of white, and sometimes a family will tell you which of their people are now "in the wall" and which are "still open." Nobody hurries it.

## The others who live here

The wrens nest in the moss walls in great numbers, small and brown and quick, and the people love them with a dry, unspoken love. You hear nothing of their song, but you see the throats tremble on the rims at dawn, and the town has a saying that a house with wrens is a house well counted. Children are told never to touch a nest and rarely need telling.

In the warm pools the little translucent amphibians drift with their pink gills held out like tiny fans. They eat almost nothing, so far as anyone can tell, and live almost forever. The people say some of them are older than the oldest house. When they move a rim stone they first check the pool for these creatures, and if any are found they lift them out in wet cupped hands and set them in a safe basin nearby, murmuring in the soft way they use to speak to the very old.

The goats wear bells of hollow tufa that make a dull, woody *tock* unlike anything else on the mountain. They are clever, sure-footed on the scale, and unhurried. They are not pets, but each has a name, and each is known by its tock.

And in the deepest of the warm basins, on moonless nights, the water glows. The people go out to watch it sometimes, in small groups, without lamps, standing at the rim in absolute stillness, which is the one time they are perfectly quiet. It is a faint blue-green light that seems to rise from below and drift. It does not flicker. It does not pulse. It only brightens slowly and fades, in a rhythm that no one has managed to knock out, though the tuners have tried for generations. Some say it is the stone thinking. Some say it is only the stone breathing. The oldest counters say it is the stone counting, and that it has been counting the town for far longer than the town has been counting itself.

## Late afternoon

When the slanting light turns the terraces to gold, the sawyers put down their blades, the tuners lift their palms from the rims, and the whole population drifts, without arrangement, to the roofs. They sit with their feet in the shallow basins and their backs against warm tufa. Nobody speaks. The falls go on ringing all around them, each in its own voice, tuned by their own hands across generations, and the sixty of them sit inside that sound as inside a house that they are still building and that is still building them.

Then the first bell of the dusk counting sounds from the old plaza, and one by one, from the hearths and from the roofs, each household answers.
