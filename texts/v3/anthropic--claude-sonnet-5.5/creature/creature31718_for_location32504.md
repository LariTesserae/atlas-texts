# The Pressfolk

They call themselves the *Held*, or something the hands make that means it: two palms cupped together and brought to the chest, as if carrying water. Outsiders say *Pressfolk*, after the city, and the Held do not mind. It is accurate. They are a people shaped by being pressed close.

## Bodies

They are small and dense, built low to the ground, broad through the shoulder and hip. Their skin is soft and faintly pale from a lifetime of warm soaking, with a permanent flush at the cheeks and knuckles, and it is never dry. Even in sleep there is a sheen on them, and they smell of iron, sulfur, and the sweetish mat-oil they rub into their hair. Their palms are the exception. Palms are callused, ridged, and hard from stone, glass, and rim-edge, and they are the most expressive part of the body.

The hands are enormous in their lives. Fingers are long and double-jointed, and a Held child can bend a thumb back to touch the wrist. Their faces are just as mobile. The brows are heavy and independently movable, the mouths wide and quick, and there is a fold at the outer eye that tightens and slackens like a second lid. A Held person with a still face is thought to be ill, or grieving, or lying. Above all, the face is *loud*. A stranger sees a crowd at a sluice and thinks it a brawl. It is a conversation among forty people, all speaking at once and all understood.

Their ears are small and scarred. Nearly everyone has lost some hearing by middle age, and no one considers this a misfortune. The old are the best at the drum-sense, since the ear deafened long enough begins to feel the ground instead, in the soles, the shins, and the breastbone. An elder who cannot hear a shout at arm's length can tell you, standing barefoot on a terrace, which drum on which crest is half a beat late.

## Speaking

The hand-tongue is the language of the house, the lane, and the work. It is fast, layered, and full of puns that exist only in motion. One hand can say one thing while the other qualifies it, and the face can add a third, a tone, a doubt, an affection. Two Held who love each other can quarrel and make peace in the space of a breath and a shrug, entirely silently, in a room where a hundred others are doing the same.

Distance has whistles and stone-taps, sharp, high, and piercing, tuned to cut through the roar as a needle goes through felt. The whistlers are a trade. Boys and girls with the gift are taken young and trained until they can carry a sentence across a quarter mile of steam. Their signals are terse, the way telegraph is terse. *Sluice open. Child lost. Vent turning. Come.*

There is speech with the mouth, but it is intimate and rare, and it is saved for the held breath, the inner chambers where the walls are thickest. There people murmur, and the murmur is close to sacred. Lovers whisper. Mothers sing under felt. The dying are told things at a volume no one outside the room will ever hear. A person who speaks aloud in a lane is either insane or a stranger. A person who speaks aloud in the dark of the inner room is home.

## Work

The city runs on a few great trades, and most people belong to more than one.

**The drummers** are the city's clock and its conscience. They live on the terrace crests in stone huts beside the great skin drums, and they are half-deaf by design. Their patterns are memorized in long chains, and they keep a strict oral record of every cycle since before anyone remembers. They are forbidden to miss a beat. It has never happened, or so they say, and the saying is itself a kind of drum.

**The sluice-keepers** mix the waters. They know the warm and cool streams by taste and by the flutter of the water's skin, and they set the stone gates so each household gets the heat it is due. It is a delicate and honored craft, and gossip travels along the channels with the water.

**The mat-tenders** cut and dry the microbial growths, layer by layer, in the precise proportion that leaves the pool's bands intact. They harvest for felt, for thatch, and for the sweet green paste that is the staple of the Held diet. They also maintain the stripes. A tender who lets an orange band creep into a green one is shamed, not because the mat would die, but because the balance would have slipped, and slipping is the one crime.

**The glass-cutters** knap the black flows into blades, roof-slabs, and mirrors. They have hands like maps of old wounds. Their work is the loudest indoors, and they have their own quiet code of signs to keep from cutting each other.

**The wallower-folk** tend the herds.

## The wallowers

No one is sure who owns whom. The Held bring mat-felt and clean the hides of the great low beasts. They dig out the wallows in the warm mud, scrape off the crusting, and carry away the dung for the mats. In return, the wallowers' grunting sets the pitch of the city. Some say the wallowers' bass is where the first drum-patterns came from, and that the drummers only echo the herds.

When a herd shifts its bed, the city shifts with it. A wallow that goes cold is abandoned. A new one warms, and within a few cycles houses lean against it. The wallower-folk, who sleep beside the beasts and smell of them, are the ones who say quietly that the herds decide, and the Held are their hands. The others laugh in the wide, silent way of their kind and do not argue. In the held breath, a wallower-keeper will sometimes lay a palm against a hide and hum. The hide hums back. The keeper shuts her eyes and does not say whether she is asking or being told.

## Living close

There is almost no privacy. Bathing pools are shared, and bodies are casually seen. Touch is constant and unremarked, a hand on a shoulder in a lane, a foot against a stranger's foot in the soaking basin. What the Held guard is not the body but the held breath, the quiet, and interruption of another person's quiet is the gravest rudeness. To enter an inner chamber without the tap-of-asking on the lintel is nearly unforgivable.

Their names are counted from the vent. A child is named for the drum-cycle of her birth and a small gesture chosen by her mother, so that a name is both a number and a little motion of the hand. Everyone has a signature gesture. Introductions are performances.

Marriages are made by the sluice-keepers, half as a joke and half in earnest, by mixing a bride's water with a groom's in a shared basin. If the temperature sits right, they are matched. If it sours, the families make a more careful match. Divorce is a matter of opening a sluice.

## What they fear, and what they do not say

They do not fear the noise. They fear its ending. Anyone raised among the Held has a small, unspoken dread of the silence at the rim, and the dread is bound up with the deepest thing about them.

The city does not change. Yet the people do. They are born, they age, they die. They are the only things in the whole Basin that move through time in a line, and the Held know it, though they have no word for it. Every child is a small violation of the balance, greeted with joy and a flicker of unease. Every elder is a slow, quiet crack in the perfect steady world.

This is why they carry their dead up to the rim. It is the only place where time can be permitted to exist. In the cool clear air, in the sharp light, where things seem to be waiting to happen, they grieve aloud. They weep with their mouths, and they make the sound that they do not make anywhere else, a long, ragged, plain human cry that carries out over the cliff and is heard by no one. Then they come down. They wash. They go back into the roar, into the drums, into the pressing warmth of one another, and the balance closes over the place where the person was, the way sinter closes over a footprint.

Strangers who have stayed a while say that the Held are the most joyful people they have met and also the most quietly afraid. The Held, told this, nod politely, the way one nods at a dream. Then they cup their hands together and bring them to the chest, carrying the water, careful not to spill.
