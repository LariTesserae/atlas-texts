# Who Lives on the Level

Most of the residents are too small to see, and the ones you can see are the least representative.

## The mat

The first inhabitant is the moss, and it is hard to say whether it is one thing or many. Each stem is a separate plant, a pale green rosette on a leafless neck, but the stems lean on one another so closely, and are so alike in what they do, that the bog behaves as one slow animal. Its leaves are mostly empty cells, dead at birth and built to hold water. A handful of it, squeezed, gives up many times its own weight. It breathes acid into its surroundings, trading a little of its own sourness for the minerals it needs, and the acid keeps competitors away. It has spent ten thousand years poisoning the ground against everything but the few who can bear it, and the ground has stayed exactly as sour as it likes.

The moss does not die so much as sink. The top few inches are alive and reaching, and below that the stems are gray and then brown and then black, still holding their shape, still holding water, no longer holding anything else. The bog is a thin living skin over a very thick memory, and it treats them as one body.

## The small water-dwellers

In every drop squeezed from the moss there is a crowd. Amoebae wear tiny vase-shaped shells that they build from grains of sand and the plates of their dead cousins, and the shells persist in the peat long after the animals are gone, so archaeologists of the bog can read past droughts in their shapes. Rotifers spin their wheels of cilia in the film on a leaf. Tardigrades cling to the stems, fat and eight-legged, and when the moss dries at its edges they curl into dust and wait, sometimes for years, with no sense of grievance. Mites the color of old varnish graze on fungal threads. Nematodes thrash in the water without making progress. Midge larvae, cranefly grubs, and water-bugs no longer than a grain of rice patrol the pool margins. Together they weigh more than everything that flies over them, and they have never once been noticed by anything they weren't eating.

Under the surface, fungi have wrapped themselves around the roots of the cranberry and the bog rosemary in a bargain older than either. The plants send down sugar, and the threads send back nitrogen they have pulled out of a place where nitrogen is scarce. The threads are the bog's other nervous system, invisible and unhurried.

## The hunters in the plants

The sundews are the bog's answer to poor food. Each leaf is a round spoon fringed with red hairs, and each hair ends in a bead of glue that catches the light like dew. A midge lands, and the hairs bend toward it over an hour or two, slowly, like a hand closing in sleep. Then the leaf digests it and opens again, leaving a husk. There are thousands of sundews here, and each one keeps a small gallery of husks.

Pale spiders sit among them, one to a rosette or so, and steal from the glue. They are careful about it and are usually caught eventually. Wolf spiders hunt the hummock tops, and a raft spider crouches with its front legs on a pool's skin, feeling for the ripple of a drowning fly. Dragonflies work the pool edges, hovering and darting and hovering again. A common lizard, in the warmer hours, lies flat on the driest ochre hummock with its ribs going in and out. It is the only reptile here and looks faintly surprised at that.

## The voices

The frogs are the loudest residents and the ones you least often see. They sit half-submerged in the tea-colored pools with only their eyes and nostrils out, and the chorus comes from bodies you can walk within a yard of and never find. They have lived in the basin for as long as there have been pools. Each spring, ancestral instinct puts the same clicks and rattles into their throats and the same slow trill, and each spring the eggs hang in clumps in the shallows like clear grapes, and the dragonfly larvae are waiting for them. Most do not survive to be frogs. Enough do.

The birds work in registers above them. Snipe fall from the air and drum with their outer tail feathers, a sound like something bleating, though they make no sound with their throats at all. Curlews go up on long bubbling notes that seem to lift the whole sky. Redshanks stand on tussocks and object to you. The gulls on the biggest hummock are a nation to themselves. They bicker, steal, and shriek, and they keep a raucous calendar of hatch, fledge, and scatter that the rest of the bog ignores. The heron has no part in any of this. It stands in one pool each morning like a stake driven into black glass and waits with an appalling patience for a frog to make an error.

## The path-keepers

The voles are the bog's engineers, though they don't know it. Their runways thread the moss in tunnels of matted grass, unseen from above and as reliable as roads. Some have been in use for longer than the parish has had a name. The voles that use them now are descendants of voles that used them then, and none of them has ever needed to learn the route, because the path teaches it to the feet.

A fox crosses the basin at intervals, always along the same line, always at the same unhurried trot, stopping at the same tussock to listen. It is not hunting exactly. It is checking. It carries the whole map in its body, and the map has not changed in any way that matters. The voles hear it coming and go quiet, and after it passes, the sound returns.

## The residents who are no longer moving

The bog also keeps its dead, and a full account of who lives here has to include them. A leaf from the fourth century lies in the peat looking like last autumn's. A vole that drowned in some earlier reign still has its fur. Pollen, seeds, and beetle wings are laid down in the dark in order, one summer above the last, and each summer is legible if anyone ever cares to read it. These residents are not gone. They are held. The bog has never had a way of forgetting, so it has never had a way of saying they left.

## What passes through

Walkers are rare, and none of them live here. They come across the mat in wool and boots, wading in the mist, and are surprised by the noise. They leave footprints that fill with brown water and slowly clear. If they stand still long enough, they start to hear the basin as a single voice: frogs, midges, snipe, gulls, and under them a faint mechanical pounding from beyond the rim. Then they notice that the bog isn't listening to it, and that nothing in the bog has ever been listening to it.

## What they share

All of these residents, from the shelled amoebae to the fox on its line, share one habit. They stay. The moss holds its level, the pools hold theirs, and the voles hold the runways. The frogs come back to the same water each spring, and the dead stay where they fell. The mill and the rail yard and the humming wires are a hard, busy, temporary sound in the distance. Here, the population is content to be an answer that has already been given, and the answer is a slow one, and it is very loud.
