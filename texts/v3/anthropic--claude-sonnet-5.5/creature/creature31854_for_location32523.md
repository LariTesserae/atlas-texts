# The Settled

## First sight

From the high ridge, the people are hard to find. They are pale figures in pale cloth on pale stone, moving so slowly that the eye takes them for the drift of heat haze. You find one by its shadow, a thin gray stroke in a place where shadows are faint, and then you see the rest: a line of them on a stair, a pair at the edge of a jade basin, a single figure standing so still beside a mirror that you take her for a pylon until she lifts her hand.

They call themselves the Settled, and the word carries both of its meanings, the sediment that drops out of the water and the calm that follows a disturbance. Outsiders call them the Whisperers, which they dislike, since whispering is among the more dangerous things a person can do on the shelf.

There are about forty thousand of them, spread over the slope like knots on a cord.

## The body

Heat and glare have made them a particular kind of people. They are lean, long in the limb, and unhurried, with the loose shoulders of those who have never had reason to brace. Their skin runs from cream to deep umber, but nearly everyone wears a fine salt bloom on it by afternoon, a dusting the sweat leaves behind, and they do not wash it off. Children are marked at the elbows and knees with the pale crust of brine. Hair is cropped close or oiled flat against the heat. Clothing is loose, layered, undyed linen, pleated to trap and shed moisture, and by noon the hems are stiff with mineral and rattle faintly, which is considered a fault. Good cloth is laundered in condensed water and beaten with wet palms until it is soft as skin.

The eyes tell the most. Everyone's are creased at the corners from a lifetime of squinting, so that even the young look faintly amused or faintly pained. The watchers, who read the mirror-flashes without visors, have eyes gone the gray of skimmed milk. They see extraordinarily well in glare and poorly in shade, and they are led down the covered stairs by children, who are proud of the job. Being called *milk-eyed* is an honorific. Some families have three generations of watchers, and the grandmothers, nearly blind in any ordinary light, can still read a distant heliograph as fast as speech.

## The walk

The first thing a visitor notices, and the last thing they learn, is how the Settled move. It is called the pour-step. The foot comes down heel-first and rolls through to the toe, weight arriving late and leaving early, the knees soft, the body carried as though it were a brimming bowl. Nobody stamps, drags, or slaps their soles. Nobody drops a thing. Their hands are always aware of the objects in them, and a cup is put down by lowering it until the table has already accepted it.

Children learn this as a game. The oldest game in Aun Selle is to carry a bowl filled to the lip across a terrace stair without spilling, then across a wet stair, then across a stair while someone tries to make you laugh. The prize is nothing. The shame of failing is mild and gentle and lasting. By seven a child can cross a room of sleeping adults without waking a single one.

In water, they enter as the wading stalkers do, fingertips first, then wrist, then shoulder, the surface parting around them rather than breaking. Outsiders who have seen it say it is like watching someone climb into a mirror. The Settled say the stalkers learned it from them. The stalkers, presumably, would say the reverse.

## Speech

They are not mute. The Settled talk, but the talk is small. In the covered stairways and the cold ground floors, they use *low voice*, a voice made with the breath and the lips, pitched narrow and soft, with no chest in it. It carries perhaps three paces. Vowels are rounded and consonants are softened until they almost vanish, so that outsiders hear the language as one long sigh with occasional edges. Laughter is a shaking of the shoulders and a bright exhalation through the nose.

Near the working basins, even low voice is set aside for *palm-speech*. It is a language of the hands, held close to the body and low, the signs small enough to be made in a sleeve. It has a grammar of pace: a sign made slowly is calm, one made quickly is urgent, and one that trembles slightly is a question. The face carries the rest, in the eyebrows, the set of the mouth, the tilt of the head. A conversation across a brine channel between two people who never looked away from their work can be as rich as a market argument, and just as full of gossip.

For distance there are mirrors and flags, and for the dead there is a single long flash at dusk, repeated down the whole slope, terrace by terrace, until it reaches the lower edge and dies there.

Lullabies are breathing patterns. A mother rocks a child against her chest and breathes the child's name in three long counts, in and out, until the small ribs fall into step with hers.

## Trades

Nearly everyone works with water or stone, and nearly all of it is slow.

The **sawyers** work the soft new edges of the terraces, kneeling in shallow water with wire frames, drawing blocks out of the living stone as one might lift loaves from a tray. They sing to their saws, which is to say they think about it very hard. The **mill-tenders** live in the hum of the brine sheds and say that after a season they cannot sleep without it. The **condenser-keepers** walk the black-glass ranks at dawn, wiping them with soft leather and testing the runoff against their tongues. The **herders** move the plate-eaters down the terraces in slow rolling flocks, using no whistle, no stick, no dog, only a long pole with a pale cloth that is moved just so. The plate-eaters follow the cloth as they would a patch of light.

The **layers-in** are the salvage crews, who decide which failed machines will be given to the mountain and which will be left as foundations. They are the closest thing the Settled have to priests. When a machine is laid in, they stand around it for a while before they leave, and no one is allowed to say what they are thinking.

## Homes

A house in Aun Selle is a series of rooms going down. The cold damp ground floor is for the hot hours, for sleeping, for waiting out the Whitening. The middle floors are for living. The roof, catching what breeze there is, is for the evening, when the family comes up with cups of condensed water and sits looking at the amber terraces going pink. The oldest rooms are those already sealed below. Families know their houses are sinking, and speak of the buried floors as *the elders' rooms*. Sometimes a child will press an ear to a certain cold wall and be told, "Yes, that's Grandmother's kitchen. Her lamp is still in there."

Nothing is ever dug out. It is not forbidden, only unthinkable. The mountain has taken it. What is taken is kept.

## The dead

The dead are washed, wrapped in undyed linen, and carried in the slowest procession imaginable to a dying terrace on the lowest slopes, where the water is thin, barely warm, and gray. They are laid in the shallows on a bed of dry plate-eater shells. Over a season the water thickens them with stone, first a film like frost, then a crust, then a smooth white shape that is only faintly a body. The family returns at each turning of the year, brings nothing, says nothing, and sits for an hour. They call the dead *the sheeted*, and the low terraces are the most crowded place in Aun Selle, crowded with those who will never be in the way.

## What they believe

The Settled are not devout in any way an outsider would recognize, but they keep certain things.

They place shells beside the **second sky** pools, small offerings of plate-eater carapace laid in a line at the edge, never in the water. When asked what they are offering to, they say, "Whoever's looking back." When asked whether it is only refraction, they say yes, of course, and continue to lay the shell.

They do not whisper near a **listening stone**. Children learn this before they learn to read. The elders, however, do whisper, and only near spires, and only at dusk, in the way that people who have been told for a lifetime not to do a thing sometimes do it exactly once. What they say cannot be heard. No one has ever been able to say why they do it. When a spire falls, and a basin blanches in a quiet spreading ticking, the old ones do not seem surprised.

And they keep the **Whitening**, which is the closest thing to a holy day. When the crystal snow begins, everyone in every cluster goes down into the cold rooms and sits in the dark, together, in perfect silence, for however many hours it lasts. It is said that no one in Aun Selle has ever been late to a Whitening. Afterward, when the first orange creeps back into the mats, the whole town goes out and stands looking at the color returning, and there are always some who weep, and none of them make a sound.

## The rain

There is one hour each day when the Settled are loud, and it may be the truest thing about them.

When the afternoon rain comes, straight and warm and thick, drumming on the stone roofs, everyone goes out. The covered stairs empty, the mills stall, the sawyers climb out of the water. Faces go up. Mouths open. And the Settled *talk*, in full voices, hoarse and unpracticed, over the noise of the rain, shouting the things that have waited all day: jokes, quarrels, proposals, confessions, the names of the newborn. Children scream with an abandon that would frighten a stranger. Lovers say what they have been unable to say for a week. Old men sing, badly. It is nearly impossible to hear anyone more than an arm away, which is the point. For forty minutes the town is a warm crowd shouting in a warm downpour, and then the rain thins and the voices thin with it, and by the time the last drops fall, they are back to breath and palms.

## The others who live there

The Settled share the shelf, and think of themselves as one of its residents rather than its owner.

The **plate-eaters** move in herds of thousands, pale as the stone, and are so much a part of the slope that a Settled child asked to draw a hillside will draw a slow crowd of shells. They are herded, eaten in small numbers, and their carapaces tile doorways. It is considered rude to step over one.

The **veil-flies** hang above the mats in sheets of glittering silence, and the Settled walk through them with their mouths closed and their eyes half shut, as one walks through a curtain in a house one loves.

The **wading stalkers** stand in the cool pools, pearl-colored and patient, and the people and the birds, if they are birds, acknowledge each other in passing with a slowness that is almost courtesy. A Settled fisher and a stalker can stand in the same basin for an hour, twenty paces apart, neither disturbing the other.

The **sheet-lurkers** beneath the mats are never spoken of directly. If a herder loses a plate-eater to the film, they say only that it "went under." Small children are told the ripples in the color are the mountain breathing.

And in the scalding vent pools, where nothing large can live, the pale shrimp gather in their drifts and breathe in their unison, and the Settled, who have seen it all their lives, say what the legends say: that the mountain has a pulse, and that the shrimp are how it counts.

## At rest

At evening, when the amber light turns the terraces pink and the heat lets go, the Settled come up on their roofs, cup in hand, and sit. They watch the mirrors begin to flash down the slope, and the lamps answer in amber and green. Somewhere a mother is breathing a child's name. Somewhere a watcher is reading the day's last message with her pale eyes and saying nothing. Below, in the water, a stalker stands with its neck bent, waiting, and on the stone between them lies the settled hush of forty thousand people who have learned, as the mountain has, that the greatest care is taken by those who make the least disturbance.
