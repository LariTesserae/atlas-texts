# Who Keeps the Hours

You could cross the tableland for a week and believe it empty. The stilt-grazers would seem the only large lives on it, the stalker a rumor asleep on a stone dome, the veil-moths a trick of the fog. Then on the seventh morning you would notice a comb-reed field that had been combed. Not flattened or grazed, but combed, the fans drawn through something fine-toothed and left neatly damp, with the stalks standing at the same slight angle, as if the field had been tended the way hair is.

That is how the people are first found, by the evidence of their patience.

## The Hollow-Keepers

They call themselves by a word that means *the ones who are inside the dead center*, and the nearest translation is Hollow-Keepers. They live in the old cushion-mounds. When a dome has grown for two or three thousand years, its heart dies and opens into a dark, dry, round chamber, and the living green closes over the top like a lid. The people enter through a low gap on the leeward side. Inside it is dim and smells of resin and old salt. The walls are the packed brown remains of a thousand generations of rosettes, springy and warm, and the floor is fine dry grit. A single mound can shelter a family or a whole kin-line. Some mounds have been lived in for so long that the doorways are worn to the shape of shoulders.

They are a slight, long-limbed people, built for walking. Their skin is the color of the pale stone, a dusty, warm off-white that takes on a grayish sheen in the fog, and it does not burn under the hard sun, though they cover their eyes at noon with visors of woven reed-hair, slit narrow. Their hands are large for their bodies, with long, sensitive fingers whose pads are finely ridged. Their hair is fine and grows long, and they do not cut it. They comb it through with their fingers each morning, and it holds the beads of the Breath the way the silvered mounds do.

They do not build. This is not poverty or ignorance. They have the skill to build, and it is the first thing a visitor gets wrong. They will tell you, if you stay long enough to be told anything, that a thing built is a thing you must then keep from falling, and the plateau has already done its building. The one craft they have carried to great subtlety is weaving. From comb-reed filament they make nets, mantles, visors, and long trailing veils. They spread the veils on the ridgelines before dawn and gather the water that runs down into shallow cups of pressed clay. It is a modest supply. It is enough, because they ask for very little.

## How They Drink

The children are taught to drink the way the stilt-grazers do. When the fog comes over the rim, the whole band goes out onto the meadow and stands with faces lifted and arms held slightly away from the body, and they lick the beads from their own forearms and from each other's shoulders and hair. It is done in silence, and it is a kind of grooming as much as drinking. To be groomed by another is how a bond is spoken. A person who has no one to lick the fog from their back is considered to be in a grave, quiet kind of trouble, and nobody says so. Someone simply steps up beside them.

They also carry a tool for the hours when the fog is gone: a curved horn cup, lined with wax, that they hold under the leaves of the lantern-trees. Only the dead are allowed to take from the frost coins. The living wait until the sun has taken them.

## Time

Their language has no word for day. It has three words for the hours that matter: the one before the fog, the one inside it, and the one after. Everything else is called *the long glare* or *the cold*. A promise is made by saying at which of these you will return. Ask a Keeper how old she is and she will tell you how many autumns of the color wave she has walked through, and that is a wave you can feel in the feet.

They are most awake in the fog and in the dusk. The glare of midday is for sleep. Then the whole population retreats into the mounds and lies in the dim, warm hollows, the way the veil-moths do, and it is not fanciful that they say the moths are their cousins. Many of them keep a dormant moth in the wall of the chamber, pressed into a hollow of the dead rosettes like a page, and when the fog comes they open the wall and let it go. Nobody remembers when this began. If the moths were ever asked to pollinate anything, that was never the point.

## The Warning

About an hour before the Breath, something happens in the people's bodies. It comes as a tightening behind the ears, like the pressure before a change of altitude, and a faint metallic taste on the back of the tongue. They call it *the drawing-in*. Everyone feels it at once, the old and the very young, and all of them turn to face the edge before any of them could tell you why.

The Keepers do not think this is a sense of their own. They believe that the plateau is telling them. They believe that the tightening of the cushion-mounds and the tilting of the lantern-cups and the lifting of the grazers' heads are all the same signal reaching different bodies, and that the signal comes from below.

## What Is Under

There is a story they tell to the young, very slowly, in the last hour before sleep, and they tell it with their palms flat on the floor of the chamber.

Under the crust of pale salt, they say, a single living thing lies spread across the plateau's whole width, thin as a held breath and older than the mounds. It has no face and no voice. It is a mat of threads finer than the comb-reeds' fans, running through the stone's small cracks and the salt-blooms and the roots of everything green, and it holds them all together like the warp of a very large cloth. The mounds are its knots, and the lantern-trees are places where it has been pulled up into the light. It reaches down over the escarpments to the very margin, where the cloud sea begins, and it feels the cloud. It feels the rock warm before the first light, and it feels the air begin to climb. Then it tells everything else, through the ground, an hour ahead.

The Keepers call it the Tide-under, or, more often, just *the Sleeper*, and they are careful not to say whether they think it is one thing or many. They do not pray to it. What they do is closer to listening, and closer still to manners. They walk lightly on the salt-crust when they must cross it. They do not dig, and they bury their dead in the saddle soil among the lantern-trees, in shallow graves, so that whatever they were can be gathered in.

Whether the Sleeper is real is not a question the Keepers find interesting. They say the plateau keeps time, and that a people who did not keep time with it would be like a note played in the wrong place in a song, not wrong exactly, only unheard.

## The Stone Writing

The layers in the cliff faces, with their slow handwriting curves, belong to a separate office. A few of the Keepers, called *palm-readers* though they read with the fingertips, spend their lives walking the exposed strata and passing their ridged fingers over the banded stone, and they can tell, from the fineness of a layer, what sort of year it was. A thick pale band is a year of heavy Breath. A dark, thin line is a season of drought. They count backward and can go, they say, further than a hundred thousand years, though they treat the count as a courtesy rather than a fact.

They do not write any of it down. To copy the stone would be to imply that the stone was insufficient. The palm-readers memorize the record the way a river memorizes its bed, and they teach only the pattern of it, never the sum. The oldest of them can sometimes be found sitting with her hand on a curve of stone that looks, from a distance, like a word, and she is not reading it. She is only keeping it company.

## The Tone

Now and then, from the higher pillars, comes the low note, soft as a breath across a bottle. The Keepers know it and stop when they hear it. They do not turn toward the sound. They turn their backs, gently, and stand until it has passed, with their heads a little bowed, the way you would stand if you had walked in on someone in prayer. If you ask why, they say only that it is not for them. When pressed, the old ones will add that it is not for anyone, that it is something the plateau does when it is alone, and that the kindest thing you can do for an old friend is to let them think they are.

## Others

The stilt-grazers walk among them unafraid, and the Keepers walk among the stilt-grazers, at the edges of the groups, and learn from them where the fog will thicken. They do not herd or keep them. They do not hunt them. The stalker takes the young when it takes them, and the Keepers say nothing about that either. They leave a little of their water-cloth at the edge of the saddles as a courtesy, and sometimes it is gone in the morning.

The spiders they treat as fellow weavers, and they do not brush the morning webs from their paths but walk around them, so that at dawn the trails of the Hollow-Keepers curve and double back in loops that look, from a ridge, like someone thinking.

## The Last Hour

At dusk the Keepers come out of the mounds and gather on the western swells to watch the sun go down into the cloud sea. The floor of amber lights up from beneath, and the pillars throw their kilometer shadows, and the people stand with their long hair loose and their visors pushed back, silent, their faces the color of the copper mounds. Nobody speaks. Then the light thins to violet, the first cold breath comes across the salt, and they go back in, one by one, through the worn doors, to lie in the warm dead centers of the domes and wait, with their palms flat on the floor, for the drawing-in.

They are not waiting for anything they have not already had. That is the thing a visitor comes to understand, lying awake in a borrowed hollow at the edge of the sleeping meadow, with the frost feathering the roof above and the stars thick enough to cast shadow. Nothing here is being built and nothing is being lost. The people have found the one thing the plateau does, and they have arranged themselves to be present for it, morning after morning, for longer than anyone has counted.

And somewhere under the floor, under your palms, something very large and very slow turns toward the edge of the world, and feels the cloud begin to climb.
