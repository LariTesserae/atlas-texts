# The Held

The people of Ossary Reach do not call themselves anything so tidy as *people of the Reach*. They say *the held*, meaning those who are being held up, and they mean the whole arrangement: bodies, animals, dust, stone, and the tone that keeps the stone from running. If you press them, they will admit that no one is sure who is holding whom.

## Bodies

Ten thousand years of heat, damp, and pressure have made them a particular kind of animal.

They are broad through the ribs and short in the leg, built for stairs that slope wrongly and floors that shake. Their skin is slick and dark-toned, and it carries stains that are not tattoos though they are treated as such: the violet of mat-pigment on those who work the flats, the gray of dust-lung on those who tend the swarms, the pale scalded patches of the condenser-tenders, whose hands and forearms are bleached by acid drip. Nobody wears long hair. It rots. Scalps are shaved or oiled, and the vanity goes into thin wire braided through the ears and lips, which rings faintly when touched.

Their eyes have a second lid, translucent, that sweeps sideways against fog and drip, so a conversation is full of small quick blinks like the flick of a fish. Their feet are splayed, the toes long and half-prehensile, and the soles are ridged to grip wet ceramic. They walk with a slight forward tilt, as if always leaning into the sound.

Their hearing is stranger than their bodies. Everyone in the Reach is partly deaf. The roar burns out the ordinary range by the age of ten, and what is left is a narrow, scarred, extraordinarily acute hearing for the few bands that matter: the tone and its overtones, the pulse of the pumps, the pitch of a spouse's voice, the specific complaint of a crab colony about to shed. Outsiders assume the Reach's people are dull-eared. Then a woman at the far end of a market lifts her head because a lattice beam three levels up has changed its note by a fraction, and eleven hundred strangers turn with her.

## Speech Through the Walls

Voices are too weak to carry, so the Reach speaks through the structure itself.

*Wall-talk* is done with a knuckle, a ring, or a little bone striker against the lattice. It runs through the buildings faster than any runner. News of a death, a price, a slump, or a bad batch of brine crosses a district in a few minutes, and each listener adds their own inflection as it passes, so that every message arrives slightly edited, like a rumor with a pulse. Children learn it before they learn to speak. Their first word is a knock.

For anything private, there is *bone-speech*. Two people press brow to brow, or jaw to collarbone, and talk in a low hum that passes through the skull. No wall hears it. The roar does not touch it. Lovers do it, and so do conspirators, dying parents, and merchants closing a serious deal. To decline the pressing of a forehead is a heavy insult. To offer one to a stranger is to hand them your trust, and every trader knows that a lie told through bone feels different in the other person's teeth.

## Names

Nobody has an address, because addresses do not last. Everyone has a *signature*: a phrase of three to five notes, hummed or chimed, followed by a knock pattern. A baby is given the chord of its household and, at adulthood, adds a single note of its own. Marriage is a harmonizing, and the notes are adjusted until they sit right together. Disgrace is punished by the removal of a note. A person who has lost all their notes is called a *still*. It is the worst thing in the language, and there are people who live in the crevices between districts, unnamed and unhailed, whom others walk past without lowering their voices.

The dead are not buried. There is no ground to bury them in. Their bodies are given to the mollusk-farms and the glass crabs, and after a season the clean bones are cut, drilled, and tuned. Each household hangs its ancestors in a chime at the doorway. When the damp wind moves them, the family's dead are contributing to the tone in their small way, and this is understood as the last honor. A wealthy house's chime is a genealogy of a dozen generations, each grandmother a slightly different pitch. When the maintenance swarm dissolves a district and a family must move, the chime is carried out first, before the children.

## Trades

Most of what the held do is sound-work or water-work, and the two overlap.

**Beat-children.** Every household with a child under twelve owes the block a share of hours. At fixed times the children take their resonant tubes to the walls and beat the block's assigned rhythm, a patient, syncopated thing, each in their own alcove. Small drummers are the city's most precious labor, and the foremen of districts know each child by pitch. Where children are scarce, the block sags.

**Drift-ears.** These are the listeners, born or trained, who can feel a change in the tone before it is a change. They walk the terraces with sounding rods pressed to their jaws, tapping, listening, tapping. They are consulted before any construction, any marriage of households, any large gathering. Most go deaf to everything else by middle age and are cared for by their blocks with real tenderness. They are quiet people. They stand very still in a place where nothing stands still, and it unnerves visitors.

**Flicker-readers.** The glass crabs cling in millions to every drip surface, and their shell-lights answer sound, pulsing in patterns that may carry meaning along the lattice or may be only the ghost of it. The readers sit at the edges of drip curtains for hours, murmuring, and sell their interpretations at the price of a meal. Some are frauds. A few are not, and they are the ones who cannot stop shaking when they leave the wall.

**Dust-wranglers.** The gray maintenance swarms that eat old stone and lay down new are neither wild nor tame. The wranglers work with them by leaving offerings of broken ceramic in particular places and humming particular intervals, and by never, ever, standing in the wrong doorway on the wrong day. They breathe swarm-dust all their lives, and their voices go grainy and their fingertips gray. When they die the dust is said to take them in, and their bones do not go into any chime.

**Sump-drowners.** Below the market crust, in the flooded galleries, the eels rise at dusk in glittering ropes, and there are families who have lived among them for so long that they hold their breath for eight minutes, sleep in shallow air-pockets, and speak a clicking, pressure-borne dialect that the upper city cannot follow. They bring up eels, mud-iron, and old machine parts that gleam strangely. They trade at the grates and go back down.

**Lightning-harvesters.** Rooftop crews climb, in the hour before storm cells break, to string copper vanes on the highest ceramic. They are bold, paid well, and die young. Their signature is always a single high note, and their households mourn quickly, because there is always a new one coming up the stairs.

## The Edge People

At the shoreline, where the mud is blood-warm and the mats hum in the sun, live the *flat-folk*, and the city is ambivalent about them.

They are thinner than the held, with long legs for the silt and eyes shaded to the glare. They harvest the mats in slow sheets, cutting and rolling them like bolts of cloth for the upper markets, where the slime is pressed, fermented, and eaten. They speak little, in soft voices, because the mats respond to loud sound by clenching, and a clenched mat is dead food. They alone in the Reach live where the roar is a distant weather rather than a substance, and they are the only ones who have seen the city whole from outside, a single dark mass on the shining plain.

The held call the flat-folk *quiet cousins* and mean it half as affection and half as a warning. When the tone wavers, it is the flat-folk who feel the vibration first, through the soles of their feet, through the mud. They send the news in by runner and by mat-drum, a slow thump on stretched slime, and a drift-ear will stop mid-sentence to listen. Neither side quite trusts the other. Neither can do without them.

## The Others

Not everything that lives here is an animal or a person, and the city does not insist on the distinction.

The **swarm** is the strangest resident. It is a gray, fine, sifting presence that moves through walls and eats and builds. People speak of it the way they speak of a large, slow, useful relative who has never entirely been understood: with courtesy, with small gifts, with the assumption that it has moods. It follows the very old, some say, to lay a soft place for them when they lie down. It avoids certain notes. It has been known to take a whole staircase away in the night and return it, changed, a week later with a rail on the other side.

The **tube-nesters** are the true chorus. They are hairless and pink, and they live in the honeycomb vents in flocks of hundreds, calling in bursts that the held have taught them, over generations, to time to the tone. Whether the birds understand what they are doing is a matter of debate, and the wrong answer starts fights. Most households keep one or two in a cage by the door, less as pets than as small, screaming reminders that everyone contributes.

At the very top, in the newest and glassiest lattice, where the structure is still being laid and may never be finished, there are said to live the **glassed**. No one agrees on what they are. Some say they are the old builders, or what became of them. Others say they are the ones who went up to listen too long and did not come back down: still, translucent, faintly humming, folded into the stone. Sometimes a drift-ear will return from the upper terraces with a new note in their voice and no explanation, and be treated afterward with a careful, frightened reverence. The tone, they say, is not entirely a machine. It is at least partly a voice. It has at least partly a preference.

## The Low Hour

Once in each night, deep in the dark, the pumps of the great drainage arteries fall out of step. It is a known thing, a property of their cycles. For a few minutes the thudding thins, the rain-hiss softens, and the tone, which has never depended on any single source, thins with them. This is the Low Hour, when the city is most likely to slump.

Everyone wakes. Nobody is told to. Children stop sleeping and sit up. The old lift their heads. In every alcove and doorway and stall, ten million throats begin to hum, not loudly and not together, only steadily, each at their own pitch. The sound builds under the roar like a second floor laid beneath the first. The glass crabs answer, flickering. The tube-nesters call. The chimes ring with the ancestors' bones. For a few minutes the whole Reach is a single held breath let out.

Then the pumps find their step again, the thudding thickens, the tone firms, and the humming stops in the same slow, ragged way it began. The held lie down and are asleep within moments. In the morning nobody mentions it, because there is nothing to mention. It is simply what one does, and the city is still standing.
