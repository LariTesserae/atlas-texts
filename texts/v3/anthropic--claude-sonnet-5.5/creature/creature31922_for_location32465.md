# Who Keeps the Hall

Nothing walks here, and yet the place is inhabited. The residents are slow, or very fast, or not quite alive, and none of them would call the others neighbors.

## The Felt

The obvious one is the moss in the outfall gallery, the door-sized patch that looks painted on. It is a spring-moss, the kind that belongs beside cold, lime-rich seeps, and it found the one wall in the mountain that imitates a seep. It has no roots, only pale anchoring threads, so it drinks through its whole body and takes the spray as a skin takes rain. Each shoot is a tiny upright fir, and together they make a pile so dense that the water doesn't run through it but is held in it, a green sponge that is always slightly heavier than it looks.

It has learned the day. For twenty minutes at noon the light-shaft delivers real sun, and the moss does most of its year's earning in that window. The rest of the hours it lives on the pale leak of daylight bouncing down the shaft and on the amber of the sodium lamps, whose color it takes without gratitude and uses badly, like a tenant living on a neighbor's porch light. It grows a few millimeters a season. It has no interest in the rainbow that stands over it, though the rainbow is made of the same water it drinks.

The moss is also being buried, and this is the truest fact about it. The spray is loaded with dissolved lime, and every wet day the moss pulls carbon dioxide from the water around its leaves and lets a little of that lime fall out as crust. The lower stems are already sealed in pale grit. The moss climbs upward out of its own tomb, living in the top centimeter of a wall that is turning slowly to stone underneath it. This is how tufa begins, and it takes centuries. The moss doesn't know it is founding a rock. It only knows that the bottom is dark and the top is bright, and it goes up.

## The Crusts

Around the moss's edge, where the spray thins to a mist, the lichens have set their borders. Orange first, a scabby disc the color of a dying ember, then a black crust like spilled tar in the zone the splash reaches every hour and the dry never does. The black one is an aquatic lichen, a thing that breathes water and pauses in air, and it has the calm of an organism that has never once been in a hurry. Each disc widens by less than the thickness of a fingernail in a year. Some of the discs on the wall are older than the station's paint.

Beside them are the threads of pale grass in their drift of silt. Three or four blades. They are less a plant than an argument that a plant might work here, and the argument is being made in good faith, one blade at a time, against the wind of the flushes.

## The Slime

The larger population is one you would never see as a population. In the sumps, along the gutters of the grating, and in the brown ribbons that stream in the tailrace shallows, a film lives on the iron. It is a community of bacteria that eat the dissolved metal of the penstocks, the same metal that is rusting in the pipe above. They spin it out behind them as rust-colored filaments and stalks, so that the slime looks like wet orange wool and feels, if you touch it, like nothing at all. There are more of them in one sump than there are people in the valley below. They have no faces, no seasons, and no complaint about the noise. They are, in a sense, the station's other maintenance crew, taking the pipe apart at a pace the pipe can afford.

Fine threads of fungus work through the wet insulation of the cable trays, and a dull green algal bloom rides the weeping seams under the lamps at the third rib. The hall is one of the few places on the mountain where these can live all year, and all of them are there.

## The Attending

Then there are the ones that are not alive but are not idle.

The governors on the turbines watch the frequency of the grid the way a rider watches a horse's ears. When the valley switches on its evening lights, the governors feel the load drag at the shafts and open the wicket gates a little, so that more water can lean on the blades. They are a kind of continuous attention, and they answer in milliseconds, hundreds of times an hour, without ever having been surprised. The float switches in the sumps are the same, and so are the pressure sensors on the penstocks, the temperature probes in the windings, and the small, faithful lamp behind the grille that blinks its slow fault. Together they hold a shape of watchfulness over the hall. If you wanted a name for the thing that lives here, you might give it to this: not a mind, but a habit of noticing that has been built into iron and copper and left running.

It has moods that are only physics. It sleeps in the small hours when the melt is low and one turbine winds down, and the whole room drops a register. It rouses at dusk. When the spillway gates open and the tailrace goes white, it gets something like a fever, a great doubling of everything, and then it settles.

## The Visitors

Twice a month, or less, a person comes down the ninety steps. They wear ear defenders and a headlamp, and they walk the catwalks reading gauges the sensors have already read. They are not residents. To the hall they are a kind of weather, a warm, brief, upright disturbance that leaves boot prints in the lime dust and takes them away again on the next flush. They touch the moss once, sometimes, and are surprised by how cold and heavy it is. They do not see the rainbow, because they come in the morning.

## What Lives Here

If you ask what lives in the Machine Hall at Kessel Notch, the answer is not one thing. It is a slow green felt turning itself into rock. It is a black crust older than the paint. It is a wool of iron-eating cells, an attention of governors and switches, and a scatter of ice that grows at night and falls at dawn. It is a river that stays only long enough to be used.

None of them own the room. The moss will be stone, the stone will be mountain, and the governors will one day be listening to a silence. But for now they all keep house together in the amber dark, in a noise so large that it has become a kind of quiet, and each of them, in its own time, is doing exactly what it was made to do.
