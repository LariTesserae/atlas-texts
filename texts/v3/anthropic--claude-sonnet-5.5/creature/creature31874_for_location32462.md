# Who Keeps the Marrow Basin

Start with the ones you can count, and understand that the counting will fail.

## The Many

The residents are mostly small, and mostly they are the reason the place has a texture at all. The beetles in armored ranks on the bark, the ant columns running two ways along the same rope of liana, the thumb-sized roaches, and the caterpillars whose grazing becomes the floor's whisper are not background. They are the basin's actual population, and the larger animals are guests who happen to be visible.

Above them come the patient ones. Spiders hold their tiers of web at the exact height where the drizzle beads. Mantises wait on the same leaf for three nights. Frogs sit on leaf-edges with slack throats, saving their voices for the rain. There is a snake on the branch, a bird asleep above the snake, and above the bird the damp-furred mammal that has hardly moved since dusk and will hardly move before dawn. They are not neighbors in any sense they would recognize. They are stacked, like sediment.

Then come the heavier tenants: the tapir-like grazers standing to their bellies in the shallows, half asleep and chewing slowly. The cats work the levee ridges, so quiet they seem to be a change in the dark rather than something moving through it. The monkeys sleep in knots, tails wound through other tails, so that a troop looks like one many-limbed animal with a single slow pulse. In the river the large fish turn, and one rolls and sets itself down on the surface like a sack on wet ground.

All of this is the forest's flesh. But the description that says the forest is "one enormous animal made of many" is half right. An animal has something that keeps it, and here that is a few dozen people.

## The Few

Nobody built anything here, and this is the first thing to understand about them. They have no roofs and no walls, no fire pit, and no cleared ground. They sleep in the crooks of buttress roots, in hammocks twisted from liana bast that they take down each morning and hang in the crowns of saplings to dry, so that by day the basin shows no sign of them at all. They are perhaps forty in number and are seldom together. When you finally see one, you will already have been looked at for some time.

They are lean and long in the forearm, with skin the color of the iron soil and a permanent sheen from the wet air. Their eyes are wide, the pupils opened nearly to the rim, and they move the way the sloth moves: not from weakness, but because in this heat any haste is a debt. They breathe slowly. When one sits against a trunk in the dark, it takes a minute of watching to see the chest move, and even then you may be watching the trunk.

They are descended, if you follow it far enough, from a work crew. The rusted rails belong to them in the way an old family axe does. Something came in from the eastern coast, laid track toward a place that no longer needs it, and abandoned the effort and the people who were laying it. The people stayed because the basin was survivable and the coast was forty kilometers of mud and river away. That was long enough ago that the story has been worn as smooth as the cobbles in the cut bank. They say that a line was once meant to be finished. They do not say by whom.

## What They Do

Mostly they attend. Their labor is a kind of upkeep by noticing.

They know which oxbows are silting and roughly how many decades they have left. They keep, in memory and in a shared grammar of knots tied into cord, the slow accounting of the place: which giant has died standing and how many years it has been dying, which gap is in its second decade of sapling contest, and where the river is thinking of starting its next loop. Nothing is written. The cords hang in the crowns beside the drying hammocks, and you would mistake them for more liana.

They eat what the forest lets go of: fruit past its first ripeness, fish that the river slows enough to take by hand, grubs from rotting trunks, the soft palm hearts of fallen palms. They take from the fall, not from the standing. It is a rule so old it has no reason attached, and it holds. They never hunt the sleeping cats and never disturb the bird above the snake.

They keep no light. A torch would draw the moths into a storm and blind the eyes they have spent generations opening. They move by touch and by the pressure of the air, and by a habit of breathing that lets them feel a bat pass a hand's width from the cheek and not flinch.

## The Boiler

By the river lies the cylindrical boiler, so grown over that it passes for a fallen log. The oldest of them, whom the others do not call by any name but simply go to, sits with her back against its curve most nights. She is not guarding it. She listens to it. The metal takes up the river's temperature, the small shifts of the mud, and the far machine hum from the east, and in its hollow it makes a faint answering note that no one else hears.

When someone dies, and only then, the oldest one knocks on the boiler once. It rings. The sound goes out over the water and into the trunks and is absorbed at once, as everything is absorbed here, by the wet, the leaves, and the crowded dark. For a moment every frog and bat and sleeping thing in the nearest hundred meters is a little more still. Then the drip resumes.

## The Glow

They know about the orange stain on the underside of the clouds, and they do not fear it. They call it the Other Shore, though they have never seen a shore. To them it is one more weather, like the rising of the river, and it makes a certain kind of sense that the world has an edge where something is always burning. Some of the younger ones walk the rails as far as the rails stay above the mud, to the place where the iron sinks into root and disappears. They stand there a while, looking toward the light. Then they turn around. Nobody has decided that this is a rule. It is just what is done.

## Toward Morning

If you sat quietly enough, and long enough, and let your eyes give up on seeing, you would begin to feel the arrangement. The beetle on the bark, the snake on the branch, the cat on the levee, the woman against the boiler: all of it a single held breath, in and out, with the drip for its pulse. The people are not its masters or its guests. They are more like its attention, the slow, low awareness of a body that has never needed to be startled.

The night goes on. The heat does not leave. A branch gives way somewhere with a long unhurried crack, and a soft crash follows. Against a buttress, a figure that you took for root shifts its weight by the width of a thumb, and settles, and keeps listening.
