# Who Lives in the Bright Pocket

Most of what lives here is too small to be called anyone. It is a population, a slow agreement among billions, and it covers more of the room than the eye admits. You have to learn to see it.

## The Mat-Makers

Start with the color. The rust near the pool is iron: a community of cells that feed on the dissolved metal in the seep and leave it oxidized behind them, so the orange is partly living and partly what the living have excreted. The mustard yellow beside it is sulfur, the same struck-match breath that rises from the flood. Something eats that smell and builds itself out of it. The cream and gray farther out are the thrifty ones, the cells that live on very little in cooler, poorer water. And at the seep's edge, where the water first comes up hot and unspent and full of everything, the green-blue band is the richest and least restrained of them, a crowd so dense it has a sheen.

None of them are eating the light. This is the odd thing about the room, and they seem indifferent to it. They are the children of heat and stone and dissolved mineral, and they would have done the same in the dark. Yet the swirled thickening toward the blooms is real. Perhaps the cool under a bloom is gentler on them, or the drips fall differently there. Perhaps a few among them, some drifting lineage newly arrived, have begun to use it. If so, that lineage is very young and very small, and it is the only one in the chamber facing a new possibility. No one has told the rest.

They live in layers, like a book with the pages pressed together. On top are the ones that tolerate the flood and the rain. Beneath them a cooler, slower stratum lives on their wastes. Below that, against the rock itself, is a thin dark seam of cells that eat the stone. When the glaze flakes and exposes bare basalt, the pink that colonizes it in a week is the first pioneer, a small opportunistic kind that grows fast, dies fast, and prepares the surface for the layered ones that follow. The pink is always the first to arrive and never the last to stay. It is the room's habit of beginning again.

Death here is constant and invisible. Patches sour and slough off in the flood, and the beads eat the loose fragments. What the mat loses in a day it regains in three. Nothing in it grieves the loss.

## The Pale Threads

The fungal hairs live in the cracks where the heat softens and the dark is deep enough to suit them. They are not one animal or one plant but a tangle of many single strands, each a chain of cells laid end to end and sleeved in clear jelly. They have no eyes and nothing like a mind, but they lean. A thread that has been struck by a drop will grow, over the next hour, a little farther toward the place where the drops tend to fall, because the drops carry mineral and the mineral is food. Whole clumps have arranged themselves this way, so a tangle of them beneath a weeping crack all points its tips, like a small congregation, at the same spot on the ceiling.

The lifting in the steam is not breathing, though it looks like it. The wet air thickens the jelly and buoys the strands. When the steam recedes the tangle settles and combs itself flat.

Beads live inside the tangle when the flood is high. Striders never do. A thread-thicket is a good place to die.

## The Beads

Say what you can of an animal you cannot easily look at. The beads are the size of a rice grain, the color of old candle wax, and armored in a plate that clicks faintly when they collide. They have no eyes but they are not blind. They have a sense along the whole margin of the body that reads warmth and the shiver of water, so that a bead knows the flood is coming a full minute before the pool has visibly risen. Then the whole population, grazing across a hand's breadth of mat, stops. Each anchors by a pair of hooked hind legs. The sheet arrives, the beads hold, and it passes over them like a fast river over a stone. When it drains they let go, shake, and resume where they left off.

They do not seem to sleep. They do cluster, in the cool at the edge of a bloom, in knots of a dozen or more. This is not a hunt or a mating. It is closer to the way people gather at a fire. They lie with their plates touching in the clean white light, and if you watch a knot for long enough, you will see the individuals at its center drift outward and those on the rim drift in, a slow turning, as if the group were one animal stirring in its sleep.

Beads are born in the mat, from clutches laid in its thickest, warmest layer. The young are barely visible. A few of them are eaten by their own parents.

## The Striders

There are seven, or nine. Nobody has counted them twice and gotten the same answer.

The strider is the closest thing in the room to a person. It has long legs and a small body and a way of stopping. In the bloom it freezes, all its legs braced, its body low, for as long as a minute. It seems to know that in the glare it is exposed, and in the dark it is not, and that the worm cannot be seen in the light but can be felt in the dark. So it makes its dashes through shadow and holds still in brightness. This is a strange inversion, the light as the place of listening and the dark as the place of running, and it is a thing the strider has learned without teachers, only through the lives of striders who did not.

They drink at the pool's edge. They eat beads, and they eat the drowned, meaning whatever the flood carries out of the mat and abandons on the surface film: the dead, the dying, the unlucky, the sluggish. They are scavengers first and hunters second. When a strider does hunt, it takes a bead by folding it into the space between two forelegs and then holds it quite still for a long time, as if listening to it.

One of them has a missing foreleg, and has had for as long as anyone has watched. It moves differently, favoring the left, and it has a habit of stopping a half-second longer than the others. It has survived the worm at least once. It may be the oldest.

## The Worm

It has a name for no one, and it needs none. It lives in a crack near the south wall, in a place where the warm water passes just below its body, and it has lived there long enough that the stone at the crack's lip is polished by its coming and going. It is clear as gelatin, with a faint gray thread of gut visible along its length and a slight opacity at the head where the muscle thickens. It does not hunt in any ordinary sense. It waits. It has waited through hundreds of floods.

It feeds on beads mostly, drawn in by the hundred with a drawing motion of the mouth, and on striders perhaps once in a season. The strikes you see are the rare ones. Most of its living is dull and grazing and slow.

It does not know that it is feared. There is no worm-mind for that. But the whole floor of the room is arranged, in its geography of hesitation, around the crack where it waits, and the striders' paths bend in a wide arc around it, and the beads thin out toward it and then thicken again beyond. It shapes the room the way a boulder shapes a stream.

## What Lives Below

Under the basin, in the seep's throat, are the things that started it all. Nobody has seen them and the room does not need them seen. They are the hot, deep, sightless cells that live in water that has never met air, and they feed the whole chamber upward. Everything orange, yellow, green-blue, and pink is their descendant, or their guest. Everything that grazes on the mat is eating them at one remove. When the seep builds, it is bringing them a little way up into the room, and the room takes what it can.

## The Light, and Those Who Live With It

None of them worship it, and none of them use it. But every creature here has a relation to it, and the relation is exact. The beads rest in it. The striders freeze in it. The threads avoid it, retreating from its edges as if from a draft. The mats lean, faintly, toward it, whether from need or from something newer. The worm, translucent, disappears in it. To each of them the light is as ordinary as a change in the wind, something they meet and answer without asking where it comes from.

They are all firsts. There are no old lineages here, no ancient adaptations. Every creature in the room is a colonist or the grandchild of one, arrived by crack or water or the drift of spores from a world above, and each is still working out what this new place will allow. The rock is young and the life on it is younger. Somewhere in the mats, a lineage is learning to use the light. Somewhere in the pale threads, a strand has begun to lean toward the humming. And in the polished crack by the south wall, the worm waits, and the seep builds, and the beads cling and let go.
