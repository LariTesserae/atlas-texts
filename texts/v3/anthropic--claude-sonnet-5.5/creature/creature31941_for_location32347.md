# Those Who Keep the Doors

## The Keepers

Their bodies are the pale of the inside of a shell. The mantle is thin enough that in the dimmest phase of the glow you can see the darker organs moving in it, slow as silt turning in a jar. There is no bone in them anywhere but the beak. Each has eight or nine limbs, and "nine" is often a matter of one limb never having finished growing, or having been lost to a crab or a collapse and grown back thinner. The keepers do not count each other's arms. A keeper with a small ninth arm is simply that keeper.

The eyes are large, set wide, and silver-lidded, with a horizontal slot of pupil that opens almost to a circle in the dark. They are useless for anything so crude as looking at a shape. They are made for the color that runs down an arm, and for the slight difference between the green of one bacterial film and the blue of another. A keeper reads a wall the way a person reads weather.

But the real sense is in the fingertips. Each limb ends in a pad of tiny cups, and each cup tastes. A keeper drawing a hand across a lintel knows the age of the lime, how much water has passed through it, whether the stone beneath is still cobble or has become only the idea of cobble, and whether someone has been there before. It can tell the touch of its own repair from its mother's, thirty years old and softened under new crust. A keeper who cannot find a door in the dark has never lived. Its hands go to the door as water goes downhill.

The beak is horn-black and notched along its lower edge, and the notches are a sort of ruler. Every keeper is born with the same pattern, but wear and use alter it, and the old ones carry it like a signature. A keeper checks a doorway by setting its beak against the lip and feeling how many notches of clearance remain. The town has no numbers and no need of them. A door is "two notches" or "four notches" wide, and when it falls to one, it is time.

## The Work

The work is not heroic, and the keepers do not treat it as heroic. A keeper wakes, if that word applies, when the glow of the nearest wall brightens toward the warm phase of its slow day. It unfolds from its house and goes along its lane, the stretch of doors that came to it from someone before, touching each. Most days nothing needs doing. It lays a limb on the lintel and receives the news: still open, still open, still open. This too is the work. A door has to be checked more often than it has to be cleared, or the clearing comes too late.

When there is scraping to do, it is done in very small amounts. The keeper braces four limbs on the wall and works with the other four, shell scraper in one, pumice in another, a wet cord in a third to draw away the powder, and the fourth simply resting on the stone to feel. The lime comes away in a fine pale smoke that the current takes. The keeper takes off only what is needed. To clear a door wider than it was ever made would be its own kind of damage, and the keepers seem to know that the town is a body, not a ruin to be restored. They are not reversing the sealing. They are negotiating with it, door by door, for a little more time.

Resetting a cobble is the slowest labor. A round stone has been shifted, perhaps by a current, perhaps by a step, and the paving around it has begun to lean. The keeper studies it for days. It probes the gap, tastes the lime that has filled it, and works a bone wedge in along the line where the stone will want to go. Then it waits, and the lime does part of the job. A stone set in autumn may not be settled by spring. The keeper returns and touches it, and touches it, until it is a stone that has always been there.

## The Chord

The keepers do not know they are musicians, and they may be. The town hums one long chord through its hollow houses, each doorway a different pipe. When a keeper clears a lintel, the pipe changes. The note it sounds lifts by some tiny fraction, and the whole chord shifts with it.

The oldest keepers navigate by this. In their private dark, with the glow at its dimmest, they will stop in a lane, tilt the body, and go still with a limb flat to the stone. They are not reading the wall. They are listening to the chord through the wall, and they know which pipe is narrowing because its voice has gone thin. They go to it. Afterward the chord is a little fuller, and something in the color of the keeper's arms, some faint slow shading, seems to say that this is what it wanted.

Whether they mean to tune the town or only find that the town rewards this, no one can say. The chord has never resolved, and the keepers do not seem to want it to.

## Touch and Color

When two keepers pass in a lane, they stop. One lays a limb across the other's, and a ripple of color runs the length of the arm, from shoulder to tip, and back. It takes several seconds. In that time there is passed, in whatever tongue this is, an account of doors: which are open, which are near to closing, which lamp is running low. There is also something else, harder to name, which is closer to *I am still here* and *so am I*. The color is a pale rose and a green like the inside of a leaf they have never seen, and it travels slower than any speech.

The young are taught with it. A juvenile the size of a fist rides on an elder's back for its first years, holding to the mantle with hooked fingertips, and the elder lays a limb over the young one's small arm and *moves* it. Scraping, resetting, waiting. The color runs with the motion, over and over, as a pattern of hand-shapes and gentle tones, so that in time the young one is not learning a skill but growing into one. When it is old enough to work alone it will still, on quiet days, come back to the elder's lane and lie a limb against a wall the elder has just cleared.

## The Lamps

The lamp-creatures are kept as an inheritance. Each house has its lineage of them, jelly-bodied and thumb-sized, pulsing at the pace of a slow heart, and a keeper is as attached to them as to any of its tools. They are fed on crumbs of shrimp. They are carried in a cupped fingertip from door to door when a keeper's work takes it out of its lane. A borrowed lamp is a courtesy, and returning it with a bit of food is the closest thing the keepers have to manners.

When a keeper dies in its house, the lamps outlast it. They go on pulsing in the dark long after the last arm has stopped, and the lime slowly fills in the doorway, the glow dimming through a thinner and thinner aperture, until at last it is a green ember seen through an eyelid and then nothing. The other keepers, passing, do not open the door. They lay a limb on the smooth new wall, and the color runs down.

## The Crowds

The keepers are not alone, and they do not want to be.

The **shrimp** are the town's static. They live in the cracks in such numbers that a keeper's every scrape starts a small storm of scattering, and they return in minutes to the disturbed place, picking at the fine lime powder for what grows in it. The keepers do not eat them so much as *harvest* them, carefully, a few at a time, in a way that leaves the crowd undiminished. They are, one suspects, more a herd than a prey.

The **coin-snails** graze the glowing films, leaving their clean spirals behind. Where the bacteria have been grazed the glow dims, and where they have been left they flare, so the walls are a slow map of what has been eaten. The keepers watch the pattern and encourage it here and there, moving a snail from a dull wall to a bright one, as one might move a goat.

The **eyeless fish** hang head-down in the doorways, chin whiskers trailing. They are listening. The chord passes through them, and they turn slightly with its changes, as if being tuned. A fish will hold the same doorway for its whole life, and when it dies the keepers do not clear it away at once. It becomes a small white ornament of bone in the lintel, until the lime takes it too.

The **tube worms** stand in tufts along the vent-seams, white and swaying together. The keepers seldom touch them. They are the warmest place in the town, and the young sometimes cluster near them the way children sit near a hearth.

The **crabs** are the strangest and the most comic. They wear the town: a chip of shell, a bead of clay, a curl of bone. Some have worn one piece so long that the lime has grown over it and it has become a part of them, more mortar than animal, slow, heavy, and unbothered. One old crab sits in the mouth of a certain lane, so encrusted that a stranger would take it for a bollard. The keepers pass it and lay a limb across it, and it lifts one claw a fraction of an inch. Whether this counts as an answer, no one asks.

## The Ones Who Are Not Animals

And under all of it, and in all of it, are the films: the bacteria that make the light and the food and, in a sense, the town. They are the true population, uncounted, uncomplaining. They glow because glowing is what they do. They are grazed and regrow. They breathe the vent-chemistry and turn it into the first small sweetness from which everything else draws. In the warm season they brighten, and the whole town seems briefly to remember that it once had a sky. Then they dim, and the work resumes.

## What They Are Doing

It would be easy to say the keepers are losing. The doors are closing, the houses fusing, the lanes narrowing to seams. In a hundred thousand years the last chamber will seal. But the keepers do not carry that arithmetic. They carry the door in front of them, and the hand on the lintel, and the weight of a cobble that wants to lean. They have inherited a lane and will leave it a little better cleared than they found it. That is the whole of their faith, and it seems to be enough.

In the dimmest hour, when the glow is at its faintest and the chord holds one long low note, you might find one of them sitting in a doorway, arms drawn in, lamp pulsing at its side, doing nothing at all. Not resting, exactly. Listening to the pipe of its house, which it has kept open, and which is singing.
