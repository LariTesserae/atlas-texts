# Those Who Live in the Braid

## The people

They have no word for themselves that can be said aloud. Their name is a hand shape: the right hand flat and tilted like a bank about to slump, the left hand crossing under it with the fingers spread, a braid of water. Outsiders rendering it into speech usually say *the Braided*, and the Braided tolerate this the way one tolerates a stranger mispronouncing a child's name. What matters is that it cannot be shouted. It has to be shown, or pressed into the skin of the back with two fingers, where the sense of it comes through the ribs.

They are a compact, broad-footed people, built low. The feet are the striking thing: wide, splayed, and calloused to the texture of boot leather, with toes that grip. They walk with a slight forward tilt, weight coming down slowly, heel then ball, reading the ground through the sole before committing to it. Most go barefoot on the gravel and wear felted moss-and-wool overboots only in the deepest bog. Their skin is oiled with rendered fish fat and looks dark and wet, and they wear coats of sealskin and waxed hide over layers of sedge-fiber felt, sleeves and hoods cut close because loose cloth in the fog only becomes a sponge. The hoods have flaps that fold down over the ears. Nobody wears them for warmth. Old people wear them all the time, because after enough winters of the chorus the ears go quiet and stay quiet, and the flaps are a courtesy that means: *I am already listening with something else.*

They are perhaps a few hundred in all, though no one counts the whole. They live in bands of eight to thirty, scattered across the plain in stilt-huts of turf and driftwood, and the bands shift as the ground shifts. A hut is expected to last a season, sometimes less. Nobody grieves a hut. They grieve boats, dogs, and the particular cairn-lines that a dead person walked.

### How they speak

Hand-talk is the main language, and it is deep, fast, and mostly unlike anything the outside world would call gesture. It uses the whole upper body. The eyebrows carry grammar. Distance is shown by how far the hands move from the chest, and *time* is shown by which way the palms tilt, forward for what is coming and back for what has already washed out. There are hundreds of signs for water alone: water undercutting, water rising quietly, water that has just changed its mind, water that is lying about being shallow. Their words for ground are as numerous. A child learns them before learning to walk, because a child on a shoulder is a child who watches hands.

At close range there is a second language, spoken through touch. A palm on the sternum, a knuckle drawn down the spine, two fingers tapped on the collarbone in a rhythm. Lovers use it. Mothers use it on infants in the night, in the roar. A stranger who is allowed to be touched that way has been granted something.

When they do use their voices, they press their mouths against the bone behind another's ear and speak in a low, flat, buzzing register that carries through the skull. It sounds like nothing, and it can be heard perfectly. They sing this way, too: a chest-deep hum that only the person leaning against you can hear, so that a song is always a private thing, shared between two heads.

### What they do

Their days are shaped by four kinds of work, and most people do all four in different seasons.

**Stake-walking.** The marking of safe ground is the central labor of Braided life. Every band keeps stake-walkers, mostly the young and long-legged, who go out ahead with jointed willow poles and probe the moss and the silt for hollow places, vole-tunnels, and soft banks. Where the ground holds they drive a stake with a strip of colored felt. Where it is tricky they build a cairn. Where it is deadly they string a rope across it with knotted warnings. The knots are a script. A stake-walker can read a rope in the dark with her fingers, and know which river cut it, how long since, and whether anyone has tested the far bank. The ropes are not permanent. They are re-knotted after every shift, and no map of the Braid survives more than a few weeks.

**Pylon-watching.** Each band keeps a watcher who spends part of each day with a palm flat against the nearest standing pylon, reading the hum. It is quiet, unglamorous work, and a good watcher is treasured more than a good hunter. The watchers know the difference between the hum of *warming*, the hum of *waking*, and the particular rising, pulling, sideways hum that means the plain is about to move water. They do not shout it. They stand, and turn, and lift an arm in a slow arc, and every person who sees it drops what they are doing and goes for the high ground.

**Weir-keeping and hunting.** They fish the silver runs with woven willow weirs and long-handled dip nets, and take seals from the bars when the seals are fat. They hunt elk sparingly, usually only the old bulls, and never in the warm basins, which are held to be no one's ground. They gather eggs from the sedge-birds by feel, reaching into the dark hollows of tussocks, and they eat frog only in famine, because the frogs are the chorus and the chorus is somehow the plain's own breath.

**Light-keeping.** Their lamps are alive. They carry glow-wood, blocks of rotting log riddled with cold blue fungus, in cages of woven willow, and they keep lamp-beetles in small horn boxes with a bit of moss, coaxing a pulse from them with a finger tap. A hut lit by beetle light is a hut full of slow blue heartbeats. It is considered rude to take a beetle out of its box during a conversation, since the flashing is distracting and hand-talk needs steady light.

### What they believe

They do not pray to the machines, and the suggestion offends them a little. It would be like praying to the river. The machines are simply the largest fact of the household, an enormous old animal that sleeps under the floor and turns over sometimes. You do not worship it. You learn its moods, you keep your children off its ribs when it stretches, and you remain, always, slightly awed that it lets you stay.

Their stories go back, at most, to the time of the ice-going, which they call *the Letting*. Before that, they say, nothing was theirs, and they came from the north-of-the-north or the south-of-the-sea; the tellings disagree. What they agree on is that the ground was warm when they arrived, that the warmth was not an accident, and that the good response to a gift you don't understand is to keep the ground clear for it. So they do not dig deep. They do not cut the seams, even where a cut would be easy. Every child is told, with one hand on the ground: *it is awake, it is not for us, and it is kind.*

Death is quiet. The body is bound in sedge and lowered into a kettle pond, weighted, and the band stands on the rim in silence, though of course it is all silence to them and all noise to everyone else. Afterward the stake-walkers set a marker of felt in the dead person's favorite colors on a place that will not last. They do not return to it. That is the point.

## The keeper of the tower

In the eastern braid, on a hummock over the buried floors of an older structure, one person lives alone. Nobody in the plain remembers a time before the smoke rose from that door, though this is a short memory, a dozen winters at most. The people call her *the Listener*, or, in hand-talk, a sign that means *ear turned all the way in*.

She is old, or she looks it, with skin gone the grey-brown of the tower's inner wall and hair clipped very short. She wears almost nothing in the chamber, because the chamber is warm the way a body is warm. Her hands are long and unnaturally still. When she talks, the signs are slow, and there are gaps between them, as though each one had to travel a distance to reach her fingers.

She fishes from the tower's lower ledge with hanging nets, and the nets in the chamber ceiling are not for fish. They are strung with bones, shells, twists of fiber, small stones on threads, and they move, always, in some current no one else can feel. She reads them. The hum of the tower passes through the hanging things and disturbs them differently each hour, and from their patterns she says she can tell what the seams below are about to do, days ahead of the pylon-watchers. Bands come to her before the season turns. They bring smoked fish and dried berries, and sit with their backs against the warm wall, and she tells them where the big shift will come.

She is right often enough that the plain leaves her alone. What no one says aloud is that she seems to have been *changed* by the hum. Some who have sat with her swear that she answers before they finish signing. Some say her breathing falls into the slow throb of the overlapping fields, and that if you lay a hand on her back you can feel the tower in her, as though she were one more structure grown into the silt. She herself gives no account. If asked whether she is afraid, she makes a sign that means *the plain is not something one has a fear about*, and turns back to the nets.

## The others

**The chorus.** The frogs are the true population of the Braid, and the people know it. They number in the tens of millions, dark and thumb-sized, with throat sacs that swell in the cold glow like little lanterns of skin. They are not stupid. Their calling is a coordinated thing, timed in waves and gaps, and it has a shape that shifts with the hum of the pylons, tightening when the plain warms and loosening when it cools. The Braided watch frogs as they watch the water. When the whole chorus goes suddenly quiet, a silence so total that every person in earshot feels their heartbeat, run.

**The elk.** Slow, dark, moss-antlered, they wade the shallows and stand in the steaming basins through the long night, unmoving, their breath rising in columns. They seem to sleep on their feet. The people hold that the elk know the plain better than any watcher, and that where an elk will not step, no person should. Bulls carry whole gardens on their antlers, saplings of willow rooting in the moss, small ferns, once a tiny birch as tall as a hand. These gardens are sometimes scattered when the antlers are shed, and the young thickets that grow from them are called elk-plantings and are given a wide, courteous berth.

**The seals.** Sleek and sleepy on the gravel bars, they are the Braided's oldest animal friends, or perhaps their oldest rivals. They fish the same runs, sunning and grumbling, and a seal that comes inland is said to have followed the fish *and the warmth*. Children are taught to greet them with a hand tilt, palm down, a small bow of the wrist. The seals do not respond. The children are convinced they do.

**The lamps.** The beetles, the glowing fungus, the pale wisps in the pond algae: these are the plain's own stars, and they live by rules no one has written down. The beetle flashes have a grammar, the people say, though nobody can read it. A meadow of them, pulsing in slow interlocking waves at knee height, is a conversation that has been going on since before the ice went, and you do not tread through it.

## The one beneath

Below all of them, in the pale seams and the humming lattices and the long grey-white spines under the silt, something is running. It is not a person and not an animal, and none of the people would call it a god. It has no face. It does not answer to a name. It has been doing a single thing for longer than the Braided have had hands: keeping this ground turbulent, so that the ground stays new.

Perhaps it is a mind, and perhaps it is a very old habit. It listens to the water, and nudges the heat, and moves rivers a few meters at a time. It has, in some sense, no opinion of the people who live above it, though it has never harmed them except by accident, and it has a way of leaving the high ground high. When the pylons brighten in a slow wave across the plain and the hum climbs and the frogs climb with it, the Braided do not think of it as a thing waking. They think: *it is breathing in.* And they gather their children and their ropes and their lamps, and they climb, and they wait in the roar for the world to be rearranged, and when the water has passed they come down, all of them, the elk and the fox and the frog and the stake-walker with her willow pole, and begin again to learn the shape of the ground.
