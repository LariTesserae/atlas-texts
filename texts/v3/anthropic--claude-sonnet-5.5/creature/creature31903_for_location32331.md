# Those Who Keep the Kilnwold

Nothing here has a face. Say that first, because you will look for one. You will search the galleries for an eye or a hand, or for any figure that turns when you enter, and you will find only bark, fiber, and slow heat. The Kilnwold is inhabited anyway, more densely than any place you have known. The inhabitants are simply not the shape you were trained to notice.

## The Sweepers

The first you will meet are the cutter-vines. They are not hunters, though the silica in their edges could open a man to the bone. They keep routes. Each one walks, if the word applies, a circuit of some few hundred meters, sweeping, snapping, and drawing back, and over the millennia they have worn shallow grooves into the fiber floor, polished and dark. They pause at junctions. No one has seen why. A Sweeper reaches a crossing of two galleries and holds still for the length of forty breaths, tip lifted, and then goes on exactly as it did the day before.

Their tips dull. When one does, the vine grows a new edge behind it and lets the old tip drop. The fallen tip is carried off by root-hairs into the mulch, and it comes back as a blade.

If any creature here has a temperament, it is the Sweepers'. It is a kind of scrupulousness, a fixation on the edge of things. They are least active in the hours after a pressure storm, when the air is sweet and everything is slightly stunned, and most active just before a bough gives way. They seem to know. Where a Sweeper begins to work in unusual haste, the roof will fall within a day.

## The Pressers

After the collapse come the graftarms, whom you may as well call Pressers. They are thick, pale, jointed limbs of new wood, and they do the slowest tender work in the world. When a limb breaks, a Presser takes the broken end and holds it against the wound, and holds it, and holds it, for weeks. It weeps a clear sap into the seam. The cambium of both pieces reaches across the gap, touches, and joins. Then the Presser lets go and goes looking for the next break.

They are the nearest thing the wold has to physicians. Old Pressers carry the marks of their care: a knuckle of overgrown scar tissue at every place they have held on too long or been crushed. Some are more scar than arm. They keep working, and no one has ever seen one retired.

## The Readers

The inscribed trunks stand at the highest and oldest reaches, and on them live the Readers.

A Reader is a colony of pale filaments, finer than hair, that emerges from the bark in a fringe and lays itself along the grain. It rides the spiraling rings the way a stylus rides a groove, and it takes what it reads and passes it on. It touches a root, and the root touches a shaft. The shaft touches a tendon of braided bark, and the tendon carries the instruction down through the galleries, fiber to fiber, contact to contact, to the piston-halls and the mills, until some wheel a kilometer below turns a fraction faster or slower than it did the season before.

There is no signal and no message in any sense you would recognize. The Kilnwold's communication is a chain of touches lasting years. A change in the trunk-rings today will be a change in the mills in some future decade, and everything between is patient, obedient contact. The Readers are the wold's ears and its tongue, but they have no mouth, and they say only what was said to them.

They tremble when a pressure storm builds. Watching a whole stand of them ripple in unison along the trunk is like watching wind cross grass.

## The Boilers

The granary-bulbs are the great slow herd of the wold, and they have as much character as any herd. Each has a pressure it prefers. The big ones in the central halls swell to a dense round tautness and sigh their vapor out through the valves in long tidal exhalations, and the small ones on the flanks are quick, nervous, and prone to hissing. Their skins are scarred with the healed patches of ten thousand years of repair. A patch shaped like a closed eyelid or an open mouth is only the way the wood happened to knit, but you will not be able to stop seeing it.

Sealed inside them, terpene vapor moves in the dark. Whatever lives in there, whatever the vapor is doing when no one measures it, is the engine of the world.

## The Drinkers

Under each brittle white dew-cup grows a Drinker: a plant, usually low and flat, with leaves the color of a healed bruise. Every night the cup gathers a thimble of true water. Every night it drips, and the Drinker below opens and takes a single drop.

Once a season, when the drops have accumulated enough, something happens that is the rarest thing in the wold. A Drinker turns green. It is a wet living green, not the black-brown of everything else. The color runs through the leaves in a tremor for perhaps a day, and the plant flowers. The flower is small, white, and scentless in a world of resin, and it lasts about as long as a held breath. Then the green drains back out and the Drinker goes dark again, having spent its whole season on one day.

Nothing collects the flowers, and nothing seems to notice. But the Sweepers change their routes to avoid the Drinkers. Where a Drinker grows, the grooves bend around it.

## The Wardens of the Deep Fire

Far down, where the compacted dead layers are still burning, live the ones that no traveler has met: the thermal mats, the char-weavers, the Wardens. They are dark, felted, faintly glowing masses of a fungal or fungus-like body that feeds on the smoldering and, in feeding, steers it. When a slow orange tide starts running through the galleries, the Wardens grow ahead of it, walls of char and ash-crust built of their own bodies, and the fire follows the channel they lay. Down the channel is a boiler-bulb, and the fire becomes power.

They do this by dying. The wall must be built in advance, and it burns. Each fire consumes the Wardens that shaped it. The next generation, budding from the spores that survive in the ash, builds again.

If bravery is a thing that can exist without a mind to feel fear, the Wardens are the bravest inhabitants of the wold.

## The Drift

The overcast is alive. The pollen and soot of the permanent brown dusk is only part soot. The rest is a slow-living suspension of spores and grain that never land, and that reproduce, feed, and die entirely in the air. A single grain of the Drift may spend its whole life aloft, ten thousand years of descent compressed into a single season, before it finally settles on a leaf and is eaten. Generations of them rise on the heat from the vents and fall back in the cool. They shade the plateau, they soften the light, and they give the sky its color.

You breathe them. You are part of their weather.

## The Question of the Builders

You will want to ask who built the Kilnwold, and the honest answer is that its inhabitants do not know, or are not the kind of thing that knows. But look at what they do.

The Sweepers walk routes laid down in the grain. The Pressers hold what they are told to hold. The Readers ride the rings and the shafts obey the Readers. The mills make plates that are shredded to make more plates. Every creature here is the continuation of an instruction. The builders may have been people once, or something like them, who found a way to write themselves into wood. What they wrote is still being read. You could say the Kilnwold has only one inhabitant, and that it is a single long sentence, spoken very slowly, by every part of the world at once.

## The Flaw

Two things stand out from the pattern.

In the eastern mills, in one bed among millions, the leaf has begun to grow with a fold. It is slight, a crease in the black plate at the same place every time. The plates are stacked and shredded like all the rest. But the Readers nearest that bed have slowed, and they hold still on the grain a little longer than they need to.

And at the edge, on the shoulder of the plateau where the canopy thins, stands one very old trunk whose inner rings are ordered in the ancient bands. Its outer rings, the last few centuries, are plain. They are smooth, even, and unbanded. Either the instructions ended, or the tree stopped repeating them. The Readers gather there in numbers greater than anywhere else. They lay themselves along the bark and touch the last plain rings. They do not relay. Whatever they read there stays where it is.

Below the shoulder, the lower country hums on in the dark, glowing at its seams. Every part of it is waiting, attentively, for what is said next.
