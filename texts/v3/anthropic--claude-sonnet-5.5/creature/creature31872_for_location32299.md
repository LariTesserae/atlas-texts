# Who Lives in the Held Breath

Nobody has ever counted the inhabitants, and nobody has seen one. They are known the way a house is known to someone who grew up in it and now sits in it blind: by where the sounds come from, and by what stops when you move.

## The Frogs

The frogs are the room's voice, and they are so many that they are less a population than a weather.

Each is the size of a thumbnail and the color of a wet page held up to a window. Their skin is so thin that the small beat of the heart shows through it as a pale flicker, if there were anyone to see. The eyes have shrunk to dark pinpricks under the skin, kept the way a house keeps a bricked-up window. They don't need them. They hear through the jaw and the ribs and the soles of their feet, and they feel the floor as much as the air.

Each frog has one note, or a note and a variation, a thin bright *tik-eeee* that rises at the end like a question. The note is not chosen freely. The hum from the cube sets a scaffold of harmonics, and each frog sings the one that fits its patch of floor. Where the fern bank is thick, the notes sit low and round. Near the pools they go high and glassy. A frog that sings off the scaffold is not punished. It just isn't answered, and a frog that isn't answered goes quiet, and soon sings something else.

The frogs listen to their neighbors and adjust by a hair, and that is how the waves happen. A chorus starts in one corner, passes to the next frog and the next, and rolls across the floor over a minute or more. You stand still and it washes past you, a slow tide of small voices, and then it is over the horizon of the dark and you hear it going away. Then the next one comes. Every wave lands on the pulse of the hum, one long chord every few breaths, so the whole floor of frogs works like a single lung.

They eat springtails and mites and the small pale beetles that graze the fern-mold. They lay their eggs in beaded foam at the edges of the seeps, in runnels too narrow and too shallow for fish. The tadpoles live in water no deeper than a fingernail, and the whole life of a frog may pass within thirty paces of where it hatched.

## The Silent Majority

The rules say that whatever is silent is stone. This is a useful rule, and it is wrong.

Under the ferns and in the flowstone's smallest cracks live the quiet ones: springtails like drifts of grey flour, mites, pale worms, and centipedes as fine as threads. They make no sound a human ear can catch. They are most of what lives in the room, and every frog depends on them. Sometimes your hand will come away from a fern with a faint dust of them on the wet skin, moving.

Between them and the frogs lie the ticking crickets. They have no wings and no eyes, and their antennae are three times the length of their bodies. They don't sing so much as tap, dropping their hard heads against the flowstone in patient little rhythms. The beetles answer with clicks. When a person walks through the ferns, the clicks ahead go silent and the clicks behind take up again, so you carry a small moving hush with you, a circle of politeness.

## The Fish

The pools are thick with pale fish, long as a finger, some as long as a forearm. They are the color of candle wax, with the ghost of a vein along the back, and their whole heads are sense organs, pitted and ridged. They feel the water more than they sense anything else.

The hum makes standing ripples in the pools, and the fish have learned the ripples. In each pool there are places where the water shivers hard and places where it barely moves, and the fish gather in the still ones, hanging with their fins almost idle, out of the wash of the tone. Fish that hold those places live a long time. Some are older than the oldest guide's grandmother, and they have grown slow and large and deliberate.

Now and then one rises and breaks the surface to gulp air, with a small wet sound like a kiss. It is one of the few sounds in the room that means nothing and asks nothing, and it is very pleasant to hear.

## The Bats

The bats live high up, out of reach, in domes and pockets of the ceiling that the water carved when it was still carving. They are small, and their colonies mutter and shuffle all through the day, which is no different from their night. They are not the pale ghosts you might expect. Their fur is the brown of old leather, because they still fly out through the fissure when the season asks it of them, and return from the cloud forest smelling of rain and resin. In the room they fly on echo alone.

Their calls make the finest structure in the room: lattices of high, bright, brittle sound that cross the black volume in every direction, so that the emptiness is not empty but crosshatched. Standing under them you feel yourself being read, and each of you is drawn and redrawn in tiny clicks a hundred times a second. The bats know the roots as a map. They know where the fern-crowns are, and where the posts are, and the stair to nowhere. They do not fly near the cube. Its hum confuses their calling, or perhaps it doesn't confuse them at all and they simply go around.

Their guano rains down through the darkness and feeds the ferns and the beetles, which feed the frogs. The whole floor sits at the bottom of a slow rain from above.

## The Roots

The roots are not quite animals, but they are the most patient residents, and it would be dishonest to leave them out. They come down from the forest as pale ropes as thick as thighs, and where their tips ooze water they draw the pools' mineral taste into themselves. Their sap pulses against your palm. The pulse is slower than a heartbeat, and it is not in time with the hum. It is in time with something further up, a distant tide of rain and wind and season, that is pouring down through the fissure the way water goes through a hand.

They grow a hand's breadth a year, in no hurry. One will touch the floor in some century and become a pillar, and the whole room's slow dripping will find a new route.

## The Heavy Thing

Something large lives in the ferns. There may be several. It moves with a long, wet, dragging rhythm, many legs falling in a wave, and it stops when you stop.

Nobody has seen it. Once, a guide says, she put out her hand in the dark and touched a ridged, warm surface, like a roof tile, or a shell, or a very old leaf, and it moved away from her without hurrying. She doesn't know what it was. It might be a millipede the size of a sleeping dog, or a crustacean that came in with the water when the mountain was young and never left. It eats the ferns, or the fungus in the ferns, or the frogs' spent foam. Or it may only wander.

When you stop, it stops. This isn't fear, or it isn't only fear. It is what everything in the room does. Everything is listening to everything else, and the heavy thing is listening to you the way you are listening to it, with the same care and the same curiosity, at a distance of twenty paces across the dark.

## The Sleepers by the Cube

Against the base of the cube, where the ground is dry and warm, small animals sleep in a heap: pale voles, or things like voles, and cave crickets in their dozens. They breathe together, and their sleep-sounds are so soft that you find them only by kneeling and putting your face near the stone. Sometimes you trip on one and it squeaks. Then the whole heap stirs, mutters, and settles, and the dry warm smell rises around you, like bread.

They are the only dry creatures in the room. They must know it. They have chosen the one place where the walls are warm and the tone is loudest, where you can feel it in your teeth, and they sleep there anyway.

## The Ones Who Visit

The guides come down a few times a season, and they are inhabitants in the way weather is: a disturbance that always ends. They carry no lamps, because a lamp would be a kind of lie. They hum as they walk, a low steady note, so the room knows where they are. They tap the flowstone with a stick, twice, to say *I am here*. The frogs seem to take this as good manners.

They name the voices. There is the Thin Choir by the eastern pools, and Old Basso, who is a fish, and the Drag, which is the heavy thing. There is the Lattice, which is the bats. There is the Long Wave, which is the frogs when they roll across the whole floor together. They know these as you know the sounds of your own street.

## What the Room Is

If you ask the guides who lives here, they do not list the frogs and the fish and the bats. They say the room lives here. They say the frogs stop singing when a person stands near the cube because the room is holding its breath.

There is a sober explanation, and the guides know it too: a body near the cube changes the way the tone lies in the air, the frogs feel the change in their feet and jaws, and they stop, as a crowd stops when a door opens. But the pause is too total, and it comes too suddenly, and it lasts exactly until the next pulse of the hum. That is long enough to make a person stand still with their hand on the warm wall and listen back.

In that silence you hear the ticking of the crickets thin out and the bats' lattice fall away. You hear the sap in the roots, or think you do. You hear the water settling in the pools, and the blood in your own ears, and under all of it the hum, which does not stop, and which the frogs are waiting for. Then the tone swells, the chord comes down, and ten thousand small voices take up the note together, as though nothing had been asked.
