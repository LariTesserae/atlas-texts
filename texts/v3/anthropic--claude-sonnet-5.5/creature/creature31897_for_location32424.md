# Who Keeps the Boiler Sink

Nothing here has a face, and at first you will think that means nothing here is home. The mistake is natural. You look for eyes among the ferns and find only fronds, look for a hand on the machinery and find only moss. But the wheel has turned for longer than its makers could have imagined, and things do not run that long unattended. Something attends. It takes a while to see how.

## The Whistlers

The leaves with slits in their edges are the first to notice you, though "notice" claims too much. Stand in a stand of them and the pitch shifts. The kettle-notes climb a half step, or drop out one by one as you pass, and resume behind you. They may be reacting only to the warmth of your body bending the steam's path. But the change is always in the same order, the near leaves first and the far ones after, like a rumor crossing a room, and after an hour you will find you can read your position in the sink from the choir around you.

The Whistlers are the sink's sentries, if it has any. They are also its clock. When pressure builds toward the peak of the breath, they tune themselves higher together, and for a few minutes their voices agree, a single clear note over the vents' disorder. Then the exhalation comes, and the note breaks into a scatter of falling ones.

They are not the ones who tend the wheel. They are too shallow-rooted, too fond of light.

## The Pale

Under the shade, where the leaf litter is thickest, the colorless growths stand in clusters: fleshy stalks the color of candle fat, shelves like slices of cold tallow, bulbs that weep a clear sweet-smelling fluid. They eat rot, and what they eat is everything: fallen fronds, dead pumps, the dark cores of old timbers. But if you kneel and lift a corner of the moss, you find that they eat in a peculiar way. Their threads do not spread evenly. They run in lines, and the lines follow the pipes.

Pale white cords sleeve every conduit in the sink. They lie along the iron under the fern-fiber and go down into the flowstone, finer than hair, thousands to the finger's width, warm as a vein. Where a pipe splits, they have bridged the crack. Where a seam weeps steam, they have packed it with a gray felt that hardens in the heat, so the leak sings smaller. Someone has been mending here. The mending is done in the same slow way that sinter is laid, by a body with no hurry in it.

The Pale are the nearest thing the sink has to hands. Their cords run out through the engine-house wall and up into the bearings, and the seep that fills the bearing cups is not simply seep. It is thick and faintly sweet, and it is made. A pale shelf grows at the lip of each cup, and a bead forms on its underside, swells, and falls into the cup at the moment the wheel's rod dips toward it. The timing is exact. No one could have taught it. Over centuries the wheel's turning has become part of the shelf's growth, and the shelf's oil part of the wheel's turning, and neither could say which began.

## The Weave

The belts that run from the flywheel to the pumps are not belts. Or they were belts once, leather and canvas, and are now something else that kept their shape. Fine roots have grown through the old leather and replaced it thread by thread, as a vine replaces a post after the post has rotted, retaining the outline of what it took. The fern that did this is a plain tree fern, ordinary in every way but the patience of its roots. It reaches out along the belt-line, and where the leather wears, it grows more of itself into the gap, and pulls the tension true.

None of them wants anything. That is the hard thing to say and the truest. The Pale do not oil the bearings so that the pumps may run. The ferns do not mend the belts so that the pools may be drawn. Each does its one thing, at its own pace, and the sum of the things is the works. What has been left behind is not a machine and not a creature but a habit, the makers' habit of moving water and heat from the deep place to the shallow one, taken up by whatever could hold it. The makers themselves are gone. Perhaps they climbed the stair. Perhaps they are part of the flowstone by now, one more thin layer under the tide marks.

## The Audience

The broadest leaves, the ones turned in ranks toward the sun's crawling shaft, are the most watchful-seeming, and the least. They want only the light. But they have arranged themselves so that as the shaft moves across the floor, each rank in turn lights up and tips its face into it, and the rank behind it, still dim, shifts its angle a little in preparation. Watch long enough and you see a slow wave of turning move through them, one leaf handing the sun to the next. There is no one leaf that leads. There is only the handing.

It is like watching a congregation. It is also like watching a lock work, chamber by chamber, water passed down a stair. That is what the whole sink does: it passes things along. Light from leaf to leaf, oil from shelf to cup, tension from root to belt, heat from fissure to vent to the cold lip of the sink and back down again as rain that never falls. Nothing keeps any of it. All of it is held only as long as it takes to hand it on.

## What Is Attending

If you are asking who lives here, the answer is the passing. The Whistlers, the Pale, the ferns, and the leaves are its organs and not its residents. It has no center you could find. It has a pulse, the day-long breath that swells the pools and quiets the wheel, and you could say it sleeps in the hush after the exhalation and stirs as the pressure returns. That is a fancy, and it may be a wrong one. But stand in the aftermath of the breath, when the vents sigh and the pools sink from their rims and the wheel drags a beat slow, and you will feel the whole green place lean toward the next pressure with a kind of readiness. Every leaf a little turned. Every cord a little warmer.

You will feel, too, if you stay, the Pale creeping toward you. Not fast, and not with intent, but the clear sweet fluid will be standing on the leaf litter where you've been sweating, and a small cold-white bulb will have raised itself at the edge of your boot print. You are warm, and wet, and full of salt, and you are passing through. The sink has no objection. It will feed on what you leave, and mend around your absence, and go on.

Above, on the stair, the bowls in the steps hold their skins of warm water. They are kept that way by a film of green so thin you take it for shadow, that wets itself from the steam-breath and never lets the stone go dry. Whoever cut the stair is long gone. But the stair is kept. Whatever lives here has, in its slow and uncomprehending way, kept the way out open.
