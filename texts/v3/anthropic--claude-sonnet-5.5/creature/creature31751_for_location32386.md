# Who Lives in Halvane

The question is harder to answer than it looks, because the basin resists the idea of a resident. Nothing here lives *in* the place. Everything lives *as* it, and the boundaries are soft. Look at any inhabitant long enough and you find it is partly someone else's home, someone else's meal, someone else's tool.

Still, there are those who would say they live here, and they can be counted in layers.

## The Ones Who Chew

The first you will meet, though you will hear them before you see them, are the **sweeps**: the long, whiskered, otter-bodied climbers who move along the root-bridges in gangs of six or nine. They are the color of wet bark, with a pale blaze down the belly, and their hands are startlingly human, long-fingered and patient. They carry themselves like foremen. A sweep will stop mid-span, put one ear to a copper pipe, and stay there, eyes half shut, listening for the particular gurgle of a clogged joint. Then the whole gang converges, and there is a brisk, wet, industrious chewing, and the sap moves again.

No one taught them this. Or someone did, four hundred years ago, and the lesson has been passed down in play, in the way mothers let their young hold a clay elbow-joint between their teeth. A young sweep's first toy is a length of pipe. Its first grief is a pipe it could not clear. The sweeps do not seem to know that they are the reason the engines run. They seem to believe the engines run and they are helping.

They are affectionate with one another in a way that fills the bridges with noise: a constant low trilling, a chirred running commentary. When a gang passes overhead, the frogs below shift half a tone in their direction, as if nodding.

## The Ones Who Glow

At night the **wicks** take the mat. They are flightless birds the size of large dogs, long-legged and hunched, with a sac of pale rendered fat in the breast that glows the yellow of a low candle. It shines through the feathers, so that each bird walks inside its own small lantern. They patrol in loose lines, heads down, eating the slugs and beetles and pale pinkish things that come up out of the mat after dark.

They are the basin's night watch, and they behave like it. Wicks do not hurry. They stop and stand, and stand, and their light dims almost to nothing as they listen, and then it swells again and they move on. A wick that has found something wrong will not cry out. It stands at the edge of the wrongness with its light at full, and the others come and stand with it, until a small ring of lanterns marks the spot like a vigil. In the morning the humans come to see what the wicks found.

## The Ones Who Listen

There are people here. Perhaps forty, perhaps fifty. Nobody has counted well, because counting requires standing still.

They call themselves the **Tenders**, though the word has worn down over the generations to something closer to *tenn'rs* or just *the tend*. They are the descendants of the last shift, the ones who stayed when the mine or the works or whatever Halvane was for stopped being a business and became a habit. They do not remember why the engines were built. They remember only that the engines must not stop.

They are small, most of them, wiry and low-slung, because the root-bridges are narrow and the fern-ways are low, and because tall people spend their lives ducking. Their skin has a faint greenish cast in the dim, some of it real, some of it the light. Moss grows in the seams of their coats, and they do not brush it off. They keep it the way sailors keep salt. Their hands are always slightly damp. Their hair is always slightly damp. They have never been dry in their lives, and when one of them climbs the spiral stair to the surface, which happens rarely and reluctantly, they come back with cracked lips and a look of having been scoured.

They speak softly. Loudness pushes the frogs up a key and unsettles the clockers, and a settlement that lives by ear cannot afford to be a source of noise. Even their arguments are murmured, in a clipped, rhythmic speech that has drifted toward the four-second stroke of the engines. Sentences arrive on the beat. A Tender who talks fast is a Tender who is frightened.

**They navigate by sound and name by sound.** A child is given a name at the first rain after birth: a phrase drawn from the chorus, a frog-call or a clocker-tick or the pitch of a particular bell, and then a second, human syllable to hang it on. A woman might be *Glass-in-the-West Ilse*, for the gold frogs that chime near the foundry. A man might be *Four-Tick Marrow*. To call someone is to imitate them, a small whistle or a click of the tongue, and they will come. Strangers are named on arrival and are always a little embarrassed by it.

**Their work is mostly attention.** No one stokes the boilers. What the Tenders do is watch: the level of sap in the vats, the color of the foam, the stroke of the engines. One of them, called the **Ear**, sleeps in a hammock slung in the Engine House itself, among the sleeping bats, and is chosen for a peculiar gift. The Ear can feel a stroke arrive a hair's breadth late. In a world where the engines have never once failed in living memory, the Ear's whole vocation is to notice the failure a quarter-second before it occurs. The post has been held by a woman, a man, then a woman, in a line back further than the records go. It is not clear that anyone has ever been *needed*. It is very clear that the post will not go empty.

They mend what can be mended, and they let the rest go into the moss without complaint. They do not fight the mat. They set nothing down that they are not prepared to lose. When they build, they build for the eventual lifting-in of the thing, and their tools have holes drilled in the handles so they can be hung on pegs, out of reach of the floor.

## How They Die

The Tenders do not bury their dead. They **lay them down**.

A body is carried to a terrace of the mat, arranged on its back with the hands open, and left there with a family member sitting close. The sitter does not speak. The sitter also does not move, which is the point. Small things begin to arrive: springtails, then pale beetles, then the first millipede, forearm-thick and unhurried. The sitter watches the basin begin its work and says nothing, because to say something would be to interfere. They sit until the mat has closed over the shape, which takes days. The engines beat on beside them.

This is not grief in the way a surface-dweller would recognize it. It is closer to a handing-back. The Tenders have a phrase, murmured by the sitter as the last of the outline fades: *Held long enough.* It is both a eulogy and a receipt.

## The Ones You Feel

Then there is the thing in the southern ferns.

The Tenders have a word for it that they do not use in front of visitors, and when pressed they will offer only a gesture: a flat palm pressed downward, then lifted, as if feeling a pulse. They call it the **Tide**. No one alive has seen it whole. Some have seen a flank, dark and damp and roughly the width of a doorway, moving through the deep green like a slow cloud. Some have seen only what it leaves behind: a long trough in the mat, filled back in within a day.

Its footfalls are felt in the soles and the knees. The frogs do not fall silent for it, and neither do the clockers, so it is not a predator, or not one that any of them fear. The sweeps pause on the bridges when it passes, and the wicks turn their heads all together, and for a few seconds the whole basin seems to hold a single long breath.

The Tenders' most private belief, which none of them would say aloud, is that the Tide is what the engines are *for*. Not that the machines feed it, but that they keep time for it. It moves, they suspect, exactly as slowly as the engines allow. When the engines stop, they think, it will stop too. And they think, though they do not think about it often, that it has been waiting a very long time for something to finish.

## The Hour Before Dark

At the end of the day, when the light-column thins to its gold thread, you will see all of them at once, if you know how to look.

The sweeps come down out of the high roots and gather along the pipe-lattice, chittering. The wicks stir from their day-hollows, their breast-lamps kindling one by one. The bats begin to unpeel from the trusses. The frogs double their voices. In the doorways of the rowhouses the Tenders come out and stand, not talking, faces tilted to the fading coin of sky, damp hands loose at their sides, each one breathing in time with the stroke, *hwoom*, four seconds, *hwoom*.

If you have been standing still long enough, one of them will notice you. She will cross the terrace, unhurried, moss in the seams of her coat, and study you with an expression that is neither welcome nor wariness. She will cock her head to the west, where the giant frogs are humming the bells, and to the north, where the clockers tick. Then she will make a small sound, a soft click of the tongue and a whistle, the beginning of a name.

You will not be able to tell, yet, what you are being called. But the frogs will, and they will shift, very slightly, half a tone toward you.
