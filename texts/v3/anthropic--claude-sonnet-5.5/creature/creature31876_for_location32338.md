# The Inhabitants of the Boiler Vug

## The smelters

Start with the weight of them. A smelter is the size of a large dog but three times as dense, and when one shifts its stance you hear it: a low scrape of plate on plate, like a drawer of cutlery pushed shut. The back is a single arched shield, scaled in overlapping lamellae that have oxidized to the color of old pennies, with green bloom in the seams where sulfur has bitten. The shield isn't smooth. Each animal carries a record on its back in ridges and pits, the burns and cracks of its life, and among themselves they read these as we might read faces.

They have no proper eyes. Where eyes would be there are two shallow, glassy pits, useful only for telling the dull brown of the seam from the darker brown of everything else. Their real senses are elsewhere. Heat-sensitive membranes line the underside of the jaw and the soft joints of the legs. Fine bristles on the feet feel the stone's trembling. A smelter standing still is a listening instrument. It stands with its belly nearly on the rock and its legs slightly bowed, taking in the chamber through its feet: the hammerers' overhead rhythms carried down through the ceiling, the tread of neighbors, the far shudder of the seam, and the particular pulse of a relative it has known all its life.

The chest is the engine. Behind the head the shield lifts slightly, and under it is a pleated, leathery bellows that draws in air and pushes it through a set of chambers the animal keeps at different temperatures. In the hottest, ore is roasted. In the next, the roasted mineral is reduced by gases the smelter makes itself. The last is a cooling gland where metal condenses out of vapor and gathers into a bead. A young smelter's pellets are pea-sized and lumpy, poorly refined, mottled with slag. An old, well-fed one drops beads as smooth and heavy as ball bearings, silver-gray, with a faint cast of blue. The tenders know the difference and prize them. A shelf of good pellets in the wall is an inheritance.

They eat slowly and constantly. The wide rasping mouth works in a circle, scraping the sulfide crust from the walls in curling yellow shavings, chewing iron-bearing rock into a paste. A smelter may spend a week in one spot, grazing a patch down to bare basalt, then shuffle a body-length along and start again. From above the whole floor looks like a slow-moving herd, all heads down, all shields rising and falling with the pumping of the bellows, an ocean of pennies breathing together.

Their blood is heavy and dark, an amber syrup that is nearly a resin when it cools. When a smelter is wounded it seals almost at once, and the plug becomes a bright scar on the shield. The oldest animals have so many scars that their shields glitter like mosaic.

## Growing up in the press

Smelters are born small and soft, pale as wax, in the warm gaps beneath the shelving of the tenders. A hatchling is nearly all mouth and gut, and it must reach the crust and start eating within hours or the heat will cook it. So its first movement is a blind, frantic crawl over the backs of the adults, following the vibrations of grazing. Many don't make it. The ones that do are pushed from the seam's warmth outward almost immediately, because the great smelters take the best places. The young are raised at the cool margins, on rock that has already been grazed thin, where the air is only deadly rather than lethal.

The young harden there. Their shields take on their first color, a pale gold that darkens to copper over years. They learn the language of the stone: a stamp, a drag, a double tap, a long trembling pressed through the belly that means *food here* or *the ceiling is loosening* or simply *I am here, and I am yours.* The last is the most common message in the chamber. It is repeated thousands of times a minute, a constant murmur of belonging beneath the roar.

As a smelter matures and grows heavy and bright-shielded, it works inward through the crowd, toward the seam, shouldering past others by patient pressure rather than violence. Fights happen, but slowly. Two smelters lock shields and lean, each trying to push the other off its spot, and the contest can last days, the pair grinding against each other in a shower of copper dust while the crowd rearranges itself around them. The loser is not hurt. He is simply moved.

At the height of its life, a smelter may hold a place near the seam for a decade, its shield taking on the dull red of the rock itself. Then the seam shifts. The place it held cools, the heat moves a few body-lengths along, and it must move with it or slowly starve. Some follow. Some, stiff with age, don't. They are gradually crowded outward and end their lives at the far edges, in the cold, where the air is thin with hot sulfur and the walls are new and cracking, and they grow quiet, and are ground into the crust.

## The tenders

Between the legs of the smelters, where a dog would find nothing but dark, live the tenders. Each is the length of a forearm, hard-shelled, flat, and many-legged, and they move in a jointed scuttle that sounds like a handful of keys dropped on a table. Their front pair of limbs are pincers, blunt and wide-jawed, built for carrying. They have no faces to speak of. They have a cluster of feelers and a small dark mouth, and they seem to think with their legs.

The work they do is not planned. A tender finds a pellet and picks it up, and something in it wants the pellet in a crack. It scuttles to the nearest crevice and jams the bead in, then taps it with its pincers until it seats. The next tender finds that crevice a little narrower, a little more comfortable, and adds to it. Over generations the walls fill up with metal, a riveted lattice of gray ribs and beads and short stubby bars, grown together with resinous smelter blood, sealed with the varnish of molten salt. Nobody designed it. But it holds. Whole sections of the chamber's walls now hang on this accidental ironwork, and the young hammerers' eggs lie warm in its pockets.

Tenders are the chamber's memory. They know where every good shelf is. They can sense, through the vibration of a wall, whether the metal behind it is sound or has been loosened by heat. A shelf that begins to slump will be quietly shored up within a day by a crowd of tenders that appear from nowhere, wedging beads, tapping, packing, and then leaving.

They also eat, and what they eat is everything the others cannot: the shed plates of molting smelters, the chitin husks of dead hammerers, the sulfur-caked fragments of the dead. When a smelter dies at the edge of the chamber, tenders arrive within the hour, and the body is dismantled and carried away in pieces, some to the crust as sulfur-rich powder, some into the wall, where a dead animal's plates become part of the shelving. The chamber does not bury its dead. It builds with them.

## The hammerers

Up on the ceiling, a different world. The hammerers hang packed together in the thousands, pale as bone, with narrow bodies, long clinging legs, and hard wing-cases folded flat. The forelimbs are their tools, thickened at the tip into knobs of dense chitin like small mallets. Each strikes the basalt above it, tick, tick, tick, a ringing note that depends on the rock and on the force of the blow.

They don't strike randomly. A cluster of hammerers will fall into a pattern, three quick strikes, a rest, two slow ones. A neighbor picks it up, alters it slightly, and hands it to the next. The rhythms move across the ceiling like weather. Every so often something catches, and a whole quarter of the vault locks into unison, thousands of mallets falling as one, and the sound swells to a shuddering clang that sets the loose flakes of rock trembling. Then the pattern breaks, and the noise goes back to a thousand overlapping ticks.

It is courtship, and also argument, and also maintenance. Females choose mates by the quality of rhythm, and a male who cannot hold a steady beat against the roar will find no partner. The striking keeps the ceiling cracked in a fine web that lets gas escape, and without it, pressure would build until the vault failed. The hammerers do not know this. They only know that the rhythm feels right, and that the crust above them rings true.

Their eggs are laid in the tenders' shelving, in pockets of warm iron, glued down with a transparent resin. The larvae are pale worms that drop from the ceiling and are collected by tenders, who shelter them, and who are, in some obscure way, paid for it with the shed skins the larvae leave behind.

## The stokers

At the seam, the tall, black, thin ones. Stokers stand on legs too long for their bodies, with narrow heads and a long tubular snout that they push deep into the glowing cracks. They are the only creatures in the chamber who look directly at the light, and they do not look; they drink. Something in their gut takes heat and stores it, and they carry it in bright, hot organs behind the ribs.

They breathe in bursts. A stoker draws its snout out of the crack, holds still, then exhales all at once in a long hiss, and a fan of orange sparks flies out and dies in the air. In that flare you see, for an instant, everything: the humped backs of the smelters gone gold, the ceiling bristling with pale hammerers, the metal shelving glinting like a wall of teeth, and then the dark again. The stokers are the closest thing the chamber has to a sun. They flicker all through the night that never ends, one flaring, another fading, a slow lightning that goes on forever.

They are solitary, or nearly. Each has a crack that is its own. When a crack cools, the stoker must find a new one, and this is the most dangerous moment in its life, because a cracked seam can burst. Old stokers have scorched, pitted snouts. The oldest have snouts fused to stone, and they stay where they are, still drinking what little heat remains, until they stop.

## What they are together

It would be easy to say the vug is a place with animals in it. It is closer to say that it is one animal, with parts that do not know one another. The smelters make the metal, the tenders make it into walls, the hammerers keep the vault open, and the stokers give the light that lets the whole thing be seen, if anything could see. Each takes what the others leave. Nothing is wasted, and nothing is thanked.

None of them know the world above. They don't know there is a plateau, or a sky, or a crack in the scree breathing out a shimmer of heat. Their world is the chamber and the slow shifting of the seam. It is warm, loud, and crowded, and it is enough. Every animal in it is born pressed against another, lives pressed against another, and dies pressed against the stone, and the last thing any of them feels is what they felt first, the trembling of a thousand bodies in the rock, saying, *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

And the chamber grows. The seam shifts a hand's breadth a decade, the vault swells, fresh rock cracks and cools at the edges, and it is the old and the young, pushed outward to the cold margins, who first touch the new walls. The exiles are the pioneers. Every generation, the ones the seam has left behind are the ones who find the next place, and the whole furnace-city follows them, slowly, into the dark.
