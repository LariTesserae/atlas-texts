# Those Who Are in the Room

The first inhabitant you meet is a sound. Before you see anyone, you feel a knocking in the wall you are resting against: two soft, one hard, a pause, then two soft again. That is a person. It is also the name of the people, since they have no word for themselves that can be voiced. They are *tok-tok-TOK*, meaning roughly *those who are in the room.* Strangers, if there were any, would be called *the ones still in the corridor.*

## The body

They are long-limbed and narrow, built for slow, economical movement. A grown person is a little taller than a land-dweller would expect, but so lightly boned and so unhurried that they seem stretched rather than large. Their skin is pale, taking a green-gold cast from the light and a faint dusting of white from the water. Along the ribs, just under the arm, sit the fine paired slits of their gills, and when they rest, these flutter in a rhythm slower than a heartbeat. Their hair is long and fine, and everyone wears it uncut. In the vent-current it streams the same direction as the ribbon-grass, so a crowd on a terrace looks combed by a single hand.

Their eyes are large and dark, with a mirrored layer behind them that flashes green when a lamp swings past. They can see far into the tea-colored afternoon. Their ears are small and nearly useless, but they hear through the jaw and the breastbone. A person listening properly leans a cheek on a wall, closes their eyes, and goes still, and the wall tells them who is home, who is angry, and who is dying.

Their hands are the most marked part of them. Every adult has a palm crossed with pale scars from basalt, and the scars are a kind of ledger. A knapper's hand is a map of every mistake made in the first ten years. A gas-cutter's hands are smooth but cracked at the nails from the lampstalks' brittle joints. The old also carry the plateau in them. In the knuckles, elbows, and knees of the very old, mineral blooms into small cream-colored crusts. These are called *lips*, after the scalloped terraces. To have lips is a mark of age and standing. The very oldest walk stiffly, with a faint tick in their joints that children pretend to keep time to.

They dress lightly: skirts and sashes of twisted grass-cord, and harnesses of small sealed bladders. Each person adjusts their bladders by feel, letting out a little gas or taking in a little, until they hang neutral in the water. On dry land they would be helpless, but here they stand, drift, and sit in the water alike. Most wear a pendant of travertine with a fern-ghost cast in it. It is the leaf from the year of their birth, chosen by their mother from a wall that was still soft.

## What they do all day

Very little happens quickly. That is the point of them.

**The reopeners.** Every household owns a set of chisels, and every household sooner or later opens a door that has begun to close. The stone thickens a thumb's width a year in the best-fed lanes. Doorways shrink, and children who once ran through them must turn sideways. So each family has a season, usually after the shudder-years, when they work along their own walls with obsidian chisels and mallets of black wood, cutting back the growth. The chips are kept. They are ground into a pale powder used for scouring, for making paste, and as an offering. A person's age is often told by how many times they have reopened the same door. *I have opened this one nine times,* an old woman says, and everyone understands her.

**The seep-wrights.** These are the builders, and they are the most patient people in the city. They weave a house-frame from lampstalk and grass, lash it, and carry it to the place under a seep where the water runs right, neither too hot nor too rich. Then they wait. A good wright never finishes a house. They start one, and their child's child moves in. They say the house is a conversation between the family and the spring, and that the spring is a slow talker.

**The lamp-keepers.** Each household keeps horn jars of the small luminous weed, and someone must feed it a pinch of crushed shell each dusk and turn the jar so it lights evenly. It is a small, sacred, tedious job, usually given to the children and the very old. The light of the city is really their light, a dull amber breath in a thousand windows, and the keepers know each house's glow the way a shepherd knows sheep. A jar that goes dark is a household matter, and neighbors will knock on the wall to ask whether all is well.

**The gas-cutters.** Their work is dangerous. They harvest the swollen lampstalks just before the release, tapping the hollow trunks to find the ripe ones, and drawing off the gas into the great bladders that serve as cisterns and floats. A wrongly cut stalk bursts and can throw a cutter upward. Cutters are known by an easy, watchful manner and a habit of glancing at the ceiling.

**The knappers.** They work the black glass, sitting in circles in the open fields of fresh basalt and striking flakes off the pillow-lobes. The blades are beautiful, thin as a leaf and translucent at the edge. The fields are the one place where people talk loudly, since the knock of stone on stone is the only sound, so the knappers have to shout, and it is a running joke that they are all a little deaf and a little rude.

## Speech

They do speak with voices, but sparingly, and the voice is for things that cannot be said any other way: grief, love, a joke, a name said to a sleeping child. Everything else is knocked. The knocking language has a grammar of pause and weight. Soft and hard are the plainest elements, and beyond them lie *hollow* (struck against a house wall), *dead* (struck against a full stone), and *wet* (struck against a floor of soft travertine). Each of the three answers differently, so a sentence tapped against a hollow wall means something a little different from the same sentence on a solid one. Skilled speakers use the city's architecture like an instrument. A message can be sent across a district by a series of strikes that travel wall to wall, passed along, changed slightly at each house, and arriving with a faint accent of everywhere it has been.

Every house has a voice. It is the note its walls ring when knocked, and it changes as the walls thicken. A family is known first by their house-note, and a person who moves away carries it in their knock for the rest of their life, a kind of accent. Married couples are said to *tune*: after some years their knocks come to sound alike.

Children learn to knock before they learn to talk. They begin by tapping on the mother's breastbone, and the first real word a child says is very often not a word but a rhythm, and the parents laugh, and knock it back.

Quarrels are conducted by silence. When two people are angry they stop knocking on each other's walls, and the city notices at once. Neighbors begin to tap softly, on both houses, without anyone asking, until the silence breaks.

## The evening

As the light turns to tea and then to nothing, the city begins to tap itself to sleep. No one leads. Someone on a high terrace starts a slow figure, and the next house takes it up and bends it a little, and the next bends it again, and the rhythm passes downhill and around, overlapping, ragged, thinning. The lamp-jars glow. The lampstalks are still letting go of their silver spheres, and the city drifts up toward the Lid in a slow rain of small lights. The knocking gets slower. Then a single wall somewhere gives one last soft tap, and nothing answers, and the tick of the growing stone is all there is.

## The sleepers

The dead are not a separate people. They are part of the city, and everyone knows where their own family lies.

The dead are carried to an open basin on one of the old terraces and laid on their backs, hands folded, with a fern-leaf in the hair. The water in the basins is rich and slow. In a generation the body is a pale sculpture, its edges softened, its face still recognizable, with every fold of the cord-skirt printed in stone. The oldest terraces are galleries of sleepers, hundreds of them, long limbs and long hair frozen mid-drift, with fern-ghosts in the stone around their heads.

The living visit. They come with a smooth stone and knock on a sleeper's shoulder, once or twice, and then lean an ear on the cool white curve and listen. What they hear is the tick of the mineral, still growing, still thickening. *They are still building,* people say, as comfort. The dead go on becoming the plateau, a little at a time.

## The others

They are not alone, though the others are few.

The pale wide-mouthed fish, called *held-breaths*, hang in the meadow-shadows, and the people rarely disturb them. Some old superstition says each fish is waiting for something, and children are told never to startle one, in case it is at the last moment of its wait.

The great snails cross the black fields of fresh basalt, scraping the red moss and leaving clean roads behind them. The people follow the snail roads. It is the safest way over the glass, since a road means the rock beneath is already worn a little smooth, and a snail's slow route through the field is the closest thing the fresh lands have to a map. Children learn the names of the oldest snails. The oldest is called *Long Sentence*.

The drifter comes rarely, sheet-bodied and translucent, rippling at its edges, wider than any house. It moves along a seam between two strata, apparently for its own reasons. When one appears, the city stops. Work halts, and every person within reach of a wall knocks once, very softly, and the sound goes across the terraces like a single held note. Then the drifter is past, and the city goes on. No one has touched one. No one knows what it eats, or whether it knows they are here. They say it comes from above the Lid, and this is the only thing they say about it that anyone would call belief.

## The Lid, and the edge

The young sometimes go up. Slowly, as they are taught, resting in the seams and letting their joints settle, they climb toward the bright wrinkling ceiling to look at it from below. The forbidden thing is to touch it. Everyone knows someone who claims to have, and the stories are always slightly different. In one, the Lid is cold and hard. In another it is soft, like skin. In a third it opens, and there is *only more*, and the person who touched it came down and did not speak for a year. The elders say nothing about which is true. They say only that the ones who go up return quieter.

And when a person has a story they cannot finish, a quarrel with no ending, a grief that has no shape, a question about a parent's last night, they take a small pale stone from the hearth of their house and walk out to the far rim, where the terraces end and the black rock drops into the cold blue. They say the story into the stone, or knock it, or only hold it, and they let it fall. It sinks a long way. The people believe the cold water keeps unfinished stories, and that somewhere below, in the unlit deep, they are all still being told.

Behind the mourner, the whole warm plateau glows like a lit room, tapping quietly to itself, growing an inch, a finger, a hair at a time.
