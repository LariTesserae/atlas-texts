# Who Lives at Low Kiln

Start with the small ones, because they are the ones you will meet.

## The Tenders

The birds, the beetles, the moths and the cedar deer are the residents in the sense that they are the ones present, and they are not a population. Nothing breeds. No clay beetle has a parent or leaves an heir. They are *issued*, the way a stitch is issued from a needle, at the moment a seam needs one. Watch a bald patch of brick for long enough and a beetle will already be on it, wet-shelled, its flint legs still pale from the knapping, burnishing before it has finished setting. It has no youth and no idea it is new.

They have habits that resemble character. One quill-bird in a row always turns its head a beat late, and the others wait for it. A cedar deer in the third aisle has been holding up the same sagging trunk since before the trunk was a trunk, and where its shoulder meets the coils, the clay has grown a saddle-shaped hollow to receive it. A single bark-cloth moth with a torn wing, mended in a slightly different weave, has been mended so many times that it is now mostly mend. If you asked whether it is the same moth, the question would seem to the grove like asking whether a river is the same knot.

They do not fear you. They do not notice you the way animals do. They notice you the way a hem notices a tug. A bird may lean and tuck a loose thread of your sleeve back into the weave, and it is only later you see it has re-hemmed you.

## The Hand

Behind the tenders is the thing they tend for, and it has no body, only evidence.

The evidence is the thumbprint. Come close to any coil and the whorls are there, and the whorls are not one person's. Some are the pads of a child pressing clay for the first time, uncertain and too deep. Some are enormous, a palm that could cover a well-mouth, pressed with the slow patience of something that has never been in a hurry. Most are a middle size, quick and sure. The ridges run in different patterns: loops, arches, one rare double whorl like a pair of eyes. The nearest word for what lives here is a lineage, but a lineage implies death and succession, and no one here has died. It is more that the Hand has been every size it has ever been, all at once, and never set any of them down.

It is not a god. It makes nothing grand. It has made a grove, a floor, a cistern of doubtful clear liquid, and a great many small kindnesses for its own upkeep. If it has a mind, the mind is the mind of practiced work: attentive, unhurried, satisfied by the fit of a thing to its purpose. It thinks in turns of rope. It reasons by what will hold. It has never had an idea that was not also a pot, a cord, or a way of standing a tree up.

And it is not entirely at ease. Every finished thing is a thing that can no longer be made, so it never quite finishes. That is the reason yesterday is always the day of completion. The Hand keeps the moment just before the last press, when the piece is whole and still warm and still might yet be improved. The gloss on the glaze-drips is wet in the way of something a maker has not yet decided to leave.

## The Sleeper in the Kiln

Under the herringbone, the warmth has a source, and the source has a rhythm. Kneel on the brick, lay your cheek against it, and beneath the resin's slow crawl you can feel a much slower pulse: a swelling and settling of heat, once in what might be a long afternoon.

This is the part of the Hand that is not working. Call it the Sleeper, though it may only be the Hand's rest, the way a lung is the body's rest between breaths. The kiln has been out an hour, and always will have been. The Sleeper is what holds that hour open. It is a low, banked, glowing patience. Everything above it is dreamed out of the heat: the coils are its slow exhalation, laid down as it turns over. When the leaves shift toward light, that is the Sleeper's eyelids.

It is not unconscious of you. Stand still on a warm brick and the warmth under your soles will, very slightly, adjust to your weight.

## The Knapper

There is a second maker, or a second habit of the first.

Far off, unlocatable, a flake is struck from a core. Then a long wait. Then another. Everything else in the basin is made by adding, one coil upon another, and this is made by taking away. It is the only subtraction in the place, and the place seems to need it the way bread needs salt. The axes on the shrubs are its work, or its fruit. They are the only objects in the Weft that were not built up but revealed inside stone, and they fall ripe with their edges still keen, as though the Knapper's last blow had only just landed.

Nobody has seen the Knapper, and it is possible there is no one to see. The blow may be struck by the shrubs' own ripening, by the clay of the ground learning stone. But the waits between strikes are the waits of someone reading the core, turning it, deciding. The Knapper and the Coiler may be the same Hand in two moods, one that says *and then this*, one that says *not that*. Between them they keep a floor from becoming a swamp of accretion and a stone from staying only a stone.

## What the Place Wants

The Weft wants to use you, and it means this as a courtesy.

The pressure at the back of the hand is the first gesture: a warmth in the shape of a thumb, as if you were a piece of work it had reached for. It wants nothing cruel. It wants to add you to itself the way it adds a coil, smoothing and pressing until you are load-bearing. Stay long enough and the grove's attention settles on your gait, and the rope under your feet begins to take its pattern from your stride. The unfinished sentence in the rope is one that has been waiting for someone to say its ending.

The old rule about legible prints is the whole ethic of the place, and it is an ethic of care. Nothing here is discarded, only worn, and what is worn is mended by the many small hands that are not hands. To be pressed into the weave is to be kept. Those who become part of it do not vanish. They acquire a print, somewhere, at some size, and a tender to tuck in whatever comes loose.

If you leave, the leaving is not opposed. It is only noticed, the way a weaver notices a thread has gone taut and pauses, hand resting, before letting it run. On the rim, you may look down at your palm and find, faint in the pad of your thumb, a whorl a little deeper than you remember. Somewhere below, another pale coil is beginning its week-long turn, and the brick is still warm.
