# What Lives on the Weeping Stair

Very little lives here, and what does is hard to count. Visitors report one inhabitant, then a second, then find the first was the second seen from another hour. The dwellers fall into several kinds, and the kinds are not sure of one another.

## The Skin

The oldest residents are the ones nobody would call inhabitants: the rust-orange and violet film on the wettest walls, and the green filament in the deep pools. They have no eyes, mouths, or habits, only a patient appetite for what the stone lets go of. The film feeds on the mineral seep and grows a fraction of a hair in a season. It is slick and faintly warm, the only warm thing on the open rock.

The filament is stranger. It sways in still water, and anyone who watches long enough sees that its sway keeps time. It leans in when the ridge draws its breath and streams outward on the exhale, minutes before the falls falter or the horns go quiet. The stair-people say the filament does not follow the breath. It is the breath's first small notice of itself. They do not pick it, and they do not step over pools where it grows thickest.

## The Keepers of the Cairns

There are people, though "people" is a generous word for how rarely they are seen. They are small and narrow, with a stride the length of the old steps, and they are the color of the stone: bread-crust skin, hair like grey glass wool, and cloaks spun from the same mineral fiber as the cords on the copper pegs. The cloaks shed water in beads that roll off like mercury. Their hands are the most striking thing about them, broad-knuckled and scarred white from stacking stone, with fingertips worn smooth as river pebbles.

They do not live in the way of villages. They live in the *rebuilding*. One Keeper goes out at the turn of the light to a junction where the path could go two ways, kneels at a cairn that has leaned in the night, and stacks it true again, flat stone on flat stone, each set with a small pressed turn of the wrist. The lower courses are fused by crust, so the Keeper works only in the top third, the part that still belongs to the living. Beneath that is a column of every hand that came before. They say the cairn is not built. It is *kept from falling*, which is different, and which cannot end.

They count constantly: tally notches, cup rows, knots in cord. They know the numbers never agree, and they have stopped minding. To a Keeper a count is an act of attention, not a fact. If two counts of the same row differ, both are kept. A Keeper might say, without distress, "It was forty-one and it was forty-four," and consider that a complete answer.

They speak little, and mostly in pitch. The gong-slots ring before the drop falls, so a Keeper who wants to say *water is coming, wait* strikes a certain slot with a knuckle. The bell-note goes out and, because sound runs ahead of cause, arrives among the listeners before the striking is finished. Keepers learn to speak into the gap, answering questions before they are asked. It makes them seem uncanny and calm. They do not ask where you are from, because they have already said *rest here* before you appear at the bend.

They go up, and they arrive lower. They understand this the way sailors understand tides. A Keeper sets out to reach the highest terraces, climbs a full day, and comes down into the cold shade of a valley she left that morning. She is not troubled. She sits and wrings out her cloak, and the next morning she climbs again. Some say the top is not a place but a condition of the going. Some say the Keepers have reached it many times without knowing, and that the tally marks disagree because everyone has been counting from a different summit.

## The One in the Dry Hut

In the corbelled hut with the slate roof there is a hearth without fuel, and the hearth is warm. Whoever visits reports someone sitting there, but never the same someone. It may be an old woman turning a spindle, or a child feeding nothing to the coals, or a man asleep with his back to the door. The visitor always agrees on the dryness, the smell of ash and lanolin, and the feeling of having interrupted nothing.

The Keepers do not enter the hut. They leave a flat stone by the threshold, a small round one, chosen for how it sits in the palm. In the morning the stone is warm and is not the one that was left. Whatever tends the hearth is not a person. It may be the only dry hour the mountain has, kept alive by being sat beside, and the shape you meet is simply the shape you would most easily believe.

## The Ones Below the Water

The pools contain other skies, and the skies contain other people, or people-shaped attentions, each fixed to its own hour. Some stand in dusk with their faces lit orange from a low sun. Some kneel under slow stars, and some are lost in a noon that is not yours. They are not reflections, since they do not mirror you. They look *up*, as you look down, and at the same pool it is unclear who is on which side of the surface.

Their hands rise when yours reach in. The water is cold on your palm, and the Keepers say it is cold on theirs. A dusk-woman once held her palm flat against a visitor's for a full minute, and neither felt anything but wet, yet both were sure they had been touched. They never speak. But where a shadow falls toward the sun at the edge of a pool, the Keepers say that is one of them leaning out to look at you.

## The Second Voice

Somewhere among the spires, in the places where an echo returns in a voice not your own, there is a listener. It has no body that anyone has found. It answers, and what it gives back is nearly what you said: *hello* becomes *below*, *is anyone* becomes *this is any one*. Sometimes the changes seem like mistakes and sometimes like corrections. Children of the Keepers learn early to leave it small offerings of speech, a name or a counting-rhyme, and to be gentle about what returns.

A rumor holds that the Second Voice is made of everything ever shouted into the ridge, worn smooth and reassembled, and that it is learning to talk the way the stone learned to weep, through very long repetition. When the ridge exhales and the roar swells and every cairn shivers, some Keepers say it is not water at all. It is the Voice saying the whole of what it has learned, all at once, for a minute, before it forgets and begins again.

## Whoever Is Counting

The last inhabitant is the one you would be, given time. The stair is worn smooth in both directions because more than one kind of walker has used it: those who climb and end lower, those who descend and end higher, and those who stopped noticing which was which. The marks say *you were expected*. The cups are always full because someone fills them, or because being expected is enough to fill a cup.

Ask a Keeper how many live on the Weeping Stair and she will look up at the white coin of the sun, at the cloud rising in its columns, at the far blue ranges laid layer behind layer, and answer truthfully, and differently each time. *Some. Few. Enough to keep the cairns. One more than yesterday.*
