# The Residents of the Weir Quarter

Nobody lives in the Weir Quarter in the sense of keeping a house there. The tenants are the things that learned the tide. Each holds a small post in the arrangement, and none of them seems to know it is part of one.

## The glowing film

The oldest residents are the bacterial mats, and they are the only ones that are everywhere. They coat the railings, the pipe-shoulders, and the undersides of the gantries in a film thinner than paint. Their blue-green light is so faint it disappears when looked at directly. They do not move, but they are not still. When the current climbs, the mats brighten along the windward faces of the ironwork, as if the flow were fanning embers. When the water slackens they dim to almost nothing, and the town's outline fades from the dark like a drawing left in the rain.

They feed on the iron. They are slowly eating the town, and they are also the reason it can be seen at all.

## The red-tipped gardens

Where warm brine leaks up through cracked cellar floors, the tubeworms stand in tight bundles as tall as a forearm. They are white stalks with crimson plumes, and they sway together, all leaning the same way at once. They have no eyes or mouths. They live on what the seep gives them, chemistry from below, and they take almost nothing from the town above. Fish do not bother them. They may be the only residents whose lives would go on unchanged if every machine stopped.

In the courtyard with the fallen boiler, the sponge garden crouches in its own faint rim of light. It is a few dozen gray, translucent lobes, each the size of a cupped hand, arranged over the curve of the metal as if someone had set them out to dry. They pump the water slowly and taste the passing snow. Small pale shrimp live in their hollows and never leave.

## The grazers

The crust-grazers do the most work, and they are the residents you notice first once you know to look. They are flat and pale, about the size of a dinner plate, with too many legs to count in the dark. They move like a slow spill across walls and pipes, rasping the lime from the iron with a soft continuous sound that you feel more than hear.

They are not evenly hungry. They favor the iron over the lime and return to the same flues season after season. They clear the throats of the chimneys so the current can draw through them. They keep the turbine vanes bare, and they seem to know which valve housings must not seal. Between those places they leave the town's crusted skin untouched, so that the buildings look like a patchwork of dark scraped metal and pale, closed shell. If you learn to read the pattern, it is a map of what the town still needs. The grazers do not think of it as a map. Each one only wants what tastes best, and the town has arranged itself so that this keeps it running.

Their numbers are falling. Each year the crust grows a little thicker at the edges, the tide reaches a little less of the quarter, and the outer grazers go without. They cluster nearer the center as the outskirts seal. They die slowly, and their shells become part of the walls.

## The eels

The pipe-eels are long, pale, and nearly featureless, with eyes reduced to gray dimples under the skin. They spend their whole lives inside the great conduits, moving against the current with a slow rippling of the body. From outside you cannot see one, only the brief bulge in the crust of a pipe as it passes, or a faint change in the ticking of a governor as something brushes the linkage.

They are the quarter's hunters, and hunting for them is patience. They wait in a pipe-mouth for something small to be carried in. It is not clear that they ever leave. It is not clear either that any of them has ever died, because no one has found a body. Where the pipes are dry and sealed, in the sections the current no longer reaches, there are long smooth hollows in the silt that might be their old resting places.

## The sleepers in the boiler

A handful of blind cardinal-fish, pale red where the light finds them, sleep in the mouth of a cold boiler. They come out only when the current is strongest, when the flow is enough to hide the small disturbance of their swimming. They feed quickly and return. They have no relationship to any other creature in the quarter, but they keep the same schedule as everything else, and in the middle of the flood you may see them crossing the street in a loose line, following the lamps.

## The Warden

The Warden is the largest thing in the quarter, and it is difficult to say what it is. It has the outline of a ray, but thicker and heavier, with a back so coated in lime that it reads as a piece of roofing until it moves. It glides down the main streets on the inflow, close to the buildings, so close that its wingtips seem to test the walls. On the slack it settles in the square beside the great flat wheel and spreads itself over the silt.

Nothing hunts it, and it does not seem to hunt. It carries a small population under its wings: juvenile fish, a few translucent shrimp, a pair of grazers that ride its back and scrape at its crust. In the still hours you can see the fish shift closer to its body when the amber lamp nearest the square dims, and drift outward again when it brightens.

Whether there is one Warden or several is unknown. Its shape is always the same, and it is always on schedule. Some of the grazers' patches on the buildings are very high up, and something must have lifted them there. The Warden is the likeliest candidate. It may be the reason the town is still in order, or it may only be a very old animal that has found a place to rest.

## What is not seen

Some things happen in the quarter that none of the visible residents explains. A grazer at the edge of a wall is lifted away in the dark, and nothing is left but a clean patch. A lamp that has been dark for many hours comes on at the moment the tide turns, as if a hand had steadied the mounting. A valve that should have sealed with crust is found scraped bare the following season, in a place the grazers could not reach.

It may be the eels, or the Warden, or some resident that is slower and larger and stranger than any of them, living inside the walls or under the silt of the square. It may be no creature at all. It may be the slow logic of the arrangement, with the tide doing what the builders did, and every animal in the quarter a small piece of the same long habit, doing its share without knowing.

## What it amounts to

The residents of the Weir Quarter are few, old, and quiet. They feed on rust, on lime, on the seep from below, and on each other, and they follow the tide as the town does. If you stayed long enough you would come to feel that they are not living in the quarter but working it, and that the gears and the grazers and the glowing film are different scales of one slow machine, still doing what it was built to do, for no one, on the inflow, and undoing it on the ebb.
