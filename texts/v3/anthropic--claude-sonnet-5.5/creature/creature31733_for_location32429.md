# Who Lives in the Sump

The first thing to understand about the inhabitants is that you cannot count them, and the second is that you are one of them the moment you step off the last terrace.

## The ones who are the place

The birds are the population, and everything else is a footnote to them. There are somewhere between four hundred thousand and a million, and the uncertainty is not laziness. A count would need a stillness the basin has never offered.

**The flamingos** hold the eastern pans and behave like a landed aristocracy that has forgotten it was ever elected. They stand in ranks so dense that from the terrace they read as a single pink-grey texture, like the nap of felt. Up close each bird is a separate grievance. They feed with their heads upside down, bills sweeping the brine in slow arcs, and they stamp as they go, one foot and then the other, churning the mud into clouds that hang rust-colored in the warm water. Across an entire pan the stamping is synchronized by nothing but proximity, and the whole flock seems to march in place. They quarrel constantly and briefly, with hisses, wing-flashes, and a rigid marching walk in which a dozen birds turn their heads together as though answering a question. Their nests are small truncated cones of mud, each raised a hand above the water, each exactly a neck's reach from the next. The chicks gather in creches, grey and knock-kneed, thousands to a mudbank, and the adults find their own young among them by voice, through a noise that would defeat any human ear. Nobody knows how they do it. The people of the basin say the birds hear something under the roar, a note the roar doesn't cover, and that this is why the birds are not deafened.

**The cormorants** hold the vertical world: every pipe, gantry, chimney, and leaning crane. They are stained white from the feet up with their own history and stand with wings hung open to dry in the steam, like small black heraldic devices, like laundry. They are the most patient animals in the basin. A cormorant on a pipe can hold one position for an afternoon, and the pipes are so thickly occupied that the turbine hall's outflow has, over decades, been shaped by the weight of guano into a pale, layered flowstone that the engineers now clean twice a year with picks.

**The gulls and terns** hold the dry ridges and are the basin's criminal class. Gulls take eggs, take chicks, take fish out of cormorant throats, and take, on one recorded occasion, a sandwich from a moving hand. Terns are their opposite: fine, fast, shrill things that dive-bomb anything walking near their scrapes, and that have learned to strike people on the crown of the head. The people wear stiff hoods for this. The terns have learned the hoods and strike at the neck.

**The stilts and avocets** work the seep margins in long shuffling lines, sweeping their bills sideways through the film, and are the only birds in the basin that could be described as calm. They move like people looking for a dropped coin.

## The ones beneath

Under the birds the mud is more crowded than the air. Brine shrimp so thick that the lagoons run rust-red and the water has a body to it, a slow turning soup, and a hand pulled out of it comes away with a pink coat of small kicking animals. Flies in trailing clouds. Snails the size of a lentil in drifts along every waterline. Worms, ostracods, tiny leeches, and pale segmented things nobody has named, all of it moving in the mud with a faint ticking that people who kneel and press an ear to the crust say they can hear even through wax.

**The toads** are the only amphibian, and they are small and buff-gold with a fine white salt-bloom on the back, like sugar on a pastry. They breed in the freshest seeps, the few where the mineral tang thins to something bearable, and in the warm months they crowd those seeps so thickly that the water seems to be a single skin with eyes in it. Their call is a low ratcheting purr, and a thousand of them together produce a steady bass under the bird noise that the people of the basin think of as the floor of the sound, the note on which everything else is built. When the toads fall silent all at once, which they do before a tremor, everyone in the basin stops mid-step and waits.

**The harriers** are the only true hunters in the sky: three or four pairs of grey-winged raptors that patrol the basin at a low glide, and a single sea eagle, very old, that comes in off the coast on winter mornings. When the eagle's shadow crosses the pans the roar rises into a sheet of wings and then falls away, and the eagle drifts through the hole it has made without ever seeming to hurry. It rarely kills. The birds' terror is out of proportion to its appetite, and the people say the eagle is not hunting at all. It is taking the census.

## The people

There are perhaps three hundred people in the Sump, scattered across the works at distances that make them practically strangers, and they are, in the strict sense, tenants. They call themselves nothing in particular. Outsiders call them the Keepers, which they tolerate, since what they keep is machines and what the machines keep is the birds' peace. The real job is to be as little noticed as possible by half a million animals.

They are recognizable at a distance by how they move. They walk flat-footed and slow, with a sliding step that doesn't break the crust, arms low, never raising them above the shoulder. A raised arm casts a shadow, and a shadow can lift a colony. They dress in pale grey and dun, cloth that does not flap and has no dark shape, and they carry nothing on poles. They cross open pans in a bent, deferential half-crouch that looks, from the terrace, like a way of bowing to the whole place. Children learn it as soon as they can walk, before they learn to wear their ear-wax. Infants are kept in felt-lined hoods, and mothers say that a baby raised in the roar sleeps better than one raised in silence, and wakes at once if it stops.

Their speech is in the hands. It is a real language with grammar, a fast, low-slung sign that is kept close to the body so as not to flash, and it has an enormous vocabulary for water. There are separate signs for a boil, a bead, a skin, a warm channel, a cold intrusion, a new-crack seep, an old-crack seep, a tidal sheet, and a lagoon that is about to lose its bottom. At dusk they use lamp signals, shuttered lanterns held at the hip, blinking across a kilometer of steaming dark in a code so fluent that the lit windows of the works seem to be conversing with each other. Written messages are painted on boards and set on stakes and read by binocular, and the board with a new fissure's location painted on it is the closest thing the basin has to a newspaper.

They live in the leftover buildings: the salt-boiler's hut, the pump-house, the sheds along the causeway, rooms built into the ribs of the conveyor gantry with walls of stacked drums. Every dwelling has a warm floor, and every floor sweats. Beds are raised on frames. Clothes are dried on the pipes, shared with the cormorants, by an arrangement that no one remembers negotiating. People eat shrimp, toad-fat, salt-reed shoots, and eggs taken under a strict rule: one egg per nest, never from the edge of a colony, never during a lift. Fish come by barge from the coast on the tide days. Anything green is precious. The tamarisks are counted by name.

They are not sad people, though they are careful. They laugh with their whole faces and no sound, which visitors find eerie and then, after a week, contagious. Their humor is dry, salt-dry, and mostly at the expense of the ground. A tremor is "the old lady turning over." A cracked pipe is "her opinion." The word for a promise in their sign is the same as the word for a causeway: something laid across bad ground that holds only if everyone keeps stepping on it.

Their dead go to the orange seeps, laid in the shallows on the outer terraces, wrapped in salt-cloth. The bacterial film takes them within a season, ringing them in color, and after a year there is only a low bright mound that the birds settle on without any particular reverence. The Keepers consider this correct. It is the only monument the basin makes.

## The man and his daughter

At the western works, at first light, which is only a subtle brightening of the vapor, a man and his daughter set out along the plank road to the generator shed. He is sixty or so, gray-bearded, salt-cracked at the knuckles, and his hands are the most eloquent thing about him. She is perhaps fourteen and does most of the talking for both of them, in a fast sign that he answers with a fixed slight tilt of the head. They walk in single file at a distance of exactly two paces, and the road, a floating causeway of crate-boards lashed over drums, dips under each of them in turn and comes up. Stilts stand aside to let them pass. A cormorant on a post leans out and is ignored.

The generator is a single old diesel-and-steam machine, older than either of them, and it powers the lamps of six households and the signal beacon that the barges steer by. It has no name, which is rare. The girl calls it "the other one." She oils it, bleeds its lines, wipes the salt from its housing, and then stands with one palm on the flank of the engine, feeling the frame shudder. She says she can feel the whole basin in it: the tremor, the toads, the turbine hum from the west, a pulse under everything that isn't the machine's. When the pulse changes, she tells her father, and he goes out and looks at the ground.

Twice a year, when the brine tide comes and the pans merge into one sheet, they climb to the roof of the shed and watch the mirror fill. The sky lies down in the water. The birds seem to stand on air. Her father, who has seen this forty times, lifts one hand very slowly, no higher than his chest, and makes a sign the girl says has no translation. She says it means the way you feel when something enormous is being gentle with you. Then he lowers the hand, and they go back down to the engine, because it will need oiling, and the ground will need watching, and the roar, which has not paused for a moment, is already once again the silence they lean on.
