# Who Lives on the Lathe Plain

## The Smallest Residents

The oldest tenant is the crust. It is a lichen the color of old paper, pressed into the lee of stones where the grit slides past. It has no hurry. Between the hour before dawn, when frost briefly veils the plain, and the hour after, when the frost is gone, it drinks. It spends the rest of the day dissolving the rock beneath it, a grain at a time. Some of the gray cushions the size of a palm are older than any recorded name for the plateau. Their brittle spines hum in the wind. The hum is far too faint to hear, but the insects that ride the storms land among the spines, and the spines seem to be how the lichen knows they have come.

The insects are pale and hard-shelled, and they are mostly wing. They are lifted from somewhere below the plateau's edge, carried up on the long thermals, and dropped exhausted onto the pavement. Most die within an hour. A few find the cushions and live long enough to lay eggs in the crust. The eggs will wait through years of storm for a season that suits them. This is how the plain keeps a small, patient, unlikely zoology.

## The Striders

The animals that follow the storms are best called striders, though they have no name that anyone gave them aloud.

They stand as tall as a large dog at the shoulder and are built like a drawn line. The legs are long and jointed twice. The barrel of the chest is wider than the rest of the body would suggest, because the lungs must be very large to work this thin air. The nostrils are slits that close in a gust. The eyes are set far apart, dark and lidded twice, and they weep a clear oil that keeps the grit from scoring them. The hide is the plain itself. It is ochre and slate on the flanks, with a speckling of green-black glass along the spine, where ten thousand storms have driven flecks into the skin and the skin has grown around them. A strider standing motionless on a banded field cannot be seen until it moves, and then it seems less to move than to be revealed.

They travel in single file, head down, along the margin where a storm front meets clear ground. That is where the feeding is. The wall of grit lifts the insects and the crust-flakes, and the trailing edge lets them fall, freshly sorted, onto pavement that has just been combed flat. The striders walk that edge for days. Their wide cupped feet spread across the gravel and do not sink. They make almost no sound, and what sound there is stops a meter away. The line of them moves like a thought passing along a nerve.

Each in the file keeps its nose near the tail of the one ahead. The lead animal does not stay in front. Every so often it steps aside and lets the others pass, and joins the line again at the rear. Nothing is signaled. It simply happens, and over a lifetime each strider walks in every place in the file, and no one leads.

And sometimes one stops. The file parts around it and closes again. It raises its head, turns its chest toward the nearest filament, and stands for an hour or more while the wind pours over it. When the low tone comes, the pressure with no pitch, the whole animal shivers along its length. It does not startle, and its hide does not flinch. It leans into the tone the way a sleeper leans into a hand.

## The Filaments

If the plain has a native population, it is these. No one has seen one grow, or fall, or die. They stand in ranks to the horizon, thinner than a hair and taller than the sky permits the eye to follow, and the wind bends itself around them into weather.

Whether they are alive is a question for people who have time. What can be said is that they attend. A storm that meets a rank of them is altered, and the alteration is deliberate: a spiral is unwound here, a front is combed into parallel bands there, a column is split so its halves spin against each other and cancel. The stones are sorted by size and color into their long ribbons, and the ribbons are the filaments' handwriting. When the striders stand and listen, they are listening to something that is, in its way, also speaking, though what is said might be no more than *flat, flat, flat, flat*, the one word of a very old and very patient mind.

Sometimes, when the filaments come into tune, the tone rises. This may be their laughter, or their grief, or the simple sound of a large instrument being set right. The striders lean into it. Whatever it is, they seem to find it sufficient.

## The People

There are still people, though not many, and they do not think of themselves as its owners.

They come and go in small parties of six or eight. They wear pale, close-fitting layers the color of the crust, with visors of scratched glass, and they carry everything they own on their backs, because nothing left on the plain stays where it was left. They walk behind the striders. They have learned the file, the stepping-aside, the rotation. They too take a turn at the front and then drop back, so that no one among them leads.

They do not speak much. A voice dies at the mouth, and radios were something the plain took from them long ago, slowly, storm by storm. They talk with their hands, in wide, clear gestures meant to be read across a hundred meters of shadowless air. When they must be close, they press their foreheads together, visor to visor, and the vibration of a murmured word crosses the glass. It is a strange intimacy: a whole sentence delivered through the bones of the skull.

They lodge in the glass house on the not-quite-rise. They never leave anything in it. The floor of swept pale sand is their doing as much as the wind's, since each party, on leaving, brushes it smooth behind them with a strip of cloth, and the air curls through the door and finishes the pattern. Anyone arriving would think no one had ever been there. This is the courtesy of the place.

They tend nothing. They do not repair the inverted tower or ask what lies beneath it. On the day when the sun stands at its angle and the grooves throw their slow ripple across the ground, they sit in a ring and watch the light move, and no one explains it to the youngest, because no one can.

What they do is read the cairns.

Each small stack of green-black stone is a different height, and each is rebuilt by the plain after every storm, exactly as it was. The people understand what the first builder did not. A thing that returns unchanged is a thing that can be read. They walk from cairn to cairn across the plateau, counting the stones, and the counts are a message that has been written and rewritten for ten thousand years by a hand that does not tire and does not vary. They do not add to it. They are not sure what it says. Some think it is a list of names, and some a chart of the filaments, and some a prayer. A few think it is only the plain repeating *I was here* to itself, so that it will not forget what it is. They read it anyway, the way you might read a long letter from someone who cannot receive an answer. Reading is how they say they are here.

## What Lives Here, Then

Take the lichen, the insect, the strider, the filament, and the slow line of pale walkers. None of them is the inhabitant. The inhabitant is the attention they hold in common.

The filaments attend to the wind. The wind attends to the stones. The striders stand and attend to the filaments, and the people follow the striders and attend to the cairns. Each is a listener to the one before it, and the whole chain is a single act of listening, spread across a plain so vast and so still that it needs many bodies to hold it. The tone that shivers the sternum and the teeth may be nothing more than the sound of that attention closing a circuit for a few seconds, before the storms turn and the work resumes.

Whoever lives on the Lathe Plain lives in that circuit. They are small, and quiet, and none of them is in charge. And every one of them, on some given hour, stops in the wind with a head raised, as if listening.
