# Who Lives in the Fen

## The Stitchers

The people of the fen call themselves by a word that means *those who sew*, and the name is literal. Every adult carries a bone needle as long as a forearm, a coil of the pale vine-cable, and a flat mallet of cypress knee. When two crests meet and the mat tears with its sound of wet canvas, the Stitchers go out. They go in the first hour, before the raft heals itself into some shape they haven't chosen, and they draw the edges together and lace them, working in lines of six or eight, kneeling in warm brown water that comes up through the seam. The mat would heal anyway. They stitch so that it heals *toward* something: a path, a pool for the fish, a floor wide enough to raise a child on.

They are a long-limbed, loose-jointed people, built low through the torso and wide through the hip, and they move with a bent-kneed, gliding walk that looks like wading on dry land. Their skin is stained tea-dark at the hands and feet from a lifetime of tannin, and the stain climbs a little higher every year, so age is read from how far up the shin the color goes. They wear bark-cloth beaten thin and oiled against the wet. On their feet they wear pattens of woven reed, broad and splayed. They will tell you these were copied from the mat-walker birds. They will also tell you, with the same face, that the birds copied them.

They are quiet people in the one place where quiet is expensive. Over the frogs and the drone, ordinary speech is wasted breath, so out on the mat they talk in a whistled code pitched under the insects, and with their hands. Words are for the root-halls.

## Halls of Talk

Each Stitcher family keeps a root-hall, and it is the only place they truly speak. The woven walls drink the noise, and in that cool dimness voices come out soft and strange, close to the ear, as if the speaker were sitting inside your head. Children learn language here. It is common to see a whole family gathered in an old arcade, shoulders touching, talking for hours in low voices, saying everything they have held back all day. Outsiders find this unnerving at first and then can't stop wanting it.

They sleep in the halls, slung in hammocks of cable. They cook on hearths of egg-pebbles heated in the rare dry ring and carried out in baskets. The halls are also where they keep what they know. A hall with a good stair is prized, because the stair is a ruler: each step is a remembered depth, and they hang the day's knowledge along it. A Stitcher will say a thing is "on the fourth step" and mean it belongs with the other things they know about mat thickness. Some halls have twelve or fifteen steps of lore. No one can say whether the original builders meant a stair to be used this way. The roots kept the shape, and the people fill it.

They do not own halls so much as inherit obligations to them. The roots keep growing. A hall that isn't tended slowly closes, and the plan of a building that no longer exists is lost a second time.

## Holding Breath

The great discipline of the Stitchers is the pulse. Their whole body is trained to it.

Nobody can predict the calls. This is the first fact of their lives, and they've built no myths to escape it. Instead they have a practice. When a swell comes, they take a breath, and they *hold*, and they ride the mat up and over and down again on locked knees with soft ankles. They let go only when the rain begins. Children practice on the bellies of sleeping sextons. Elders practice by leaning into wind. A person who breathes out early on the crest is said to have *spent it*, and a Stitcher who spends it at the wrong moment near a tear may end up in the water, at eleven meters above the streets with nothing to stand on.

The warning comes from the snails. When the counting snails start climbing the reeds, everyone in earshot has a few seconds. Conversations stop mid-word, tools are set down, children are gathered by the wrist. No one calls it a ritual. It is simply what a body does after enough generations.

There is a small school of readers, mostly old, who study the ancient snail shells for the interval. They collect the oldest spirals from dead reeds and line them up in the halls and argue about the ratios. They have never agreed. Some claim the shells record a pattern that repeats every ten thousand pulses and will one day be spoken aloud. Others say the shells only record that the snails were afraid. The rest of the Stitchers listen to this with the patience they give to weather.

## The Governors, Politely

They do not worship the Governors. They do not fear them, exactly. The closest word in their speech translates as *the neighbors who do not answer*. Each of the seven has a name, given by whichever family lives nearest, and the names are affectionate and slightly rude. The dry rings are treated with a kind of formal good manners: you may step on one to dry a net or warm a sick child, but you do not sleep there, you do not raise your voice, and you do not turn your back on the column for long. When the rain of clean stone falls, some hold out their palms. Some hold out their children. No one has ever said why.

The Stitchers do not dig under the moss at the hexagon places. They stitch around them. Nobody remembers who decided this.

## Divers

A few of the Stitchers are divers, and they are the only people who go down to the streets. They work from the thin places, where the mat is a skin and the water is clear, and they descend in long slow pulls along a weighted cable, eleven meters, with lungs that have been trained for the pulse. Down there the light is green and steady and the smooth streets run away in every direction, poured-looking, curbless, furred with moss.

They go for the egg-pebbles. The glassy ceramic ground down over ages lies in channels like a mosaic, and each pebble is a little different: a blush of blue, a fleck of pale gold, a hairline of something darker. The Stitchers use them for counting, for trade, and for memory. A handful placed in a particular order is a genealogy or a debt or a map of the mat as it was last week. Every hall has a bowl of them. The dead are set to sink at the thin places, feet first, wrapped in bark-cloth and weighted with pebbles, and they settle into the channels. It is understood that they become part of the mosaic. Divers say they have seen them there, and that they are not afraid of them.

## The Others

**The sextons** are fellow tenants more than livestock. The Stitchers read their humps the way other people read roads. A hump dragging vines means a sexton has found good browse and will stay a day; a hump moving fast means the mat above it is thin and no one should step there. There is an old custom of leaving the first cable of a new stitch across a sexton's path so it will drag it taut. Sextons rise to breathe when the pulse comes, through holes they keep open, and at that moment they are the most peaceful animals in the fen, long dark faces lifted into the light, nostrils sealed, eyes half-closed. Stitchers who need to think go and sit near a breathing hole.

**The mat-walkers** nest in the root-halls, in the upper arcades where the roots weave densest, and share them with families below. Neither party has ever tried to evict the other. The birds stride the raft on their splayed feet, plucking insects and the little pink lantern fish that come to the thin places, and the Stitchers take a percentage of the eggs and leave the rest. Their cry, the sound of a sheet being torn in half, is considered good luck at a birth.

**The lantern fish** are tended. Every Stitcher child learns to keep a pocket of open water inside the mat, feeding it, keeping the edges from sealing. Some pockets are generations old and have been closed for so long that the fish inside have never known anything but a room of warm brown water the size of a bathtub. The Stitchers visit these sealed pockets the way people visit a shrine. They bring small gifts of insects and lie down beside the pocket with an ear to the mat. The fish glow up faintly through the peat like coals through ash.

**The frogs**, a dozen voices at least, are left alone and are the reason the Stitchers whistle.

## Late Afternoon

At the hour when the light turns copper and lies flat on the water, the Stitchers come out of their halls onto the mat. There is no work in it. They come out to be present. Someone is mending a net, someone is asleep on a warm patch, a child is trying to walk on the pattens of an elder. The air is thick and sweet and metallic, and in every direction the green plain stretches out toward the far rim, with a few hundred trees standing in it like people who have stopped mid-sentence.

Then the snails begin to climb.

All along the mat, mid-word and mid-stitch, the Stitchers go still. They take the breath. A Governor calls, a note in the sternum, and the whole plain lifts, trees flexing, reeds folding, blue flames walking and dying, and the Stitchers ride it with soft ankles and held lungs and their eyes on the sky. At the crest the water climbs the column and hangs, and the warm rain comes down out of the clear air onto the ring and the people and the moss. It smells of clean stone.

They let go, all together, and breathe out.

Somewhere a heron-thing tears its sheet. Somewhere a child laughs. Someone bends to check a seam.
