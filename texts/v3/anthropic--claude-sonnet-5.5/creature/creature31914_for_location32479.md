# Those Who Live Here

They have a word for themselves that means, roughly, *the ones who are above someone and below someone*. It is less a name than a description of the arrangement, and it is also a manners. Everyone here lives on somebody's roof and under somebody's floor, so everyone is a little careful. Before crossing a neighbor's yard, which is a neighbor's ceiling, you tap your heel twice on the stone. It is not a request, only a courtesy, a way of saying *I am walking over you, and I know it.* From a distance, at dusk, you can hear the taps ripple across the terraces, and the answering scuff from below, one soft knuckle on a lintel, meaning *heard, pass*.

## The Body

They are compact, deep-chested people, built like their houses: broad at the base, low, weight held close to the ground. Their ribcages are barrels and their calves are heavy with a lifetime of stairs. The blood shows through the skin at cheek and lip and under the nails, a dusky rose that reads as flushed health, though it is really the mark of thin air. Their eyes are narrowed by glare, creased at the corners in children, and many smudge soot beneath them from the hearth-pot. Their hands are the truest record of what they do. Masons have knuckles like walnuts and copper-green stains in the cracks of the palm. Net-keepers have hands rubbed raw by wet knotted hair. Weavers have a groove worn into the side of the forefinger. Everyone's lips are chapped, and everyone carries a little pot of rendered fat for them.

They are slow, not from laziness but from arithmetic. At this height a hurried person is a person who will sit down suddenly on the stairs with a gray face. So they move at the pace of the beasts they live among, with a rolling, unfussy economy, and they judge one another's character partly by breath. A person who arrives at a door already panting has been rude to their own body.

## Dress

They wear layers, and the layers are a language. Against the skin goes fleece, then felted wool, then a heavy woven mantle pinned at the shoulder with a bone or copper pin, and over everything, in the Long Frost, a hooded cloak of unshorn hide with the hair turned in. Their boots are hide stuffed with dry grass, changed daily. Their hats have flaps and long ties, and a person's lineage is knotted into the tassels.

The dyes come from the walls. Lichens scraped from the old stone give mustard, rust, and an orange so bright against the gray that a child in a new cap can be spotted across a ravine. Each herding family has its color, and the same color is daubed on the fleece of their beasts beside the notch in the ear. So a slope of grazing animals is also a slope of ownership, and you can read who has passed by which patch of scrub by the tufts of dyed wool caught on it.

## Work, and the Lineages That Hold It

Nothing here is owned in the abstract. What is owned is a duty. A lineage does not own a channel of meltwater so much as owe it, keep it, and answer for it, and the right to draw from it follows from the keeping. The same is true of a cistern, a stretch of fog-net, a bridge, a stair. Ask someone what they are and they will tell you what they tend: *I am of the third gate*, *I am of the north nets*, *my mother's people keep the rope bridge at the split.*

The masons are the aristocracy, though no one would say so. They walk the quarries at dawn, reading the pale fault-faces for planes, and they talk to the stone. It is not exactly prayer. It is a kind of professional apology, muttered as the wedge goes in: *forgive the opening, we'll put you somewhere warm.* A master mason can look at a slab and say where it will crack and how loudly, and can look at a wall and say how many earthquakes it has been through, by the tilt of its courses. Apprentices spend their first years only carrying and sorting, and are not permitted a hammer until they can lay a dry corner so tight that the master's blade won't enter.

The net-keepers work in the cold hours before dawn. They walk the windward ridge in lines, checking knots, combing ice off the grass-and-hair mesh, clearing the gutters. They are lean, quiet, and rather superstitious about fog, which they speak of in the second person, as one might speak to a difficult relative. The mist-tenders of the upper city are said to be able to smell the hour the afternoon climb will begin.

The herders live in motion, moving up and down the slopes with the long-necked grazers, and come into the city at the turns of the season, smelling of lanolin and smoke, to trade wool for tubers and news. They are the only ones who shout, and they do it on the open high pasture, well away from the scree, for the sake of hearing their own voices.

There are dung-gatherers, who are respected, and bridge-renewers, who are celebrated once a year and otherwise forgotten. There are the notch-keepers, one for each district, who hold the post in the doorway and once a generation ceremonially mark the new height of the ridge. And there are the children, whose duty in the dawn is to walk the channels with wooden mallets and tap the skin of ice loose, so the water can run to the houses. It is the first responsibility any child is given, and it is treated as a serious one. A child who lets a channel freeze shut is not punished, only walked the length of the dry line by their grandmother, slowly, so they can see the empty basins downstream.

## Speech and Silence

They speak low. In the tight lanes it is close to a murmur, pitched to stay within the walls of whoever is speaking. Loudness is impolite in general and in some places lethal, since a shout on a loose slope may bring the slope down. Over gaps they use a soft two-note call, a falling third, carried on the breath and not the throat. It means *I am here*, and the answer is the same call slightly higher, meaning *and I*. Many days the whole exchange between two houses across a gorge is only this.

There is also a whistled speech, thin and precise, that carries between ridges. Children learn it before they learn to count, and it can say anything the ordinary speech can, though it is used mostly for weather, births, and deaths.

Silence is not awkward. People sit together for a long time and say nothing, and it is considered a compliment. When they do talk, they tell each other things slowly, in the order they happened.

## The Mountain and What They Think of It

They believe the mountain is alive and growing, and that they are its guests. This is not a mystical stance so much as a working assumption, the way a farmer assumes the field has moods. The low groan and tilt of an earthquake is *the mountain turning over*, and the correct response is to stop what you are doing, put a hand flat on the nearest wall, and wait until it settles. Afterward the household walks its own house, checking the courses, and mends what has shifted with the same unhurried attention they give a child's scraped knee.

The notched posts are the nearest thing to a holy object. Once a generation, at a certain thaw, the district gathers at the doorway. The oldest person present sights along a ruled line from the notch to the ridge and says how far the ridge has climbed. There is no cheering. They stand for a moment, everyone, looking up at the pale serrated edge of the sky, and there is a feeling in the little crowd that is not fear and not awe but something like the feeling of standing beside a very large sleeping animal that you love.

Water is the other sacred thing, and its sacredness is mostly sound. People who are troubled go and sit beside a cistern in the dark and listen to the slow drip. It is what the priests would be, if there were priests. The people who have most need of quiet go up to the tarns, black and still and rimmed with rime, and sit there until whatever is in them has settled. Quarrels over water rights, which are the only quarrels of any weight, are conducted there. Both parties walk up together, sit at opposite edges, and wait. It is understood that the tarn will produce a resolution eventually, out of tiredness if nothing else.

## The Dead

The dead are carried to the flat funeral roofs, high and open, on the windward edge of a district. There the great birds come, the ones with wingspans wider than a door, and the body is given to them with no fear and considerable ceremony. Family sit nearby, wrapped in wool, and watch the birds work and the sky. It is said the dead ride up on the thermals.

Bones of the great birds, later, are made into flutes. Nobody thinks this is macabre. The flute's breath is the breath of the one who went up, and the evening songs, hummed between roofs at dusk, are partly conversations with them. A person may be able to pick out a grandmother's tune in the thin bone-flute music drifting from a far terrace, and stand still a moment to listen.

## The Others Who Live Here

The people are not alone in their houses, and they would say they are not even the majority. The long-necked grazers come down through the upper lanes at morning and are given right of way. Small brown rodents live in the floors, warm in the straw, fed on scraps, and treated with a cheerful fondness that is not at all complicated by the fact that they will be eaten at the festivals. Dogs sleep against doors in heaps, and the people step over them without looking. Swifts and finches nest in the wall chinks, and ravens keep watch from the roofs and are considered to have opinions. Marmots whistle from the scree, and children whistle back.

In the deepest cold, the whole arrangement is a single warm thing. A family sleeps in a pile with the dog and the elder's blanket and one or two of the rodents. The wall behind them is shared with the next house, and the hearth beyond that wall is shared with the next, and the warmth passes through stone, through fur, through breath, until it is impossible to say where one household stops. They say of a cold night, *the city held us*, and it is quite literally true.

## An Evening

At dusk a woman comes up the stair-lane with a basket of dung cakes on her back, and she pauses on the worn hollow of a step to catch her breath, the way one does, hand flat on the wall. Across the gap a whistle goes out, thin and clear, and someone answers. A child taps twice on a neighbor's roof and waits for the knuckle-rap from below. Somewhere a bone flute begins and stops and begins again. The mist is climbing, pale and slow, and the lens cloud over the far basin has turned pink at the edge.

She goes in through the low door, ducking under the hide, into the short tunnel of the wall, and the warmth of the house closes around her: dog, wool, smoke, the faint sweet reek of barley. Someone is already making room in the pile. Outside the mountain rises, a hair's breadth, unnoticed, and the stars come out in their thousands over the roofs of a country that is holding itself together, stone on stone, breath on breath, against the cold.
