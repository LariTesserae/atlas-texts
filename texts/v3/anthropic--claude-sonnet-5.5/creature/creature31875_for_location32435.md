# Those Who Live in the Cordillera

## The People of the Lean

Fewer than four hundred people live on the plateau, scattered across the benches in households of five or six. They have no single name for themselves. When they need one, they use a word that means *those who are leaning the same way*. Visitors, who are rare, call them the Lean.

They are small and dense-boned, with wide chests for the thin air and skin weathered to the color of the rosette trunks. Their hair is the first thing anyone notices. They keep it long and unbraided, washed in fog-water and never oiled, and it is so fine that it lifts on its own near the works. Walk with one of them toward a pylon line and you will see the hair on their neck and forearms rise in a slow wave. They know the field the way a sailor knows a swell. They can tell by the prickle at the nape which way the current is drifting, whether the screens are running heavy, and whether the afternoon mist will come thick or thin. They call this *reading the fur*. Children are taught it before they are taught to count.

Their clothes are felted from the silver fur of rosette blades, gathered at molting and pounded with river stones into a grey, glittering cloth. Because the cloth holds charge, they wear it in layers, with the outermost layer changed daily so it never builds a sting. On a dry evening a household sitting together will glow faintly at the edges, and no one remarks on it.

## The Manner

They are quiet, but not from any vow. The plateau swallows sound, and so they spend little of it. Speech is short and low, said close to the ear, and a long sentence is felt to be an imposition, like taking a large portion of food. They have an extensive hand-language, made mostly of pressure, tracing, and the placement of palms, and much of what passes between them passes without a breath.

Their other channel is the fence. Long ago a wire fence was built across the fault, and the creeping ground has parted it a hand's width, so the strands are drawn taut and hum with a tension no ear can catch. The Lean lay their fingertips on it and feel it. They have learned to pluck it in patterns, and other people miles down the line, palms on the same wire, feel the message arrive as a tremor in the bones of the hand. This is the plateau's only long-distance speech. Births, deaths, the arrival of a stranger, and the coming of an unusually thick tide are all sent along the fence. The messages are brief. The fence is old, and they do not wish to trouble it.

## What They Do

Their work is tending, and they would not call it labor.

Each dawn, when the beaded air is coldest, someone from each household goes out with a comb of bone and a pot of fog-water and walks a stretch of the lattice. They do not clear the moss from the insulators, and they do not cut the lichen from the cables. They only *steer* it. A small nudge to a moss front, a blade of rosette tilted a few degrees, a cushion plant rolled aside with a boot to free a bus-bar stand. They believe the plants and the wires have an agreement, and that their part is to keep the conversation from stalling. They treat the whole pattern of leaning groves and curving moss as one large sentence, and they try to be its careful punctuation.

They also empty the troughs. The condensers drip into iron channels and the channels run to tarns, and the Lean draw drinking water from the tarns in skin buckets, taking never more than a third. The crustaceans in the water are counted, by eye, at the turn of every season. A drop in the count is discussed for days. Someone is sent to sit by the tarn and listen. This is a figure of speech, since there is nothing to hear, but it is meant seriously, and the person comes back with something to report.

They eat the small brown rodents sparingly, take lichen and the sweet inner pith of young rosettes, and boil a bitter tea from the red-brown shrub leaves. Food is not abundant, and they are not hungry. They have the lean contentment of people who have worked out exactly how much the place will give.

## Where They Sleep

The Lean do not build. They have no rule against it; it is only that nothing they might make would sit as well as what is already there. They live in the lee of the old works.

The warm casings of the transformer houses shelter most households, with hides and felted screens strung between the iron and the ground. The moths that feed on the residual heat come with the household as neighbors, opening and closing their pale wings on the walls. The Lean count moths on the wall as they count the fire elsewhere: one is comfort, none is worry.

The rectifier hall is kept for winter, when a whole district gathers there. They sleep among the dark cabinets on beds of dried cushion moss, under the arched windows, with the insulators glowing in the tiny lightning-branches of their own moss. They speak of the hall softly. No one raises a voice inside it, though on that plateau nobody raises a voice anywhere.

They do not go into the foundry stack's floor of green slag. They walk the rail spur to its end and back, out of what seems like courtesy, and sit for a while by the ore car with a hand on the green loaf of it. They avoid the winding house at the scarp's lip. Every household has a story about the drum, and the stories do not agree. In one, the cable goes down to hold the fault closed. In another, it lowers the fog to the world below each night. A third, told only to older children, says the cable is not for lowering anything at all: the drum is turned to *listen*, and it has been listening for so long that the fault has grown used to it. No one has disproved any of these accounts, and no one has climbed down.

## The Dead

The dead are carried at dusk to a bench of unbroken cushion plant, wrapped in felted silver cloth, and laid on the green with their heads toward the nearest conductor. They are left. Over a few years the moss takes them, in the same branching patterns it draws on the insulators, and those who return to the bench say the patterns grow clearer and more intricate around the person than around any other. The Lean take this as an answer. It tells them the dead are still leaning the same way as everyone else.

## The Hour Before Dawn

There is one practice the Lean do not explain to visitors, and on this the households do disagree.

In the basins with the most wire, at the darkest edge of morning, the charged mist stands in its perfectly straight columns. On certain mornings, a few of the Lean go and stand among them. They go without combs or buckets and stay motionless for as long as the columns hold, sometimes more than an hour. Their hair lifts and stays lifted, and their breath rises straight up in the still air, and no one moves or speaks. Even the hand-language is put away.

Ask what they are doing and some will say, *Standing in the hall.* Others will say they are being read for a change. A few, if they trust you, will press two fingers to your wrist and say nothing, and the skin under their fingers will be raised in gooseflesh, and you will understand that the question was wrong. When the sun clears the ridge and the columns thin and fall, they come out of the basin looking neither moved nor troubled, only *aligned*, the way a compass looks once the needle has stopped.

Some of the older ones hold that they are not the only ones standing there, that the columns are a gathering and the pillars are being kept company. They say it in the faint, unemphatic way they say everything, and then the conversation is over.

## Others

The plateau holds other lives, and the Lean count them as kin. The rodents, whose tunnels dent the moss, are called *the quiet neighbors*, and a household will leave a pinch of dried lichen at a tunnel mouth in a lean season. The crustaceans in the tarns are *lamps*. The single dark bird that crosses the sky, gliding without a wingbeat, is greeted by every person who sees it with a slow lifting of both hands, palms outward, and no sound. Nobody knows why. It has always been done, and the bird has never once changed course.

It is said there was a time when the Lean spoke more, and that the silence was learned, the way the plants learned the wires. If that is so, the learning is not finished. The younger children still shout sometimes, on the open benches, testing the cloth of the air. Their voices go small and close and are lost. They laugh, startled, and an older hand comes to rest on their hair, which is already rising, and they lean the same way as everyone else, toward the wires, toward the cloud coming up from below, into the held breath of the morning.
