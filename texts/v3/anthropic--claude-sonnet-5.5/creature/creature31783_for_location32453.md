# The Tenants of the Warm Ledger

Nobody here lives alone. That is the first thing to understand about the inhabitants. Every creature is a tenant of some larger creature's work: a colony, a mound, a thatch, a web, or a warm seam in the rock. If you ask who lives in the lowland, the honest answer is a few dozen kinds of builders and, under them and around them, everything else.

## The mound-builders

Start with the ones who make the spires, since the rest of the forest is arranged around them.

An individual builder is a soft, pale, blind thing about the length of a grain of rice, and you will almost never see one alone. They are unhappy in open air. They move in the covered lanes, or in files under a roof of their own carried mud, and if a roof breaks they stop and stiffen, then all turn toward the breach. Within a minute a wall of workers has been laid across it, each one pressing a pellet into place with its jaws and smoothing it with a wipe of the head. The repair is dark and wet against the older, dried clay, and by evening it has dried to the same color.

The colony has castes, and each has its own body. The **workers** are the majority, pearl-white, with small strong jaws and a gut full of the microbes that let them digest wood and leaf. The **soldiers** have heads like amber bullets, hard and glossy, with jaws too big to feed themselves. Workers put food into their mouths. They stand in the lanes with their faces to the outside, and when something breaks the roof they crowd forward and clamp. A bitten beetle can be carried off still alive, dangling from three or four heads that will not let go even after the soldiers themselves are dead. There are also **couriers**, slimmer and quicker, which pass along the middle of the lanes carrying nothing at all except news. When a courier meets a soldier they touch antennae briefly, and the soldier moves a few paces one way or the other. That is how a mile of road knows where the danger is.

At the center of each spire, in a cell of hardened clay no bigger than a cupped hand, are the **queen and consort**. She is a swollen white bag, the size of a man's thumb, wedged in a chamber whose doors are too small for her to leave. She has not seen light in a decade. Around her, attendants lick and turn her, carry away the eggs as they come, one every few seconds, and feed her from their own mouths. The consort is a small dark figure who lives beside her, mating with her every so often, almost unnoticed. If she dies, the spire does not die at once. The workers raise a replacement from the nursery, or the colony slowly loses its will and empties. Some ancient mounds stand silent and full of nothing but clay and fungus rot, and are taken over by other tenants.

The builders do not think, in any way that matters. But they do something that looks like thinking from a distance. They open vents on the sun-warmed side of the mound when the fungus gardens are too hot and close them when they cool. They tend the gardens with a patience that seems almost ritual: chewing the leaves to a mash, laying it in the combs, seeding it with fungus from the last crop, and pulling out any tuft of mold that has not been invited. They send out new roads in the early morning, when the mist covers them, in long soft lines that push a few inches a day across bare rock. Every night the road is a little longer.

## The weavers

In the cycad crowns, the birds have their own long-running argument with time.

A weaver is small, olive above and butter-yellow below, with a black mask that makes its eye look bigger than it is. Both sexes build, but the males do it first and more proudly. A young male will pull long strips from a frond with his beak, holding one end down with his foot and tearing along the grain, and knot them onto a branch in a loop. The loop becomes a ring, the ring becomes a cage, and the cage becomes a chamber with a tunnel entrance underneath. He hangs from it, wings quivering, and calls. If a female inspects the chamber and likes it, she lines it with soft down. If she does not, she pulls out a piece of it in passing, and he begins again.

A colony's nest is a mountain of these single tries. There are hundreds of entrances, and the birds know their own by sight and sound. The great nest is thatched over and over, season after season, until the branch beneath it groans, and sometimes breaks. When it does the whole town comes down in one soft crash, and the survivors start rebuilding on the next tree, using the same fibers, still green.

Weavers are gossips. From before dawn until well after dark the nest holds a background chatter: contact calls, complaints, the buzzing of the young. It is the sound the rest of the forest is set against. When a hawk crosses the sky, the sound stops all at once. That silence spreads down the ridge like a wave, and the fauna below stiffens and listens too.

Under the nests lives another set of tenants that depend on the weavers without ever seeing them. The droppings feed a bright, raw green of fast weeds, and in the weeds live beetles, snails, small frogs, and a species of millipede that does nothing all day but drag its body through the guano and grow.

## The burrowers and their sentries

On the thin-soiled flats the towns are underground. The burrowers are round, soft, densely furred, brown-gray and dusty like the ash they dig in. They stand about a foot long, with short ears, small hands, and long incisors stained a rusty orange from the iron in the soil. They are anxious animals. They eat with their heads up. They rarely go more than a few yards from an entrance, and when they do, they move in small fast rushes, stopping between them to stand upright and look.

Their warrens are so old and so extended that they have become part of the soil. Chambers are used for sleeping, for storing seed, and for latrines, and are kept separate. The walls are polished by generations of fur. In the coolest chambers, at the deepest level, elders sleep in heaps, and in the warmest, nearest the rock, mothers nest with litters of naked pink young.

At each town there are sentries. They take turns, and the job is not always the same animal's. A sentry climbs to a post, an old fence of dead wood or a fallen branch, and stands stiff upright with its forepaws folded, staring outward. It does not eat. It does not groom. When a hawk's shadow crosses it gives one sharp, rising whistle, and every burrower within earshot vanishes underground in the space of a breath. When a snake or a banded carnivore approaches along the ground, the sentry gives a different call, a rattling chirr, and the town reacts differently: the adults go to the entrances and face outward, the young go deeper. A lawn of grazed grass stretches around each door. The burrowers keep it short so that nothing can approach unseen, and the lawn is so neat it looks planted.

## The silk-cities

On the trunks, there is another kind of town, a much quieter one.

A colony of sheet-spiders is born from a single egg sac, and the young do not disperse. They stay, and they weave. Over weeks the web thickens. Each spider adds its own threads to a shared sheet that hangs across the bark, or spans between trunks, or fills the gap between the buttress roots of a fern. The finished web can be twenty feet wide and as dense as felt in places. Tens of thousands of spiders, each smaller than a grain of barley, crawl across it. When a moth blunders in they converge, wrap it, and feed together, and the sheet trembles with the small shifting of hundreds of tiny bodies eating.

They do not fight one another. They hunt as a body, and they defend the web as one. If something too large comes against it, such as a bird or a lizard, a wave of vibration passes across the sheet and the spiders rush toward the disturbance, biting. A hand laid against the web is soon covered in small, cold, light crawling.

At dawn, when the mist is thick, the silk cities are the most beautiful thing in the forest. Each thread is beaded with water. The whole sheet sags with its own weight of dew, and the spiders move across it like stars.

## The wanderers

Between the settlements move those who belong to no town.

The **plate-digger** is a low, armored animal, gray-brown, about the size of a cat, with a long tapering snout and a tongue that seems to go on forever. It ranges from mound to mound at a steady, shuffling trot, nose down, and when it reaches a wall it stops, rears up on its haunches, and rips at the clay with claws as long as its fingers. Within a minute the outer wall is open. The soldiers stream out and cover its back and face, and it does not seem to notice. It eats. It gets what it can in five or six minutes, and then, as more soldiers mass and the couriers race inward with the alarm, it drops, turns, and walks away. The builders will spend days repairing the hole. The digger will be at the next mound by evening. This has been going on for longer than either of them has had a name.

The **stilt-birds** work the roads. They are tall, thin, and gray, with legs that seem too long for the rest of them, and they walk with a slow, high-stepping gait, bill lowered. They do not attack the roofed lanes. They wait at the places where lanes cross open ground, and take couriers as they run the gap. It is the couriers' quick pace, almost their identifying trait, that makes them easy for a bird to notice.

The **banded carnivores** hunt in families of six to twelve, low and quick, with dark stripes across their backs and long tails held up like question marks. They keep constant company, chirping, nudging, one on watch while the others dig. They eat beetles, eggs, lizards, snakes when they can get them, and occasionally a burrower, if a sentry is careless. They are the reason the sentries are what they are.

The **pale snakes** live in the warrens. They are thin, blind-eyed, ghost-colored things, the shade of old bone, and they follow scent down the burrows into the chambers, where the young are. Nothing else in the forest is so patient. A snake can spend a week in a single warren, sleeping in a side gallery until its meal has been digested, then striking again.

## The small tenants

Beyond these, the forest is made of things too small and too many to name. The tree frogs, glass-green with gold eyes, live and breed in the fern cups and never touch the ground. Their tadpoles are the sole predators of their own little worlds. Bark lizards take the sun in rows on the warm stone and turn their throats up to it. The night moths, gray and lichen-mottled, sleep by day with their wings pressed flat against the bark so exactly that a person can put a hand on one without seeing it. The bats leave the top vents of the spires in long curving ribbons at dusk. Under every rotting log are the pale pale things: the eyeless beetles, the wingless flies, the mites, the small white worms that live on death and nothing else.

## What they share

They have no common language. They have no common purpose, and if there is a single wish among them, it is too plain to be called one: to stay warm, to stay wet, to stay hidden, and to be fed. But they have a shared understanding, made of a thousand small habits. The weavers' silence tells the burrowers to go under. The sentry's whistle tells the frogs to stop. The morning mist tells the road-builders it is time to extend. The warm rock tells the mounds where to stand.

And underneath it all is the slow ledger of the ground itself, where each tenant has written its own line over the last. A spire stands where an older spire stood. A weaver's nest hangs from a branch that carries the scars of nests before it. A burrower's town has been dug and redug so many times that the soil is more tunnel than earth. Each generation builds on the work of the last and adds its small entry, and none of them will ever read the whole record.

## The ones who go first

At the edge of a new flow, where the black rock is still ticking and the air over it wavers with heat, the first tenants arrive. They are not the builders, and not the birds. They are small and pale and blind, and they live on the dead things the wind carries in, a dry dusting of pollen, a wing, a husk. Behind them come the mites, then the springtails, then the first lichen. Then the moss.

Years later, sometimes a decade later, a builder's road reaches the edge of the flow and stops, and a single courier goes out ahead across the cooled rock, alone, quick, and exposed. It touches the stone with its antennae. It goes a few inches farther. It comes back. And behind it, in the dark of the lane, the workers begin to lay the roof.
