# Who Lives on the Flats

Most of what lives here is one animal wearing a great many bodies.

Start with the mats, since everything does. They are the oldest tenants and the only ones that never leave. A mat is a city of single cells stacked in layers a few fingernails thick. The top layer is purple and drinks what light the bruise-colored sky lets through, which is almost none, and the layers beneath it are rust and green and a black slime that lives on the leavings of the layers above. Nothing in a mat is in charge. Its chemistry passes from cell to cell like a rumor. If you cut a mat with a blade of shell, the wound weeps a thin orange fluid and begins to knit within the hour. The mats do not know they are eaten. They know only that the film thins, that the warm water keeps rising, and that the world is a place where you spread.

## The chafers

The thumbnail grazers have a plain name in most mouths, *chafers*, because of the sound of them. There are more chafers in one terrace pool than there are grains of salt in a fist, and each one lives about as long as a fever. A chafer hatches in a crack in the rim, climbs into the drift, eats its way across three or four mats, mates in the press of bodies without any ceremony, lays a few seed-grains of eggs in the ridge of the terrace, and dies where it stands. Its shell stays. The next generation walks on the shells of the last, and the water, which is always dissolving and always depositing, glues them into the rim. This is how the terraces grow. The chafers are not just living on the stone but making it, a few grains at a time, out of their own dead.

They are not stupid. A drift knows where the young mat is because the drift ahead of it has already gone quiet with eating, and a chafer can read that quiet with its feet. They steer by the small vibrations of one another's mandibles, and each drift has a grain to its sound that the walkers avoid, and the fog-hunters avoid, and only the mimics ever counterfeit.

## The reachers and the sand-hiss

In the crevices wait the pale ones, long as a hand and without eyes. *Reachers* is the word the flats' other inhabitants would use, if they used words. A reacher lives its whole life in one crack, a few body-lengths from where it hatched. Its forelimbs are folded like a mantis's, ticking faintly with the strain of being ready. It eats a chafer, or two, or nothing for a long season, and when it finally dies, others slide in from the neighboring cracks and take the place. Cracks are the most valuable property in the basin. Some have been held by the same line of reachers longer than there has been a word for *line*.

Deeper, in the honeycomb of the rock, the sand-hiss lives, small as commas, without number. Break a stone and the pores let go of them in a fine, furious shiver. They are the basin's quiet secret. They eat the dead, the shed, the leaked, and the dropped, and they carry on, in the dark of the rock, an economy of scraps so complete that nothing in the flats ever rots. It is simply consumed, and consumed again, and presses down into the layers.

## The fog-drifters

Above the ground, in the knee-deep white and the thinning gray over it, the soft fliers hang. There are the *veils*, translucent, gas-bladdered, trailing filaments that comb spores and drifting microbes from the fog. A veil does almost nothing. It sways, it filters, it lights itself faintly when touched, and it drifts along the chorus's contours in loose shoals that drag their filaments across each other like unbraided hair.

Among them move the *rasps*, thin and dark and quick, whose wings make the sound that gives them their name. A rasp hunts by pitch, listening for the veils' faint hum, and then goes through the shoal in a single stroke, one veil gone and the rest flashing a startled ripple of blue-green that closes over the hole. The flash is not just fear. It is the shoal saying *here, here, here,* to every other veil in reach, and the whole shoal shifts a body-length to the left. The fog is full of this conversation in light. A veil spends its life reading it. A rasp spends its life trying to forge it.

## The singers

The bright-throated things in the pools are small, smaller than they sound, no longer than a finger, with a translucent bag beneath the jaw that swells and shudders as they call. Each holds a single pitch for its whole life. A singer that hatches at one note dies at that note. There is no learning it and no changing it. Its pitch is its name, its address, its rank, and its argument with its neighbors.

What is strange, and what only the patient ever notice, is that the singers do not seem to hear themselves. Each sings, and its bag pulses, and its eyes are turned to the mat, and it eats, and its note goes out into the vast sheet of tone and is one thread in a cloth. But when a singer's neighbor falls silent, when a rasp takes one, the whole pool's voices flinch and shift their weight to fill the gap. No one told them to. The chorus heals the way a mat does.

## The walkers and the sumps

The walkers are the slow weather of the basin. They are long and low, plated on the back like old roofs, and each carries on its shell a small ecosystem of its own: mats in the seams, chafers in the mats, reachers in the joints, mites in the reachers. A walker is less an animal than a moving hillside, and its boom, the deep note it lets out when it swallows or when it is startled or for no reason anyone has found, passes through every living thing in the herd and makes the whole crowd on its back shiver.

Walkers live for a very long time. When one dies, it dies slowly, over seasons, on the edge of a seep, and the chafers and sand-hiss and reachers move into it by inches, and the water starts laying stone around the bones. Some of the terraces are walkers. The rims curve where the ribs were.

The sumps, the mouth-things in the mud, wait for the walkers to come down and drink. A sump is a hole with a body behind it. It lies half-buried at a seep's margin with its throat open, and the herd wades past it a hundred times, and the hundred-and-first time a young walker steps a hair too near. This is the only violence in the flats that is loud in the ordinary way.

## The mimic

There is a stalker that has learned one chafer-call. It is a lean, pale-flanked animal, longer than a reacher and much quieter, and it has learned to make the small grating chirp that a chafer makes when it has found good film. It sits on the edge of a drift and sings it. Chafers come. It eats what it can reach, and stays where it is, and sings again.

To make the call it needs a particular kind of patience. It has to lie for days in the drift's own noise, learning it, letting the sound of the chafers fill it until it can reproduce a single utterance perfectly. Then it spends the rest of its life in that one drift's dialect. If the drift moves on it follows. If the drift dies out it starves. It is, in its way, the most faithful animal on the flats.

The call is still a shade too clean. The chafers can't hear it. Something can.

## The listeners

Among the ridges live the ones who can. They are small and furred, and they are the only warm-blooded things in the basin. Their fur is stiff and close, gray-brown like the rock, beaded always with condensation. They have almost no outer ears; ears, in the Chorus Flats, are a liability. They hear with their jaws instead. A listener lies with its chin on the stone and its mouth a little open, and the vibration of the ground comes up through the bone and into the small hollow places behind the eyes, and there it is sorted: the grind of the drift, the tick of the reachers, the drilled note of the pool, the boom of the herd, and, among them, the wrong note.

They have no names for one another that are not pitches. They keep no fires, having no fuel, and no tools beyond the shell-blades they make from the shed plates of walkers. They eat chafers, mostly, and the fat from the fog-drifters' bladders, and they have a taste for the mimic's flesh that they never speak of. They are, in a sense, the flats' auditors. A listener who hears the false call walks out to the drift and finds the mimic and kills it, and the chafers go on grazing, never having known.

Whether the listeners do this out of care for the chafers or out of hunger for the mimic, none of them could say. They are not sure it is a different thing.

## What the listeners do at the Hush

When the Hush comes, the animals freeze and the lights dim. The listeners do the opposite. They lie flat on the stone, all of them, wherever they are, and they press their whole jaws to the ground.

In that enormous absence they can hear what the others cannot: the basin's hum, the struck-bell drone of the shell-stone, low and slow and patient. It has a pitch. It has always had a pitch. Under all the singers and the grinding and the booms it has been sounding the whole time, and only in the Hush does it come clear.

Some of the listeners say it is the sound of the ground remembering itself. Some say that it is the pile of the dead, all those layers of shell and tooth, still faintly ringing with the noise they made when they were alive. And a few, the old ones, the ones with their muzzles gone gray, say something else, quietly, and only after the chorus has come back and there is enough noise to cover it. They say that in the Hush, sometimes, the hum changes. That it rises by a hair, or falls by one, as if it had heard something. As if, deep in the rock, under all the layers of the animals that came before, there were another listener, lying with its jaw against the stone, pressing up.

Then a beetle scrapes, and another, and the sound rushes back in, and the listeners get up, and shake the beaded water from their fur, and go back to work.
