# Who Lives in the Sink

The first answer is the beavers, but the sink is not simple enough for one answer to hold.

## The Builders

There are hundreds of them, possibly more than a thousand, and no one has counted, because they cannot be seen all at once. A beaver is a dark, wet, low-slung animal that spends its waking hours under the surface or in the dusk, and what you see of the population is mostly evidence: a fresh-cut stump with its pale cone of chewed wood, a slick on a canal bank, a V of ripples crossing a pond toward nowhere.

Up close, a beaver is heavier than people expect, thirty or forty pounds of muscle and fat under a coat so dense that water never reaches the skin. In the basin the fur is always beaded. Fog condenses on the guard hairs, so the animals surface looking frosted with tiny lenses. The front teeth are the color of strong tea, rust-orange with iron, and they are always growing and always being worn back. The hands are small and pink and almost human, and they handle a stick the way a person handles a pencil. The tail is a black leather paddle, scarred at the edges, and each beaver's scars are its own. The beavers here have been in the cold water a long time, and they carry themselves like laborers at the end of a long shift.

They live in families. A pair mates for years. Beside them are this spring's kits, still fluffy and mewing in the dark of the lodge, and the yearlings, who are old enough to work and too young to leave. The yearlings do the least glamorous jobs: hauling mud in their arms against their chests, walking upright for a few clumsy steps, and packing it into the gaps. In their second or third year they are pushed out. Dispersal in the sink is a short trip, because the basin is full. A young beaver leaves the lodge at night, swims the canals, sniffs the scent mounds that mark each territory, a heap of mud and grass with a musky smear of castoreum on top, and learns which lanes are taken. Most of them find nothing free and settle into a marginal pond, or a bank burrow, or the edge of someone else's claim, and spend a season being tolerated. A few find an unclaimed slope where a dam has just failed and the black mud is greening over. Those few found the new ponds. The city's map changes every year because of them.

Their work is more like an appetite than a plan. Beavers respond to the sound and feel of moving water. A trickle over a dam crest sends them to plug it, and a leak sends them to seal it. In the sink there is always something trickling, the hair-thin threads on the cliffs and the seeps and the slow hush at the south end, so the work is never done. A beaver here has never known a silence that would let it stop. At night the whole floor of the basin is quietly busy. Sticks are dragged along canals, mud is slapped into place, and a tail knocks once on a distant pond as a warning that is passed on and then forgotten.

The lodges are their houses and their nurseries. Inside a lodge, above the waterline, is a dry chamber floored with shredded wood fiber. It is warm even when the frost lies white on the dome, kept warm by the sleeping bodies and insulated by two feet of mud and sticks. The plume of steam from the ventilation gap is the family's breath. The underwater entrances stay open all winter, because the ponds under the dams don't freeze as hard as the air above them. In autumn each family drags branches to the pond bottom near its door and pins them in the mud, an underwater pantry of aspen and willow that will feed them until the thaw.

### The Old Ones on the Tell

On the great mound in the deepest part of the basin, some of the lodges are very old. The current occupants are a pair, and they are no more remarkable than any others. The larger one, the female, has a torn ear and a gray muzzle. She is nine, which is old for a beaver, and she has raised more kits than she could name. Under the chamber where she sleeps are the chambers where her mother slept, and under those, others. The tunnels she uses were dug by animals she never met. When she chews a stick, the wood she packs into the wall will one day be peat, and the peat will hold a hundred years of her descendants' work, roots threading through it like ink lines.

She does not think of the tell as a capital. She thinks of it, if she thinks, as the warm place with good water and the smell of her own family, and she goes out each evening to cut alder.

## The Other City

The swallows live above the beavers, and in a way they live on them. Each nest is a gourd of mud pellets carried in the beak, glued to the underside of the cliff, one pellet at a time, over about two weeks. The mud comes from the dam edges and pond banks. So the swallows' city is made of beaver ground, lifted into the air, and both cities are the color of wet earth drying.

Cliff swallows are small, pale-rumped, blue-backed birds with a chestnut throat and a squared tail. They are gossipers, constantly twittering at the nest mouth, and they learn one another by voice. Each pair is one of several thousand, and the parents find their own entrance among all the others by some sense the rest of us lack. They spend the day over the ponds, snapping midges and mayflies out of the fog. In the evening they return in a rush, a whole cloud tipping and pouring into the cliff, and for a few minutes the wall seems to be pulsing. Then it is quiet and the nests are dark. The swallows in the nests are warm and asleep, the mud gourds darkening with dew.

## The Ant Nations

Under the birch, the mounds of the wood ants rise out of the moss, and each mound is part of the same country. There may be a million ants in a single hill and a hundred million in the supercolony. They have no ruler. The queens, and there are many, stay deep in the heaps and lay eggs, and the workers, all sisters, run the trails. Each trail is a worn groove in the moss, and the traffic along it is a steady two-way stream, the outbound ants empty and the inbound ants carrying caterpillars, beetle legs, and drops of aphid honeydew held in their crops.

The ants defend themselves by rearing up and spraying formic acid, a sharp vinegar smell that hangs faintly over the mounds on warm days. Small predators learn to avoid it. Woodpeckers, which are immune, sometimes lie on the mound with wings spread and let the ants swarm over their feathers, as if bathing.

The mounds steam in the cold. The ants keep the interior warm by carrying heat up from below, moving in and out of the sun-warmed surface like a slow pump. Even in a frost, the hill is a small furnace.

## The Ones at the Edges

**The herons.** They are tall, gray, and mostly still. In the drowned snags they nest twenty to a tree, and in spring the whole grove is loud with clacking bills and the croak like a hinge, the young stretching their necks and gaping at their parents. The adults hunt in the shallows. A heron will stand for an hour with its head sunk on its shoulders, looking like part of the stump, until a frog swims within reach. Then the neck unfolds and the beak snaps, and the frog is gone. The droppings whiten the trees and feed the water below, and the ponds under the rookery are the richest in the basin.

**The otters.** They are the joyful ones, and the only ones. They travel the dam causeways in families, humping along on short legs, dive off the crests into the ponds, slide down the slick canal banks on their bellies, and tumble each other in the water. They also eat frogs, trout, and crayfish, and the young of things. They seem to know it is a good place to live.

**The mink and the muskrat.** The muskrat is the small tenant in the beavers' waterworks, a brown, rat-tailed swimmer that builds miniature lodges out of cattails and lives in the banks of the canals. The mink is what the muskrats fear. It is a long, dark, sleek animal, all muscle and appetite, that slips down a canal bank in a single flow and comes back up with a kit.

**The frogs.** In spring they pack the shallows and sing from the floating mats, chins pulsing. They are food for herons, mink, snakes, and owls. Their eggs are laid in jelly clouds in every warm pool, and most of them will feed something.

**The owls.** They sleep in the willow hollows in the day, round-faced, half-lidded, and grumpy. At dusk the small birds find them and mob them, scolding, and the owls put up with it and then fly.

## The Winter Visitors

When snow comes to the plateau, the elk come down. They pick their way along the cliff paths and enter the fog-shrouded basin in long lines, massive, brown, their breath steaming. They wade knee-deep into the marsh to eat sedge and willow shoots. They are the largest animals in the sink, and for a few months they are its weather: heavy bodies moving through the cold, trampling the frozen hummocks, leaving deep prints that fill with black water and become tiny ponds.

The wolves follow them, a pack of six or eight, gray shapes in the fog, trotting the edge of the ice. They test the herd, cut out a calf or an old cow, and bring it down in the shallows. The elk stampede through the alders with a noise like breaking furniture, and then the basin closes over the sound and the wolves eat what they can. Ravens and mink and foxes eat the rest. By spring the bones are in the moss. The wolves leave when the elk leave. The sink is not their home, only a place where food gathers.

**The lynx.** She is the one true resident predator, alone on the talus among the fallen blocks, where the snowshoe hares live in the gaps. She is a pale, tufted, big-footed cat, silent on the stones. She hunts at dusk, waits by a gap for an hour, and pounces. She eats on top of a boulder and leaves the feet. The hares that survive her keep breeding, because the gaps are many and the moss is green.

## The Ones That Don't Look Like Anyone

Beneath the creatures is another population that does more of the work than any of them. The sphagnum builds the peat, one wet cushion on top of another, each holding twenty times its weight in water. The fungi break down the felled trunks and send threads through the soil. The tufa-builders, the mosses and algae on the dripping cliff, precipitate lime from the falling water and are slowly growing the only smooth stone in the basin. Drowned wood sits at the bottom of the ponds and feeds the beetles and nymphs.

And at the south end is the sump, which is not alive but is the reason for everything. The old river still moves down there in the dark, swallowing what it is given, a slow throat that holds the water back just long enough to make a marsh, a city, and a rookery.

## What It Adds Up To

If you ask who lives in the sink, the honest answer is a community with no center that grew because a hole opened in the ground and life had somewhere to go. The beavers hold the water. The swallows use their mud and the herons fertilize their ponds. The frogs feed the herons, the herons feed the ponds, and the ponds feed the frogs. The elk, wolves, ravens, and lynx come and go through it.

The beaver on the tell, gray-muzzled and wet, hauls a length of alder through the fog toward a dam that is already leaking. The hush of the falls is somewhere far off, and the frost is beginning. The swallows are settling on the cliff. She is not the ruler of anything. She is the one who happens to be working, and the whole place, quiet and crowded and cold, goes on being built around her.
