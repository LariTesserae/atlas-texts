# Those Who Keep Hesper Col

Count the living things on the plateau and the number will be small, and it will change while you count. This is not because they hide. The city has a loose grip on what is present in it, and that includes the inhabitants.

## The Tenders

They are the first thing you notice, once you have learned to notice anything. From a distance a Tender is easily taken for a column that has wandered off. It stands a little taller than three people, and its body is a narrow, slung, chalk-colored thing, ribbed like the inside of a bell. Six legs, jointed in two places and far too thin for the height they carry, lift and set down in a rolling sequence so slow that you have to look at the shadow beneath to be sure it moves. The skin is mineral-dusted and takes the exact color of whatever band of rock it walks on. A Tender crossing a rust-colored terrace goes faintly rust. On the bone-white stretches it nearly vanishes, leaving only the shadow, which is the one thing about it that never matches its surroundings.

The head is small and hung low, on a neck that folds like a bellows. What matters is the mouthpart, a broad, flat, slightly cupped blade of something between horn and slate, held a finger's width from the stone. It moves in long, deliberate strokes. Behind it the silvery crust comes away and leaves a clean pale stripe as wide as a hand. Eyes sit at the sides of the head, dark and lidless, with the wet look of river stones. They are not looking at anything in particular. They seem to look at the middle distance in general, the way you might listen to a room.

Tenders are not lonely, exactly, and they are not sociable. Two of them will share an avenue for days, each working one wall, never nearer than a long stone's throw. Where their stripes meet, they stop. Neither crosses the other's line. Later, if the crust has crept back over one of the stripes, the other will sometimes come and clear it again, and it is unclear whether this is courtesy or housekeeping. On the plateau there may be no difference.

### The stripes

Look at a wall a Tender has worked and you will see that the stripes are not uniform. They vary in width and in the small ridges left where the mouthpart lifts. They curve, hesitate, double back, and here and there make a shape like a hooked figure, repeated at intervals. Travelers who stay long enough begin to suspect that the stripes are a kind of record. The Tenders have never been observed reading them. But a Tender arriving at a wall someone else has cleaned will stop, lower its head until the mouthpart hovers a hair's width from the marks, and stay there a long time without touching them. Then it goes on.

If it is writing, it is writing on a surface that heals. The crust closes over the words within a season, sometimes within a week, and the message is gone. The Tenders do not seem to mind. A city that eats its own text might not need it kept. Perhaps the point of the stripe is to have been made and read once. Perhaps the wall is a page that is always being turned.

The word *grey shift* is theirs, or came from them. Nobody remembers who first understood it. Someone found the same stripe-shape cleared at every wall at the hour when the light comes from everywhere, and named the hour after it.

### How they walk

The Tenders are not troubled by the elasticity of distance. They do not look at where they are going. A Tender moving toward a tower keeps its head bowed to the wall it is scraping, and the tower comes to it. Tenders do not arrive. They are simply, at some point, beside the thing they were headed toward, and they continue scraping. Watching one, you understand that they are doing on purpose what travelers must learn by frightened trial: the destination is best not looked at.

At the Turning, when the ten thousand doorways sound their chord, the Tenders stop mid-stroke and lift their heads together, across a mile of plaza and avenue. The lifting is the only synchrony they show. For the few breaths the chord lasts, the eyes seem to focus on something. When it fades they lower their heads again and resume the stroke exactly where it broke off.

### Where they go

Tenders do not die where they can be seen. An old one, its mineral skin gone glossy and its legs slow even by Tender standards, is sometimes seen heading for the deep quarter. It goes down stairs that end higher than they began, and it does not come back. The others make no note of this. But the powder in that quarter drifts in curves that follow no wind, around a bulk that is not visible. In the districts where up is undecided, some travelers have felt, standing very still, that the large absence beside them was old, and patient, and had once been small.

## The Skitterlings

They live in the cracks in numbers no one has tried to establish. A Skitterling is the size of a thumbnail and looks like a seed with legs. The shell is hard, dry, and pale, with a faint sheen in the shell-gloss of the sanded stone. Eight legs, or ten, so fine and fast that they seem to blur when disturbed. There are no visible eyes. There are two feathery structures at the front that swivel toward the drift of the wind.

They live on what the Tenders leave: the flakes of crust that fall from the mouthpart, and the mineral dust. They also live inside the Tenders' wake, in the clean stripes, where the stone is bare for a while and salt blooms fresh. A newly scraped wall is soon dotted with them, drawn out of the seams, feeding in long lines along the edges of the stripe. From a distance the line looks like handwriting, a second script beside the first. Whether the Skitterlings are readers, editors, or scavengers of that writing is a question no one on the plateau seems eager to settle.

Their one collective act is stillness. At the Turning every Skitterling on every wall goes motionless in the same instant, as though a hand had closed over the plateau. They stay frozen through the chord and a few breaths after, and then resume, each where it stopped. They are so many that the moment is audible as a change in the texture of the silence: a dry rustle you never noticed stops, and you realize it was there only by its absence.

## The Colonies

The true population of Hesper Col is not animal. The crust-organisms, the lichenous mats on the north faces, outnumber everything else by a margin that makes counting foolish. They are the city's builders and its digestion at once, dissolving stone at the advancing edge and laying it back down as glossy salt. Each patch is not exactly an individual. It is more like a long argument being carried on very slowly by a vast number of participants.

They have moods, in the sense that their light does. The blue-white glimmer in the crust moves in slow waves across whole districts, and the waves have rhythms. Some are long and even, like breath. Some are quick, jittering, brief. Some travelers who have watched for a season claim the rhythm changes when a Tender is near, brightening along the stripe it is making, as if the colony were expressing something about being eaten. It is not distress. It is more like the glow of a conversation held with the thing that is consuming you, when that thing is also the only one who tends you.

The Tenders clear the crust so that it can grow back. The crust grows back so that the Tenders have something to clear. Skitterlings feed on the leavings and carry spores in the seams of their shells. The system is a circle, and everything on the plateau turns inside it slowly. Anything that fell out of the circle would take a long time to be missed.

## The Ones Who Are Not Sure

There are also those whose presence is uncertain in a different way than the deep quarter's. Sometimes in the Rime, when the frost feathers every surface, a traveler sees a tall figure standing in an arch, with the crystals gathered along its shoulders. It is a Tender, surely. Then the vapor climbs and strips the frost away, and the arch is empty, and there is no Tender within a mile. Perhaps there was one. Perhaps the city, in the hour when it is upholstered, tries on shapes.

Some of the faceless statues have been seen to hold different postures on different visits. The Tenders do not react to them, but they do not scrape them either. Whatever the statues are, the Tenders treat them as neither wall nor neighbor, and leave a wide clean margin around each.

## What they make of you

A visitor is a stranger fact in a place where facts are still being decided. Tenders do not flee you and they do not approach. They go on. But if you stay in one place long enough, hours without hurry, breathing the flint-and-stone-jar air, you may find, when you next look at the wall beside you, a fresh stripe, still faintly damp with what the mouthpart leaves, and shaped in a hooked figure you have not seen before. It will be at exactly the height of your shoulder. The Tender that made it will be far off, head bowed, not looking.

Whether it is a greeting, a correction, or a record that you were here, you cannot tell. By the next grey shift the crust will have begun to close over it, and by the season's end it will be gone. But for a little while you were written down, by hands that were not hands, on a page that is always being erased, in a city that has only just begun to imagine you.
