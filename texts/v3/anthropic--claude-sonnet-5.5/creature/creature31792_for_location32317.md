# The Ones Still Standing in It

They have a name for themselves that means, roughly, *the ones still standing in it*. Nothing about it is grim. It is how a person might describe themselves in a river they had decided to stay in.

There are perhaps a few thousand of them, spread so thin across the flats that a traveler could cross the whole country and see smoke from only three or four hearths. They are not a single nation. Each dome has its people, and each dome's people know the next dome's by the shape of their walk.

## The Body

They are lean and long in the shank, with wide flat feet and toes that splay in the wet. The soles are pale and permanently wrinkled, and tough as leather in spite of it. From childhood they rub their skin with a wax boiled out of sedge seed, which beads the water on them and keeps the warm, constant wet from rotting the flesh. They smell of it: green, faintly waxy, a little like a candle made from grass.

Their eyes are narrowed by a lifetime of glare off the water. When the sun is high they wear visors woven from split sedge with a slit for each eye, and then look like a people who have all decided to squint at once. Both sexes wear their hair in coils stiffened with peat-clay, dark as the rings at the base of the domes. On the backs of their hands they tattoo channels, branching and rejoining, one line for each channel they have personally waded end to end. An old woman's hands are dense with them, a whole gray-blue delta from wrist to knuckle.

Above all they are still. A child of four can stand motionless on one leg in the shallows for as long as a heron does, and this is taught as a courtesy, not as a hunting skill. Whoever cannot be still cannot be trusted near the water, because the water is always telling you something and you cannot hear it while you are moving.

## The Reading

The whole of their knowledge is a knowledge of slope.

They have no number for a meter, but they have more than sixty words for degrees of tilt, most of them too fine for an outsider to feel through the feet. There is the tilt a seed needs to cross a field in one day, and the tilt it needs to do so in two. There is the tilt that will fill a hoof-well by morning, and the tilt that will only make it think about filling. A child's first test is to stand blindfolded on the pavement, the water at ankle depth, and say which way it is going. The best of them can feel the draw against their skin, a slight leaning of the whole liquid skin of the country toward a sea they have never seen.

Their tools are few:

- **The hand-gauge**, a stick notched in finger-widths, used for the depth of everything.
- **The still bowl**, a shallow cup of water balanced on a flat stone. A bowl left overnight tells you by morning whether the stone has moved, or the land beneath it.
- **Seed-boats**, tiny hulls of dried sedge with a single seed as ballast. They are set on a channel in the morning and looked for at a neighbor's field the next day or the day after. This is how they send messages, and it is also how they tell time. "A seed's crossing" is their word for a day, and to say that a thing will take *two crossings* is to give a promise.

## Where They Live

Their houses stand on the sand rings at the feet of the domes, among the twisted island-trees. They are platforms of lashed limbs and sedge mats, roofed with the same pale moss that hangs from the branches, so that from ten paces a village is only a slightly denser part of the grove. It is never a quiet place to sleep. Cicadas saw all day in the branches overhead, and the whole ring is pocked with crayfish chimneys, so at night the ground beneath the houses rustles and clicks with the coming and going of small armored things. The people say this is how you know the house is well placed. A house with no crayfish under it has been built on a dead ring.

Fire is difficult in a country of permanent damp. They keep their hearths on the dry crowns of the domes, in stone-lined pits crusted with orange lichen, and a family's fire may be tended without break for generations. Every meal is cooked there or on stones heated there and carried down in baskets of green sedge. The hot stones hiss the moment they touch the water and are set in a pot to steam a fish or a crayfish. The first thing a stranger tastes is smoke and sedge and a green, cool sweetness from the bladderwort flowers, which are sprinkled on everything.

The dry crowns are also where they lay their dead. Not in the ground, since there isn't any. The body is wrapped in sedge, set at the head of a channel, and left to be carried by the slope. Nobody follows. The water takes a body slowly, over years, and they say that this is right, because the mountains too were carried out grain by grain, and a person is only a smaller mountain.

## The Round of the Year

In the wet months the country floods across hundreds of hectares and the villages become islands, then archipelagos. They lash together rafts from floating fern and algae mat, and families drift out along the new water to fish the flooded margins. The rafts rise and fall with the daily heat and move a handspan in an afternoon. Nobody paddles. It is thought vulgar to hurry a raft.

In the dry months the water shrinks to a mosaic of pools, each crowded with fish and ringed with birds. The people gather at the solution hollows, the deep green bowls that never dry, and there they trade, marry, argue, and settle disputes. Everything smells of warm mud and green rot, and no one minds. It is a hard season and a sociable one.

They follow the stocky, long-haired grazers, not as herders but as something nearer to neighbors. The herds wade through the sedge and leave hoof-wells, and the people wade after them and seed the wells with fish from the hollows, so that a trail of small bright ponds runs behind each herd like stitches. In return the people leave the sedge alone in the herd's chosen fields, and the egrets on the grazers' backs tolerate them.

## Sound

They are not a loud people in a loud country. When the frogs begin at dusk, speech stops. They talk instead with their hands, a fast small language of the fingers that can carry a joke across a whole village. It is considered rude to speak over the chorus, the way it is rude to talk during a song.

And when the chorus dies in a widening ring, when a heron passes or a shadow crosses the water, everyone stops mid-motion. A woman with a pot halfway to the hearth will hold it there. A child in the middle of a step will stay in it. They all watch the silence spread out across the flats, and they wait for it to swell back inward from the edges. They do not know why they do this, only that everyone has always done it, and that the silence deserves witnesses.

They also play the domes. Under each weeping crack they set hollow reeds of different lengths, so that the seep falls into them and sings. A well-kept dome has a dozen such voices, each with its own pitch, and the players walk among them at dusk, adding a reed here, taking one away there, tuning the falling water by ear. Each dome's tune is different and is known to the next dome over, and children can name a dome by its drip alone with their eyes shut.

## The Law of the Unfinished

They have few laws, and most are variations on a single one: *do not finish.*

You may dredge a channel a hand deep and no deeper. You may not join two pools that the land has kept apart. In every piece of work, whether a basket, a raft, or a house, one thing is left undone: a lashing left loose, a rim left unbound, a last row of weave that is not woven. Outsiders assume it is superstition, and it is, but it is also a kind of argument. The land is settling toward a flatness it will never reach because the sea is too far and the slope too slight, and the people hold that they too are only permitted to approach. To finish a thing would be to claim something the country itself refuses.

Their history is not a history of events, because they don't believe events happen here. It is a history of channels. An old man does not say *when I was young*. He says *when the long channel below the split dome was open*, and everyone within earshot knows exactly which decade he means, because that channel silted shut in the year the wet season came late. A life is told by which waters it saw run and which it saw close.

## Evening, and One of Them

On the crown of a dome, in the last amber light, an old woman sits by a hearth that has burned since before her grandmother's grandmother. Her visor is pushed up. The tattooed channels on her hands are a gray-blue delta, and she is watching a real channel far below, the one she waded end to end as a girl. It has narrowed since then by the width of two fingers. She knows this the way you know a friend has aged.

She sets a seed-boat on the water at the dome's foot, though there is nobody to receive it. The seed will take a day to reach the first tussock and another to reach the next, and it will arrive, if it arrives, long after she has stopped wondering. That is not the point of it.

The first frog starts, then another, and the old woman puts her hands in her lap and lets them go still. Far out across the mirror-sky, a heron lifts and flies low, and its reflection flies beneath it, toward a dome that was weeping long before there were herons, and long before there was anyone to stand in it and listen.
