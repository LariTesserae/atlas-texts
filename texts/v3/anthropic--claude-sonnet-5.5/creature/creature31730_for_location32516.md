# Those Who Live in Hush

You hear them before you see them, and even then you may not know which sounds are theirs. The first human voice you pick out of the din is low. It is a chesty, humming murmur pitched under the insect rasp, the way you would speak to someone across a room where a saw is running. The people of Hush learned long ago that the shrill part of the noise is not worth competing with. They talk in the cellar of the sound. Their speech is full of long vowels, closed lips, and hum-consonants that carry through walls and moss. Under it runs a second language of hands, quick and small, held near the chest, so that a conversation looks like two people counting something private.

## The Bodies

They are broad through the ribs. A person of Hush stands no taller than a lowlander, but the chest is a deep barrel, the collarbones sit wide and level, and the breath is slow and enormous. Thin air taught their blood to be thick and their lungs to be generous. The cheeks carry a permanent dusky flush. The lips and nail-beds run a shade toward violet, which the lowlanders take for sickness and the people take for health. The skin is the color of the wet quartzite after fog, brown with a grayish bloom, and it always has a faint sheen, because the air deposits itself on everyone.

They wear little, since the basin is warm. Skirts and wraps are woven of pounded bark and moss-fiber, soft as felt and always faintly damp. Hair is cropped short or worked into tight braids sealed with resin. Older women often trim their hair the same way they trim their roofs, and the joke about this is very old and is told to every child. Feet are broad, callused, and prehensile at the toe. They spend their lives on stairs cut into stone, and they can descend a wet stair in the dark at a dead run.

Their hearing is another matter. Children have it sharp enough to separate the drum-language from the din, and then it fades. By the age of fifty most people can hear nothing above the mid-range. They consider this a mercy of age, the world's shrillness folding away so that the old may finally rest. Grandparents are respected in part because they live in a softer, deeper noise than the rest.

## The Wet

Everything about how they live comes back to water, and they have more words for it than you can hold. There is water in a leaf-cup and water in a fruit. There is water that is owned and water that is still on the way. Fog before it touches a comb-leaf is *the breath*. Once it has dripped into a cistern it is *the kept*. A word exists for water that has been drunk and is now part of a person, and it is the same word they use for family.

A few habits follow from this, and outsiders find them strange.

They do not spit. A child who spits is not scolded but looked at, quietly, until the child understands.

They do not waste sweat. Cloths are kept at the neck and brow, and at day's end these are wrung into a small clay dish that goes to the moss beds. Nothing is thrown away.

And they weep openly. In a place where every drop counts, tears are not shameful but generous. To weep at a funeral is to give, and mourners are expected to let the tears fall on the moss bed where the body was laid, so that grief feeds the ground. A person who does not cry at a death is thought stingy, and the whisper will follow them for years. Laughter has a similar economy. It is loud, shared, and meant to be spent.

## The Lids

Every family owns a cistern lid. This is the closest thing they have to a deed or a name. The lid is a flat stone, usually the size of a plate, carved on its underside with marks that only the family can read. Lids are passed down and rarely traded. When two people marry, each brings a lid to the other's house, and the two stones are set side by side on the same cistern for the rest of their lives. A widow keeps both.

Children begin as lid-watchers around the age of five. They sit at the corner cisterns through the dusk and dawn, counting drops, chasing geckos off the stone, and calling out when the level has risen a finger's width. Many of them fall asleep sitting up, chins against their knees, with a hand still resting on the lid. The town regards this as the truest picture of itself: a small sleeping figure, guarding the water.

Lid disputes are the only quarrels that ever turn violent. A stolen lid is the beginning of a feud. A family that loses its lid but not its cistern has lost its name, and may spend a generation carving a new one.

## The Trades

The town organizes itself around what it does with heat, water, and fiber.

**The rim-keepers** live at the edge, under the outermost stillwoods, in hammocks strung between the great trunks. They comb the first fog, mend the drip-channels, and watch the trees for sickness. They are the only quiet people in Hush. They speak less than everyone else, they listen more, and when they descend to the town they seem slightly dazed by it. The town treats them the way one treats a person who has been away. Their word carries weight, and their trees are never touched.

**The drum-wardens** hold the hours. Each warden tends a hollow log, tunes its slit with a bone rasp, and answers the last drum in the chain. A warden must never miss a stroke, and the training takes twelve years. A late drum in Hush is a small disaster, since the town has no other clock. Wardens are proud, rhythmic in all their movements, and are said to tap the hour in their sleep.

**The vent-cooks** work the hot stone in the low kitchens, steaming fruit, drying lizard, and baking flat cakes of ground seed. They have thick hands and no eyebrows, and they are the town's gossips. All news passes through the kitchens sooner or later.

**The rope-walkers** twist vine-fiber into cordage along a track forty meters long, walking backward as the strand lengthens. It is the slowest job in the town and the most meditative, and rope-walkers are usually the ones who settle arguments.

**The bee-keepers** move among the hanging hives with clay bladders of vent-breath, a warm mineral exhalation that quiets the bees. They are the only people who go into the canopy without fear of falling. They hum constantly, and the bees seem to hum back.

**The herders** live with the goats on the terraces, and smell like them, and are fond of them the way one is fond of a tiresome relative.

## The Company They Keep

They are not alone, and they do not think of themselves as alone. Geckos are treated as guests. At dusk, when the slate-colored lizards gather at the lids by the hundreds to drink, people step around them and do not sweep them away. The centipedes are permitted the alleys. The bats are considered neighbors on the upper terraces, and a household that hosts a colony is thought fortunate, since bat dung is the richest thing you can put in a moss bed. Owls are respected and not loved. When one lands on a roof, everyone below goes quiet for a breath.

The people talk to the goats, the bees, and the trees. Most of this is idle, but not all of it. There is a belief, half serious, that the stillwoods remember how they have been treated, and that a tree cut in anger gives less fog to the whole basin the following year.

## What They Fear

Fire, above all. Every child is raised on stories of the Burning, which may or may not have happened, in which one careless spark took the whole green in a night and left the mountain bare and smoking for a generation. The vent-kitchens are built of stone, kept clean, and watched by two people at a time. A person seen carrying an ember through the canopy would be tackled.

They also fear silence. It is the town's deepest superstition. A house that goes quiet is a house where something is wrong: a sickness, a death, a stolen lid. Neighbors will knock on the wall to check. The worst punishment is exile, and exile is in essence a sentence of silence. The condemned is walked to the rim and sent over it, down into the cold, bare, windy country where nothing hums, and told not to come back. Few would survive the descent. Fewer would want to.

## The Sevens

They count their years in vines. The vine that flowers every seven years is their calendar. A child is "half a vine" at three and a half. A young adult has "two vines and a bit." A very old woman may have "ten vines" and has seen ten flowerings. The whole town gathers beneath the vine when it blooms, in a gathering that is neither quite a feast nor quite a ceremony. People bring their infants to be shown the flowers. The oldest present tell the youngest what the last flowering was like, and the youngest do not listen, and forty years later they say the same things to their own grandchildren.

## Visitors

A few times a year, traders climb up from the lowlands with obsidian, copper, and salt, and leave with resin, incense, and sour honey. They arrive gasping and sweating, half-blinded by the warmth after the cold climb, and stagger through the alleys with their hands over their ears. The townspeople find them comic and treat them kindly. They are given fruit and shown to a warm stone to sleep on, and most of them lie awake, overwhelmed, listening to the town sing itself to sleep. Some go down changed. A very few have stayed.

## At Night

In the last hour before sleep, the town gets close to quiet. The insects thin out. The vents draw their long breaths. On the rooftops, families sit together on warm stone, foreheads touching, because they share breath the way they share water. A grandmother tells a story with her hands. A child lies against a lid-stone, half asleep, listening to the drip.

Somewhere at the rim a keeper lies in a hammock among the combs, eyes open, hearing the fog arrive, leaf by leaf. She is thinking of nothing but the water. Below her the town keeps its small orange lights burning in the green, and its people breathe together in the dark, warm and damp and close, at the top of the world.
