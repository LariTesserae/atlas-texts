# The Tenants of Harrow Deep

Nobody lives in the Sluice Hall, if by nobody you mean someone who knows what the place was. The people who poured the walls and hung the gantries left no trace of themselves except the shape of the rooms. The hall is inhabited all the same, thickly and by many kinds. The tenants have made it a town without meaning to, and without any of them knowing there is a town.

## The commons

The center of the hall belongs to the mussels.

They cover the fallen crane from hook to cab in blue-black shell, so dense that the girders have stopped being iron in any way that matters. The rust lace underneath is only a scaffold. The mussels hold on with their beards, gape a hair's width to the tide, and close again. Ten thousand small closings ripple along the crane's length whenever a shadow crosses it, which produces the faint crackle that sounds like distant fire.

They are the hall's stomach and its larder, and everything else lives off their leavings. Shell fragments drift down into the silt. Starfish come in slow ranks across the floor, dozens of them, orange and purple and dull rose, each heading for the crane at a pace too slow to see. The starfish are the mussels' one great fear, and the mussels cannot run, so the whole beds tighten at their approach. A starfish arriving at the crane has hours of patient work ahead. It hunches over a single shell and pulls with its arms against the muscle until the muscle tires. This is the hall's slowest violence and its most constant.

## The tenements

Around the buried boilers the ground rises in furred humps, and in each hump, in the rows of empty rivet holes, live the small fish.

Each hole has a tenant: a goby with its bulging, disapproving eyes, or a blenny with a frilled brow and a face like a bewildered old man's. They back into the holes and stay there with only their heads showing, hundreds of faces in the curved iron, all turned into the current and waiting for the tide to bring them something. They are territorial to a degree out of all proportion to their size. A blenny will fly out at a fish ten times its length to defend a hole the width of a thumb, then return and back in again, and the entire tenement will seem to settle. Boundaries here are drawn in rivets. Neighbors know each other by the bobbing of their heads.

When the tide slackens, they are the first to relax. Their faces loosen. Some come out to hover an inch from the hole, and a few even cross to another, briefly, to look at something.

## The aisles

In the low vaulted aisles, where the light is a rumor, the walls are hung with anemones. White ones stand in colonies like lit candles, and orange ones cluster among them. On the inward tide they open all at once, and the dark wall turns to a field of soft combs raking the water. On the outward tide they draw in to fat, tight knobs, and the wall looks as if it has been left studded with pale buttons.

Below them, in the tumbled masonry, are the lobsters and the big edible crabs, who make their homes in the gaps between fallen blocks. You see them mostly as antennae, bundles of wire feeling out of the holes, testing the water and withdrawing. The lobsters are solitary and irritable. A lobster keeps its hole and improves it, dragging out grit and small stones in its claws and arranging them at the threshold like a householder sweeping a step. The crabs are less fastidious and more sociable, and sometimes four or five will crowd into a gap that would suit one.

None of them go far. They are the hall's permanent residents, the ones who would say, if they could say anything, that they have always lived here.

## The drifters

Under the broken roof, in the column of gray light, the cod and pollack hang.

They hold station against the current with the barest movements of their fins, a loose school of forty or fifty, facing seaward, all tilted the same slight degree. They look idle. They are not. They feed on what the tide brings, and they read it with astonishing precision, sliding a body length left to meet a drifting shrimp cloud, dropping a foot when the current shifts. When the tide is strong, they are still. When it slackens, they lose their formation and turn slowly in the light, and the whole school seems to be thinking about something.

The largest cod do not join them. They lie in the shadow of the flywheels, heavy and mottled, fat with age, and they move only at dusk, when the whole hall begins to knock. That is the cod's language, the rolling wooden thud of muscles drumming against swim bladders, one answering another across the nave. It goes on for an hour. It is territory and courtship and something else that no one has a word for, and then it stops, the way a room of people stops talking when a door opens.

## The night shift

At night the congers come out.

They live in the mouths of the pipes, thick as a man's thigh and longer than he is tall, with heads like gray stumps and small, cold, patient eyes. In the day they are barely visible, a smooth shape in a round dark. When the light goes and the tide turns outward, they pour from the pipes and flow along the silt like something poured, muscular and unhurried. They hunt the floor. They take small cod, crabs, whatever sleeps badly. They have no fear of anything in the hall except, perhaps, each other. Two congers meeting in the open nave will stop, face to face, and stay that way a long while, mouths slightly open, before one gives way.

The hall's other tenants know when they emerge. The blennies back deeper into their holes. The lobsters retreat behind their thresholds. The mussels, who cannot know anything, close.

## The one at the turbine

In the flooded penstock at the seaward end, where the tide drives the turbine and the drone is loudest, lives a conger older than any other in the hall.

It is very large. Its gray has gone nearly white along the belly and its skin is scarred by decades of small quarrels. It lies coiled in the pipe just downstream of the blades, in a place where the water is fast and loud and no other fish will stay. The vibration is enormous there. It seems to like it. It lies with its head resting on the concrete lip and its body rocking very slightly with the turbine's pitch.

It rarely hunts. What it does, mostly, is keep watch. It has learned, in whatever way an eel learns, that the lamps in the control gallery brighten with the current, and that a bright lamp draws plankton, and plankton draws the small fish, and small fish draw everything else. Long ago the amber bulbs became the hall's hearth. On strong tides the plankton swirls around them in gold-lit clouds, and the fish gather to feed, and the old conger, without leaving the penstock, has only to lift its head and wait. It waits well.

Nothing in the hall is more aware of the tide. It seems to feel the turn before the turbine changes its note. When slack water comes and the drone sinks to a murmur, it draws its head back into the dark of the pipe, and for a little while the whole hall is without its center.

## The visitors

Once in a while a seal comes down.

It enters through the breach in the roof, dark against the light, and swings through the column in a long easy loop with the water streaming off its whiskers. The cod see it before anything else does. The school tightens, then breaks. The blennies vanish into their holes. The mussels shut. The anemones, all at once, draw in, and the wall of white candles goes out. For a full minute the hall holds perfectly still, listening to the turbine, while the seal takes what it came for and climbs back toward the light.

Then the hall resumes. The anemones reopen. A goby edges out. Somewhere a crab clicks, and someone knocks, and the sour chime of the chain comes down through the vaults once more, and the residents, who have never known what the building was for, go on using it for everything.
