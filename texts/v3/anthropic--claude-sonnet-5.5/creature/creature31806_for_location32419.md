# Who Keeps the Nursery

Nothing in the hollow lives alone, and it is hard to say where one inhabitant stops. If you count what you can see, you find beetles, amphibians, moths, and worms. If you keep counting, the list goes strange, and by the end you are not sure whether you have counted residents or organs.

## The Agreement

The oldest resident has no name, because naming requires a someone. The nearest word is the **Agreement**.

It is the vine-braids, the ribbon-ferns, the bladder-weeds, the white filaments in the seep, the moss in its three layers, and the canopy of downward leaves, all at once. It has no seat of thought. When the Agreement decides something, such as where a rib of stone should thicken, no part of it issues the order. A thousand filaments touch a thousand others, each passing along a small adjustment in the pattern of its charge, until the whole hollow leans one way, the way water leans toward a drain. Then the crystal aligns.

It thinks in placements, not words. Its memory is the stone. Every pale rib is something it once concluded, and it can be reread by the same filaments that wrote it. The old ribs near the rim, fully hardened and clear as ice, are its long convictions. The soft, warm ones near the floor are things it is still turning over.

It wants very little, and steadily. It wants the temperature held just above freezing. It wants the hum in tune. It wants no bare surface. It wants the chorus loud enough to set the stone and never so loud that the beaded canopy lets go early. It is not kind and not cruel. It is attentive, the way a hand cupped around a candle is attentive.

It notices visitors. Your wet sleeves carry salt and iron into the moss, and the filaments taste both. For a few minutes, if you stand still, your pulse enters the hum. The beetles near you will drift toward your heart-rate and chime in time with it, and the vine will pulse a half-beat behind, as if trying it on. Then the interest passes. You have been read and filed, perhaps as a shelf, perhaps as a small warm rib that moved briefly through the room.

## The Glass-beetles

The beetles are the treble of the place and, in a sense, its youth. They live one season. Each hatches from a pearl of clear jelly in the top layer of moss, already ringing, its wing-cases pitched slightly flat and sour. The elders correct the young by ringing at them, sometimes for a whole day, until the newborn's note slides up and locks into the chord. A beetle that fails to lock is not punished. It is simply not carried, and it wanders to the edge of the moss, where it rings alone until it dies and is drawn down.

Their shells are transparent, and when the blue wave passes you can see the light go through them and out the other side. A thousand beetles across the moss flare together, each a small struck bell. They do not know they are building anything. They know only the key, and the deep contentment of being in it.

## The Cave-peepers

The peepers are the bass and the argument. They are pale and soft, their throats clear as blown glass, so that when one calls you can see the sac swell with faint blue light and empty again. They gather at pool-edges in groups of a dozen or two and pulse in unison, then split into two rival rhythms and hold the disagreement for minutes at a time, each side leaning louder, until one yields or both fall silent together and begin again on a shared beat. Nobody has established that this is a quarrel. Nobody has established that it isn't.

They are the warm ones. They spend their days half in the steaming pools and their nights on the moss, carrying heat outward into the floor as they move. They live for years. When one dies, the others gather around the spot and sing a long, low note, and the moss draws it down. By the next blue wave a vein of new crystal shows beneath the place, a little brighter than its neighbors. The peepers know these veins. They sit on them to call, and their voices carry differently there.

## The Sheet-moths

The moths hang from the canopy like pages left to dry. Their wings are the size of hands and heavy with a fine scale-dust that carries the same patterns as the vine. They are the slowest of the animals, some of them decades old, and they hardly ever fly. When the blue wave passes beneath them they beat their wings against the leaves in a papery clatter, and this clatter is not noise but punctuation. It marks the end of one phrase in the hollow's long music and the start of the next.

They are also, without intending it, the makers of rain. When enough of them clatter together, in the seconds after a wave, the whole canopy shivers, and the held beads let go at once. A fine rain crosses the room and stops. The moths settle, and the leaves begin filling again.

## The Threadworms

Below everything are the threadworms, in the root-felt, in their millions. They are the hiss under the chorus. They are thin as hair and pale as spit, and each carries in its body a single bright grain that it will not spend. They are the clerks and couriers of the place. They knit the layers of felt together and thread themselves through the vine-braids, and some of the grains that move against the flow in the transparent tubes are almost certainly worms, swimming in ranks against the current. Others may not be. No one has looked closely enough, and splitting a braid kills the thing you meant to observe.

The worms do not sing so much as tally. Their thousand clicks are the running sum of the room: how much sound, how much heat, how much stone set, how much left to do. The Agreement listens to them the way a mind listens to its own heartbeat.

## The Line-tenders

At the rim, where the frost stops at its ruled edge, live the smallest residents: bristled gray-white mites, no bigger than rice, in a ring around the hollow's whole circumference. They keep the line. Each exhales a mote of warmth against the cold, and the ring holds the exact boundary between the frost above and the wet below. They march slowly along the boundary in both directions, passing without pause, and where one has died the line wavers for an hour until another fills the gap. They never come down into the moss, and they never go up into the slot. Nobody has seen one sleep.

## The Flat Note

There is one voice in the hollow that belongs to no animal: the flute-note of the algae-mat, released each time a bubble breaks. It is always slightly flat, and the chorus around it does not correct it.

The Agreement could correct it. It tunes everything else. Its refusal seems deliberate, a small unresolved interval that keeps the music leaning forward. A perfectly resolved chord has nowhere left to go. The flat note is what keeps the hollow from finishing.

## What They Are Together

Ask whether the hollow is a community or a creature and you get no answer, because both are true and the difference has not yet been decided. The beetles are the shivering skin of something. The peepers are its slow blood. The moths are its breath, the worms its nerves, the line-tenders its lips, and the Agreement is what they are all inside of, the way a dream is inside a sleeper.

It has been building toward a shape for longer than the frost has held the rim. It may be a den, an animal, a mind, or simply a very good room. The residents do not need to know. They ring and clatter and click and hum, the stone hardens a fingernail further, and the whole half-built creature turns once in its sleep and goes on working.
