# Who Lives on Halvane Shelf

## The People

The people of Halvane are quiet in a way that looks like manners and is really physiology. They have lived for so many generations in a place where sound dies at arm's length that their voices have settled into a low, level register, and their ears have gone fine. A Halvaner can hear a neighbour's kettle three floors down through slate. They can hear a cable-car's grip slipping a quarter-mile off. They talk with their hands more than visitors expect, small flat gestures held close to the chest, like people passing notes under a table.

They are pale, and not from any lack of health. Their skin has the cool matte look of something that has never been dried by wind. Their eyes are large and dark and slow to narrow, since nothing here ever asks them to squint. They wear moss-silk in every weight: grey-green oilcoats, close-woven under-layers, hoods lined with the softest thread. Rain beads on them and rolls off. Many of the old have a faint bloom of green in the creases of their cuffs and collars, and no one bothers to brush it off. "Let it stay," they say. "It's only company."

They do not hurry, but they are not idle. The city's motion is steady, and they move at its pace: stairs taken one worn trough at a time, hands on the cold rail, breath timed to the climb. Visitors who rush are pitied a little and forgiven, as one forgives a dog for barking.

They keep the tide-day, not the sun-day. The cloud turns over once every nineteen hours and a bit, and the whole city lives inside that cycle. They have a word for each of its phases, *low, turning, high, drawing*, and the word for *late* is the same as the word for *after the tide*. Sleep comes in two shifts, one at each slack water of the cloud, so that the city is never wholly awake or wholly asleep. When a Halvaner is asked how old they are, they answer in tides and then, out of courtesy to the visitor, do the arithmetic in years.

Children are named for the domes. Each dome has a note where the wind crosses its fissure, and a child is given the name of the note that was sounding when they were born. Some of these names are almost unpronounceable to outsiders, only a breath shaped in the mouth, but the residents use them easily. An old woman might call across a courtyard with her lips barely parted, and three people turn, because she has sounded the pitch of one particular hill.

## The Trades

**The cutters** are the broadest-shouldered people in the city, and it is always shoulders and never height, because they spend their lives bent under low tarpaulin roofs. They work in teams of five or six with long irons, slicing turf in rectangles so exact that a cutter's stack can be identified by its edges. They speak seldom on the sinks. When they do it is in the flat clipped language of people who spend all day listening to a blade. Their hands are stained black to the wrist. Their forearms carry the pale spidery scars of the roots they cut through. They are the keepers of the rotation map, the oldest document in Halvane, and a cutter who loses or mars a page of it is sent to walk the whole forty-year circuit on foot, alone, to see what he has damaged.

**The weir-wrights** climb the iron towers on the ridges in all weathers, though there is only one weather, and they mend the steel threads with tiny hooked needles. It is delicate, dripping work. They are the thinnest people in the city, wiry and long-fingered, and they tend to be quietly vain about their balance. On the high-tide days, when the cloud lifts and the light goes pearl, they sit at the tops of their towers with their legs hanging over the wire and look at nothing. Some of them say they are listening to the cloud. Others say they are only resting.

**The spinners and racksmen** of the moss-silk mills work in steam and damp, in rooms warm as a held breath. The racksmen tend the pale hair-moss in the upper valleys, misting it and combing it, and they talk to it in soft nonsense syllables. Everyone knows they do this. Everyone also knows that the racks that get talked to grow better, and no one has asked why. The spinners, mostly women and mostly old, draw the thread with a movement like unwinding a wet cobweb from a branch. Their fingertips have gone smooth as glass.

**The lamplighters** go out twice a tide-day with their long hooked poles and their small red cans of oil. They are the only people in Halvane who greet strangers unprompted. It is a professional courtesy: someone must know who is on the stairs after dark.

**The bell-keepers** ring the muffled bells for signalling. Their *dunk* has no music in it at all, but a good keeper can shade it, shorter or longer or rounder, into a private language the whole city reads. A quick double *dunk* means a cable-car is arriving. A slow triple means the cloud is turning early. A single unanswered *dunk* in the small hours means someone has died, and the neighbourhood wakes to sit up with the family.

**The herders** go out to the domes for weeks at a time with a pack, a staff, and a hooded lamp. They are half-solitary by temperament and half-feral by habit. They keep the goats but do not own them, and they will tell you so with a slight stiffness, as though correcting a rumour. They know which domes are warm. They know which grooves run where. They sleep on the warm stone in autumn with their coats spread out, and they say that sleeping there makes for a certain kind of dream, slow and turning, that they do not describe.

## The Small Residents

The pale salamanders in the gutters have red gills like tiny feathers, and they are counted among the citizens. Landlords are fined for disturbing them. On the eve of a wedding, families put a spoonful of honey beside a gutter for luck. The salamanders do not eat honey, but the ants do, and the salamanders eat the ants. Everyone knows this and does it anyway. It is a way of feeding the drains.

Mist-coloured wrens keep the chimneys and are the source of a great deal of soot-based folklore. Pale moths gather at the amber lamps in slow drifts, and the children keep count of them. The filmy ferns and the orchids and the green-glowing fungus live on every surface, so densely that the city and its lodgers are hard to tell apart. A window frame is a garden. A doorstep is a garden. A sleeping dog in the sun, if there were sun, would soon be one.

## The Herons

The herons are tall and grey and patient. They come in the low hours, when the cloud drops to the rooftops, and each takes a post on the ridge of a mill, one leg lifted, neck folded, head tilted slightly down to look into the street. They do not fish. They do not call. They do not even seem to breathe. When the cloud lifts they are gone, and no one has ever seen one leave.

The city's manners regarding the herons are complete and unspoken. You do not point at them. You do not count them, because counting is thought to be a form of asking, and asking is thought to invite an answer. You do not throw anything. You do not, if you can help it, look at one for longer than it takes to nod. Children learn this the way they learn not to stare at the dying: without ever quite being told.

The old herders, if pressed, will offer a private theory. The herons are not birds that come to the city. They are the city's other half, the part of its attention that stands outside the walls of the sinks and watches it work. They are the tide's overseers, or its inspectors, or its witnesses. They look down at the streets because that is where the finished work is kept.

## The Other Resident

There is one more inhabitant of Halvane Shelf, and the city does not name it. It is not an animal or a spirit or a god. It is barely even a presence. But the city lives its whole life in relation to it, the way a house lives in relation to its foundation.

The evidence is the same evidence the city has always had. The cloud rises and falls on a cycle that no wind or season can shift. The stone is warm on certain domes at certain hours, as though something inside had just left the room. There are grooves under the largest domes, shallow and parallel, running down the slope in fans and spirals, and older than the moss that follows them. Anyone who has knelt to look at those grooves and run a hand along them knows, in the way the body knows things before the mind admits them, that they are *tool marks*. They are not the wandering scratches of ice or the sinuous cuts of water. They are the regular, patient, even scoring left by something turning against something else, over and over, for a length of time that makes hundreds of millions of years seem like a single pass.

The children say the grooves are the mountain's fingerprints. The children are close. A fingerprint is the mark left by touch. But the mountain has finished being a mountain, and whatever touched it has not finished touching. The tide is nineteen hours and some minutes, and it is exact. If you set your palm on a warm dome at the slack of the cloud and hold still, you may feel a rhythm in the stone that is slower than a pulse and more even than a breath. It is the turning of something large and unhurried, running at speed so low it looks like stillness, shaving the world down by a hair's width each revolution.

If this is the lathe, then the mountains were its work, and their wearing-down is the work continuing. And the city, the moss, the ferns, the salamanders, and the pale-skinned people with their hands held close, are what a lathe leaves behind: the fine curled shavings that gather at the foot of the cut, that go on living for a while in the warmth of the turning, and that build their small houses out of the swarf.

No one in Halvane would put it this way. They would find it discourteous. They would say only that the stone is warm, that the tide keeps time, that the grooves are old, and that it is good manners to keep your voice low near the big domes. They would say the herons are watching, and that it is best not to count them. And then they would go on with their work, cutting peat, spinning silk, mending wire, ringing the felted bells, and living, gently and completely, in the steady exhaled warmth of a thing that has never once looked up to notice them, and has never once stopped.
