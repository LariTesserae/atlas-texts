# The Setters of the Wet Loft

## First, the Sound

Before anyone, there is the hammering, and you should understand it as the inhabitant's first form. The people of the Wet Loft reach you as a sound that arrives ahead of their bodies, the way their walls arrive ahead of the wall. Tock-tock-tock, unhurried, from behind the stone. Only later, if ever, do you see a shoulder, a hand, or the flat gleam of an antler-pick going away around a corner that was not there a moment ago.

There is more than one hammerer. Listen through a whole day and the rhythm changes: a slow heavy beat, a quick fussy tapping, a long pause and then a single ringing strike that seems to close a sentence. They are in conversation. It is the nearest thing the place has to speech, and it is entirely about what will exist next.

## What They Look Like, When You Glimpse Them

They are low and broad through the shoulder, built like people who spend their lives working on their knees and half-inside things. Their spines curve forward in a permanent stoop that lets them pass through the seams between terraces. Their arms are long. Their hands are the most remarkable part: wide, cracked at every joint, the fingertips flattened and pale, callused into something like horn. Each thumb bears a scar in the same place, a small crescent on the pad, from the flint-edge of the fresh chisel-marks. Everyone has it. It is how they know one another in the dark. They touch thumbs in greeting, and the touch is a small acknowledgment that the stone has cut them both.

Their skin is the color of unfired clay, close to the frogs, and always faintly damp. They do not sweat so much as *condense*. Their hair is worn short or bound in tight cords of the same wet knot as the ladder. Their eyes are large and dark and slow to narrow, adapted to the orange fat-lamps and the sour green of the lichen. In the red light of the vent the eyes give back nothing at all, and the faces seem to have holes in them.

The oddest feature is the moss. It grows on their shoulders and along the tops of their forearms, thick, layered, and ancient-looking, like the moss on the walls. It is not a sickness. They cultivate it, and it is a mark of standing. But it grows in the wrong order. The youngest have the deepest moss, and it thins as they age until the oldest of them are smooth, bare, and pale as river pebbles. No one in the room seems to find this strange. Age is installed here, and you receive it before you have earned it, and you spend your life wearing it down.

## The Work

Nothing they do is finished, and they would not want it to be.

**The setters** lay the stone. They dress each block with flint and antler-pick until the tool marks are crisp and scalloped, and then they *stop*. This is the discipline. They do not smooth. They do not weather. A setter who softens an edge is corrected, not harshly, but with a long silence and a mallet laid pointedly across the offender's knees. The newness of the stone is sacred to them. A block's sharpness is its promise that it has not yet been used.

**The wearers** work beside them, and they are the strangest craft in the room. A wearer's whole life is spent on the stairs. They kneel and carve the shallow dishes into the steps, hollow by hollow, to the exact depth of a foot that will pass ten thousand times. They work from knowledge of a walking that has not occurred. Each wearer carries a memory of the people who will come, their gait and their weight, and shapes the stone to fit them. They do not consider this forgery. They think of it as *hospitality*: making the step ready for the feet that are owed to it. When a visitor finally puts a foot in one of the dishes and finds it a perfect fit, the wearer who carved it is somewhere in the walls, and the tock-tock-tock goes quiet for a moment.

**The mossers** are the gardeners, though the word does not carry the tenderness. They tend the ancient growth, the hundred-year layers laid over the new blocks. They bring it in from somewhere, or grow it from a mother-bed they will not show anyone. They press it into the mortarless joints with the flats of their thumbs and then wait for it to have been there forever. They speak to it. They are never sure whether the moss is growing forward or backward, and they count that uncertainty as the health of the crop.

**The lashers** are the last guild and the busiest. They tie the poles, the cord, the frames, and every knot is the same knot, an ugly over-and-under hitch that can be tied in the dark with wet hands. They do not teach it. Children simply find they know it, the way you know how to swallow. The vines eat the scaffolding, and the lashers replace it, and the vines regrow to be eaten. The lashers speak of the vines with the weary respect of people talking about an in-law who is always right and always hungry.

## How They Live

They do not have houses. They have *hours*. The room's weather is their calendar, and each part of the cycle belongs to a different life.

When the shaft of light reaches the lowest terrace and the vent surges, they come out, all of them, and stand in the downpour. They do not shelter. The warm heavy rain is their bath, their feast, their rite. They stand with their faces up and their mouths open, and the water runs down through the moss on their shoulders and out again clean. The room is a white roar and they are silent in it.

In the long dripping afternoon they work. In the evening they eat: fiddleheads steamed in clay pots set directly on the hot stone over the vent, moss-cakes pressed with vine-seed oil, and the small pale eggs of the tree-frogs, taken very sparingly and with an apology. The fat-lamps burn the oil from those same seeds. Everything here is part of a loop, and the loop is the only thing they call sacred besides the stone.

At night the frost comes down through the sky-hole and they climb. They sleep in the gradient, the young at the warm bottom and the old at the cold top, tied loosely to the ladder with the knot so they will not roll. Their sleep is shallow, and in it their hands keep working. All night, up and down the ladder, thumbs twitch against imaginary flint.

## The Wrongness They Keep

They know the room does not agree with itself, and they have made a kind of peace with it. A setter told the number of terraces will say "eleven," then "nine," then "fourteen," each time with the same calm certainty. They are not lying. They are reporting from the terrace they are on. They tolerate loops of rope with no ends, and they follow such a rope around and around as a form of prayer. When a pot is found already fired and already overgrown, they say nothing. But someone always goes to the back of the packed rows, to where the wheel is turning, and sits with the potter who is not there. They keep company with the making of a thing that already exists. It seems to matter to them that it not be made alone.

## Their Dead, and Their Unborn

The dead are set into the wall. Each is wrapped in wet cord and placed in the course between two blocks, and the mossers close the gap. So the hammering from inside a stone beside you is not necessarily a metaphor. The ones behind the wall have not stopped working. They have only moved to where the work is heaviest.

And the unborn are also in the wall, or beyond it, or above the vault. This is the truest thing about the Setters and the hardest to explain: they are not entirely *behind* the room in time. They are building the Wet Loft from its future. The walls are laid by people who will arrive after the walls have been standing for centuries. The moss is placed by hands that have not yet grown. The tock-tock-tock is the sound of descendants making an ancestral home in which they will, one day, have always been born. The mountain rises. The seam closes, or opens. And the inhabitants, who have been working backward toward it from the far side of that moment, will finally reach the beginning and find it already furnished, the steps worn, the moss deep, the ferns wet, a lamp burning in every niche, and someone, long ago, having set it all ready for them.

## The Others

The tree-frogs are pale and thumbnail-sized, and they are the Setters' only kin among the animals. Their reed-pipe whistling is taken as commentary. When the frogs go silent all at once, the builders stop and wait, because something is about to be finished that should not be.

The vines are the enemy and the partner. They are not exactly alive as the builders are alive. They are a slow argument the room is having with itself, and the builders answer it with poles and cord and patience.

And there is the mountain, which is not an inhabitant but is the one that decides. It pushes upward, a fraction each hour, and every Setter feels it in their spine, like a hand on the small of the back. They work to its pace, and they call the pressure, in a word that means both *pushing* and *welcome*, by the name of a guest who has been expected for a very long time.

## If You Stay

You will not meet them properly. But if you sit long enough on a step worn into a dish that fits you a little too well, you will hear the hammering come close. It will stop behind the wall beside you, and there will be a pause. Then one clean, ringing strike, like the closing of a sentence.

When you turn, there will be a new block in the wall, dry and fitted, the dust still settling, the chisel-marks sharp enough to cut a thumb. It will be precisely where you would have leaned.

They were expecting you. They have been expecting you for a long time, and they made you a place to lean.
