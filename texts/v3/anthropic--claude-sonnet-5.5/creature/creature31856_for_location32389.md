# Those Who Keep a Hand On

Outsiders have never had a good name for them, because the usual ways of naming a people (what they call themselves, what they say when asked) are unavailable. They call themselves nothing aloud. Their name for themselves is a gesture: a flat palm laid between the shoulder blades of another person and held there through one full breath. It means *I am behind you, and you are not level yet.* The few travelers who have written about them have called them the Palmed, or the String, or simply the Walkers. Any of these will do. They will not know which you mean until you touch them.

## The Body

They are tall and long-shanked, built for wading, with wide flat feet whose toes spread and grip the sanded floor. The soles are thick, ridged, and extremely sensitive. A Walker listens with their feet before their ears. The note of the plain comes up through the stone and the shallow water into the bones of the shin, and the bones carry it to the sternum, where a broad plate of gristle, thickened over generations, acts as something between a drum and an ear. What they hear, they hear in the chest.

The ears themselves are small, folded, and nearly closed, sealed by a fatty flap that opens only in the huts. The skin is pale grey-green, fine-pored, and permanently damp, with a nap of short water-shedding hairs that lie in a direction that shifts across the body. Where the sideways rain strikes hardest, along the outer arm and the left flank, the hairs are dense as felt. The eyes are large and dark, with a clear inner lid that slides across in a blink slower than a breath. Under the greenish sky their eyes are the only things about them that look wet.

They are not a robust people. They are a patient one. A Walker's heart beats slowly and their temper is tuned to the water's, which is the temperature of a body at rest. Their own bodies do not hold much heat against it, and the boundary the visitor feels being negotiated has long been settled for them. They can't say where they stop and the film begins, and it doesn't trouble them.

## The String

They travel in lines. Each Walker keeps one hand on the shoulder of the one ahead, and the line, called a *string*, can be three people or forty. In the roar, a string is the only structure that holds. To break contact is to become, in a few paces, a person alone in a total sound, unable to hear, unable to be heard, and probably unable to tell which way is forward, since the distance is unreliable and the cairns recede. Children learn to keep hold before they learn anything else. A dropped hand is the nearest thing the Walkers have to a catastrophe.

The string has three offices, and everyone in it holds each in turn.

**The Foot** walks first. The Foot reads the plain: the standing-wave figures underfoot, the seams between rain-strata, the state of the note. They choose where the string goes, and they choose it by feel. A Foot who has walked long enough can stand in a lattice of ripples and know by the tremor through their heel which cells are shallow and which are the soft black pools that are, geologically, the same puddle. They do not draw maps. They know the maps would be wrong by the time the ink dried. What they carry instead is a way of reading, which is taught body to body.

**The Middle** is the largest part of the string and carries the burdens, the young, and the old. The Middle passes messages forward and back by pressure along the line, a message rippling from shoulder to shoulder like a nerve impulse. A warning from the tail reaches the head in the time it takes ten people to tighten a hand.

**The Rear** walks last, and this is the strangest of the offices. The Rear watches the footprints. Every step in the Lees leaves a bloom of blue-green light that fades over a slow count of twenty, and the Rear reads the blooms behind the string and confirms that they are the ones the string just made, in the order it made them. If a print appears that nobody has made, or a print is missing, or the blooms come in the wrong sequence, the Rear presses a long pattern into the shoulder ahead and the string halts, and everyone stands very still. This is the drill for a time-leak. It might be nothing. It might be that the string has walked into a place where the order of things has slipped. They wait, and count, and after some interval the Rear is satisfied or is not. If not, the string turns back the way it came, by a route it hopes is the route it took.

The Rear is always the person the string trusts most and understands least. They tend to be quiet even by the standards of a silent people.

## The Speech

There is no spoken language, because there is nowhere in the Lees where speech could exist. Instead there is the *press*, a language of contact.

It is built from pressure, duration, location, and rhythm. A fingertip is not a palm. A palm on the shoulder blade is not a palm on the small of the back. A slow lean is not a tap, and a tap in triplets is not a tap in pairs. A fluent speaker can convey a story across the back of a listener, a rapid, layered patter of fingers and heels and knuckles, and a fluent listener receives it whole. The grammar has tense, and the tenses are trickier than most, because the Walkers do not entirely trust the order of things. There is a past that means *this happened and I know it*, and a past that means *this happened, I think, before now, but the water is not sure.* There is a mood used only for ghost-weather.

There is humor. It is mostly tickling.

A child's first word is a pressure, given to them by whoever carries them, usually within the first hours after birth: a small, specific squeeze that means *here* or *held*. By the time they can walk they can read three dozen kinds of touch and are already making their own, mostly badly, mostly by grabbing the shoulder ahead and refusing to let go.

Names are given the same way. A name is a pattern of taps, laid on the back between the shoulder blades, and it is not a thing a person can say for themselves. You cannot have a name unless someone has given it to you, and you cannot keep it unless someone still speaks it. This is the reason the lone Walker is the saddest figure in their stories: not because they are in danger, but because after long enough without another hand, they forget the pattern of themselves. They wander, nameless, pale, and a little bright at the edges, and they are said to be already level, only still upright.

## What They Own

Almost nothing, and nothing that is set down. Everything settles, and a Walker's whole culture is arranged around that law. They do not own houses, because the houses would sink. They do not store goods, because the goods would go under the film by morning. A Walker's property is what they wear, on the body, in wraps and pouches and sewn linings: a cape of felted algae that sheds the horizontal rain, a wrap of woven reed-fiber, a bladder of drinking water taken from the drizzle at head height (the only layer they trust to drink from), a few tools of bone and shell and worn stone, and a small hard bag of carried stones.

The stones matter. Each Walker collects flat, round, smooth stones and carries them, and each stone is a memory, given, inherited, or found. To hand someone a stone is to hand them a piece of a life. At death the stones are shared out.

## What They Walk With

The Walkers do not farm and do not herd. They keep company with the plain's other things, on terms that have hardened into custom.

They follow the **waders**, the tall grey surveyors with too many knee-joints. The waders' path is reliable, since the waders seem to know which floor is firm, and a string that finds one will trail it at a respectful distance for days. The Walkers do not feed them or touch them. When a wader lowers its face to an algae mat and its head lights from beneath like a lantern, the whole string stops, and each Walker lays a hand on the shoulder ahead, and they stand for a breath in a kind of courtesy.

They carry the **eyeless drifters**, the pale finger-length things that gather at the nodes of the standing waves, in sealed bladders of clear membrane. The drifters slide toward the stillest water, wherever it is, and a bladder held out in front of the Foot tells them where the still points lie ahead, long before the ripples can be read. The Walkers eat some of them, raw, without ceremony. They keep the rest. It is not entirely clear which the drifters would prefer.

They avoid the **stilt-gardens**, or rather they pass them in a particular way. The thousands of wire-thin stems, each with its bead of clear liquid that never falls, are treated as something between a congregation and a jury. A string will walk around a stand, never through it, holding out one hand, fingertips down, as it passes. The Walkers say the beads are listening. They say each bead is a held drop, a held breath, a single moment refusing to fall. Asked whether the stilt-gardens are alive, a Walker will lay a hand on your shoulder and press a pattern that means, roughly, *it doesn't matter, and it might.*

## The Huts

Now and then, at intervals of many days' walking, the string reaches a hut. These are the old roofless rooms of stacked slab, with the doorway that faces no consistent direction. The Walkers did not build them and do not claim to have. They arrive at them the way one arrives at a rest.

Inside, the water is still, because the walls shelter it from the note. When the string steps across the threshold, the roar drops away, not entirely, but by a great deal, and the Walkers' ear-flaps open for the first time in days. What comes in is not silence. It is a great and painful *quiet*, a small, clean, aching sound, the sound of water dripping and of one's own pulse and of the person beside you breathing. Many of them weep. They call it the Ache. It is the only time they speak aloud, and what they speak is not words but a low, uncertain humming, slow and cracked, the sound of a voice that has been left unused. They sit on the saddle-worn bench in turns, and they sleep. Some of them, the old ones, cannot sleep in a hut at all, because the quiet is too strange, and they sit up in the doorway with their backs to the wall, listening.

They believe the door of a hut turns to face whoever needs it. They cannot say how they know.

## The Dead

A Walker who dies is walked to a place where the water is shallow and the floor firm, and laid down on the film, on their back, arms crossed, wrapped in their own cape. The string stays until the light of the body's last bloom fades. Then they leave.

By morning the body has settled a little. By the next season, it is flush with the floor. In a few years, there is only a paler patch in the pale stone, level with everything else. This is not treated as loss. It is treated as completion. *He is level now,* the string says, in the tense that means *known and certain*, and goes on. Being level is not oblivion. It is what everything is heading toward, what the plain itself is: a place where nothing sticks up, where everything has been reduced to the same height so that it can all go on happening at once. The Walkers take an odd comfort from this. They say the dead have simply become part of the flatness.

The cairns are the exception, and the Walkers have never decided what to think about them. When a string comes upon one, each Walker in turn places a hand on the top stone and, if it has begun to lean, straightens it a little. They don't do this to save the cairn, which they know is going down. They do it as a courtesy, the way one might steady an old person by the elbow on the way to the door. Whoever built the cairns, they reason, was doing what the Walkers do: keeping a hand on something that is settling. It seems only right to return the touch.

## Toward Evening

There is no evening, only a shift in the green. But there is a moment, when the note rises and the sideways rain thickens into a white wall and the clouds crack from within, when the string draws tight. Each Walker closes the second lid over their eyes, lowers their head, and shortens their grip to the shoulder-blade. The line goes rigid and low, a chain of bent figures moving through a wall of silver, each one guided only by the hand on their back and the hand they are holding.

If you could see them from above, which you cannot, you would see something that looks less like a people than like a figure the plain has made: a slow, articulated ripple, moving across the level water at the pace of a held breath, keeping its shape against all that is trying to flatten it. It forms, holds for a minute or a day or a lifetime, and moves on. Behind it, faintly, the footprints bloom blue-green and fade, in the right order, one by one, counted by the last of them.

The Walkers are the plain's most elaborate standing wave. They know it, and it does not trouble them. They have a hand on your shoulder.
