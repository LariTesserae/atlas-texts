# Who Lives in Karrow Cut

Nothing here is alone. Even the emptiest stretch of meadow is occupied by something calling, digging, nesting, or listening. The valley is young, but the life in it has settled in quickly, and it is dense enough that the people of the Cut think of themselves as one voice among many, and not the loudest.

## The People

There are about four hundred, spread thin along a mile of floor. Visitors from the coast call them *damp-folk*, and they don't mind. Among themselves they say "in the Cut" and leave it there. Most are descended from the first families who came for the spring, tinsmiths and wool-handlers and a few boilermen from the coal ports, who found a warm place that nobody owned and sank their savings into brick.

They are a listening people. Living among the pipes and stamping presses, they lose their hearing in strange, particular ways, so that a woman may be deaf to a shout and still notice a change in the pipe's hum from three terraces off. Everyone can tell by ear how far into the pulse the spring has come. Ask a child what day of the swell it is and she will cock her head at the ground, then answer, and be right.

They speak across distance by other means. The terraces stand far apart and the valley is loud, so the washing lines carry messages. A red sheet means a birth, a blue one illness, a striped one a market day or a dispute to be settled. Someone hanging out laundry is always saying something as well, and the neighbors read it from a hundred yards without lifting their eyes from their work. Between the mills they use whistles, pitched to cut under the noise of the presses. New arrivals hear only birdsong until someone points out that the birds are not answering the whistlers.

**The boilermen** keep the Boiler House and are the closest thing the Cut has to a priesthood, though they would spit at the word. They work in shifts, in the wet heat under the dome, with rags around their necks and hands that look boiled. They do not use gauges as much as they use their ears. A senior boilerman can put a palm to the iron and tell you the pressure within a few pounds, and they argue about it with a seriousness usually reserved for scripture.

**The wrappers** maintain the steam pipe: a crew of lean, sure-footed people who walk the trestles with coils of rope and buckets of tar over their shoulders, forty feet of open air below their boots on the worst stretches. They patch the lagging, scrape the algae from the joints, and listen. Every joint has a name. Every wrapper can hum the note their stretch of pipe makes at the height of the Chime, and they disagree, mildly and forever, about whether a new length of pipe should be cut to match the old note or the note it *ought* to have. The old note has always won.

**The millhands** are marked by their trades. The oil-pressers smell faintly of rapeseed and have soft, shining forearms. The felters have raw red hands, chapped from wet wool, and a tendency to shout because the drums are deafening. The tinners are quiet, burn-scarred people who eat lunch with their backs to the walls and talk in sign, because the stamping press drowns out any voice inside the hall. The three mills view each other with fond suspicion, like cousins.

**The tram-keeper** is a single post, held for life. It is a small, weathered job, mostly waiting, with occasional bursts of importance when the car is late. The car coasts down the line at a walking pace and the keeper walks alongside, oiling and tapping. Passengers ride it for the company more than the speed. It is understood that a person may step off, walk to the next mill, and be there before the car.

**The counting women** are the oldest residents, and their authority rests on a ledger no one else is allowed to read. Over generations they have recorded each birth against the day of the swell it fell on. They claim to see a pattern, and they will tell you what kind of child was born at the Chime (loud, restless, quick to leave) and what kind at the ebb (patient, broad-handed, slow to speak). Young mothers roll their eyes and, at the first pains, send the children running to see how the pipes are singing.

## The Children

Children in the Cut grow up fast and wet. They spend their days on the moss cushions, which are deep enough to lie in, and along the drainage cuts where the herons stand. Each child is taught the edge-test early: you take a gloved hand to a fresh basalt lip, and if it catches the leather, you leave it alone. Every child also collects a scar or two on an ungloved hand, and the scars are compared with pride. The cliffs are the great forbidden thing, and also the great beauty, and the children look up at the raw columns as at some enormous, unpredictable animal that has agreed for now to be still.

They keep time by slab-falls. Grown people will say, "It was the year of the Long Slab," and everyone will know: the night the north wall dropped a twenty-foot column into the meadow with a sound like the world being folded. Slabs are named for their sound, and sound is how the valley remembers.

## The Eleven-Day Week

The Cut runs on the pulse. There are eleven days to the swell, and each has a name, though the names differ from terrace to terrace and are argued over at market. Laundry is done at the rising, when the steam is strong and lines dry fast in the warm mist. Market comes near the crest, when the tram runs its fastest. Quiet work (mending, letter-writing, courting) is done at the ebb, when the mills idle and the valley goes soft.

Everyone knows the Chime is coming. For a day beforehand the pipes tighten their song, the frogs fall silent, and something in the population gathers itself. People step out on doorsteps and stand with their heads tilted. Then the chord builds, three or four notes swelling from somewhere in the pipes, shifting as the flow rises and falls, and it rolls across the meadow and up the basalt walls and returns, doubled. Work stops. Nobody says anything. Then, gradually, someone starts to hum along, and someone else, and by the crest half the valley is singing a low, tuneless harmony against the pipes, not on purpose exactly, but as an animal answers an animal.

## The Birds

The stacks that the mills never needed are the best real estate in the valley. **Starlings** hold most of them, in the thousands, noisy and iridescent and mimicking everything: the tram's wheeze, a valve blowing, a child's whistle, a wrapper's cough. Above all they mimic the Chime. In the weeks before the pulse peaks, the starlings begin to whistle its chord, a little early, and the old wrappers take this as a good sign. The question of who taught whom, birds or pipes, is a favorite of the pub, and it is never settled because no one really wants it settled.

**Jackdaws** hold the upper brickwork, bickering in pairs. **Swifts** come in summer and scream in tight bands around the Boiler House dome. **Gulls** come up from the coast and strut along the roof ridges as if they held the mortgages. In one of the stacks a pair of barn owls has nested for longer than anyone can remember, and the millhands leave a saucer of milk on the ledge for luck.

The stack-birds are not a nuisance but a resource. Their droppings, gathered in spring from the stack bases by a licensed few, enrich every allotment in the Cut. The gatherers are known as *stackmen*, and they wear scarves over their faces and are paid in eggs and gossip.

## The Ones in the Meadow

The floor of the Cut belongs to the small and the wet. **Frogs** are the true population, uncounted, in choruses so thick at dusk that the sedge seems to hum. Several species are here, and the residents can tell them apart: a bass croaker in the alder carr, a rattling tree-frog in the ferns, a tiny piping kind that sounds like a nail on glass. At the ebb they come out in their thousands and the valley is theirs for two days. Then the steam rises, the noise of the works returns, and the frogs go silent, not from fear but from what looks like courtesy.

**Voles and shrews** riddle the moss with tunnels, and their quick lives are watched from above by **herons**, which stand unmoving in the drainage cuts like grey punctuation. The herons are tolerated, even admired. Killing one is bad luck, and nobody can say why.

**Small deer** come down at night from the rim paths, delicate and unhurried. They stand in the warm mud near the spring, unbothered by the thump of the mills, and eat the allotment cabbages, and the residents grumble and leave a row for them. In the dark the deer's eyes, in the light from a cracked shutter, are pale green coins.

**Bats** pour from the Boiler House eaves at dusk, a slow dark river. The boilermen use the emergence as a clock, and it is the only clock they entirely trust.

## The Shape of It

Everything in the Cut listens to everything else. The frogs stop when the pipes rise. The starlings copy the pipes. The people copy the starlings, absently, whistling as they walk. The deer ignore them all and eat the cabbages. The basalt walls send every sound back a half-second late and doubled, so the valley never quite hears itself in real time, and the residents, animal and human alike, have learned to live a little behind their own echo.

At the ebb, in the low light after rain, you may see a girl on a moss ledge with her ear against the warm rock, and a heron a few yards off, and above them a starling on a pipe trestle running through the first two notes of the Chime, tentative, days before the swell. The girl will whistle the third note without looking up. The starling will take it, turn it over, and hand it back a little wrong.
