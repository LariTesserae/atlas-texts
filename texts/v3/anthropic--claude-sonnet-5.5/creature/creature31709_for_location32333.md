# The Ones Who Are Set Down

You will mistake them first for something else. A figure standing at the edge of a clearing, motionless, draped in green, is a stump, or a young pillar tree with a heavy load of epiphytes. Then the head turns, slowly enough that the movement seems a decision the whole body has to agree to, and you see that the stump has been watching you for some time.

They call themselves something that means *the set-down*, or *those who were put here and stayed*. The word is soft, mostly breath, and it can also be used for a stone laid in a cairn, or a seedling pressed into a crack. They do not distinguish sharply between these meanings. To be set down is to have been placed by a hand you can no longer name, and to be held afterward by the ground, and they feel this is the whole of what needs saying about a life.

## Bodies

They are tall but rarely stand at full height. The habit of the mat is a soft-kneed, forward-leaning walk that keeps the weight low and spread, and over many generations their feet have followed it: broad, long-toed, the second toe as dexterous as a thumb, the soles callused pale and slightly webbed at the base. They leave prints that fill with tea-brown water and are gone in an hour. Their legs are long and their gait unhurried. A set-down crossing a basin looks like a heron thinking about something else.

Their skin is the color of the underside of a leaf, a grey-green pallor that is partly them and partly what lives on them. Algae grows in the fine hair along their forearms and in the long, unbraided hair of their heads, and they do not scrub it out. The old ones are noticeably greener. A child is pale, and turns green as it is carried through the forest and slept beside and rained on, until one day it is said to have *taken*. There is no ceremony for this. Someone simply remarks on it, the way you might remark that a boy has grown.

Their eyes are large and dark, with wide pupils that hold the dim light like water in a cup. In direct sun, which they almost never see, they weep and squint. They can read a face at dusk by the milk-blue glow of the rotting wood, and they can tell one moss cushion from another by how long its phosphorescence takes to return after a hand has passed over it.

Their hands are the most striking thing. The fingers are long, with an extra flexibility in the last joint, and the palms are always damp, faintly cool, faintly sticky, like the underside of a frog. They touch everything before they use it. A set-down handling a new object will hold it against the inside of the wrist for some time and then hand it back, satisfied or not.

## Clothing

They wear cloaks of living moss. A cloak begins as a lattice of woven root-thread, and moss is pressed into it and kept damp, and after three seasons it has knit into a single fabric that is soft, heavy, and slowly alive. It sheds water and smells of the forest. A person is buried in theirs, so it goes into the mat in the end still growing. Each person tends the cloak by directing its growth, pinching it back at the shoulders, coaxing a fern into the hood, letting a small orchid find purchase at the hip. The cloaks are recognizable individuals, and you learn to identify people by the shape of their green before you know their faces.

## Dwelling

They build almost nothing. What they have is a practice of *asking*: wandering the forest with a patient eye for a place that is nearly a room already, where lianas have fused into a vault, or a fallen trunk has left a hollow, or six stems have grown close enough that a little training will bring them to meet. They coax these places with twine, patience, and small daily nudges over years. A dwelling is not finished in a lifetime. A person inherits a half-grown room from a dead relative and adds a finger's width to it before they die.

They sleep in these rooms in groups of four or five, in hammocks of woven root-thread slung low over the mat, so that a rising of the water will lift them rather than drown them. Their fires are small, smoky, and rare, because wood is wet and the forest resents burning. Most of their warmth is body heat and the wet warmth of the air. They cook little. They eat the pale grazers' milk when the herds allow it, the fat white grubs from rotting trunks, tubers pulled from beneath the mat, and a fermented paste of the unnamed mint-scented shrub that they take at dusk and that makes their conversation slow and sweet.

## Speech and Silence

They talk little, and most of what they say is in fragments, half-sentences that trail off and are completed by the listener's nod. Long silences are companionable. They have a range of small sounds for the things a word would be too large for: a click at the back of the tongue for *I see you*, a low hum in the chest for *I am here and content*, a sharp inhaled breath for *the water has changed.*

But they have one thing that is not quiet. At dawn the large dark birds with pouched throats give their hollow call, a single note, three beats apart, and the set-down answer it. They stand at the edges of their clearings with their faces lifted and give the note back in their own throats, a low round sound like blowing across a bottle. It travels. Each settlement answers the bird, and the bird answers the settlement, and the note goes out across the plateau in a wave that takes the better part of an hour to reach the far basins and return. They say that in this way the whole shoulder of the mountain *counts itself*: that every dawn each living place is heard, and if one does not answer, its neighbors will walk out across the mat to find why.

At dusk the notched trunks along the ridge do something similar with light. The set-down carry lantern beetles in cupped hands and place one in each notch, and when the beetles are set in their rows and grow frightened at the dark, they pulse. The whole ridge flickers with a slow chain of amber, each mouth of the old trunks lit in turn. Do the set-down know this is what the notches were made for? They shrug. It works, they say. The light goes out along the ridge and comes back from the far end, and that is enough of a reason.

## Time

They keep no calendar but the water. A person's age is given in *risings*, the number of high-water years they have seen, and someone of four risings is a young adult and someone of ten is old. They feel the water's turning in their joints days before it comes. They know the low years by the way the mat begins to settle and the dark seams to open, and they know the high years by a sense of buoyancy in the whole forest, like the moment before a held breath is let out.

They are not alarmed by the cycle. They plan in sevens: what will be planted, which room will be extended, which path will be cut across the mat when it is stiffest. Whole projects are begun by one generation with the calm expectation that a grandchild will finish them and that the finishing will look different from the plan.

## Their Dead, and the Cairn

Bodies are given to the mat. The dead person is laid in the moss in their own cloak, face up, and within a season the forest has taken them. The set-down do not mark the place. They mark the *death* instead, by carrying a stone from the river.

The river is a full day's walk from the nearest settlement, and the stones must be the size of a fist, smooth and grey. A mourner walks there and back with a single stone held against the belly under the cloak, and places it on the cairn in the clearing. The cairn is very old. It is also, in effect, no taller than it was a thousand years ago, because the mat is slowly swallowing it. Every stone that is added takes one down into the moss. The set-down understand this perfectly well. The cairn is not a monument that grows. It is a level that is held, and what is being held is the fact that the dead keep being carried, and that the carrying does not stop.

## The Marks

They did not make the steps. They did not make the ring of six trunks, though they train new rings in imitation and have gotten quite good at it. They do not know who made the notches. They say, without much interest, that the makers *went down*, and the water closed.

They do not tear things apart to find out. The steps are approached with a kind of courtesy, and on the top step, worn hollow by feet older than any story, the set-down and the pale grazers sometimes stand together in the rain, looking into the water below as though it were a room whose owner had stepped out.

In the lowest years, when the mat cracks and the water sinks, the steps lift a little way out of it, one or two, then three, dark and shining with wet. The set-down gather. They do not go down. They descend one step at a time, each person to a single step, and leave something there: a shell, a braid of hair, a knot of green thread. Then they climb back up and say nothing about it. When the water rises, these things go under. No one knows what is at the bottom of the stairs. They seem, on the whole, comfortable not knowing.

## One of Them, at Dusk

If you sit long enough in a clearing, one may come and sit beside you. She will not speak first. She will be old, greener than the others, with a thin orchid growing behind one ear, and she will smell of wet stone and mint. She will hold her damp cool hand out, palm up, and wait for you to place something in it, anything, and she will press it to the inside of her wrist for a long time.

Then she will look up at the roof of leaves, where the last light is going, and the fungal threads in the trunks are beginning to glow their faint milk-blue, and the dripping has begun its thousand clocks. She will say, very softly, the word for *set-down*, and you will not be sure whether she means herself or you, or the stone in your pocket, or the forest, or the slow green drowned mountain, patiently keeping all that it once was.
