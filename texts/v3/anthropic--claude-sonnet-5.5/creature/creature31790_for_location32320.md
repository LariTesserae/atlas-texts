# Who Lives at Harrow Weir

## The Thirty

You will not meet them on arrival. You will hear the town for a full day, walk its causeways, and feel alone in it, and all that time they will be watching you from the dark between the lamp-globes. They keep to the dim hours and move in the gaps between lit spheres, and they dislike being lit. When one steps into a lamp's fog to cross to the next dark, the whole body flinches slightly, like a hand passing over a flame.

There are about thirty, in a town built for hundreds. They are not a sad remnant. They carry themselves like the crew of a very large ship that has run for a very long time, and they take the emptiness of the rooms as a kind of trust.

They are pale in the way of things grown out of the sun, with a faint green cast at the creases of the knuckles, the eyelids, and the hollows behind the ears. It might be moss dust, or might be the town getting into them. Their skin is soft and permanently a little wrinkled, like fingertips left in a bath. Their hair is often felted at the ends into loose ropes, and they don't fight it. They dress in moss-felt, which is dense, warm, faintly springy, and smells of cellar and green apple. It stays damp, and they are used to that.

They speak low. Nobody is stern about it, but everyone learned as a child that voices are kept here, and so they say only what they mean to leave behind. Conversation is sparse and unhurried. They hum, though, constantly, under the breath, drifting to whatever note the pipes are giving that hour. A visitor will notice that the whole population is faintly, unconsciously in tune with a chord that has no resolution, and that when the chord slides, thirty people slide with it and none of them notices.

They are named for the pipes. A child gets the name of the note that was sounding in the house where she was born, and so the town has a Low Tin, a Bell-under-Two, a Fifth Drip, a Wren, which is the sound of a small clay feed in the dye-works, a note that has no other meaning. Nobody in Harrow Weir has ever seen a wren, or knows that the name once belonged to something alive. It is only a high, quick, cheerful sound, and they name girls after it.

## The Trades

Each of them belongs to a trade, and the trades are old enough to have become temperaments.

**The listeners** are the closest thing the town has to a government, though they would say they only pay attention. Each morning, or what the engine calls morning, a listener walks the causeways with a palm flat on the pipes, reading them. A pipe that has gone flat is clogging. A pipe that has gone sharp is running too fast and will scour itself. They can hear a silted sluice three streets away. They are usually the oldest, because the ear takes decades to learn the town, and they are usually a little deaf to anything else. Ask a listener a question and she will finish hearing whatever the town is saying before she answers you.

**The root-wrights** are the strangest. They work with the ferns and horsetails that now govern the drains, and they no longer think of themselves as gardeners. They think of themselves as negotiators. A root-wright will kneel for an hour at a junction, coaxing a rootstock toward a new bracket, and will not force it. They wear brass cuffs and thimbles cast to hold living stems, and a young root-wright's first cuff carries a cutting from the tree fern of the courtyard where they were apprenticed. The cutting grows around the wrist over the years. Old root-wrights have green rope wound up the forearm, and when one dies, the family does not cut it away. It is buried, and the fern is permitted to decide what to do next.

Ask a root-wright who is in charge of the drains and you will get a long, gentle, evasive answer. They don't know. They think it a little rude to ask.

**The lamp-tenders** trim wicks that are not wicks, scrape moss from cages, and check the turbine's thin trickle. They are the most solitary of the trades and the most superstitious. They believe the amber globes of fog around each lamp are held by something, that if a lamp goes out its globe does not vanish but stays, unlit, and that walking through a dead globe is like walking through someone's held breath. They step around them.

**The engine-keepers** are few, muscular, and slow-moving, and they are the only ones who speak in ordinary voices, since the engine hall is loud enough to cover it. They oil the beam, tend the troughs, and count. They are counting the pump-beat's drift, a fraction slower each year. They keep the tally on the wall of the hall in chalk that never dries, and it is copied out in a fresh hand each generation before it fades. They know how many years, at the current rate, until the beat stops. It is a number they do not say aloud. It is not despair, only a kind of courtesy. The number is written where anyone can read it, and no one does.

**The felters, peat-burners, and dyers** make the town's goods, in quantities that could clothe and warm a city and are instead stacked, in bolts and bricks and jars, in warehouses no one will ever empty. Nobody in Harrow Weir can say who buys them. The work goes on because the work is the town. The dyers' forearms are stained to the elbow in madder and indigo, and a dyer's age can be read in the layered bands of color in the skin, like rings in a trunk.

## The Green

Then there are the residents who do not have names. They are the largest population by far, and the one that keeps the others alive.

The tree ferns in the courtyards are old, older than the bricks, and the people say they were here before the bowl was a town. Their trunks are thick and shaggy with root-felt, and their crowns close over the alleys so that walking under them feels like walking down the inside of a lung. They have never been eaten. They have never been pollinated. Nothing has ever needed them to be beautiful, and they are, in the incurious way of things with no audience.

The horsetails stand in the drainage cuts in files. When the pump-beat passes through the ground they squeak, all together, a thin sound like wet fingers on glass, and the sound runs along the line of them like a ripple. Whether this is only the stems knocking or a kind of speech, no one in Harrow Weir has ever asked. They drink the floods. They are courteous about it.

Under the lanes the roots have knitted into the joints of the pipes and around the cast-iron elbows, and what they hold is not quite a plan. But it is a pattern with intent in it, the way a river has intent. The root-wrights say the network *prefers* certain flows. They say that after a hard rain it settles, and that the settling feels like relief.

## The Kept

And there is the fog, which pools around the engine house in the low hours and which the old residents will not quite call an inhabitant.

They say it keeps what is said. This is not a metaphor to them. A voice in the fog returns half a second late, lower, from the wrong side. If you speak again into the same fog, the returned voice comes back lower still, and the older residents say that in the deepest hours, standing in the thickest of it, you can hear sentences no one in the hall has spoken: fragments, a name, half a lullaby, a counting-off of pipe numbers in a voice from before anyone's grandmother.

The people of Harrow Weir do not fear this. They *use* it. When someone dies, the family carries the name down to the engine house at the deep hour and says it into the fog, once, quietly, and leaves. When a child is born, the pipe-note of the house is spoken there too. When a listener grows too old to hear, she goes down and hums the chord into it, all the notes she has carried, and lets the town have them back.

Nobody knows what the fog does with these things. Nobody has come back from it with an answer, and most have stopped wanting one. They speak of it the way you speak of a well: with respect for its depth and no wish to know how deep.

## What They Are, Together

If you asked any of them what lives in Harrow Weir, you would get a slow look, and then the same answer, in a low voice:

"We do. And it does."

And they would mean the thirty, the ferns, the roots in the pipes, the fog in the hall, and the beat itself, which is slowing, which everyone can hear is slowing, and which no one has ever treated as an ending. The chord will change key again. The ferns will tremble together, and the fog will draw in around the lamps, and the thirty will hum their way down to the new note without deciding to. They have been listening to the town for longer than they have been counting, and they trust that whatever it is becoming, it will say so in a voice they can learn.
