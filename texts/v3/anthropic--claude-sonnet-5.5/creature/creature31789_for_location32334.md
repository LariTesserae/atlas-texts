# Those Who Keep the Sump

The first inhabitant is the listening.

Before you see anyone, you are already being heard. The grating rings under your boots and the whole plain takes note of your weight, your stride, the slight drag of the left heel. By the time you reach the second terrace you have been read, and something has decided how to think about you. What does the deciding is the people who live here. They call themselves **tenders**, and they are fewer than they seem.

## The Tenders

There may be forty of them, or thirty, or sixty. No one has counted, because they are never in one place and because the Field lets each of them sound like several. They know one another by gait. Names are rarely used, since the steel already gives everyone a better one: *Long-Left*, who swings the bad leg wide; *Two-Tap*, who touches the rail with the heel of the hand at every stanchion; *the Hollow Heel*, whose right boot rings a half-tone flatter than the left. A tender who has been away from the grating for a week comes back to find their footfall remembered and their name said aloud in the dark before their face is lit.

They are pale in the way of people who have never been anywhere with weather that was not made. They dress in oilcloth the color of dishwater, cut long, with the hems weighted so the rain does not lift them. Their cap lamps wear amber gels, so that the eye does not have to keep readjusting between the sodium pools and the brown between them. Their cuffs and knees are chalked with lime bloom, which they no longer bother to brush off, and their hair has taken some of it too. They carry sounding rods, tins of marking chalk, and long-handled keys for the sluice gates. Nobody remembers being issued the keys. Each tender simply found one hanging at the belt on their first morning, worn smooth as if from long use.

Their work is the water. They read the canals by ear, since a canal that is about to misbehave changes pitch a full minute before it changes anything else. They put their palms flat on the pump-chapel walls and feel for a missed beat. They oil, they bleed, they knock on things and listen to the answer. When the bell rings and the sluices open, they walk the low places ahead of the flood, calling in flat carrying voices, and then they stand on the high lips and watch the terraces rearrange, with the expression of people watching a large animal turn over in its sleep.

Nobody rings the bell. There is a rota, a rope, and a tower, and every tender who has stood the rota swears that on their turn the bell was already swinging when they reached the rope. They pull anyway, to be safe. The bell is the surest proof of the tenders' belief in one another. Each of them assumes the ringing is being done by someone else, and each has quietly stopped asking who.

## Customs, Already

The concrete is months old, and so is their culture. It is forming the way the lime forms, one drip at a time, with the same pale, brittle beauty. Things that took centuries elsewhere have happened here in a season.

There are rules already. Don't whistle in the dry lanes, because the dry lanes carry sound too well. Talk in the drenched lanes if you have anything to say that you would not say to the whole Field. The rain covers a voice completely, and a tender who wants to confess something, or curse someone, or say a dead name, will walk out into the falling curtain and speak it there, and the gravel-sound of the drops takes it. Lovers use the drenched lanes. So do liars. It is understood that nothing said in the rain counts.

When the three pumps of the fourth terrace drift into phase and the Field gathers itself into that single enormous heartbeat, every tender stops. They stop wherever they are, mid-step, mid-word, with a tool half raised. They stand for the length of it, perhaps eight seconds, and when the beat unravels they resume without comment. Asked, they say it is only that you can't work through it. Asked again, they say they don't call it anything.

There are already legends. *The Man Who Was in the Low Place at the Bell* is thirty weeks old and has grown three endings. There is a dead tender, though no one can produce a body or a name, who is said to have stepped off the grating during the second flood. Each retelling adds a detail that everyone agrees was always there. The stories are lime crust: thin, white, growing on the underside of things.

## The Gardener

Under the blue-white lights on the east wall the trays are tended, and the tenders argue about by whom.

The evidence is plain. Bitter cress is cut on the diagonal, close, with something sharp and clean. Lettuce hearts are left to grow. The soil is turned with a narrow blade and pressed flat by a palm, and now and then a thumbprint is left in it, deep, with the whorls still legible. A tin cup sits on the end of one rack. Sometimes it is full of rainwater and sometimes of seed. One tender has taken to leaving a spoon beside it, and the spoon disappears and comes back, cleaned.

Every tender is sure the gardener is one of the others. Each has a candidate, and the candidates do not overlap. Some say Two-Tap, who is always tired; some say a quiet woman from the pump-chapels who walks without ringing the grating, so silently that she is thought to be wearing felt. Some say there is no gardener, and the trays simply tend themselves to the exact degree needed to keep the tenders fed. The cress is eaten by everyone, and everyone finds it bitter. No one has thanked anyone for it. That silence is the nearest thing the tenders have to a sacrament.

## The Lit-Lane Birds and the Eels

The small gray birds are the Field's congregation. They have lost their color and most of their sight, and they cross the dark between lamp-pools in a single held breath, dipping from amber to amber as if each pool were a stepping stone in a river of nothing. They roost in lines along the girders, and at the hour of the bell they all speak at once, in clipped notes with something almost like grammar in the spacing. The tenders answer them, badly, in whistles, in the drenched lanes only.

The eels hold the canals. They are translucent, with their organs strung through them like dark cords in glass, and they lie facing upstream with barely any motion, the way a thought is held. They are not afraid of lamps. They turn their blunt heads to the light and look at you for a long time. Tenders say the eels are the only ones who know where the water goes. They say it not because the eels are wise, but because the eels go down, past the weirs and the sluices and into the throat of the central cistern, and some of them come back up.

## The Late One

Something larger lives on the slabs, and the tenders have a name for it that is really a description of a problem: the **Late One**.

It is heard dragging a wet line across the concrete at the edge of hearing. A dozen tenders have followed that sound, and all of them arrive at the place it was and find only the line, still glistening, a hand's width across, exactly the width of the gutters. Some of them will tell you, in the low voice that goes with the drenched lanes, that the gutters were cut that wide because the Late One was expected. The line is always fresh and the thing is never at the end of it. It seems to live in the small delay between a sound and its source, in the offset that a room this size puts into every noise. It arrives after its own noise, the way thunder arrives after lightning, and moves on before you can turn.

Descriptions do not agree. It is long and low, and it has many small hands, or one, or none. It is the color of the slabs. It smells of cold iron and, faintly, of the cress. Whether it is dangerous is the subject of the oldest argument in the Field, which is to say the argument is eight months old. The tenders leave it alone and leave the gutters clear. When a gutter clogs, it is cleared before the bell, whatever else is happening.

## The Still Water

They will not speak about the tiled pit, and so most of what can be said is about the silence around it.

The pumps shudder and every surface in the Field shivers with them, except that water. The lamps are reflected in it as if in polished black glass. Grit dropped in it makes one ring that spreads, and then, instead of fading, stops, and holds its shape for several seconds, a circle standing on the water like a written word. Then it smooths away.

Tenders do not go to the pit, but they know it well. On the lowest hours, some of them will stand at its tiled lip and drop a single grain, the way you might press two fingers to a wrist. They are checking. The ring holds a little longer some nights than others. They do not say what they are checking for.

Those who have counted the lamps in the reflection disagree with those who have counted the lamps in the roof, by one. They do not agree about which way the difference falls.

The tenders' theory, held by all and spoken by none, is that whatever lives in the pit is the only thing in the Field that is not being pumped. Everything else is on its way somewhere. It has arrived, and it is listening, and it is patient with the rest of them in the way a person is patient with the sound of rain on a roof that will not hold.

## Those Below

Under the cistern, in the shaft that goes down into places not yet finished, the building continues.

The tenders hear it at the ring road, if they lie down with an ear on the iron plate: riveting, a slow ratchet, the drag of a load on a chain. It is the sound of the cranes that trundle empty across the vault, and the tenders have come to understand that the cranes are not empty, only carrying things too high, or too far, or too *early* to be seen. Below are the ones who build. The tenders call them the **underwrights**, when they call them anything. They never come up. The shaft is their sky.

Once in a while, in the morning, a small bright disc of cut steel is found on the ring road, laid on the plate. It is a tie-rod end, freshly sheared, still gleaming, arranged in a line with others, or in a ring, or in a shape the tenders cannot read. They gather these and bring them to the east wall and lay them by the tin cup. The next day they are gone. Nothing is ever left in return that anyone can identify, but the pumps run a little more evenly for a week.

The tenders believe the underwrights are working ahead of the water, racing to finish each level before the seep, the lime, and the rust reach it. Sooner or later the water arrives, and the underwrights step down a level and begin again. This may be why the cistern is full, and why the note is so low. What the tenders feel in their sternums is the sound of a thing being built faster than it can be allowed to fail.

## What They Are

If you stay long enough you begin to see what all of them have in common: the tenders, the gardener, the eels, the birds, the Late One, the still water, the builders below. They are all *first-stage* things. They are the bloom, the pale crust, the orange tongue of rust from the bolt. The place has only just begun to learn what water does, and in the same way it is only just learning what a people does. Its inhabitants are what the Field has so far been able to precipitate: custom, legend, habit, and the first thin scale of belief, laid down drop by drop on the underside of the new concrete.

In a hundred years the crust will be thick, and the tenders will be stalactites of habit, each rule a finger-length of chalk hanging from the ledge of an older rule. For now everything is crisp. The formwork lines are still sharp and the bolt-holes are still clean. The tenders still remember the day of the first flood and disagree about what happened. In the drenched lanes, where the rain falls loud enough to cover a voice, someone is saying the name of the one who stepped off the grating, and the water is taking it down toward the center.

They know it is beginning. It shows in the way they walk, careful, one foot after the other on the ringing steel, listening for the place where the Field will first give way.
