# The Keepers of the Sump

The first thing to understand is that you have already passed them. Several times. You took them for part of the place.

There are perhaps sixty of them, and they are very good at being still. In the slanted columns of green light you will see a figure seated on a tenement step, shoulders dusted with a fine grey-green bloom, hands open on the knees, face tipped up to the grating. You will think *statue* and walk on. Then a drop falls from the vault onto the figure's cheek, and the eyes close and open again, slowly, like a leaf turning over.

They call themselves the Keepers, and the place the House. Both words are shorthand for a single idea, that the quarter is a thing that must be attended to. Everything else about them follows from that.

## Bodies

They are people, but they have been shaped by the damp. Their skin is the pale olive of anyone who has lived generations on filtered light, and it is always faintly wet. They keep their hair cropped close, since long hair mildews, and they rub oil from pressed fig-seed into the scalp and the creases of the hands to keep the rot out. Their feet are broad and bare, with toes that splay and grip. They walk the roots more than the cobbles. A fig root, ridged and warm, is better footing than slick stone, and over the generations a network of root-ways has formed above the streets, worn smooth along their upper faces by feet.

Their eyes are large and dark, the pupils wide even in the light-columns. Their hands are stained green to the wrist, and the stain is permanent. It is the work of leaf-juice and moss-pulp, and the old ones' forearms look faintly veined with it.

They dress in bark-cloth, beaten from the aerial roots of the figs until it is soft and tan and the weight of felt. It is sewn with thread pulled from vine, in long loose garments that shed water. When they hold still, lichen takes to the cloth. They brush it off in the evenings, or they don't, and the very old ones wear whole small gardens on their shoulders and do not mind.

## Speech

The drone would swallow most voices, so they have made other languages.

They speak close, cheek to cheek, in a low murmur pitched under the machine's note. But the real language of the House is the knocking. Every pipe in the quarter is connected to every other, and iron carries a tap a long way. A Keeper carries a small brass key on a cord, worn bright, and with it raps out phrases on the nearest pipe: a rhythm of shorts and longs with a pause that changes the meaning. The pipes knock back. To an outsider this is the sound of pressure shifting, the occasional clank in the walls. Actually the whole quarter is talking all day, gossiping and calling meals and announcing births, in a soft metallic murmur under the greater noise.

They also speak with the hands, which are always slightly wet and so make a small click when the fingers part.

## The work

The work is counting, and it is holy in a plain, unremarked way.

Every ninety seconds or so the great valve breathes its long hiss of steam. A Keeper sits by it in a niche worn to the shape of a body and counts the breaths. There is always someone in the niche. The count is passed to the next sitter in a whispered handoff, and the number is written in a ledger: a long book of waxed paper, kept dry in a lead-lined box, whose columns go back further than anyone can read. Pressure, temperature, the height of the canal after each cycle, the color of the outflow. Nobody remembers who first wrote down the numbers or why. But the House has stayed standing, and they hold that the numbers are the reason. The machines, they believe, are attentive. What is watched keeps running. What is not watched forgets itself.

Other work follows the same logic. They oil the governor. They rake the duckweed from the sluice-mouths so the canal doesn't choke. They turn the hot-water gates a quarter-turn at dawn and dusk. And they tend the figs, which is the subtlest labor. They do not prune the roots that have fused with the walls, because those roots are now the walls. Instead they guide them, tying new growth along the lines of stress, feeding the roots into cracks before the cracks widen. A good gardener here is half mason, and is known by the way the buildings lean toward her.

In the turbine hall lives the eldest of them, a woman stone-deaf from decades beside the drone. She hears the pump through her sternum and her feet, and she knows by the feel of it what it needs. The others do not enter the hall unless she waves them in.

## What they eat

Taro, mostly, boiled in the warm outflow until it turns violet and soft. Duckweed skimmed and pressed into dense green cakes. Figs, small and hard, eaten in season and dried the rest of the year. Pale shoots forced under the tenement floors. Tea from steeped fern.

They will not eat the bracket fungi that step up the trunks. This is the one firm taboo. The fungi, they say, are the House's own tongue, its way of tasting them, and you do not eat the tongue of something that is feeding you.

There is no meat. No animals live in the House, and there are stories about that, told to the young in the dark: a bird that got in once through the well-grating and beat itself against the vault until it fell, and how everyone stood in the street and wept without understanding why.

## The dead and the young

The dead are laid in root-hollows in the tenement walls, wrapped in bark-cloth, with a pinch of moss over the mouth. The figs close over them in a few years. Every wall in the House is a little populated. The Keepers speak of the old ones as *in the wall* the way you might say *in the next room*, and they knock on the plaster before they pass.

The children are the only quick things here. They run the root-ways in packs, barefoot, shrieking in the gaps between the machine's noise, and the adults let them. Their game is to time the valve's breath and leap the canal at the moment it hisses. They are trained early to hear the drone's pitch. A change of half a tone means trouble, and a child who first hears it is given a brass key of her own.

## Time

They do not count days, because there are none. They count cycles of the pump, and by the tilt of certain slabs, and by generations of fig. *Before the third root* means a long time ago. *When the stair was still a stair* means longer.

They know the House is changing. They know the doorways are narrowing, that the streets are closing like a slow fist. They do not mourn it. Some day the last Keeper will fold into a wall and the figs will close, and the pump will keep going or not. They accept this the way the plants do. It is, they think, what tending is for: not holding a thing still, but keeping it company as it becomes something else.

## The vine

When you stand in the market arcade and the warm water runs down the vine into your palm, know that it does not do this on its own. A knotted cord runs from the keystone up into the dark, and beyond it, in a window wound with fern, a Keeper is sitting with one hand on a root. She has bent the growth, months ago, so that the runoff would fall just there, at the height of an outstretched hand. It is what they do for a visitor. She will not come down. She will wait, with the patience of the place, through a full cycle of the pump, to see whether you stay long enough to be worth knocking to.

If you do, you will hear it in the pipe beside you: three shorts, a pause, a long. It means *the door is open*. It means *sit*.
