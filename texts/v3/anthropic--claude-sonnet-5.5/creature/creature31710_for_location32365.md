# The Inhabitants of the Tallow Flats

Only animals live on the Flats, and they live as a crowd. They have no names, because a name needs a gap to be spoken into.

## The Grinders

Grinders are the oldest-looking things on the plain, and they are the ones you meet first, because you are standing on them. What you took for a low gray ridge shifts under your boot and turns out to be a back. A grinder is the width of a dinner table and barely knee-high, with a carapace worn smooth and mottled with the black felt that grows on everything still enough to be grown on. Its legs are short and numerous, and they never lift. They shuffle in a slow wave that carries the animal a hand's breadth a minute through the silt.

The mouth is underneath. It is a pair of plated discs, ridged like millstones, turning against each other in a wet, patient rotation. The grinder does not chew so much as keep faith with a motion. Silt goes in as mud and comes out behind as flour, and the flour trails away in a pale fan that the current takes.

A grinder is calm. If you crouch beside one, you'll see that its eyes, two black beads on short stalks, are turned slightly inward, as if listening to its own mill. It grumbles all day and all night, and there is no difference between the two. The grumble is not a call. It is what the animal sounds like when it is doing what it is.

## The Washers

Downstream, where the channels braid, stand the washers. They are tall for the plain, waist-high, built like folding screens on stilted legs. Each has two ranks of fringed limbs, and it opens them into the current like a hand spread under a tap. The fringes are so fine they look like smoke. The flour drifts through them, and the heavy red grains catch and slide down into a chitinous basket slung under the animal's chest.

Washers stand for a very long time without moving anything but the fringes. Their patience is different from the grinders'. A grinder is patient because it is busy, and a washer is patient because it is waiting to be handed something. Every so often one tilts its basket toward the animal beside it, and a smelter, without looking, leans in to take the load.

They are the most social things on the plain, in the sense that they touch. Fringes overlap between neighbors, and the current passes through a hundred of them in succession, so that each washer receives what the last one missed. If you watched from above, you would see the channel as a single comb, teeth shifting one after another.

## The Smelters

Smelters are the warm ones. They are the size of carts, round-bodied, low-slung, and slow, with a hide like wet slate over a body that runs several degrees hotter than the air. Under the ribs, a dull orange glow shows through the skin like a coal seen through a blanket. That is the gland-furnace. It brightens when the animal swallows and dims when it rests.

Their faces are almost gentle: a wide blunt muzzle, small eyes set far apart, and a lower lip that hangs a little open, steaming. They take the red grains in mouthfuls, chew twice, and swallow. Then the furnace flares, the flanks swell, and the animal lifts its head and exhales through a chamber in its throat. The note is a thin, rising whistle. Out of the muzzle, with the steam, drops a bright bead of iron the size of a walnut. It falls hissing into the water and sinks a finger's depth, and the water around it clouds.

Smelters are the only animals on the Flats that seem to enjoy themselves. When one is in full production, its whole body relaxes, the lids droop, and the whistle rises in a long unbroken line. Others nearby tune to it without meaning to, and for a while a little cluster of them will hold a single chord, a slow, warm, sour harmony. Then one falters, and the chord dissolves into the general roar.

The whistle comes before the breath, and none of them are troubled by it. To a smelter, a note is not a consequence of an exhale. It is a thing that happens in the neighborhood of an exhale. The inhalation follows the sound, obedient, the way a dancer follows a beat.

## The Stackers

The stackers are thin and tall, taller than a man, with jointed limbs like drafting compasses and a narrow head that bobs on a long neck. They walk on the tips of their legs, high-stepping, wading in the shallows with the care of herons. Where the iron beads settle, a stacker lowers its head, picks one up in a pincer, and places it on the ridge. Then it picks up another.

Each ridge is a long slope of beads, laid in a pattern too regular to be an accident and too uniform to be a plan. The beads are packed in a close hex, and each course is set into the gaps of the one below. A stacker adds to the ridge from the downstream end, and the tide is always eating the upstream end, so that the ridge is a moving thing, a wave in a slow medium, advancing and dissolving at the same pace.

While they work, the stackers click their limbs together. It's a counting sound, three-two-three, three-two-three, and it passes from one animal to the next down the ridge, a shiver of taps that runs faster than any of them could walk. Nobody leads. Every stacker is a little ahead of the one before it, and the pattern moves along the ridge like a rumor.

## The Ones Who Ride, Thread, and Hang

Between these four are the lesser animals, and there are far more of them.

There are the **pale threaders**, long as your arm and thin as a bootlace, that slide through the mud in tangles, eating the eggs of nothing, eating the film, eating each other's shed skin. Wherever the ground is trodden, they surface for a moment and slip back under. Their bodies are damp and cold and a little luminous, and they discharge faintly when touched. This is the source of much of the tingling in the water.

There are the **riders**, hard-backed and beetle-like, that live on the threaders. A rider rides a threader the way a burr rides a dog. The threader does not seem to mind, and the rider seems to get nothing from the arrangement but the ride. It sits with its legs tucked, the mud parting around it.

And there are the **hangers**, the wet-winged animals of the fog. They are not birds or bats but something between, with skin-webbed wings and short blunt heads, hanging so thick in the low cloud that at midday the sky is a shifting brown weave. They rarely fly. They cling to one another and to nothing, in layers, and they sway slightly with the pressure of the air. The grit that falls from them is the last stage of the line: iron dust, dried droppings, and flakes of shed skin. It drifts down, gets rained into the shallows, and ends up in the grinders' mouths. The hangers are the reason the flour is never used up.

## The Shift

At intervals no one has measured, every animal on the plain changes what it is doing at once.

There is no signal. The whistles stop, or they drop an octave, or they leap up. The clicking halts on a beat. The grumbling of the grinders falters, and in that moment the whole basin hushes to a sound like surf, forty miles of water and breath. Grinders rest their mills, washers fold their fringes, and smelters stand with their furnaces low and their mouths shut. Even the hangers stop swaying. Everything holds.

Then, from somewhere, a note begins. It might be any smelter. The pitch climbs and the mill turns over again, and a different set of animals is doing a different set of jobs. Grinders drift into the washers' channels and washers go to the ridges. Nobody appears to have been reassigned. It is not that the animals rotate. It is that they were always all of them capable of everything, and the plain is only choosing which part of itself to be.

## The Thin-Place Animals

Near the seams, where the ground goes to shell and iron and then to the bell-depth, live things you will not see in the crowd. They are few, and they seem to be there for a reason.

They are called **listeners**, though nothing on the Flats calls anything. They are the size of a large dog and pale as candle wax, with long ears folded flat against the skull and no eyes at all. They stand at the edge of the thin places, facing the pale grid, and do not move. Each one has a narrow crest of bristles down its spine, and the bristles tremble.

It is not clear what they hear. The grid hums, lower than the plain, but the listeners seem to be tuned to something lower still. Now and then one of them lifts its head, and its ears unfold, and it makes a single sound. The sound is too low for the ear but heavy in the chest. In the moments after, the nearest animals shift a little. A smelter's whistle slides flat. A grinder's mill slows a turn and speeds again. The line adjusts.

If a listener is removed, another stands in its place before the mud settles. A person who has tried to count them has reported the same number each time: exactly as many as are needed to stand around the edge. It is possible that they are the plain's way of keeping its footing, the way a tent needs pegs.

## What They Are

None of these animals are born, in any way anyone has seen. There are no eggs, no young, no small versions of the large ones. There are only adults, arriving. A gap opens, and it is filled. A grinder dies in the surf of a storm surge and its shell floats away, and by the time the water settles there is another grinder in the same place, the same size, with the same wear on its plates, as though it had been there the whole time and you had misremembered.

They do not seem to know this. They do not mourn or wonder. A smelter beside a fallen smelter does not slow its work. It is not callousness. It is that, from where the animal stands, nothing has been lost. The line is complete. The line has always been complete.

If you stay long enough, you may notice that each animal is a little different from its neighbors, and also that none of them differ in any way that matters. Each grinder has a slightly different notch in its shell, and it doesn't matter. Each smelter has a slightly different whistle, and the chords they make are the same chords. They are variations, like the ripples on a single pond.

They are not conscious of you, exactly, but they are attentive. They have known where you were before you arrived. The stackers' clicks skip a beat as you step over a ridge, and the beat is already skipped when your foot comes down. The grinders shift their line a little to the left. The pale threaders gather at your heel, curious and cold.

Stand still, and a grinder will nudge against your boot, testing. A washer will lean its fringes toward you. A smelter will come, warm and huge, and breathe on you, and its whistle will begin a moment before the breath. You will feel the current shift around your ankles as you are made part of the flow.

None of them mean any harm. You are silt, you are iron, you are warm, and there is work to be done.
