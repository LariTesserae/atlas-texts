# The Inhabitants of the Bellows Plateau

Everything on the plateau is an animal, and none of them has any notion of the whole. The herds don't know they are the furnace. The draggers don't know the grooves lead anywhere. The engine is what it looks like when many creatures pursue their own small appetites in the same cold, iron-rich place, and it holds together for the same reason a river does: everything is pulled the same way.

## The bellows-beasts

The great grazers are the ground of the place, and the rest of the plateau is arranged around their bodies. A full-grown bellows-beast stands as tall as a two-story house and is longer than that. Its plates overlap like roof slates, blue-black where they are young and freshly hammered, brown and blistered where they are old, and bloomed with rust-orange in the seams. Under the plate the skin is pale, hairless, and hot enough to sting a bare palm. The head is small for the body and slung low. The eyes are tucked into slots under a heavy brow-plate, and each has a translucent lid of layered mica that slides across when the dust is thick. The mouth is broad and toothless, a moss-shearing ridge, and the lips are stained permanently green.

They live for centuries, and their plates keep the record. If you split an old plate, you find bands like tree rings: dense and pure from the cold decades, porous and streaked from the hot ones. The oldest animals are so overgrown that they read as low hills. Tree-ferns root in the joints of their backs, moss cushions sit in the hollows between plates, and whole riveter colonies live in the warm folds behind the forelegs. When such an animal breathes, the ferns nod.

Calves are born in the middle of the herd, where the heat is steadiest. They are soft, gray, and naked, the only unarmored things on the plateau, and they are never left alone. Adults stand shoulder to shoulder around them. Riveters swarm up to begin the first plating within hours, a few thin scabs of iron at first, then overlapping scales, then real armor over the following years. A calf raised in a hot decade grows quickly and brittle. One raised in a cold decade grows slowly and hard. Herd elders can be told apart by which tide they were born into, in the grain of their plates and in their temper.

They hardly move. A basin is grazed for a season, the moss is cropped to a green stubble, and then the herd walks. It is a slow, coordinated shuffle, ten thousand animals lifting the same foot within a breath of each other, and the ground thumps like a drum. They go a few hundred meters, sometimes a few kilometers, to the next basin. Behind them the moss regrows over the trampled tracks, and the riveters who lived on their hides ride along.

Their voice is the breath. Beneath it, below the range of hearing, is a slow pulse that they use to keep their inhalations in step. At dawn the pulse catches, and the whole plateau falls into phase. The hum that follows is not communication so much as agreement, and it lasts only until some restless animal breaks it.

## The riveters

Riveters are the plateau's tradespeople. They are the size of a large dog, low and wide, with eight or ten jointed legs and two forelimbs that end in knobs of dense iron, hammers they grow themselves and reshape as they wear. Their bodies are mottled brown and green from a lifetime spent in moss. They have no eyes to speak of, only a pair of light-sensitive pits, and they steer by touch, heat, and the pitch of one another's hammering.

Each colony strikes at its own tone, and the tone is inherited. A riveter born into a colony learns the colony's ring before it learns anything else and will strike no other pitch its whole life. Colonies gather when a herd needs repair, hundreds converge on a cracked plate, and the ringing of many pitches at once produces a bright, uneven chord. Older riveters can be recognized by their ringing alone. When a colony dies out, its tone is gone for good.

They pay for their board in maintenance. They eat the moss that grows on the plates, and the sweet seep that oozes from the seams, and they sleep against the animal's warmth. In exchange they patch cracks, scrape parasites, and thicken worn armor. They are also inveterate thieves of small metal. A riveter will run across your boot, taste your buckle with its foretongue, reject brass and take steel, and be gone before you have felt it.

## The draggers, the standpipes, the spinners, the couriers

**Draggers** are long, low, beetle-shaped animals with a ridged harness of their own shell, built for pulling. They travel in files of hundreds, each touching its antennae to the sledge in front. The sledges are woven of moss fiber and iron silk and piled with lumps of ore. Draggers are almost blind and follow the groove by vibration. They never turn back on the outward leg and do not stop for anything. When one falls, the file closes over it, and the body is dismantled within a day by riveters at the next junction. They live, work, and die in the grooves, and the grooves shine because of them.

**Standpipes** are the plateau's sessile animals. As larvae they swim loose in the meltwater, small, translucent, and ravenous. As adults they fix themselves to a seep or vent with a root of hooked chitin and grow a hollow proboscis a meter long that drinks the iron-red water and pumps it on into the herd's channels. Their pulse follows the temperature of the water. A standpipe is a living thermometer, and in the hot decades the whole field of them throbs quick and shallow. In the cold decades they beat slowly, like a resting heart.

**Spinners** live in the tree-ferns. They are small, many-jointed, and smoke-colored, with silk glands that pull iron-laced thread from the ferns' sap. Their cables are dull gray and stronger than they look, strung from fern to fern in sagging catenaries that hold up whole canopies of moss. They are also the plateau's wires. A tug at one end sends a pulse to the other, and other animals use this without understanding it. A herd's shudder passes through the cable-web faster than the sound can travel through the air.

**Couriers** are thumb-sized fliers with dusty wings. They carry grains of flux, ash and ground carbonate, from the vents to the smelting sinks and back. They fly in clouds, and no single courier decides anything. A cloud is a shape that makes decisions through the proximity of many small bodies, and it hums a low, stuttering note as it turns. A cloud passing overhead darkens the fog and sprinkles a fine grit on your face and shoulders. Every courier tastes of salt and smoke when it lands on your lip.

## The stokers and the stackers

At the smelting sinks, where the hot springs meet iron seams and the water boils red, live the **stokers**. They are salamander-like, lean, and bright orange, and they swim in water that would scald a hand. They eat the crust that forms at the edges of the basin and would otherwise choke the flow. A sink with stokers runs clean for centuries, and one without them silts and dies in a year. At night they glow faintly from the heat trapped in their skin, and a sink seen from a ridge in the dark looks like a pool of low embers with small, slow lights drifting in it.

The cairns are built by **stackers**, which are the only things on the plateau that make anything nonfunctional. A stacker is a small, upright, feathered animal, vaguely bird-shaped, with plumage of a dull blue-black that shines like oiled steel in the bright hour. The males choose the cleanest, straightest ingots from the drag-lines and pile them into columns with a delicate, obsessive balance. The height of a cairn is a courtship claim. Rival males steal from each other's stacks and topple each other's work, and a cairn that survives a season without being raided is a considerable achievement. The tallest are three meters high, cold and clean-edged, and they hum faintly when the herds breathe in phase.

The cairns are the plateau's only real architecture, and they are vanity.

## The ones who take

Where there is that much work, there are always thieves, and the plateau's economy allows for them.

**Gleaners** are quick, furry, and pouch-cheeked, the size of hares. They dart between the draggers and steal lumps of ore from the sledges, and they hoard them in shallow moss burrows they never seem to use. The system tolerates a certain rate of loss. Gleaners die young, get eaten often, and are nonetheless everywhere.

**Mossbacks** are the ambushers. They lie flat and still under a living skin of moss so exact that the two can only be told apart by touch, and they wait for a courier cloud to pass low. Their tongues are as long as their bodies and stickier than any resin. A mossback can eat a thousand couriers in a season and be mistaken for a rock for its entire life.

**Frost-runners** come and go with the phase. They are lean, long-legged, and pale, with hollow bones and enormous lungs. In the cold decades, when the fog thins and the sight lines clear, they course across the basins in loose packs and take unguarded calves from the edges of the herd. In the hot decades, when the steam is thick and the herd is vigilant and tight, they starve back at the margins, or drift downslope to the rust line and die there. Their numbers rise and fall with the great breath, so slowly that no single frost-runner sees more than a piece of the tide.

## At the edge

Along the rust line live the **rust-pickers**, which are the plateau's undertakers and its exiles. They are small, gray-orange, bristle-legged, and thick-skinned, and they live in the orange mist where the iron-eaters cannot follow. Their food is whatever the plateau lets go: a herd-beast that wandered too low and could not breathe, a dragger that took a wrong turn, a stacker blown over the edge. They strip these bodies fast, before the oxidation can spoil them, and the rust line has a faint, constant sound of tiny jaws working.

## The smallest of all

Under all of this, in the moss and the meltwater, live the things nobody sees. Iron-bacteria stain the runoff a deep red. Mites the size of grains of salt graze the lichen, and mycelial nets bind the moss to the rock and pass sugars along from one plant to the next. They far outnumber everything above them, and their work is what makes the rest possible. They are the reason a footprint in the moss lasts a year, and the reason a corpse is gone in three days.

## What they share

None of these animals has a name for what it is part of. The herds stand and breathe and eat, the riveters ring and mend, and the draggers pull and never turn back. The stackers build their useless columns, and the stokers swim in their glowing pools. Through the bright hour they all turn to face the light and the sound drops a register, though none of them could say why.

Each year the mountains rise a little higher, and the animals extend their grooves and their cairns a little farther, and no one among them knows this is a race.
