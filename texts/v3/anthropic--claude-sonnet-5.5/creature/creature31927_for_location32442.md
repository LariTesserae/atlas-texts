# Those Who Live in the Understory

The first thing to understand is that the Understory has no population in the usual sense. It has a **density**. Count what you can see and you will be wrong by a factor of a million, because most of what lives here is the size of a lentil and is standing on top of something else.

So begin at the bottom, where everything begins.

## The Rustmites

They are the floor, the walls, the weather, and the bloodstream of the place. Each one is a lentil of living iron, with a shell it builds itself from the oxide it scrapes off the ceiling. The shell is never finished. A mite lays down new scale beneath and sheds old scale above, so the shell is a slow conveyor belt of rust passing over a soft, pale, patient body. A mite that stopped shelling would stiffen and be walked on by its neighbors within the hour.

They do not seem to know one another as individuals, but they know the stream. A mite in a braid feels the shells ahead and behind through its feet, a constant fine chatter of touch, and steers by it. Two streams cross at a right angle, each mite passing through the other's gap with no more collision than light through light. If you drop a pebble into a braid, the stream doesn't scatter. It bulges, folds around, and closes over, and for a moment there is a small, neat wound in the pattern that heals faster than you can point to it.

What they eat is the roof. All day and night, crowds of them hang upside-down in the ribs, scraping the corroded metal with mouthparts like tiny rasps, taking the red-brown stone-bark into themselves. What comes out the other end is ore-dust, fine and sorted by weight, and that is the drift you wade through. You are walking on mite excrement laid down over ages, and every step turns up a new stratum: red for the oxide, black for the graphite from the old machine-grease, pale green-gray where copper from some vanished fitting has leached in.

They sing in the only way they can, by rubbing shells. One mite's sound is nothing. A billion is the rain-on-tin roar that you stop hearing after a day and begin to miss after a week. It rises when oil is near and drops when a sexton passes underneath. A veteran of the place can hear a sexton's erupting a full second before it happens by the sudden hollow in the hiss, a circle of silence that opens like a hand.

They die by the handful, all the time. Nothing mourns them, and nothing wastes them. Husks are the fuel, the fill, the mortar. A dead mite is picked up, carried, burned, or eaten, and never sits where it fell for more than a minute.

## The Bellows-Moths

They live in the light. The coins and blades that fall through the pinholes are their fields, and they hang in them with their foil wings barely moving, silver on amber, like scraps of the outside world that have learned to breathe.

Each moth is palm-sized and nearly weightless, and the wings are the strangest part: so thin that you can read through them, stiff as a cast of tin leaf, faintly ribbed. When they beat, they do not flutter but *ring*, a small dry shimmer of pitch. A cloud of a few hundred, rising together in a shaft of light, sounds like a great sheet of paper being slowly unfolded.

They eat oil, but not the pooled oil. They drink its **vapor**, the warm resin-breath that lifts off the seep basins, and this is why they are the couriers of the place. A moth feeds at one pool, loaded with vapor and its own faint residue, and carries it to the next. Somewhere in its travels it lands on a wall, a stoker, a sleeping sexton, and leaves a film of smell behind. Much of the Understory's information travels this way. A moth's wings are the place's slow mail.

At the engine they gather in curtains. They cling to the cylinders' flanks in trembling drifts, six and seven deep, wings half-open against the exhaust heat. When the cylinders flare, the whole curtain flinches, lifts off in a single silver breath, and settles again as the flare fades. Ten thousand moths perform this stroke after stroke, hour after hour. It is the most beautiful thing in the place, and the moths are almost certainly unaware of it.

They are also the sextons' favorite prey. A moth in a light shaft is a conspicuous, luminous thing, and the crater-traps beneath the shafts are the most carefully made in the Understory. Moths know this in the only way moths know anything. They flick up and down in the light, never staying more than a few beats over any patch of floor, and a cloud's slow rise and fall is partly its way of not being predictable.

## The Stokers

These are the ones who look like they know what they are doing, and they do, in their fashion.

Squat and broad, many-legged, and the size of small dogs, a stoker moves with the rolling gait of a creature carrying something heavy even when it isn't. Their carapaces are scorched black and rubbed shiny along the shoulders, where generations have leaned against hot iron. They have big, soft-lidded eyes set low and forward, dull as wet slate, and they blink often against the glare of the cylinders.

They work in columns. Each column begins at the drift, where husks of oil-soaked rustmite are scooped up into the stokers' hollow undersides, and ends at the fuel hoppers on the plinth, three stories up a ladder of iron rungs. The sound of a column climbing is the tick-tick-tick that runs under the engine's beat: thousands of claws finding the same rungs, the same worn hollows, one at a time. On the way up, loaded, they are slow and grave. On the way down, empty, they hurry, and when two columns pass, the upward stokers press to one side without breaking stride, and the downward ones squeeze past, and both are done in a breath.

At the top they tip their loads into the hoppers with a small shrug of the shoulders, a movement all of them share. The husks fall. There is a rustle and a flare. Then the stoker turns and goes back.

They eat at the engine too, off the warm residue that crusts the cylinder walls, a black-brown tar of half-burned oil. The stokers have no other food. They live only on the machine they feed, and so their lives are as tightly fixed as any lives can be: to carry, to tip, to feed, to return. It would be easy to call this slavery, or devotion. It is neither. It is simply what the stokers are, and they have never been anything else.

They have one ritual. When a stoker dies at its post, whether slumped against a hopper or fallen from a rung, the others do not leave it. They gather. They pack the body, limb by limb, into the fuel chute, gently and with an odd precision, and they wait. The flare that follows is a slightly different color, a deeper gold with a green edge, and the whole column stands still for the length of it, waiting until the color is gone. Then they go back to work. No one has ever seen a stoker stand still for anything else.

## The Sextons

They are glass-bodied, long and low, and they lie in the dust like a held breath.

You can see the oil moving inside a sexton, a slow amber current in a body that is otherwise clear. In the shifting light it looks like a lamp that has been laid on its side. Their shells are translucent as old windowpane and just as brittle. They are not fast animals. They are patient ones. A sexton chooses its place at the end of one shift, digs a conical pit, buries itself to the antennae, and stays there, motionless, through the next. The antennae are the only visible part, two hair-fine threads of glass sticking up from the dust, and the floor is full of them, thousands in the whole hollow, like a field of dead grass in a windless year.

When a stream of rustmites brushes the pit's rim, the dust slides, the mites slide with it, and the sexton erupts. It takes a hundred at a time, closing its jaws on the tumbling shells and swallowing them whole. The sound is a sudden crackle like ice cracking, and then it is over and the sexton is already sinking again.

For moths they use the tail, a whip of clear segmented chitin that can flick thirty centimeters into a light shaft and snatch a silver wing out of the air. The moth drops, the tail curls back, and the sexton is a little heavier, a little more amber.

That gain is the sexton's peril. The more a sexton eats, the more visible it becomes, its glass belly swelling, its oil-current running brighter. And overhead, in the ribs of the roof, the wardens have been watching.

## The Wardens

They hang at the edge of the light, thin ash-colored things with too many hinged limbs, folded up against the ribs like dead umbrellas. You would not see one unless it moved, and it almost never does. A warden may spend a full shift on a single rib with its limbs tucked and its head lowered, its gray body indistinguishable from the corroded metal.

They are the slowest animals in the Understory and the most exact. They do not chase. They wait until a sexton has grown fat, heavy, and slow to rebury, and then they drop, all their limbs unfolding at once into a wide cage, and close on the sexton in the dust. The sound is a small, hard crack. Then the warden feeds, briefly, and climbs.

What is left, the glass husk and whatever the warden could not use, sinks in the drift. The mites find it within minutes. By the end of the shift the sexton is iron-red and graphite-black and pale copper-green, laid down in a stratum somewhere under your feet, and the roof is one warden lighter or heavier, depending on the hunting.

Wardens have no enemies. They die, when they die, of stillness: a warden that stops moving for too long simply loosens its grip and falls, and the mites take it, too.

## The Others

Beyond the five, there are things you might not count as living.

There is the **engine**, which has a kind of life of its own. It breathes, in the draft that feeds its cylinders. It has a heartbeat, in the thud of the beam. It eats, in the oil and husks that drop into its hoppers. It has parasites, in the moths that warm themselves on its flanks, and servants, in the stokers. It has a voice, the deep groan that is not a call from any animal but the note that all the calls are tuned to. It is not alive. It is the place's center of gravity, the thing the living arrange themselves around, the way iron filings arrange themselves around a magnet.

There are the **remains of the works**: the gantry curled like a dead fern, the barrels rusted into one lump, the stair leading nowhere, the panel packed solid with shell. These are the fossils of the previous tenants, the ones who built the engine and bored the slot and painted the numerals on the wall. They are not here anymore. But the mites still eat them, patiently, and everything living here lives in their leavings.

And there are the occasional **visitors**, people who come down through the slot out of the level gray of the Flat and stand at the edge of the drift with their mouths open. Some of them stay. They say the pulse drifts toward the beam. They say they stop noticing the noise and then cannot bear the quiet. The creatures pay them no attention, and a person standing still long enough is soon another stone in the stream, walked around, walked over, dusted red.

## What Lives Here, All Told

If you had to say who lives in the Understory, you would say: the stokers, the moths, the sextons, the wardens, the mites. And you would be wrong, or not quite right. What lives here is a **single arrangement**, a slow loop of eating and being eaten, burning and being burned, in which each creature is a step and none is the whole.

The mites make the floor and are eaten by the sextons. The sextons are eaten by the wardens, who fall as dust to the mites. The moths carry the smell of oil from pool to pool, and the stokers carry fuel to the engine, and the engine's beat sets the tempo for all of them, and its exhaust warms the moths who carry the news of the oil that feeds the engine. Nothing is outside the loop. Nothing is surplus. The Understory has been running this way for longer than anyone can say, and every creature in it is exactly as necessary as every other, from the largest warden to the smallest mite.

They do not know this. They know the beam, the light, the oil, the dust, and the shift. They know the sound of their own kind and the silence of the thing that hunts them. They know, in the way animals know, that the roof is above them and the floor below.

They do not know that the roof is thinning, that the light strengthens each year, and that one day a rib will fail and the sky will look in at last. When it does, the engine will go quiet, and the stokers will stand at their rungs with loads in their arms, waiting for the beat.

But that is a later matter. For now the beam tilts, the flare blooms blue-white, the moths lift off the cylinders in a single silver breath, and a billion small iron creatures pour across the floor in the amber light, singing in the intervals of a machine that no one built for them, in a hollow the size of a village square, under a plain that has forgotten they are here.
