# Who Lives in the Pocket

## The Tuners

There are thirty of them, more or less. The count shifts, and the Tuners do not seem to think it matters, since they count the pipes instead.

They are lean, dry-skinned people, weathered to the color of old rope, and they move as though the air were thicker than it is. Speed is a kind of noise, and a body that hurries sends tremors through the walls that others have to read. So they walk with a rolling, heel-first care, and they pause often, one palm laid against the nearest surface. From a distance they look like people testing whether a wall is still warm. Up close it is clear they are listening, and that the wall is answering.

Their ear-wraps are thick felted fibre sealed with wax, dyed with ochre, and worn every waking hour. The wraps are less protection than punctuation. By forty most are deaf anyway, the fine hairs of the inner ear shaken to ruin by decades of the roar. They call this *going into the wall*, and they mean it without bitterness. A Tuner who can still hear is considered unfinished, someone who has not yet learned to listen with the whole body. The young are teased for it gently. The old are honored for having crossed over.

### The Hand-Tongue

Since speech is useless here, they have built a language out of everything else.

Fingers spell against the flat of a palm. Knuckles rap in cadences on plaster. Two fingers pressed in a certain rhythm along the collarbone can say *come*, *wait*, or *I am tired and am sitting with you*, and the last of these is the most common. Across the great distances between roofs, they signal with cloths, long strips of bleached fibre snapped through the light-columns so that bars of shadow flick across the floor like the hands of a slow clock. To read a sun-cloth you do not watch the cloth. You watch the shadow it throws on the ground.

Names are rhythms rather than words. A person is a short pattern tapped on the collarbone, and it is given at birth and rarely spoken. A visitor could live among them a year and never learn one. The Tuners have an affectionate habit of giving outsiders a plain gesture-name instead, like *Sits-Too-Loud* or *Walks-On-Heels*, which the outsider spends months trying to live down.

### Their Work

Each has a craft, though the crafts blur.

The **Clippers** move through the terraces with bone-handled shears, trimming fronds and thinning the filament-beds so that no one plant shadows another from the sound. They speak to the plants as they cut, though not in any way that could be heard. They press a thumb to the base of the stem before every cut and wait, as if asking.

The **Scrapers** climb the clay towers on rope-and-peg ladders, bearing tiny copper picks and stiff brushes, and clear salt-bloom from the tube-mouths. The salt grows in fine white beards that flatten a pipe's voice a half-tone at a time, and a district left untended goes flat and then goes dead. They can tell by touch how sharp or flat a pipe has drifted. Their instrument is a shallow dish of the plant-oil. Set on a pipe's rim, the oil shivers into a pattern of concentric rings, and the Scrapers read the rings the way others read a face. Wrong rings mean a wrong note. They work until the rings are right.

The **Oilbearers** are the quietest of them, and perhaps the most loved. They carry the clear oil in small stoppered clay jars, one at each hip, and it is the closest thing the Pocket has to water. They drink it, cook with it, burn it in tiny lamps that hardly seem to be needed in all this light, and they use it to anoint the joints of the city. They also carry it to the sick, and to the dead.

The **Sitters** have no formal work at all. They are the oldest, wholly deaf, and they sit in the patches of sun with their backs against the resonating walls and their palms flat, sometimes for a full day, reading. They are not resting. What they read, the city's moods, its slow ailments, the first lean of a wall that means a foundation has begun to shift, no one else can perceive until it is nearly too late. When a Sitter rises and walks toward a particular tower, the others put down their tools and follow.

### The Empty Districts

Whole quarters are silent, and the Tuners give them a wide, gentle berth. The pipes there are silted or cracked or simply forgotten, and around each dead tower runs a neat brown ring of withered green. No one talks about it. But at intervals, on days that seem to be tracked by no calendar a visitor could find, an Oilbearer will walk to the edge of such a ring, kneel, and pour a thumb's width of oil onto the ground. Then they stand for a while with a hand on the nearest silent wall. Then they go back to work.

How the city came to hold thousands and hold thirty, the Tuners have never told. Perhaps there is no telling. Perhaps the answer is in the rings.

## The Green

The other inhabitant, the one that far outnumbers the people, is the vegetation, and it is wrong to call it scenery.

It grows in ribbed jade columns and drumhead-fronds and filament-beds, and each plant is rooted in its baked-clay planter or in cracks in the floor. But the Tuners talk about it as one thing, and after a few days among them you begin to as well. It turns together. It leans together. In the late morning, when the vent gusts and the roar climbs toward a scream, the entire floor shivers into a single standing pattern, ten thousand fronds trembling on the same beat, and for a moment the whole green mass looks like one animal flexing its skin.

It is not a gentle presence. It takes the sound and does not give it back. The Tuners say that the loudest terraces are the most *attentive*, and that a Clipper's hand on a stem in one of those beds feels something like the pulse in a wrist held very still. They speak of the plants as *the Listening* and of the city as *its ear*. They do not say what it hears with, or whom it hears for.

They also say, quietly, that it has grown more attentive lately. The lean of the fronds toward the dome at noon is a little sharper each year, and the pause after the roar peaks is a little longer.

## The Small Lives

Pale beetles tick across the frond-edges in dry, patient herds, grazing the filament tips, and their footsteps make the faintest possible pulse in the soles of your feet. The Tuners tolerate them and eat them at festivals, roasted crisp with oil.

Jade lizards sit on the pipe-lips, throats fluttering, so still that a careless visitor sets a hand on one before seeing it. They are drinking the vibration, or the tuners say so. A lizard on a pipe is a good sign: the pipe is in tune. A lizard that leaves is worth following with your eyes to see where it goes next.

Translucent moth-things rise and fall along the sun-columns all day like flecks of glass in slow water. They have no mouths that anyone has found. They live a day, perhaps less, and the floor beneath the light-wells is drifted with their dry, weightless husks. Children (there are perhaps three, and they are cherished and watched with a fierce, wordless intensity) chase them, mouths open, noiselessly laughing.

## The Blind One

And there is the other thing, the one the Tuners speak of least.

It is pale, many-legged, and eyeless, and it is said to hunt by feeling footsteps through stone. Older stories give it an enormous span, and the Sitters, who would know, say it is smaller than the tales suggest. It is rarely seen. In a place where everything shakes, the theory goes, a hunter that reads vibrations must be drowned in noise, and it must have starved or gone mad long ago.

But the Sitters say something else, and only when they are asked directly. They say the blind one has not gone mad. They say it *lies still* through the entire roar, through the gusts and the scream and the dusk lullaby, with its legs spread on the stone of some deep chamber, and that it is the most patient listener in the Pocket. It is waiting, they say, for the same moment the rest of them are.

## Noon

That moment comes when the gust peaks and the sound drops out.

Everything alive in the Pocket knows it. The lizards stop their fluttering. The beetles go still in mid-step. The moths hang in the white columns as if pinned. The fronds cease their trembling all at once and lean, the whole green floor tilting its face upward toward the dome like a congregation. The Tuners stop, with shears half-closed and picks against clay and oil-dishes stilled, and turn their faces to the light-wells, palms flat on warm walls, waiting.

Below, in the dark under the stone, something with too many legs lifts one of them a hand's breadth from the rock.

It lasts a few heartbeats. Then the roar comes back, the fronds shiver, a beetle finishes its step, and the Tuners return to their work without a word, as they have every day for longer than anyone counts.

They do not look disappointed. They look like people who have knocked on a door, heard no answer, and will knock again tomorrow, and who are, more each year, less certain that the door is closed.
