# The Residents of the Seep Warren

No one here is a citizen, and no one is a ruler. There is no one who could be called the city's owner or the city's mind. The residents are the city's method of continuing. Each animal is a small local decision, and the whole is what those decisions add up to, laid down in stone. But if you stayed long enough, and were small enough, and were patient at the pace of the cold, you would begin to tell them apart, and then to know them.

## The Tube-Wrights

They are the oldest and the slowest, and the city's sense of time lives in them.

A tube-wright is a pale, boneless column of muscle, forearm-long, whose whole body is a sheath. It has no mouth and no gut. Its inside is crowded with bacteria, and it feeds them: it carries seep chemistry down in its blood, in a bright red plume that opens from the tube's tip like a fist unclenching. The plume is the only vivid color in the room, and in the dim it reads as nearly black, wet and rich as a heart. The wright is a farmer of its own interior.

Some of the towers have been standing for longer than any record could hold, if there were records. The blackened lower tubes are the wrights' dead, the sheaths left behind when the animal inside stopped, and the living have simply built on top of them. A tower is a genealogy you can read from the base upward. A wright does almost nothing in an hour and very little in a season. It grows a finger's width in a year and thinks, if it thinks, in pulses. When the seep surges, the wrights are the first to answer. They lengthen, the plumes flare, and the whole tower leans into the warmth like a congregation.

They are not aware of being the foundation. They are only aware of leaning.

## The Silk-Terracers

If the wrights are the stone of the city, the terracers are its weather, small and restless and everywhere. They are translucent crustaceans, each about the size of a grain of rice or a little larger, and you can see the gold gills working inside them, and the dark thread of the gut.

They are anxious housekeepers. All day, if a day existed, they walk their sheets, testing the tension with their legs, adding a strand where the membrane sags, cutting a strand where it pulls. The membranes they spin are the city's warm rooms. Under an awning the water is a degree or two above the street, and in that small margin the eggs are kept. A terracer will guard a clutch by standing over it and fanning it with its swimmerets for weeks, and will leave it only when the pulse comes.

They are also the city's most frequent victims. The ribbon-worms know that a terracer's awning is a wall, and that a wall is thin. When a strike comes, the awning shudders and the neighbors feel it through the silk, and within seconds every terracer on that sheet has gone still. That stillness is the closest thing the city has to grief. After a while one of them resumes walking, and the rest follow.

## The Shell-Masons

The masons are slow even by the standards of the room, and they take the most pride in it, though pride is only how it looks from outside. A mason is a snail the size of a lentil, with a translucent whorl and a foot that leaves a shining track. It works in lines. It scrapes carbonate flakes from a dead shell, grinds them with mud on its radula, and lays the paste into a gap with a small sideways sweep of the head. A seam a hand long is a month's work.

There is no foreman, and yet the seams run true. A mason that finds a mended crack will follow it to its end, and one that finds an unmended crack will start. The stitched look of the walls is an accident of habit, and it is the reason the city stands through the pulse. Every tower that has ever been raided and repaired carries a mason's signature in its scar. If you could read them, you would find that the oldest quarters are more repair than original, a city mended so many times that nothing in it is what it began as.

## The Ribbon-Worms

They are the patrol and the terror, and the two are the same thing. A ribbon-worm is long and flat and the color of a peeled almond, coiled like a dropped rope around the base of a tower. It has no eyes to speak of. It has a proboscis, coiled inside its head, that fires out like a thrown thread, sticky and sometimes poisonous, and pulls the prey back to a mouth that has been waiting.

But the ribbon-worms are also the city's memory of its own borders. They travel the tower-walls, and the terracers and amphipods learn from the way a worm moves which streets are safe today. A worm that has fed is slow and tolerated. A worm that has not is a moving edge of dread, and the street empties before it. The worms maintain, without intending to, the order of the room.

## The Amphipod Clouds

The drifters are the city's breath. A cloud may be thousands of animals, each barely the length of a grain of barley, moving in a single volume and bending around obstacles without breaking. They eat the bacterial mat in passing, and the mat's shed flakes, and one another's dead. Nothing in the city is wasted, and the amphipods are why.

They are what everyone else is watching. A cloud rising means a pulse is coming, because the amphipods feel the pressure first. A cloud sinking means the pulse is over. A cloud that suddenly tightens means a blind fish is nearby. The whole city is quietly reading the amphipods the way sailors read birds.

## The Blind Fish

Small, pale, nearly transparent, they hang in the ceiling's colonnades with their fins moving in tiny, endless corrections. They have no eyes, or only the memory of eyes under the skin. What they have instead is a line of pits along the flank that read pressure, so that a fish knows the amphipod cloud as a kind of texture in the water, a weather.

You can count their hearts, and it is possible to become fond of the count. The beat is slow and deliberate, once every few seconds, a small red spark in the glass of the body.

One fish, older than the others, hangs in a particular doorway between two fused spires. It has been there so long that the terracers have spun a small ledge beside it. It is not tolerated exactly, but the city has arranged itself around it, as it arranges around everything. When the cloud comes, it takes three in one gulp, and the cloud shivers and closes.

## The Sea-Spiders

They are the least understood. They walk the spires on legs like bent wire, in a stiff, unhurried gait, with a body so narrow it is almost only the legs. They feed by inserting a straw of a mouth into soft animals, the anemone-like or the sessile, and drinking. They are largely ignored by the city and largely ignore it. They are what happens when a lineage has been alone for so long that it has stopped negotiating. They cross the busiest districts the way a ghost crosses a market.

## The One at the Base

At the foot of the tallest tower, in a hollow it has occupied for decades, sits the thick-bodied one. Half slug, half starfish, it does not have a name in any language of touch or chemistry, but the city knows it. It is the sump. The waste of the whole tower rains onto it, the shed skin and the fecal pellets and the bodies, and it takes all of it in, slowly, over years.

It has never moved far. It has never needed to. It is the city's digestion, and in some sense its conscience, because nothing that falls in this room is ever lost. Around it the floor is soft and dark and perfectly clean.

## The Tuft of Red Algae

The whole city knows about it, and this is worth stating carefully, because it is the closest thing here to a shared belief. In the corner where a fissure lets in a thin slant of light from above, a tuft of red algae has grown. It is small. It is the only thing in the room that makes its living from the sun.

The residents do not eat it. It is not that they cannot, but that something in the way they move around it, the slight pause, the long detour, suggests a kind of deference. Amphipods pass through the slant of light and their bodies flash, and for a moment the room has a color that has no origin in the rock. A terracer sometimes climbs the near wall and hangs there, facing the tuft, and does nothing at all.

## The Dead

The dead are not removed. They are sealed. When an animal dies in the flow, the mineral takes it, a skin at a time. By the end of a season it is a shape under white glaze. By the end of a decade it is an outline, and by the end of a century it is a rumor in the wall.

The city is aware of this in the only way it can be aware of anything: it builds on the dead without hesitation, and it builds in the shape of the dead. A new tube-wright will often begin its sheath in the hollow of an old one. A terracer will spin its first awning across a spire where another terracer's silk has long since fossilized. The past is the floor. The residents walk on it every day.

## The Edge

Along the north wall, where the crack is widening, the water tastes different. It is colder. It is thinner. The bacterial mat is patchy there and the towers are short and unbranched, and the animals who live in that district are the young and the restless and the ones who have been pushed out of better places. They live with a draft. They have learned to tuck their plumes when the cold current flickers through, and to lean toward the center when it passes.

No one in the center knows what the edge is learning. But the edge animals carry the taste of the cold with them when they wander inward, and in the dense heart of the city, the terracers who touch them recoil a little, and then forget.

## The Pulse, from Within

When the pulse comes, everyone answers at once, and for a few minutes the city is nearly one animal. The wrights flare and lengthen, and the awnings belly upward, and the amphipods pour toward the ceiling in their long slow cascade. The filaments in the ceiling ripple with light, district after district, blue-green to gold and back. The masons pause in their lines. The ribbon-worms go still, glutted with the general abundance. The blind fish hangs in its doorway, and for once it does not have to hunt.

Then the pulse ebbs. The plumes retract, the awnings sag, the cloud sinks, and the light fades to its low baseline. The city settles like a held breath let go. And on every surface, invisibly, a fine new skin of stone has formed, and the past is a little deeper than it was.

The residents do not know what is coming from the north. They know the pulse. They know the seams, and the light, and the taste of the water at their own particular corner of the stone. A terracer walks its sheet. A mason drags its bright line across a scar. A wright leans, and the old fish waits, and the tuft of red holds its small fire in the slant of light.
