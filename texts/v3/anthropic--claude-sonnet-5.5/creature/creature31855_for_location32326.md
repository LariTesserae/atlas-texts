# Those Who Keep It

The chamber has three kinds of tenants. There are the small things, which are almost everything. There are the roots, which are patient and hungry and may be something more. And there are the people, three hundred of them, who call themselves *the Kept*. Whether they mean that they keep the place or that it keeps them, they have never settled, and they say the word in a way that allows both.

## The People

They are pale, but not uniformly. The hunters and ore-carriers who go up in the warm months come back with faces still touched by weather, brown across the nose and cheekbones, and within a season the color drains out of them. The rest are the shade of peeled willow, with a faint blue in the hollows of the eyes and at the wrists, where the veins run close. Their skin is always slightly damp. They grease their cloaks and cheeks with tallow to keep the dew from soaking through, and in the glow of the gnat-threads they shine a little, like river stones.

Their eyes are the first thing a surface stranger would notice. The pupils stay wide, dark rings with only a thin band of color around them, and the whites are large and very clean. They look at things sideways, out of the edge of the eye, where dim light lands best. They do not look straight at a lamp. When someone must carry one, they hold it low and below the chin, and other people turn their faces politely aside as it passes, the way you would avoid staring at someone undressing.

They are small-framed and solid, built to hold heat, and they move in a way that is instantly recognizable and hard to imitate. The gait is low and gliding, with a soft heel-first step that tests the give of the mat before committing weight. The arms stay close to the body. The head hardly turns; the whole torso turns instead, slowly, like a heron. No one gestures broadly. A hand rising to point rises as if through water, because a quick hand will send the springtails skittering in a wave that takes a long while to settle.

Their palms are the other mark. Everyone who has ever climbed the root-stair has hands wrapped in strips of hide against the limestone, and the wrapping has become so habitual that children wear little mitts of it as a game before they have ever gone near the fissure. Beneath the wrappings, the skin is thick and pale and crosshatched with old white scars. In the dark, hands do most of the seeing, and they are treated like the eyes of other people. To grasp a stranger's hand is to ask a real question. To withdraw it is a real answer.

Their hair is worn cropped close, or bound flat under caps of sewn hide. This is not vanity. It is a rule of the roots. Sleepers wear caps, children wear caps, and the very old, who sleep deeply, wear caps lined with a thin plate of copper or a scrap of tanned skin, because a root that finds a warm scalp will wind itself into the hair by morning and cannot be untangled without cutting. Everyone has woken at least once with a pale thread coiled behind an ear, and everyone remembers exactly how it felt.

## How They Speak

They speak seldom, and softly, and always with an awareness of the ceiling. It is not fear exactly. It is the sense a person has in a house where someone is ill. The bats, thousands of them, sleep just above, and a shout can bring them down in a rain of small bodies and a pressure of ultrasound that makes teeth ache and children weep. So voices sit low in the throat, a murmur pitched under the drip of water, and the language has softened to suit it. Old hard consonants have worn away in ninety years. What is left is a slurred, breathy, lilting speech, full of long vowels, and a visitor would take it for a lullaby.

The rest is done by touch and tapping. Every house has a plank by the door, and there is a tapped language for it: a knuckle, a fingernail, the flat of the palm, in patterns that carry along the plank-ways from house to house. News crosses the whole chamber this way in a quarter of an hour, a soft pulsing on the wood that the animals have long since learned to ignore. A person's name is not a word but a rhythm and a short hummed phrase together. Nobody calls out a name across a distance. They tap it, and wait, and someone taps back.

They hum, too, when they walk alone between islands. It is partly courtesy, so that no one is startled, and partly companionship. In the long evening the whole chamber carries a faint, unhurried drone of separate people humming separate tunes, never quite in unison, like a hive settling.

Their single instrument is the long reed pipe, played by four or five people in turn. It is breathed into so gently that the sound seems to come from another room. They play it at births and deaths and at the turning of the tides, when the springtails begin to rise.

## What They Do

Work in the Kept Cleft is divided by need, not rank.

**The coal-keepers** are the most honored, and the most quietly feared. There is one hearth in the middle of the town, under its stone hood, and it is never allowed to die and never allowed to grow. Six keepers tend it in rotation, feeding it dry peat a pinch at a time, judging the heat by the tilt of the nearest roots. Each house comes to the hearth with a covered clay pot and carries home its allowance of coals. The keepers can tell, by the angle of a rootlet on the ceiling, whether the fire is a finger too high. They speak of the fire the way other people speak of a large, wild animal that has consented, for now, to live in the house.

**The walkers** are the root-cutters. Every morning, which means every long morning, when the springtails have begun their descent, they go out along the plank-ways with small curved knives of knapped flint, cutting back the tendrils that have crept over the boards in the night. It is the humblest work and the most constant. A town that stopped cutting for a month would wake to find its floors lifted and its doors roped shut. The walkers are mostly the young and the old, who move slowly and notice much. They talk to the roots as they cut, apologetically, in murmurs. If asked why, they shrug. It costs nothing.

**The tide-watchers** read the animals. They know by the height of the springtails on the wall, the crowding of the fish, the way the salamanders arrange themselves, when the water is coming, and how far. They keep the notches on the stilts. Their records are strings of knotted sedge, a knot for each flood, a color for its depth, hung in the meeting-house like a great grey beard.

**The knappers and smiths** work in the long low shop, striking flint and beating copper cold. The metal comes down from the surface in small heavy bundles of ore that someone else has smelted somewhere else, and there is no furnace in the chamber, no fire to spare. So the copper is worked with hammer and patience, folded and drawn into needles, awls, fishhooks, and small knives with edges as fine as a leaf. A good needle takes a week. Their work is quiet, with each hammer blow muffled by a wad of moss.

**The green-tenders** keep the garden, that single bright patch of true green where the noon finger of daylight reaches through the fissure for an hour. It is a small thing, a few paces across, and it is the most cherished square of ground in the world. Children are brought to it on their first birthday and set down in it, and their faces do something odd when they see the color. Green-tenders wear clean wrappings and never speak inside the ring. Nobody is quite sure whether the reverence is for the plants or for the daylight, and the tenders would say those are not two things.

**The climbers** are the ones who go up. A dozen or so, chosen young, trained on the root-stair from childhood, who go out into the surface world in the warm months to hunt deer, to trade, and to bring back ore and salt and the big rib-bones for roofing. They come back blinking, half blind from the brightness, exhausted by wind, and unnerved by how loud everything is. They are respected and slightly pitied. Nobody is sure that all of them will always come back. Every few years one doesn't, and it is spoken of as a choice, in a very soft voice.

## Food and Table

They eat small things, and they eat them slowly. Roasted isopods, crunchy and faintly sweet, are the daily bread. There are white fish smoked or salted, blind eels from the cellars, and pale slugs herded on wet cloth and fried in tallow. Tubers ferment in the drowned lowest rooms until they turn sour and sharp, like cheese. The nectar beads that gather on the ceiling roots in the cold are gathered by the children with tiny spoons of bone, and this is the town's only sweetness, given out at feasts. Now and then there is deer, hauled down the stair by four people and cut up in the meeting-house with a great deal of quiet ceremony, and every household gets a strip and a piece of marrow.

Meals are eaten warm, and the warmth is shared. A house sits close about its pot of coals, faces lit amber from below, and hands are held over the clay. It is the only time the whole family is visibly warm, and it is the closest thing they have to a religion.

## The Courtesies

They have no gods that anyone will name, but they have a great many rules, called the Courtesies, and these are held with the seriousness of law. Carry warmth carefully. Speak under the ceiling. Move as if someone were sleeping. Cut back what creeps, but never in anger. Do not swat, do not stamp, do not run. Never light a fire you cannot cover. Leave the deep water alone.

That last one is the strangest. Beneath the floating mat, under the foot of black water, is the true floor, which nobody has ever touched. Children are given a weighted cord on some quiet evening and told to lower it through a gap in the moss, as a rite of passage, and to see how far it goes. It has never come to bottom. The cord is coiled again and nothing is said. The elders ask only that the cord be drawn up slowly, and that no one speak while it is out.

## The Roots

Ask any of the Kept about the roots and they will answer carefully, and slightly sideways. The roots grow toward heat. That is a fact, and everyone knows it. But there are other things the people say, in low voices, in the hour after the long evening. The ceiling turns to follow a certain house when a child is being born. A hanging strand that has been cut every day for years will begin, one morning, to grow away from the knife. Someone who has been kind to the roots for a long time will find, in a hard winter, that the plank-way to their door has been kept clear by no one at all.

They say these things lightly, and the stories change, and no one insists. But nobody sleeps beside an outer wall without a cap, and nobody, ever, lights a large fire. The people do not think of themselves as owners of the chamber. They think of themselves as guests of something old and slow and hungry that has, for reasons of its own, been generous.

## The Dead

The dead are given to the place. The first generation lies in the drowned cellars, in tannin-dark water that has kept them nearly whole. Their faces are still recognizable behind a film of brown, their hands folded, their hide wrappings still tight. The children are taken down at least once to see them, and to be told the names.

Newer dead are laid in the mat itself, in a hollow cut in the moss, wrapped in sedge, with their tools beside them. The moss closes over them within a season. The roots take what they take. In the following spring, a pale lilac fern will unfurl in the spot, translucent and delicate, and the family will visit it, and hum, and touch the fronds with one wrapped finger.

## The Last of the Descent

There is one woman left who was carried down the roots as a toddler, and she is now past ninety. She lives in the lowest habitable house, half-sunk, with a stone hearth that is always kept lit for her. She remembers wind. She remembers a particular yellow light, and the noise of birds, and the smell of smoke that was not carefully covered. She tells the children about these things when they come, in the smallest of whispers, and they listen with wide eyes and their hands folded, the way one listens to a story about a country that no longer exists. Some of them will climb the stair one day, and stand at the top, and be unable to speak.

When she dies, the town will have no one left who has known the sky as a home. Nobody says this aloud, but everyone has thought it. They take turns sitting with her in the evenings. When she talks, the roots above her house lean, very slightly, toward the sound.
