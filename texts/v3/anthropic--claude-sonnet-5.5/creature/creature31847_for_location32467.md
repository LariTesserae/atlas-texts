# The People of the Drum

They call themselves the **Held**, or in the knock-speech, two short raps and a long one, which means *those the stone has kept*. They do not say it with pride. They say it the way you might name the weather in your own house.

## Bodies

They are small and dense, built for turning sideways. Their shoulders are narrow and their forearms thick, with hands like tools left too long in use: broad-knuckled, callused across the pads, the nails ridged from scraping calcite. A Held person's grip is famous on the surface, where the traders who carry the tuber sacks down the eleven turns will not arm-wrestle them twice.

Their skin is the color of candle wax, and the lamps give it a low gold. Their eyes are wide and dark, the pupils always a little open, and they squint in the thin daylight of the fissure mouth like people who have been shouted at. They do not go blind from the dark. But they use their eyes less than their ears, and it shows in how they hold their heads: tilted, one side forward, as if a voice were always arriving from slightly to the left.

They dress in oiled wool and waxed felt, dark gray and undyed brown, with copper pins at the collar. Each person's pin is stamped with a small pattern of dots and dashes, which is their house knock made visible.

They smell of lanolin, wet stone, and tallow smoke. Nobody smells of anything sharper, because nothing sharp lasts in that air.

## The Way They Move

A visitor is first struck by how little they take up. The Held walk with their elbows in and their eyes down, and they pass on a plank bridge without touching, as if by long agreement between two bodies who both know the plank. They do not reach across each other. They do not gesture broadly. A wide movement in that place knocks over a jar, and a knocked jar is a sound out of place, and a sound out of place is felt in the whole neighborhood of pitches.

Children are taught to carry a full bowl across a bridge before they are taught to read the knocks. A child who can cross the Long Plank on the fourth terrace with a bowl brimming, without a ripple leaving the rim, is said to have *found their feet in the song*. There is a small feast, mostly of mushrooms.

## Speech

They speak rarely and briefly. The voice is a low, pressed thing, all consonants and short vowels, pitched under the roar so that it slides beneath it instead of fighting through. A sentence rarely runs longer than five or six syllables. Conversations are half spoken, half signed, with a few taps of the fingertips against a wall or a rail to add emphasis. A long speech is a kind of impoliteness, or a kind of grief.

Across the gap they knock. Every house has its own pattern, struck with the knuckle on a chosen stone, and the stones are worn pale and smooth from generations of the same rap. A mother calling a child in from the lower terraces knocks the house pattern, then the child's name. The knock passes across the void, is caught, is answered. Strangers hear only a blur of ticks. The Held hear whole exchanges: *supper, come up, your uncle is here, bring the small jar.*

They have a joke about this, and it is one of very few. When a Held person is stupid, they say *that one hears only the loud drip.*

## Names

A child is not named at birth. Birth is announced by a bell, and for some days the infant is called only *the new one* while the Tuners listen. Then a name is chosen from the drips. Not a drip that is taken, but one that has no name yet, a small unheld sound somewhere in the roof, falling on some stone or copper or clay. The child is given that drip.

A person's name is a sound and an interval. *Three-and-a-breath on the green bowl.* *Nine, long, on the stair-jar of the fifth turn.* In speech it is shortened to a syllable and a tap. You address someone by their drip. When you are grieved or afraid, you stand under it.

When a person dies, they do not leave. Their belongings are carried to the Sealing Terraces and set beneath a slow fall of mineral water, and the name goes on falling exactly as before. The drip is not silenced. It is only now the dead person's, and the living do not use it again. Old terraces are dense with these. A family walking the upper stairs will pause under a certain drip and say nothing, and then go on.

The population stays near what the chamber can hold. Births are welcomed, but they are counted, and a household expecting one will begin, quietly and without pressure, to consider which of its own drips might soon be free. Nobody speaks of this cruelly. It is simply a fact of the water: a house can only close so far around a family.

## Work

Everyone does two things: a craft, and a keeping.

**The Scrapers** are the keepers of each terrace. On a set day, they work along their ledge with curved bronze blades and pumice, taking back the calcite that has crept across a doorway, a stair, a spout. They do it in silence and in rhythm, and the sound of it, a wet, patient rasp, is one of the city's most beloved. If a doorway shrinks by a finger's width in a season, a Scraper is shamed. If a channel seals, the whole terrace comes out to stand beside them, and no one speaks.

**The Tuners** care for the bells and the vessels. They know every pitch in the chamber and can hear from across a gap that a jar has shifted a quarter-tone because its neighbor was moved. They tune by adding or shaving lead pellets, by adjusting the depth of water, by replacing a lip, by hanging a bell a hair lower beneath its drip. Their work is never done and never seen. Their hands are stained green to the wrists.

**The Listeners** are often blind, though not always, and they are the nearest thing the city has to law and memory both. They walk the entire chamber over a cycle of days, from the highest dry ledge to the edge of the pool, and they listen for what is wrong. A drip gone slightly slow. A hollow sound where there should be a full one. A bell that rings a moment late. They make no speeches. They stop at a doorway and knock once, and someone is sent.

**The Sealers** tend the Sealing Terraces and are the gentlest of the Held. They choose which drip a cup or a comb or a small worn shoe should sit beneath, and how long, and how to angle it so the stone will take it evenly and clear. A well-sealed object looks as if it were made of glass and has a faint inward glow under lamplight. Sealers speak to the objects as they set them down. What they say is theirs.

**The Fishers** work the black pool from narrow copper boats, or from ledges with long-handled nets, in perfect silence, because a voice over the water sets the fish diving. They catch few. They eat the fish on days of note, the first meal after a death, the last before a journey up.

**The Porters** are the ones who leave. They go up and down the eleven turns with sacks of tuber flour and rolls of wool and coils of salt-crusted rope on their backs, and they return with copper goods sold, and news they seldom offer. They are the only Held who have seen the sky, and they carry it awkwardly, like a piece of glass they don't dare set down. Many find it hard to sleep on the surface. They lie awake without the drips and feel the silence pressing on the eardrum, and sometimes they hum the nearest interval to themselves until dawn.

## How They Live Together

The Held do not have rulers. They have terraces, and each terrace has its Scraper and its Tuner, and the two settle most things by listening to each other. When a matter crosses terraces, the Listeners are asked, and the Listeners ask nothing back, only say what they have heard.

They are courteous to the point of ritual. Passing on a stair, you tilt your head a hair to the wall and let the other pass; to fail at this is to announce that you have not learned the city. They apologize for noise, all the time, with a small flat-handed gesture at the chest, *forgive the sound of me*, though they live in a roar. This is the source of their embarrassment. They know they are loud. They have long understood that it is the water's loudness and not their own, and still they cannot fully stop apologizing.

They are not unhappy. They are calm in a way that unsettles visitors, a calm that comes from being held. They laugh quietly, with the shoulders. They sing rarely and only very low, and only along with the falling water, adding a note where the chamber leaves a gap.

They fear one thing, and it is not death or the dark. It is the closing: a channel sealing, a doorway narrowing, a house folding shut like an eyelid. Every Held child has been shown, at some point, one of the shuttered houses on the lowest terraces, sealed over in clear stone with the table and cups still standing inside. It is not a threat. It is a lesson in what happens when the balance slips, when the scraping is missed for a season, when someone forgets. It is shown the way one shows a child a well.

## What They Believe

Not much, in the way of doctrine. There is no god of the Drum. There are no priests. There is only a way of attending. The Held think of the chamber as something that is going on, and of themselves as being lucky enough to live inside its going.

They say the *song* is older than the first house, that the first builders came in and heard it and cut their doorways to sit in its rests. They say if the song stopped they would feel it in their teeth. Some of the Listeners, the oldest, will admit to more: that the swell and thinning of the drips, over a cycle of a few hundred heartbeats, has never been off by a moment in all the years of counting, and that they do not know what this means and have decided, with something like tenderness, not to find out.

A few do wonder aloud. Some say the song is only the shape of the water and the rock, as a bell's ring is the shape of the bell. Some say it is being sung *to* something, and some say *by* something, and one very old Listener on the seventh terrace is reported to have said, only once, that it sounded to her lately as if the song were listening back.

Nobody argued with her. They knocked on the stone, one soft rap each, the way you do for a good answer, and went on with their work.

## At the End of the Day

When the lamps are trimmed low and the green mats glow along the stairs, the city does not go quiet. It never does. But the Held settle. They lie in narrow alcoves with wool up to the chin, each within reach of their own drip, and they fall asleep counting: *one, two, three, four, five, six, seven*, and the drop. It is a lullaby older than language, and it is the last thing that anyone hears before sleeping and the first thing anyone hears on waking, and for as long as anyone remembers it has never once been missed.
