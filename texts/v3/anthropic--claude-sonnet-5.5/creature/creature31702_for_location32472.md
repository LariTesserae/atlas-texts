# Who Lives in the Throat

## The Sitters

Seven people live here now. Sometimes it is nine, sometimes five. They have no word for themselves that can be spoken aloud, since nothing spoken survives in that air. In tapping, they are the *ones who sit*: two knuckles on a low rib, a pause, then a flat palm laid against the stone, as if calming an animal.

They are small, dense people, made compact by generations of stooping under the vaults. Their skin has the color of damp clay and stays faintly slick in the warmth. Their hair is cropped close, because the drips would mat it and the beetles nest in anything longer. Their palms and the pads of their fingers are thick and glossy, callused to the texture of horn. Their hearing is mostly gone. It goes in the first few years, and the ones born here never quite have it. What replaces it is stranger. They read the room through the jaw, the breastbone, the soles of the feet, and the fluid of the inner ear. A Sitter will lift her head from sleep because a single cell in the north wall has gone flat, and she will be crossing the floor toward it before she is fully awake.

They do not think of the sound as noise. To them it is closer to what a fishing people mean by the sea: a condition, a companion, the thing that is always there and sometimes dangerous.

## Names

A Sitter's name is a place. When a child is born, someone takes the infant's palm and presses it, one after another, to hexagons across the ribs until the baby's hand twitches or the pulse quickens at one of them. That cell, with its pitch and its position, becomes the child's name. To call someone, you tap their cell from wherever you are, and the tap travels through the lattice and arrives as a small precise shiver in the rib nearest them. They turn. Nobody calls out. Nobody can.

The oldest of them, a woman with cloudy eyes and forearms like cured rope, is named for a cell near the ceiling that cracked long ago and was patched with mud and chitin. Her name is a dull *thock* where there ought to be a ring. She is unbothered by this. She says, in a slow patient drumming, that a name should carry the scar of the place it comes from.

## Work

The work is repair and attention.

The animals mend the room, but they mend it clumsily, and the Sitters follow after them. They rework the frogs' mud-patches into better masonry, tapping each new seam to check that it still carries. They clear the channels when the mineral crust grows too thick and the drips begin to pool. They know the wall cell by cell. When a frog dies in its niche, they notice the missing voice, and they will sit with the empty cell for an hour or a day until another frog drifts in and takes up the note. If none does, they carry one over from a crowded stretch, holding it cupped against their chests so their own heartbeat calms it.

They eat sparingly and mostly from the edges of the ecology. They roast beetles, and they take an eel from the deep channels when there are many. They scrape pale moss from the wet niches. They never take a bellfrog. That is the one law all of them keep. Killing a frog is not forbidden by any story anyone can tell. It simply feels like cutting your own tendon.

Once a season, whoever is youngest and strongest crawls out through the hatch and walks two days down the mountain to the herders, carrying dried beetle-paste, calcite dust (good for sores), and the odd frog-shed skin, which the herders prize as a charm. They return with salt, grain, and cloth. They also return stunned by the silence outside, as if they'd dropped something heavy they didn't know they carried. Some of them cry for a day. It takes a week before they can sleep in the open air.

## The Circles

Three rings of blue-white glaze mark the floor where sound cancels, and the animals give them a wide berth. The Sitters use them for one purpose only, and that is to be alone.

A Sitter who needs to grieve, or think, or simply stop, walks into a circle and lies down. The roar hangs around them like a curtain and does not touch them. The drips fall without sound. They can hear, in those rings, whatever is left of their own hearing: the blood in their ears, the small wet click of an eyelid. Some of them believe that the builders stood here to listen, and that the whole room was designed to be heard from these three places. If so, the design is one the Sitters have never quite grasped. When they lie in the silence and look out at the shuddering, dripping, glowing room with no sound coming from it, they say it looks like a great creature dreaming.

They do not speak in the circles. It isn't a rule. There is simply nothing to gain from making sound where sound is absent, and the few times someone has tried, the result was so alarming that no one wished to repeat it.

## The Fern

One Sitter has, for most of her life, tended the tree-fern. It is her whole occupation. She waters it with cupped hands from the basin. She brushes the frogs off its fronds when they crowd too heavily. She has stopped its roots from cracking the glaze further by packing the splits with clay. She is called after a cell in the far wall and has been alone with the plant so long that she taps to it, absently, in idle rhythms.

The other Sitters find this odd but do not discourage it. The fern is the one thing in the room that was not planned, and they believe it matters, though none of them can say why. When it sheds a frond, the frond is laid in the channels to be cemented into the floor with the bones.

## The Dead

There are no graves. The dead are put into the channels, face up, with a beetle case in each hand. The water carries silt over them. The minerals close around them over the course of a few years, and they become floor. The Sitters walk on their grandmothers. They tap on the ribs above the place where each one lies, and the lattice carries it faithfully: a soft, brief, private rhythm that the frogs, for reasons no one understands, sometimes echo.

## The Others

The Sitters are not, they would say, the ones who live here. They are guests of the room, tolerated because they have made themselves useful and because they do not sing.

The ones who live here are the frogs in their cells, the eels in their channels, the birds in their vaults. And beyond those, in a way the Sitters can feel but cannot name, there is the chorus itself: the thing that exists only when all the voices are present, that has a mood and a pulse and a slow attention. It swells at dawn and grows tender at night. It is something like an animal made of animals, and something like a machine made of animals, and it is neither. The old woman says it has one thought, and that the thought is *water*, and that this is the only thought worth having.

The grey birds in the vaults are its ears. When they shift, the Sitters watch, because a shift in the birds comes a moment before a change in the sound, and a change in the sound can mean a cracked rib, a blocked intake, a bad freeze on the ridge. The birds are the nearest thing the room has to a mind that pays attention to itself.

## A Traveler's Night

If you come through the hatch in a storm, they will take you in. Someone will find your hand in the dark and press it to a rib, letting the sound run up your arm until your body understands there is no danger, only volume. You will be given a place against the warm wall and a bowl of something bitter and hot. A Sitter will sit beside you with her shoulder against yours for a long while, to teach your ribs the rhythm.

When you leave, you will not hear well for a day. You will walk out into the enormous cold silence of the ridge, and you will find that you are still humming, faintly, in your chest, on the note of some cell you never saw.
