# Those Who Live in the Gaps

You will see the goats first, then the birds, and only after some hours will you notice that a figure on a far gantry, which you took for a fixture, has moved a hand. The people of the Pour Country have made themselves a part of the scenery. They aren't hiding. They have simply learned, over generations in a place that rewards stillness, to move at the speed of the engines and to be quiet in a quiet country.

## The people

They call themselves **the Setters**, and they say it without pride. It is only what they do: they stay in a place until it holds.

They are lean and broad through the chest, with the deep, slow lungs of people raised in thin air. Many of them breathe in time with the central beam-piston without knowing it. An old Setter at rest will draw one breath for the whole rise of the beam and let it go on the descent, and a visitor who sits beside them for an hour will find their own breathing has lengthened to match. Their skin is weathered to a dull bronze, chalk-dusted at the hands and cuffs. Their eyes are narrowed by permanent squinting, and the reflective visors they wear (thin sheets of burnished tin in wooden frames, pushed up onto the forehead when they are indoors) are so much a part of their faces that a Setter without one looks undressed.

They dress in undyed wool, pale as the slab, so that from a distance a person standing against a wall is only a slight change in the texture of the wall. Their seams are stitched with copper thread, which greens at the cuffs and collars. It marks the tradition, and it is also practical: copper deters the lagging-mites. Each Setter carries a **rod**, a slim, jointed length of drawn steel with a rounded tip, and this is the truest badge of the people. Nobody goes out onto a gantry without one. Rods are handed down, and the wear of many palms polishes a grip into a shape no one has ever carved.

### How they speak

They speak little, and low. In a place where a normal voice seems loud, the people have turned toward other languages. There is a tapping tongue, done with the rod on rail or pipe, that carries surprisingly far along the metal. There is a system of hand signs, small and exact, for use on the gantries, where a raised palm can mean *the span ahead is long today* and two fingers laid flat against the sternum mean *the engine is about to turn.* Children learn the taps before they learn words. A baby is soothed by being held against a warm pipe while someone taps its name on the lagging.

Names are given for the place of birth. A child born beside a soft stair is called after the stair, and if the stair later sets, the name becomes a small local honor. A girl called Landing-Under, or a man called Eleven Paces, walks through life carrying the exact error of the ground that raised them, and is proud of it in a quiet way.

## The trades

No one is only one thing, but each Setter has a leaning, and the leanings have names.

**Listeners** tend the engines. They do not hear them. They feel the slow pressure in their bones, and they know by its shape when a bearing is drying out or a valve is slow to seat. A Listener will stand with a palm on a flywheel housing for a full turn, several minutes of perfect stillness, and then walk away to oil something at the other end of the shed. Their judgment is nearly impossible to explain and almost never wrong. They are the most respected of the trades, and the least talkative.

**Chalkers** keep the lichen. Each colony has a wall-side record, dates and small measurements in pale strokes, and Chalkers know every rosette by temperament. There is a big orange one on the north face of the Ninth Terrace that they speak of as one speaks of an uncle. When a colony dies, which is rare, there is a small quiet gathering, and the last chalk mark is left to weather off by itself.

**Scalers** scrape mineral crust from the condensers and the gutters, working on cords and small platforms with narrow blades. They are the most cheerful, since they are always outdoors and always in the wind, and they are the only Setters who sing, though quietly, and only when they are far from the walls.

**Rodwalkers** are the ones who go into the soft places. They walk the boundaries with rod outstretched, tapping, listening to the answer. A Rodwalker can tell from the note a tapped rail gives back whether the rail is settled or still deciding. They go in pairs, always, one behind the other, with a length of cord between them, and each one counts steps aloud in a low murmur. The counts never agree, and the pairs would not think of comparing them. What matters is that both are still counting.

## Living with the soft

The Setters do not fear the soft places, and they do not love them either. They regard them as a difficult neighbor: to be respected, watched, and not reasoned with.

When a person meets a copy, whether a doubled bird, a third-eyed goat, or on rare occasions a second figure that looks very like a neighbor, the custom is to say nothing, step aside, and leave the copy room. It is the common view that a copy is a stray thought of the country, not yet given a place to stand. Some households set a lamp in a niche near such a creature, or near the place a copy was last seen, so that it may know it is noticed. Nobody claims this does anything. It is done because it seems rude not to.

There is a quiet grief attached to it, too. Now and then a Setter is found to have a copy of themselves: a figure at the end of a gantry, always a little too far to hail, facing the same way and doing the same small tasks. Families do not speak of it much. They light one more lamp in the evening. When the soft place sets, as it sooner or later does, the copy is set with it, a still figure in the slab, faintly warm to the touch on cold mornings, and the family tends it as they would a grave.

## The evening roll

At dusk every household lights its lamp in its own niche, one for each person living, and one more for each person recently lost or newly copied. Walking the catwalks then, you see the whole basin as a ledger of small flames. The Setters know at a glance whose lamp is out, and who might have gone to check. It is the nearest thing they have to a government. Disputes are settled at the lamp-niches, in low voices, and rarely last past the second cup of pumice-filtered tea.

## The animals as kin

The goats are known individually and named by the pitch of their hoof-patter, so that a Setter can tell from indoors which goat is crossing which grating. The flues are not named, but a flock's arrival is announced, and an absent flock is remarked upon. The foxlike drain-dwellers are never called by any name at all. It is considered bad manners to look for them. Setters leave a little meal on the lowest step of a stair at night and never watch it go.

## What they say about the makers

The Setters are not sure who poured the mountains, and they are not troubled by not knowing. Some say the engines poured them, and still pour, and that the Listeners are not tending the machines so much as keeping them company. Some say there were makers once, tall and unhurried, who set the formwork and went away to pour another country somewhere else. A few of the oldest hold that the pour has never stopped, that the country is a single very slow act, a breath drawn over ten thousand years, and that everyone living in it is inside the breath. That is why nothing here is ever quite finished, and why nothing is wasted.

Ask a Setter what they do, and they will say they *stay*. Ask what for, and they will tap once on the rail beside them, and wait, and listen for the answer to come back.
