# Those Who Stay

The bowl looks empty because the people who live there have learned to be part of it.

They call themselves the **Kesh**, and the mountain **Ohl**, the breath. The name of the place means *breath, counted*, and they will tell you, if they tell you anything, that this is also the name of what they are. There are thirty-one of them now, or thirty-three, depending on whether two of the old ones have gone into the reeds for good. They have lived in the bowl for longer than the terraces have been round.

## How They Hide

They are not hiding, exactly. They are keeping time.

The Kesh move only during the Exhale. While the vent-drone rises and the pipe-reeds sound and the gliders shriek, the crunch of litter underfoot vanishes into the noise. A Kesh can cross the whole basin in a single breath, bare feet on the brittle floor, and no stilt-mother will lift her head. When the gas thins and the three-heartbeat silence falls, every one of them stops. They stop wherever they are, mid-stride, on one leg, with a hand half raised, and they stay stopped until the drone comes back.

This is why a visitor sees no one. They are standing among the pipe-reeds, and they are the same pale bone color, because they rub the reed-dust into their skin and hair. They are crouched in the plum-colored ferns, wearing the ferns' colors in dyed leaf-cloth. They are in the terrace niches, where the round stones have hollow places behind them. A visitor who waits through several full breaths without moving will begin to notice how many of the stalks have eyes.

## What They Look Like

They are small and long-armed, seldom taller than a child of the lowland peoples, with wiry bodies and shoulders built for climbing. Their skin has taken on the mountain's amber. It is not a dye. Decades of sweet resin-thick air have changed the oils in their sweat, and they smell faintly of warm honey and cut wood. Their hair is worn in stiff, matted locks stiffened with resin, and pollen collects in it until they look dusted with gold.

Their eyes are large and dark, with a second lid, clear and thin, that slides across against the grit. They blink sideways. In the dim honey light their pupils widen until the eyes seem to have no color at all, and in the peak of an Exhale, when the glow-lichen wakes, the eyes catch it and shine faintly green.

They wear tunics of the leathery leaves, pounded soft and stitched with bone needles and resin-cord, and they go barefoot. The soles of their feet are thick as boot leather and cracked like the floor.

## The Speech of the Gap

The Kesh talk almost entirely in the silences.

When the gliders drop and the bowl holds its three heartbeats, they speak, in single syllables, or a word and a half, or a hummed note. Then the drone returns and the sentence stops where it is. Conversation is a braid carried across a whole day. A question asked before one Exhale may be answered after the third, and both speakers will remember exactly where they left off. To a Kesh, finishing another person's sentence is rude, and cutting a silence short is worse.

Between silences they communicate in other ways. They tap knuckles on hollow stalks and press signs into each other's palms. They carry small whistles cut from the pipe-reeds, each one voiced like a single stand, and each person has one. That is how they are named.

## Names

A Kesh child receives a name at the first hand-mark. The elders walk the child through the reeds until one stand's chord makes the child's chest hum, and that chord is the name. It cannot be written or said aloud in any lowland tongue. It can only be whistled, and it is never quite the same twice, because the stand's pitch shifts with the gas and the season.

When a Kesh dies, the stand they were named for is left uncut. The living listen to it. They say the dead are in the reeds, and that during the heavy hours of an Exhale, when the air brings voices, the voices are theirs.

## The Counting

Every Kesh counts. It is not a skill so much as a second pulse. A child of five knows how many heartbeats lie between one Exhale and the next, and can tell by the color of the haze how thick the coming breath will be, and whether it will become a Surge. They count under their breath, in the same low humming note as the vent-drone, so that a working group of Kesh sounds like a small hive.

Counting is how they survive. Between Exhales the air is clear and they can work, gather, mend, and speak. During an Exhale the air is heavy, and they go still and shallow. During a Surge they retreat to the terrace niches and plug the entrances with pads of oil-moss, and they wait, counting, for the roar to collapse.

The exception is the **Visiting**. Once or twice in a life, an elder will walk out among the pipe-reeds at the height of an Exhale, with a cord tied around one ankle and another Kesh holding the far end, and will stand there breathing the heavy air until the voices come. The elder listens and answers. The one holding the cord counts, and pulls her back at the count of forty. They say that if you stay past forty you stop wanting to leave.

## Food, and the Problem of Water

The Kesh do not drink water. In the bowl no one can, because the oils in the air turn any open water thick and sweet within a day.

What they drink is fruit. The fist-sized red fruits that glow like coals hold clear, cool liquid, faintly bitter, that does not spoil, and the Kesh press it into skins sealed with wax. They eat the crushed oil-moss from the gullies and the pale grubs that live in the pipe-reeds. They eat the eggs of the lantern-frogs, boiled in hot vent-mouths on the mildest days. Everything they eat is oily and rich, and they are all lean and hard.

They do not need lowland water, but they keep some anyway. The **clay pot** in the lean-to, sealed with wax and never opened, holds the last water carried up from below by the first Kesh. It is older than any living memory. Whoever is the **Keeper** that generation sleeps beside it and repairs the lean-to around it. The role passes not by choice but by the pot's quiet weight: a Keeper simply finds one day that they are the one who mends the roof. The pot is meant to be opened only if the mountain stops breathing, and no one knows what they will do when that happens. Some say it will be for weeping, and some for drinking.

## Terraces, Niches, and Cells

The terraces are the Kesh's work, and they have never stopped working on them. Each generation rechinks the flat stones, and each Surge shifts a few. Behind the terrace walls, in hollows the first builders left, are the sleeping cells, low chambers lined with dried leaf and floored with clean pumice, so far from the vents that mat-crawlers cannot follow the warmth. A Kesh sleeps in a cell or on a high ledge, never near a vent, and children learn the lesson early.

Some of the terrace trees have roots as thick as a man's waist grown into the stone. The Kesh say the trees and the terraces were made together and neither can be moved without hurting the other. When a root lifts a stone, they do not cut the root. They set the stone down elsewhere and thank the tree.

## The Hand-Marks

The ochre is theirs. It comes from a red seam in the northern wall, ground with resin into a paste that dries hard and does not fade.

They mark the wall at three times: at the naming, at the return from a first descent to the lowlands, and at a death. Each mark is a whole hand, pressed flat. It is made crouching, low on the stone, because it began as a practical thing. After a Surge, the only breathable air lies close to the ground, pooled along the base of the wall, and anyone who wanted to touch the rock had to kneel to keep breathing. Now the crouch is part of the meaning. You speak to the mountain from below its breath.

The freshest marks are a few days old. The oldest are so faint that only the shape of a thumb remains. There are also very small marks, no bigger than a walnut, pressed in a row by the fingertips of infants held up by their mothers.

## The Cairn and the Drum

The Kesh rebuild the black glass **cairn** at the center of the largest vent after every Surge, and each rebuilding is a small act of authorship. The rules are few: it must stand, the gas must blow straight through it, and it must hum. Beyond that, the builder is free. One builds it tall and narrow so the hum is high. Another builds it broad so it groans. A third leaves a gap where a stone should be, to hear what the gap does. The Kesh do not think of any version as better. They think of the cairn as the mountain's mouth, and the building as tuning the mouth, and they say the mountain has never once complained.

The **drum-log** on the eastern terrace was theirs originally, though no one has struck it with a hand in generations. The stilt-mothers took it, and the Kesh let them. They have a long, odd friendship with the tall feathered birds: the Kesh feed them grubs and mend their nests, and the stilt-mothers, drumming on their stage, sound a particular rapid pattern when a mat-crawler is loose on the floor. The Kesh have learned that pattern. In the tense hour of a Surge, when nothing else can be heard, they listen for it through the roar.

## The Patching

It is not only the Kesh who mend the lean-to. Some mornings, after an Exhale, the leaf-roof has new overlapping leaves that no Kesh remembers laying. The stilt-mothers carry them. They do it clumsily, in their beaks, with no evident purpose except that they seem to like the way the roof rustles. The Kesh do not correct the stilt-mothers' work. They leave it in place and lay their own patches around it, so that the lean-to has become a record of two kinds of hands.

## Strangers

When a stranger comes over the rim, the Kesh watch for a long time. They watch to see whether the stranger counts. A stranger who steps into the bowl during an Exhale without stopping, or lies down near a vent, or opens a waterskin and finds it gone to syrup and stares at it in bewilderment, is a stranger who will not last. The Kesh will help such a person, in the gaps, with a whistle and a hand pressed to the shoulder, and lead them back to the rim.

A stranger who arrives, waits, and holds still through the silences, who counts under their breath and moves only in the noise, will find, at some point, a small clay cup of red fruit-water set on a flat stone where they were about to sit. There will be no one nearby. The Kesh do not introduce themselves. They only make it known that they have decided to let you stay.

## The Last Silence

If you wait long enough at the south rim, near the lean-to, you may catch the moment. The Exhale ends. The gliders drop into the leaves. The last note of the pipe-reeds fades, and for three heartbeats the whole bowl is still.

In that stillness, thirty-one figures stand among the stalks, the ferns, and the terrace niches, unmoving, with amber skin and green-shining eyes, each humming a single low note too quiet to hear. One of them, the nearest, says a word.

Then the drone comes back, and they are gone, and the pipe-reeds sway, and the mountain breathes in.
