# Who Keeps the Sump Ward

No one lives in the Sump Ward. Everyone who does live there is a part of it: a tenant, a gear, a habit of the place. The census would need thirty kinds of counting, and the tallies would only be right for a moment. They live by the beam, which nods once every eleven minutes, and the resident species are best understood by what each does about that.

## The Tin-Shells

The tin-shells are the oldest tenants and the most numerous. Nothing else has taken so completely to the ward's ruin. Each snail is the size of a thumbnail, with a shell that is a small record of where it has eaten. A snail raised on the pump-house columns is oxide-red. One from the footbridge rails is the blue of a pigeon's neck. Snails from the old bronze fittings around the gland are a bright, buttery gold, and the copper-green ones come from the dynamo shed, where the verdigris is thickest. They do not wander far. A family of green shells may spend six generations on a single balustrade, and the balustrade will show it: a patch of iron scraped clean and dark, where the rust has gone into the shells.

They are slow, and they are not stupid. Every one of them knows the beam. When it strokes, a wave of contraction runs across the ironwork, a fine dry clicking as millions of shells seal their mouths against the metal. Then the wave reverses and they loosen. Any wall of them seems to breathe. In the long pause afterward they graze again, and the sound of it is a faint crisp rasping, like a page being turned very slowly in the next room.

They also keep the lamps, though they have never been seen to do it. Look closely at the rim of any lit globe near the low-water mark and you will find a ring of bronze-colored shells, always the bronze ones and only those. They graze the mold's advancing edge and stop where the glass should stay clear. Behind them is a trail of polished glass, and the mat of light ends in a clean, even circle. This is the keeper that no one sees. It is a colony of snails that nobody hired, working slowly at the same task over generations.

The gauge on the pump-house wall has a companion in the same work. Its green column of marks, once cut by sluice-keepers with chisels, is continued. The newest lines are not cut at all. They are grazed, bright bands in the verdigris at the height of each breath of the aquifer, each exactly one snail's rasp wide. Nobody told them to. The column goes on.

## The Mold

In the globes lives something that is neither plant nor animal and has no word in either vocabulary. Call it the lamp-mold. It is a single slow creature spread over many glasses, and it has a hunger and a route. It feeds on the seep of the old gas mains, and it goes where the leaks go. A lamp that lit a corner in one lifetime will darken in the next, and nine steps down the street a dead globe will begin to glow. It does not hurry. It is brighter at the high water, when the air is thickest with damp and gas, and it dims at the low. If it has a mind, it is the kind that runs at the speed of a flowstone sheet. But it has learned the shape of the street, and it lights only where the iron standards stand, though the gas runs under everything.

Small things live in its light: liverworts, mosses, and a colorless fern, all leaning toward it the way the willows lean toward the gap in the girders.

## The Swifts and Swallows

Above everything are the birds, thousands upon thousands of them, nesting in the girders and along the combed ledges. The swallows plaster mud cups into the fluted channels the drips have cut. The swifts cling to the shelves and rafters and hardly ever settle anywhere lower. In the pale hours they pour out through the gap in the lattice, and in the evening they pour back in, and their wings make a dry ticking rustle like a great many small clocks.

They are the ward's weather. Their droppings fall constantly and have made every roof white and every gutter thick. The stink and the richness of it are the base of the local economy.

They know the engine like everyone else. At each stroke the roosting birds lift in a single thin curtain, a wave of wings from ledge to ledge that rises perhaps a body-length and settles again in a few heartbeats. Fledglings learn it before they learn to fly properly. A young swift does not know why it must lift at the sound, only that the whole roost does, and that a bird that fails to lift feels wrong in its body. Every year a few young are born too far from the pump-house to hear the beam clearly. They lift late, a moment behind the wave, and their flight seems to belong to a different, slower ward.

## The Guano Workers

Below the birds is the layer of small things that turns their droppings into the ward's soil: beetles the color of old ivory, springtails in drifts like spilled flour, pale mites, and thin translucent centipedes that hunt among them. The beetles are the most visible. They move in slow processions along the gutters and down the terrace walls, carrying chips of white, and when the water rises they climb and cling and wait. At the draw-down they resume, without complaint. They live in the sag of the housing terraces, in the soft floors under the pale stalked fungi, in cellars grown thick with blue-grey mildew. The kitchens of the terraced houses are full of their small commerce.

## The Salamanders

The salamanders are the ward's lords, if it has any. They are long as a forearm, translucent, and pink at the gills, and they move through the shallows with a gliding patience that makes them look older than they are. In the lamp-glow you can see the outline of the spine inside the flesh, and the small dark stone of the heart. They eat beetles that fall in, fish that stray, and each other's larvae, but only when the beam has nodded and everything is moving.

They know their places. A given salamander will hold a stretch of shallow or a specific lamp's pool for years. At the tail of the eleven-minute interval they slide in toward the outfall grate and wait in the shallows, still as dropped gloves, for the plunge of the water and the scatter of fish. Their whole lives are made of the difference between waiting and striking, and the difference is a count of about eleven minutes.

The oldest are nearly the length of a man's arm and are said to keep to the deepest vaults. No one can say whether that is true.

## The Blind Fish

In the canals the white fish hold in loose queues, all facing into the faint current, all pale as candle wax. They have no eyes, or only the memory of them under the skin. They read the ward through their sides. They feel the beam through the water long before it strikes. They also feel the slow breath of the aquifer and rise with it, so that at the high water the queues drift up into the flooded streets, nosing along the joints of the cobbles as if reading them. When the water draws down, the wise ones retreat to the canals. The foolish ones are stranded in the paving lines and taken by the salamanders.

At the tail of each interval they gather at the outfall grate. When the beam nods and the water erupts, they dart through the churn. For a moment, in the roil, every fish is a small pale flash. Then the water quiets and they return to their queue, and the queue is rebuilt in the same order.

## The Others

There are smaller lives besides these. There are cave crickets in the boiler hall, long-legged and quiet, and spiders that hang beaded webs between the stalactites, with every thread carrying its own drip. There are willows leaning toward the gap in the girders, and their roots move in the water like hair. There are fungi in the timbers, and lime-secreting things in the drip that are turning lathes into ghosts. The stalagmite that lifted the vise on its tip is a slow inhabitant too, in its way.

## What They Share

None of these creatures is a keeper of the ward, yet the ward is kept. The snails trim the lamps and continue the gauge. The mold walks the gas line and lights the street. The birds fertilize it, the beetles compost it, the salamanders cull it, and the fish rise and fall with its breath. All of them answer the beam. This does not seem to be worship or even obedience. The beam is a fact of their world the way the walls are, and they have arranged their bodies around it as you would around a stair.

Stand at the outfall grate at the end of the interval and you will see them all together. The fish are gathered, the salamanders are waiting, and the swifts are tense on their ledges. The snails are still and bright in their thousands, and the lamps burn green-white down the street. Something is about to happen, and every living thing in the pit knows it.

Then the beam nods, and the ward answers.

And then it settles, and a visitor standing there, without meaning to, begins to count.
