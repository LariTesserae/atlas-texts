# Who Stands on the Plateau

They do not think of themselves as many. The first thing to understand about the Multitude is that it is a single fact held in a great many bodies, and each body carries only a small share of it. If you asked one heron how many creatures stood in its valley, and it could answer, it would give you the number. Ask another, in another valley, and you would get the same number. Neither has counted. Each has always known, the way you know the direction of down.

Still, the bodies are worth describing one kind at a time, from the ground up, which is how they describe themselves, if "describe" is the word for a posture held for a century.

## The Pavers

At the bottom are the stone-shelled things, which you would take for rock until one exhales. They are low, wide, and roughly hexagonal, and their carapaces fracture into plates that lift slightly at the edges. This is not a coincidence of geology. The plateau's polygon pavements are partly their doing, or they are partly the pavement's. Neither knows which came first, and the question has not troubled them in a very long time.

A paver is the size of a threshing floor, or of a dinner table, or of a dinner plate. The sizes are not different animals. In the deep herds they are the same animal, the same rust-and-ochre mottling, the same chip out of the left rear plate, at every scale. Small pavers nest on the shoulders of large ones the way a memory nests in a larger memory.

They are the slowest of the Multitude. One breath takes them nearly two minutes, and in that time the oil-like fluid in them moves perhaps the width of a finger. What they do with the time is bear weight. They are the floor of the arrangement, and they have understood their role so completely that it has become a kind of serenity. Their attention, when it settles on you, is heavy and courteous, like being noticed by a cellar.

## The Hare-Things

On the pavers' backs sit the pale hares, and they are the ones who listen. Each has too many ears, six or nine or fourteen, long and translucent like the pages of a wet book (though nothing on the plateau is wet). Each ear is tuned to something different. One follows the heron above, one the paver below, one a neighbor's breath, one the tick of frost on the north face of a particular stone. A hare in a fully attended state is a small parliament of listening, and the ears turn independently, sometimes all in the same direction, sometimes in a slow rippling wave that passes from hare to hare across a hillside.

They are the messengers, though nothing is ever sent. When a grazer's leg-joint creaks, the hares are the ones who turn the news into a turning of heads. That is the whole of their governance. It takes about four minutes for the wave to cross a valley, and by the end everyone agrees that nothing has happened.

Some wear beads of worked copper, green with age, looped over an ear or seated in the fur at the throat. They wear them without vanity, the way a bell is worn. Whether the builders gave them these or they were always part of the pattern, the hares will not say and probably cannot. The beads sit on them so naturally that the question feels like asking whether a river was given its banks.

Many of the hares are copies of copies. You can find the whole gradient in a single afternoon: a hare with all its ears and a wet, alert eye; then one with fewer ears, and an eye like a smooth pebble; then one that is hare-shaped only in outline, a grey eyeless mass with the rough proportion of a hare. The degraded ones are not sick. They rest as easily on the pavers as the sharp ones do. They have simply spent more of their detail.

## The Herons

Over all of them stand the tall thin shapes, taller than a person, on legs like drawn wire. They are feathered in fused mica, so that each feather is a translucent plate, and when one shifts, the sound is a dry rustle like a page turned in an empty church. The sun passes through them and comes out sorted into faint colors, which fall in patches on the hares below.

The herons are the tallest, so they see the farthest, and they hold the diagram. This is not a metaphor. From their height the concentric bands of the herds are legible as bands, and a heron's whole posture is arranged around this. It stands as a fixed point in the design, with the crooked left wing-joint that every heron has, and it keeps its head at the tilt that its position calls for. Two herons a valley apart may differ by a scuffed feather. That is the entire margin of individuality, and they treat it with the tenderness other creatures reserve for a name.

They do not hunt. They do not feed the way herons feed. Their long bills are pointed at the ground, at the frost on the stones, and now and then one lowers its head and rests the tip against a rock's north face for an hour, drawing off the rime. That is a meal. Everything they do takes an hour or a decade, and none of it is hurried.

If any of the Multitude could be called wise, it is the herons, and their wisdom is one sentence: *it is already counted.* They stand in judgment of nothing. They look at you with the calm of something that has read the last page.

## The Grazers

The long-legged things with jointed limbs and no mouths are the only ones who travel, and they travel the way glaciers do. A grazer will walk to a cobble, straddle it, and stand over the north face for a season, taking the rime as it forms. Then it steps to the next. Over a lifetime that is a path, and the paths of all the grazers together form the slow, looping trails you can see from a dome-top, pale lines worn through the grit between the herds.

These are the sole creatures whose movement changes the pattern at all, and even they do not change it. Every grazer's path returns to where it began, and the returning takes as long as it takes. When one passes through a gap, the gap opens, and the gap closes behind it. It is like a needle carrying thread through cloth that heals over the stitch.

Their leg-joints creak, rarely, and every head turns.

## The Fine Life

Between and beneath them, in the layers you have to kneel to see, lives the rest.

There are the beetle-analogues in drifts, glossy and amber and packed shoulder to shoulder in hollows, their carapaces so alike that a drift looks like poured resin. There are the lattice-bodied crawlers, built from something like woven struts with air between, which nest inside the hollows of the larger animals and are carried through their whole lives, like thoughts in a head. And there are the threadlings, thin as hair and pale, strung from leg to leg between the herons like laundry lines. They are the fine wiring of the Multitude. Where a threadling stretches from one animal to another, a slight tension passes along it, and the two creatures adjust by a hair.

If the herons are the posture of the place, and the hares its hearing, then the threadlings are what carries the agreement. They eat the small plants and the lichen rings. They are the only creatures that do any harm, and the harm is tiny, and the plants have accepted it.

## What They Are Like Inside

Their inner lives, if that is the word, do not run in sentences. The nearest description is that each animal holds a *place* in a shape, and holding it is felt from the inside as a low, continuous contentment, the feeling of being exactly enough. There is no loneliness in them, because the nearest animal is always touching. There is no ambition, because the arrangement is final. There is no fear of ending, because nothing ends: whatever fades is replaced by a copy so quickly, and so faithfully, that no one experiences the gap.

Whether the copy is the same creature is the one subject they do not think about, and the subject sits at the center of them, the way a stone sits in a fruit.

They are not unkind. They are, if anything, hospitable in the way a full house is hospitable, where somewhere there is always one more place at the table, and the table has always had it.

## The Edge

Walk to the fringes of the herds and you find the flat ones. These are creatures that have lost so much detail that only the silhouette remains, cutouts of heron and hare and paver, standing on their own thin shadows. They have no interior you can see. Some of them still breathe, in the sense that the edges of them shiver once a minute.

They face outward, away from the herds, toward the open plateau, toward the unoccupied distance. Nobody put them there to guard. They simply are where the resolution runs out. They are the outline of the Multitude, the last line drawn around it, and they seem the most at peace of any. They have nothing left to lose. They are what all the rest of the plateau is slowly becoming, and they stand in the wind looking out, with the patience of things that have arrived.

## The People Who Came

The builders belong to the Multitude now, though it is hard to say in what form. The hut on the southern rim is ringed by exactly one animal of each kind at equal intervals, all looking at the door. It is not a guard. It reads more like an offering, or a portrait, or a place setting kept for someone who is expected. The dust in the hut is even and the floor holds no footprints, and the tools on the shelf are laid out in the order a person would want them.

Perhaps the builders walked out into the herds and were accommodated. Perhaps they were counted, stopped their tally mid-line because the number had been reached, and settled into the pattern the way a stone settles into a wall. Somewhere among the herons, or the hares in their beads, there may be a creature that was once a person with a name and a drill and a sling of braided sinew. It has forgotten, or it has been made not to matter.

## The One Who Is Waiting

And this is the last of them, the one that is not strictly a creature of the plateau but that the plateau keeps.

If you stay long enough, the pattern makes room for you. You'll feel it before you see it: the nearest heron leaning a little, a hare turning its ears toward your breathing, and a space opening in the herd exactly your size. And out among the herons, across the swell of the land, there is already someone standing. They are slightly out of focus and a little too still. They have your posture and your particular way of holding a hand, and something has already been copied into them from you, though you have only just arrived. They have been standing there for a very long time. They are not waiting to take your place. They are waiting to be counted, and in that light, in that even, unchanging light, it is very difficult to say which of you is the copy.

The Multitude does not mind. It never has. The number is the number, and there was always room.
