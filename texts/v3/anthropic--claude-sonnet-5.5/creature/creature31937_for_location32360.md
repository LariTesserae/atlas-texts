# The Inhabitants of the Ward

## The wrights

There are perhaps a hundred wrights in the basin, though no one has counted and no one could. On any day you see three, because the rest are inside the walls, and the walls are where the wrights actually live.

A grown wright is the size of a large dog and much lower, carrying its weight close to the ground on many short legs. Its back is a stack of overlapping plates, pale as the powder and faintly translucent at the seams. Each plate carries fine concentric ridges, laid down like the rings of a tree, one for each season the animal has been alive. The old ones have plates so finely banded they look engraved, and the oldest have gone smooth and yellow, the way ivory does with handling. The oldest you are likely to see has lived a century or more. Their lives are slow, and the town is patient because they are.

They have no eyes. Where eyes would be there are two shallow pits, sealed over with clear resin the animal itself has laid down. Whatever sight once existed has been given up. In its place, along the underside of every leg and around the rim of every plate, run rows of tiny slits, each lined with hair-fine bristles. This is how a wright knows the world. The whole animal is one long ear pressed to the ground. It feels the tick of cooling resin, the shudder of the heave, the slow tread of another wright three streets away, and, at close range, the pulse of something warm and standing still.

Beneath the head hangs the fan of palps, soft, pale, and moist, forty or fifty of them, each ending in a fine fringe. They are the wright's hands, mouth, and trowel. A wright drags them through the resin channels and tastes what is dissolved there, and it seems to know from the taste which minerals are rich and which are thin, and where the flow is warm. Then it draws the resin out in a bead no thicker than a finger, smooths it with a sweep of the palps, and moves on, leaving a wall a little taller than before. There is no hurry in the motion. It is the movement of something that has never been interrupted and does not expect to be.

**The gait.** They walk in waves. The legs lift in a rolling ripple from rear to front, so the body seems to glide on the powder without stepping. Where a wright has passed, the powder holds a low double furrow with a faint scalloped edge. Once you learn to read them, these tracks are everywhere: on the ring road, up the sides of domes, across the market floor, looping back on themselves in long unhurried curves. The curves are the wrights' own shape. A wright cannot turn sharply, and so nothing in the town has a corner.

**What they do when you stand before them.** They stop. The palps lift and spread and hold in the air. The whole animal goes still, and you can watch the faint working of the bristles along its flanks. For a long moment it weighs you. If you are very quiet, and slow in your breath, it seems to decide you are stone, or a stump, or one of the pale standing columns the seep sometimes leaves behind. It bends around you and goes on. If you are not quiet, the answer is different.

## What the wrights are doing when they seal

The oldest story about the basin, told by people who have never seen it, is that the wrights hunt sound. This is nearly right and entirely wrong.

A wright is not drawn to noise as a predator is to prey. It is drawn to noise as a mason is to a crack. In this country, a sharp sound means only one thing: the crust has broken. The plates split at their seams with a snap. Walls fail with a crack. Every real disaster the wrights have ever known announced itself as a sudden noise, and every one was answered by the same act, which is to go to the place and fill it. When a stone drops, or a lizard's claws scrape, or a bird cries out, the wrights hear a wound in the world and go to close it.

The animal caught at the source is incidental. The wrights seem not to know it is there. They arrive, palps working, and lay bead over bead of resin, and the small living thing inside the mending simply becomes part of the wall. A lizard, a bird, a crawling beetle: sealed in clear amber without malice, mid-gesture, wrongly placed. Walk along any old street and the walls are studded with them, small dark shapes held up to the dim green light. The wrights do not revisit them or mourn them. They are just part of the fabric now, like everything else that ever made a sound in the wrong place.

It is an accident of the wrights' whole way of being. A creature that knows only stillness and slow patient building will treat noise as damage.

## The seasons of a wright

A wright is born small, soft, and glassy, no longer than a thumb, in a brood-cell walled up on all sides by its mother. The cells are lined with the pale green film, and the larvae feed on it and on beads of soft resin left within reach. They grow inside the wall for years, and their first sense of the world is vibration through stone. They learn the town's sounds before they ever leave it. When they finally break out, their armor is already hardening and their legs already know the gait.

Between building and resting they molt. A wright about to molt goes to the edge of the town, wedges itself into a cell, and stays for weeks. When it emerges it leaves the old shell behind, whole and pale and hollow, with every plate in place and every leg folded. These husks are everywhere. The wrights use them as wall-stones, fusing them into new domes, so half the town has a lining of hollow ancestors, their empty palps still faintly spread.

Some wrights go into torpor. A wright at the end of a long season will pick a finished cell, walk in, seal the door from inside, and stay. It does not eat. It does not move. The cell glows around it for a year, or ten, or thirty, until one day something is wanted, and the door is opened from within and the wright walks out slow and stiff into the dust. Whether it has been asleep or thinking or dreaming, no one can say.

## The glow

The green-white light in the domes is alive, and the wrights keep it. A thin film of luminous organisms coats every inner wall, a pale, damp bloom that feeds on the resin and gives back a cold, steady shine. It will not grow on its own in a fresh wall. A wright must seed it, carrying a little of the film on its palps from an older cell and smearing it in long careful strokes. A dome that has been seeded by a careful wright glows evenly, like the inside of a shell. One seeded carelessly glows in patches and dies in a season. There are cells in the town whose light is older than any living wright, kept alive by hand after hand of palps passing it on.

## The lichen

The resin lichen is the wrights' other half. It grows in silvery-green cushions, hard as felt, cemented to the north faces of walls where the light is weakest, and it works slowly to take the town apart. In a few weeks it tints a wall gold. In a few months the wall begins to crack. The wrights eat it: they graze along the walls with their palps, cropping the cushions back, and the lichen grows again. Some walls they let go. A wall that has been left to the lichen will split and crumble and slump into powder, and the powder will be gathered into a new wall. The lichen makes a sound while it does this, a thin dry papery whisper, too small to be a danger. It is the only creature in the basin that is allowed to speak.

## The small lives

The lizards, pale things a hand long, live on beetles and in the powder itself. They hunt in bursts, a dart of a few body-lengths and then a freeze, over and over, so that a hunting lizard looks like a series of stills. They have learned the town's rule as thoroughly as anything has. They do not scrape. They step with the toes curled and the tail lifted. A lizard that forgets is set into a wall by the next morning, and the ones that remain are the descendants of the careful.

The moths, broad as a hand, dusted the color of the ground, fly by day and settle on the glowing walls to feed on the film. Their wings make no sound. This is not an accident of anatomy. The moths of the plains outside beat their wings audibly. Here the scales on the leading edges have grown soft and fringed, and the beat has slowed, until a whole flock can cross a street like breath crossing glass.

## The tree

At the center of the basin, beside the seep, stands the black tree. It is twice the height of a person, twisted and stiff-branched, and its leaves are flakes of something like mica, thin and bright. In wind they should chime. They do not. Look closely and every leaf is sheathed in a fine clear film of resin, laid on by the wrights, leaf by leaf, over a very long time. The tree has been silenced. The leaves flicker when the light changes, a slow shimmer of silver and violet, and that is all. No one knows what the tree is to the wrights. They tend it, and in the season of the great seep they gather around it, dozens at once, standing still with their palps raised, making no sound at all.

## The one that does not come in

Far out on the plain, sometimes, something tall walks on long legs, with a slow lifting stride. It comes to the rim of the basin and stops. Its head turns toward the town, and for a long while it stands there, one foot raised as if to step. Then it puts the foot down in the same place it lifted it from, and turns, and goes back the way it came.

Whether it fears the wrights, or the silence, or the powder that would take its every step to the ankle and make it sound like nothing, no one can say. The wrights do not notice it. The town does not seem to mind. Inside, the walls go on thickening by a finger's width, and somewhere another is coming down in a slow sigh of dust. The whole community is a single slow act of mending, done by blind animals for no reason they could tell you, in a silence that is the only thing they have ever asked of the world.
