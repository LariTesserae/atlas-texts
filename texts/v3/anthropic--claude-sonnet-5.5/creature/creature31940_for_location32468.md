# Those Who Hold Their Places

Everyone here lives by holding on. The sea moves, and what lives in it has become a set of ways to stay put while the world sloshes.

## The Post-Holders

The people call themselves *holders*, and the word is exact. They do not say they live at Aru Hollow. They say they hold a post: a pump house, a barge, a stretch of penstock, a length of cable line. Whoever is on a post keeps it against the water and the weather, and leaves it to the next holder in better repair than they found it, or at least no worse.

They are a lean, weathered people with a physiology the altitude gave them over generations. Their chests are deep and barrel-shaped and their lips have a permanent flush. Their faces are burned to leather on the cheekbones and the tops of the ears, and they wear wide dark strips of felt or greased cloth across the eyes against the glare off the water. Their hands are cracked and knuckly from cold and oil. They speak quietly, because the air is thin and the wind takes most of what is said, and because they have learned that on an island shouting only adds to the gulls.

They keep time by the seiche rather than the sun. A day is *two breaths*, one lean east and one lean back, about eleven hours each. They say "I'll see you on the next crest" the way others say "tomorrow." Children are taught to feel the tilt in their bodies before they can name it. A holder half asleep in a pump house will wake a minute before the water turns, because the generators change their note, the floor shivers, and something in the inner ear registers the whole sea reversing.

Their work follows the same rhythm.

- **On the crest**, when the water is heaped east and the currents run hard, the turbines are fed and the generators sing. The holders tend bearings, scrape scale, and pour oil. The pump houses shake. Nobody sleeps well, and nobody expects to.
- **In the trough**, the summits surface and the weed steams in the cold sun. The harvesters walk out onto the wet flats in waders, hauling hooks and rakes, and cut what the retreating water has laid bare. They work fast, because the water comes back as a hissing wall and the flats are treacherous, all slick gravel and weed and corestones rounded like the backs of whales.

They burn dried ribbonwood in the small stacks of the pump houses. It burns slow and green-smelling, with a sweet, grassy smoke. Every island wears a thin blue thread of it, and a holder arriving from the water knows a post is occupied by the smell before the roof is in sight.

They are not sentimental about the machines. The turbines are older than anyone living, and the iron is patched, re-patched, and patched over the patches. Each holder knows their machine's coughs the way a herder knows an animal. They name them, mostly with plain and slightly rude names, and talk to them. The rust-red paint is renewed only when someone remembers, and lichen is allowed to spot it, because lichen holds a surface together better than paint does. Nobody remembers who built the pump houses, or why the power lines still run to places that no longer use them. The holders assume someone, somewhere, still does. They keep the current flowing the way one keeps a promise whose recipient has been forgotten.

## The Barge-Crews

The harvesters on the barges are a slightly different people, looser and louder. They live aboard for months. Their decks are slick with green, their cabins smell of diesel, wet wool, and stewed weed, and they trade stories in the lee of the wheelhouse while the steel combs drag the shallows behind them. They call the sea *the tray* and speak of it with the tired affection of people who serve at a table that never clears.

They have a private knowledge that the pump-house holders lack. From the deck, looking down through the clear water, they can read the weed beds like fields: which stands are ready, which are thin, which torn out by last week's slump and drifting. They know the herring schools as weather. When a silver wall turns all at once and streams westward, they say the tray is about to tip, and they start hauling in the combs before the crest arrives. A crew that ignores the fish loses gear, and sometimes a person.

Their engines thud into the water day and night. The fish have learned to ignore the sound, which the crews find faintly insulting.

## The Keeper of the Lamp

The red lamp-buoy has one keeper. She goes out in a small flat skiff every ninth crest to trim the wick-lamp and scrape the barnacle crust from the chain. Whether she is paid, and by whom, is not clear. The ships the lamp was placed for no longer come. She says the lamp is not really for ships. It is for the holders, so that anyone on the water at night can find the middle, and from the middle work out where everything else is.

She is old, and speaks less than most. Once she told a visiting barge-hand that the lamp had never been dark in her lifetime and she did not intend to be the one to change that. It was the longest thing she had said in a week.

## The Weed

The largest inhabitant is not an animal. It is the ribbonwood, and the beds of stonewort and lace-fern under it. They cover the plateau floor across hundreds of square kilometers, and they behave like a single slow animal that has learned to lean. Each stem is anchored in gravel, but the beds as a whole travel. Every few days a storm tears a stand loose and carries it downstream, and it lodges somewhere new and roots again. The weed forest is always half-destroyed and half-being-born. It is nowhere the same as it was, and it is everywhere.

The holders speak of the weed with something close to respect. It is their fuel, their food in lean seasons, and the thing the fish shelter in. It fizzes in the sun with the oxygen it makes, so the whole underwater forest seems to breathe out in threads of bubbles. In a way it is the lungs of the place.

## The Frogs

The giant frogs live inside the weed and are the sea's real residents. They spend their whole lives underwater, wide and mottled and smooth, and hang in the ribbonwood with their legs spread like swimmers pausing in a dream. They eat amphipods, snails, and small fish. They breathe partly through their skin in the cold, oxygen-rich water, and rarely need to surface.

At dusk they call. The sound is a deep, throbbing honk, something between a struck barrel and a bull calling across a valley, and it carries through the water for kilometers. It starts with one frog, is answered from somewhere east, and within minutes the whole forest is throbbing in overlapping rhythm. The frogs do not sing in unison, exactly. They sing in the pauses between each other, so the chorus has a kind of slow lopsided pulse. The holders on the islands hear it as a vibration in their boots. It is the sound of the evening.

The frogs have also colonized the wrecks. The old dredge on the valley floor has become a kind of frog village: frogs in the boiler, frogs in the gear cases, frogs wedged between the buckets of the raised ladder. They defend their rusted crannies with a peculiar patient stubbornness, and in breeding season the boiler is loud enough to hear from the surface. The holders consider the dredge the frogs' property, and no crew has tried to salvage it.

## The Schools

The herring-like fish are numerous beyond counting and move as if they had one mind. A school of ten thousand turns in a single motion, a silver wall that flashes and is gone. They feel the seiche in their lateral lines before anything else does, the first pressure change of the reversing water, and their turning is the earliest warning. Every other creature here reads them. The catfish tuck themselves deeper into the weed when the schools tighten. The cormorants gather. The holders glance at the water and put down their tools.

Nothing here understands the fish, exactly, but everything trusts them.

## The Grazers and the Bearded Ones

Below the flashing and the honking is a great quiet multitude. Amphipods and snails cover every stem and stone in a ticking, grazing film, eating the algae off the ribbonwood so the leaves can breathe. Without them the forest would smother itself. They are the sea's gardeners, unnamed and uncounted, and their patient scraping is a constant faint dry rasp under the louder sounds.

The bearded catfish browse the weed bases, whiskers trailing, tasting the gravel. Larger ones lie in the hollows under the great corestones, pale and heavy, hardly moving. Some have lived under the same boulder for decades and guard it against newcomers with slow, bulky indignation. They are the sea's householders.

## The Old Trout

In the deepest troughs, where the current runs strongest, live the trout. They are slow, dark, heavy, and long as canoes. They hold station in the hardest flow with barely a flick of a fin, like something that has made a private truce with the water. Most holders have never seen one. Those who have describe a dark shape rising out of the green, an eye the size of a coin, and then the shape turning aside with an unhurried indifference.

They are very old. They were old when the pump houses were built, and they will be here when the last turbine seizes. They know the seiche better than anything alive. Over a century they have felt every tilt, every storm, every slump of silt down the slopes, and they have decided that the correct response to all of it is to stay exactly where the current is strongest and let the world go by. Some of the holders half-believe this is wisdom.

## The Birds

Above, the gulls and grey-backed divers crowd every ledge and scream. Their noise is continuous and personal, an argument that has been going on since before the pump houses were built. The cormorants and diving ducks fold themselves into torpedoes and drop through the surface, hunting through the weed forests in trails of silver bubbles. They eat well, and they nest on the leeward slopes among the cushion plants and dwarf willow, in the wet green moss where the wind cannot quite reach.

The birds ignore the holders except when a barge dumps its by-catch. Then they descend in a shrieking cloud, and for a few minutes the whole surface is white with wings.

## What They Have in Common

Everything that lives here has learned the same thing: the ground does not move, but the water does, and the trick is to be the sort of creature that stays put inside the motion. The frogs hang in the weed and let the surge lean them. The trout hold the current with a flick. The holders keep their posts. The weed is torn out and takes root elsewhere, and calls that continuing. The fish turn when the water turns. Even the gulls, screaming, are holding their ledges against the wind.

At the end of each long day, as the seiche gathers for its slow lean east, the whole population of the sea does one small thing together. The ribbonwood bows. The schools tighten. The frogs begin to honk. The keeper trims her lamp, the holders check their bearings, and somewhere a very old trout adjusts a fin by the width of a finger and goes on holding still in the hardest place there is.
