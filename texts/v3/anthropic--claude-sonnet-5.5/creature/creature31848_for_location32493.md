# Those Who Live at Orrel Halt

No one lives in the Sump the way a person lives anywhere. There are no doorways worn by hands, no cups, no beds. But the basin is crowded, and the longer you stay the more you understand that the crowding is the point. The town is a body, and what lives there lives the way organs live: closely, without ownership, each thing indispensable to the others and none of them aware of it.

## The Drummers

Start with the drummers, because you cannot avoid them. By the hundreds of thousands they move under the pipe-runs, plated and low, their blunt heads nodding as they graze the grey felt from the culvert walls. Up close they are not menacing. They are placid, faintly warm, and smell of wet slate. Their plates are the same brown-black as the cinder, but where the light catches the edges there is a dull violet, the color of the foundry roofs. It is hard not to think the roofs took their color from the animals rather than the reverse.

A drummer's life is a slow, ordered circuit. It grazes, drops its leavings in the culvert where the felt will take them, sleeps against the warm flank of a flywheel pit, and wakes to the beat. When it wants to speak, it strikes its back-plates against iron: three quick taps, a pause, one long scrape. The sound goes out through the trestles and the rails and comes back changed. Answers arrive from every direction, so nothing said is ever quite finished, and the ground seems to be conversing with itself. Their rhythm never lands on the engines' beat. It leans just ahead, or just behind, like a second hand that keeps almost catching the first. If it ever did catch it, you suspect something would stop.

They have no young that anyone has seen. The numbers hold. A drummer that dies is drawn into the felt within a day, and the felt is grazed within a week, and the ring closes.

## The Hangers

Under every roof beam the hangers hang, pale as candle wax, each about the length of an arm, their skins damp and very finely wrinkled. They have no eyes. At the front of each body is the comb, a fan of hair-fine teeth that combs the beaded air, and behind the comb a soft, working throat that produces the hum. One hanger's hum is nothing. Ten thousand in a rafter space make a vowel you feel in your fillings, a long *ooh* that never takes a breath because there is always another throat to take it up.

Their work is the water. They drink from the beads, and in drinking, they keep the beads moving. Droplets that might have sat and clouded on a girder are gathered, passed through the body, and let fall clean, a little salted, a little warm, into the gutters below. If the hangers left, the works would not run dry. They would drown in their own stillness. The line of pale crust at knee height is theirs in a sense: they hold the level by drinking off whatever rises.

## The Sheet-Birds

The birds are the least present and the most beautiful. Translucent as wet paper, they turn in their gyre over the cupola, and from below you can see the sky through them, the pearl overcast rendered in their wings as faint veining. They do not land because they have no need to. The spores drift up on the warm air from the felt and the hangers' roosts, and the birds take them in passing, and what they let fall is fine as flour and settles on the cinder as nourishment for the felt. Their whistle is thin and high. It is the top of the chord, and without it the chord sounds unfinished, in the way a room sounds unfinished when the last window is shut.

## The Sealers

Nobody mentions them, because nobody has seen one. Yet the amber glaze on the iron did not deposit itself. Something laid it down, coat over coat, over centuries, until the rust had nothing left to do. Look closely at any glazed surface and you will find, at the seams and rivet-heads, tiny pits in the amber where a thing has worked. They are the size of a lentil, arranged in slow lines. If you keep still beside a warm pipe for long enough, you may see a bead of amber swell at the edge of a joint, as if from nowhere, and creep a hair's width and harden.

These are the sealers. They are small, dark, and softly armored, moving so slowly that they seem to be part of the metal. They have no interest in the other animals and the other animals have none in them. Their work is a kind of tenderness that has outlasted its reason: they go on tending iron for a town that no longer needs tending. They are the reason nothing new has been lost. They are perhaps also the reason nothing new has been added.

## The Ones in the Brick

Put your palm on the boiler hall wall and you feel the second pulse, quick and small. It belongs to something that lives in the mortar joints and the hollow cores of the brick, a fine-boned, many-legged animal too shy for daylight, which you will never see unless you break the wall. It is not a drummer, and it does not talk to them. It keeps time. Every engine in the basin has a colony of these in its housing, and the colonies answer one another through the ground in beats too fast for the ear. What the engines burn, if they burn anything, is this: a tiny ceaseless work done in the walls, gathered by the machinery and turned into rotation. Or perhaps it runs the other way, and the engines rock their animals like a cradle. The Sump does not answer the question. Its answer is in the not-asking.

## The Tenant

Then there is the windowless block, and whatever the chord leans toward.

You will not be certain there is a tenant, and it is best to say so honestly. The door stands open. The dark inside is not the dark of an empty room. It has depth and warmth, and it shifts. Standing at the threshold, you hear the town's chord change slightly, the way a sung note changes when a singer turns toward a wall. Something in there is answering. Or listening. Or holding the low root note beneath everything else, a pitch so slow that it arrives as pressure against the skin and not as sound.

If it has a shape, it is large only in the way a lake is large: no single edge, a great deal of surface. It may be one thing or a great many pressed together so long they have forgotten the difference. Those who have gone in a few paces report a floor that is not cinder or clay but soft and faintly sprung, a smell like cellars and cold pennies concentrated to the point of sweetness, and the sense of being counted. No one has reported being harmed. No one has reported going all the way in.

The drummers do not enter. The hangers do not roost above the door. The sheet-birds in their gyre bend their curve very slightly around it, as a stream bends around a stone. Only the fungus goes in freely, creeping across the threshold in a thin grey tide, and does not return.

It may be that the tenant is what the fungus feeds. It may be that it is what the whole ring is feeding, the reason the numbers hold, the reason the level never rises. Or it may be the thing the ring exists to hear, the way a bell exists to be struck. The town has no word for it and needs none. The pumps lift, the tower lets go, the steam rises from the rim, and the wet air falls back onto the dished stones in rings that spread and touch and fade. Every creature in the basin turns, without knowing it, a few degrees toward the open door.
