# Who Keeps the Cup

The first thing to understand is that the basin is crowded. It only looks empty because everything in it has learned to be small, or still, or slow, or gone by the time you turn your head.

## The Rosettes

The oldest residents are the tall silver plants, and they are not scenery. Each one grows perhaps a hand's width in a decade. The tallest, standing above a man's shoulder near the tarns, were seedlings when the glacier's last tongue was retreating over this rock. They have watched every fog that ever climbed the valley. Their trunks are wrapped in the brown skirts of their own dead leaves, a thick insulating petticoat kept for a lifetime, and inside those skirts live whole populations: pale springtails, wingless flies, spiders no bigger than a comma, all sheltered from the frost by a plant that does not know it is a house.

The crown is a household too. In the night-warm water of each folded heart, translucent larvae hang and wriggle. Drowsy bumblebees sometimes spend the night wedged among the inner leaves, too cold to fly, wrapped in fur inside fur. A small iridescent bird, green and copper, hovers at the rosettes at dawn and sips from the cups with a tongue like a thread. It fights other birds of its own kind over single plants with a fury out of all proportion to its weight. Each rosette has an owner, and the owners are always changing.

## The Cushion People

Inside the domed mounds live the small round rodents. Each animal keeps a tunnel through the packed leaves and, near the core, a chamber lined with shed fur and dried grass. Their whole lives take place under a roof of living green that is warm and dim and smells of crushed resin. They come out to the edge of the cushion only to cut a stem and pull it back in, and the mound trembles a little, then stills. A cushion may hold a mated pair and three generations of their young. When the fog is down they call to one another through the plant in a series of tiny clicks, and if you kneel with your ear to the moss you can hear the whole meadow ticking.

The marmots are the sentries and gossips. They keep to the boulders and live in colonies under the biggest stones, where the burrows run deep enough that the frost cannot reach the sleeping chambers. Each colony has a watcher, always a heavy, grizzled adult, whose whistle means *hawk* or *cat* or *stranger* depending on how it breaks. The others eat with one eye lifted. When a whistle sounds, the meadow answers in half a second: the mounds go still, the ground-birds drop flat, the herd lifts its heads together.

The ground-nesting birds are brown and round and reluctant to fly. They keep to the seeps and the moss, running in short bursts with their heads low, and they hide their nests in the shelter of the cushions where the warmth comes up through the eggs. A clutch is two or three eggs the color of the granite, speckled like lichen. The parents leave them uncovered for whole mornings and trust the plant.

## The Herd

The grazers are the basin's true citizens, and they think of it as theirs. Perhaps forty animals, sometimes sixty, dun and slender, with long necks and dark liquid eyes and white bibs that show from a great distance. They graze in a loose skein, never bunched, each one a careful distance from the next. They are as silent as anything alive: a soft hum when they are calm, a shrill bugled warning when they are not.

They walk a loop, and the loop is older than any of them. The pale terraces in the moss are the record of it, thousands of days of the same feet on the same line: down to the tarns in the morning fog, up to the sunny rim at noon, back to the lee at dusk. The young follow it without being taught. But the path is not entirely the herd's invention. It matches the line a herder would have chosen, and it passes the ruined corral. On the first cold evening of each season the herd files through the old gate, walking between the leaning stones, and stands a while in the ring as though waiting to be counted. Then it moves on. Nobody closes the gate. Nobody has for a long time.

At the head of the loop walks an old female, gray at the muzzle, one ear torn in a way that has healed to a notch. Her bib has gone the color of old straw. She is not the largest or the strongest. Younger females lead when the herd is fleeing. But when the herd is at ease, she goes first, and she is the one who stops at the biggest cairn on the rim and stands with her nose at its base. She does not eat. She does not sniff for salt. She breathes there, and her breath makes a small cloud against the stone. The others wait, and then she moves on and they follow. What the offerings mean to her, or what she thinks she is answering, is the herd's business.

At night they lie down on the cushions in ranks, legs folded, necks curled back along their flanks. The warmth rises through them. Their breath gathers into a single low haze, and under the moon the whole herd seems to be one pale, breathing animal lying in the green.

## The Ones Overhead

Two great scavengers ride the thermals off the sunlit wall. They are old, paired for decades, and they nest on a ledge no one has climbed to. The bone-white heads and the ruffs of white at their necks give them the look of elderly judges. They hardly ever flap. They tilt, and the mountain lifts them. Each morning they wait above the fog line for the basin to clear, and each afternoon they descend on whatever the night has spent: a grazer that died of cold, a marmot the cat got, a calf born wrong. They are the basin's undertakers, and the herd has no fear of them. Their shadows cross the meadow and the grazers go on chewing.

The other shadow is different. It belongs to a falcon, small and slate-backed, that hunts the edge of the fog. It hangs high and invisible, then drops into the white and comes out again with something in its feet. The ground-birds know the falcon by its silence. Everything in the basin holds its breath when the clouds thin, and the falcon has spent its whole life inside that held breath.

## The One Who Hunts on the Ground

There is a cat. It is rarely seen and never seen well: a low, long-tailed, small-headed shape the color of the granodiorite, with fur that beads with fog like the rosettes. It hunts in the last minutes before the mist rises, when the herd can no longer see the rim and sound is confused. It lies for hours among the boulders. A lift of the head, a slow flow across the moss, and then the whole meadow is running. It kills perhaps once a week. The rest of the time it sleeps on a warm flat rock high on the wall, and the marmots whistle at it all day whether or not it is there.

The herd has arranged its whole life around the cat. Its loop, its sleeping places, its stillness in the fog are all the wisdom of a thing that has been hunted for as long as there have been grazers. And yet the cat is part of the herd's world as much as the water is. When it dies, the meadow will not be safe. It will be different, and it will slowly grow careless.

## The Ones Who Leave Things

Someone comes. The offerings prove it, though the marmots do not whistle at them and the herd does not scatter. The twist of grass at the foot of a cairn is green and fresh one week. A bone whistle appears, a pebble with a hole worn through it by some other water in some other place. There are footprints now and then in the frost by the corral, made by feet in soft-soled shoes, and a small pile of ash from a fire that burned only long enough to warm two hands.

Whoever they are, they come before the fog rises and leave before it clears, and they seem to know precisely how long the light will hold. The herd is used to them. The old female with the torn ear lifts her head at their approach and then lowers it again. The marmots let them pass without a sound. They come as the last of a line who once came with animals and axes and stone-cutting hands, and who now come with almost nothing, only the habit of coming. Perhaps they are descendants of the herders. Perhaps they only inherited the trail. They add their small thing and go, and the basin absorbs it as it absorbs everything, into moss and frost and a slow, continuing green.

## What Breathes Beneath

And then there is the warmth, and whatever gives it. The cushion plants hold heat as a body does. The animals lie on it and are grateful. But the water that runs beneath the roots and vanishes into a crack at the lower end of the tarns carries something with it, and the thin outflow in the rock is never quite cold. On the stillest dawns, a low steam stands over the crack, and the ground-birds gather there, and the old female sometimes goes and stands in it with her eyes closed.

The herders said the basin was breathing. It may be that the breathing is the truest description of its inhabitants: the mounds, the animals, the fog rising and falling, the slow inhale of the valley and the slow exhale of the peak. Every one of them lives inside one another's warmth, and none can say where the warmth began. The mountain gives up its heat a little at a time, and the basin, patient at the top of the world, takes it in and passes it on. The green holds it. The rodents hold it. The herd lies down on it, and the sky packs itself with stars, and the whole cup, by every living measure, is one animal asleep.
