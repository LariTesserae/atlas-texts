# The Tuned

Nothing lives on the Throat Terrace, if you go by what moves. If you go by what is still, the terrace is crowded.

## What You Take for Clay

Along the lanes, in the wall niches and in the slumped, fused places where tiles seem to have melted into bulges, stand figures you will first read as pipes, or the ghosts of pipes. They are the color of the pipes: ochre, rust, bone, smoke. They wear the same soft skin of dust, and the standing ripples that map the chord across every flat surface cross their shoulders and the tops of their heads too. You will take them for statues, or for the terrace's own slow accretions.

They are the people of the terrace. They call themselves nothing that can be said aloud, but if their name could be translated it would be close to *the Tuned*, and most of the meaning would be in the past tense.

## Bodies

They are built like the pipes. The tallest are gaunt and lean-limbed, thin as a forearm through the chest. Others are broad and barrel-ribbed, and all of them have a slightly different lean, lip, and wall thickness, as if fired from the same clay and shaped by different thumbs. Each rib cage is a deep, hollow bell. The bones are dense, dry, and faintly glassy, and they ring when struck, though no one has heard them do it in a thousand generations.

The skin is matte and unsweating, crazed all over with fine hairline cracks like unglazed earthenware, and it takes the color of whatever dust has settled on it. They have no hair. Where ears would be there are only smooth whorls of sealed skin. The sound would ruin an ear in an hour, and over the ages the ears closed, then vanished, and the bone behind them thickened into a shell. The eyes are large, dark, and heavy-lidded, with a second clear lid that sweeps grit away at intervals of many minutes. The palms are the most striking feature: broad, flat, and calloused to a hard translucent gloss, with fingers that end in wide pads. Those palms are the finest instruments they have.

## How They Hear

They hear with the breastbone, the teeth, and the palms. The chord is not a sound to them but the medium they live in. Everything they know arrives as pressure in the body, and what they attend to is not the chord itself, which never changes, but the smallest departures from it.

Each of them hums. There is no voice, and nothing audible could survive in that roar, but the throat and chest resonate with the chord and can bend against it by a hair, a fraction of a cycle. When two of them press their palms together, or one lays a palm flat on another's sternum, their two slightly different pitches interfere, and the interference is a slow beat, a throb in the bones that comes once a second, once every four seconds, once every ten. The whole of their language is the pacing of those beats. They speak by making themselves imperfect against each other, on purpose, in tiny controlled ways.

It is a short language. It has words for *here*, *warm*, *the salt is ready*, *someone is close*, and a great many words for degrees of *nearly*. It has almost nothing for *tomorrow*, because there has been almost nothing to say about it.

## What They Do

They walk the hand-grooves, hand over hand, at the pace of the dust settling. A journey from the rim to the inner lanes takes them most of a day. They stop often, and standing is not rest for them but a kind of attention. When they stand, the ripples of dust lie across their shoulders in the same fixed pattern that lies across the floor, and they take care not to disturb it. Most of what they do is not doing. Any motion smears the map, and the map is the closest thing they have to scripture.

They feed on the salt. The white needle-frost that blooms on the pipe rims is their harvest. They lick it from the clay with narrow gray tongues and take its bitterness as food and drink, and in the coldest hour before dawn they collect a thin sour condensation that gathers on the old copper bands, the only moisture the terrace makes. They need very little. A heartbeat is a rare event in them, and a whole meal can last a season.

They keep the seven-ring court at the center empty. No one is forbidden to enter, and there is no law about it, but every lane that leads toward it stops at the same worn threshold, and no one crosses. The rings in the dust are the sharpest thing the chord has ever drawn, and a body there would blur them. So the Tuned stand at the edge, in a wide circle, facing inward with their palms flat on the nearest pipe, and let the court be.

## How They Are Made

They are born rarely, one in a long while, and each is born wrong: a little sharp or a little flat, its bones not yet fitted to the terrace. The infant is carried through the lanes and set to stand where the ripples lie smooth, the nodes where the chord cancels itself and the vibration is gentlest. Over years it is moved, station by station, toward louder places. This is the childhood of the Tuned, a slow wearing-in, as the wind wore the pipes. Every joint and bone finds the one shape that fits.

An adult is one who is nearly finished. An elder is one who is finished. When the last of the difference has been worn away and the body's pitch has locked perfectly into the chord, the beating stops. Palms pressed to an elder feel nothing at all. There is no throb, no *nearly*, and no word to be made from it. The elder does not fall or fade. It simply becomes indistinguishable from the terrace, and it is placed in a wall niche or lane-side alcove with its hands set in the groove, and there it remains, warm and unrotting in the dry air, the dust settling on it in the same ripples as on the clay.

Whether that is death, no one on the terrace could say. The language has no word to divide *finished* from *gone*, and perhaps there is no difference. It is why the hand-grooves are polished so glassy. The rope that runs from entrance to center is not only worn by the living. It is, in places, a chain of hands.

## The Ones Who Are Left

There are not many. A few dozen, perhaps, and the number falls. Each generation is smaller, and each finishes faster than the last, because the chord's equilibrium is so exact that bodies fall into it more readily than they used to. The Tuned are converging, and all of them know it in the way they know the temperature of the clay. A time is coming when the last of them will settle, the last beat will stop, and the terrace will contain no difference at all.

## The Sharp One

Yet there is a child.

It is small and dark-eyed and thin as a reed, and its note is wrong in a way that does not wear away. It is sharp by a hair, and the hair does not close. It has been moved through every station of the lanes, stood at every node, carried to the louder places and the quieter ones, and the throb in its bones has not slowed. When you press a palm to its chest you feel it, a slow, insistent beat, like a second clock in a room that has only ever had one.

The others gather around it. They come out of the niches, in so far as they come out of anything, and lay their palms on its ribs and stand for hours, listening to the one thing on the terrace that is still saying something. They do not try to fix it. They don't quite know how they would, or whether they want to. In a place where everything has finished changing, the child is the only news.

Sometimes it goes to the rim, where the roofing breaks and the sky shows, and stands at the edge above the bright cloud sea where the air is silent. It has no ears. It stands there with its palms flat on the last pipe, feeling the chord thin behind it, and for the first time the beat in its chest has room to be heard.
