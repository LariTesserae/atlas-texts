# The Residents

The first inhabitants you notice are the small ones, and they are not the important ones.

The frogs are pale enough that you can watch their hearts, a pink flicker under the throat. Each keeps to one rosette plant for its whole life and defends it with nothing but patience. At dusk each sings its single note, and the notes are all slightly different, so that the evening resolves into a loose chord no one frog is responsible for. The lace-winged insects rise with the afternoon water and are set down again with it, and they never seem to fly so much as consent to be carried. The sundews turn their arm-long paddles toward whatever moves. Even the gray needle-billed bird belongs here in its way. It visits once in a great while, steps around the sundews with a courtesy that looks rehearsed, and leaves without ever turning its head toward the rain-keys. It is the one creature that behaves as though it knows whose house this is.

Because the house is not empty.

## The ones who walk

Watch the dawn closely, from the doorway if you can bear the damp, and you will see that the mist does not rise evenly. It rises in columns, one from each pan, each about as tall as a rain-key and about as narrow. They twist a little as they climb. They do not drift with the air. If there is any wind, they ignore it.

These are the residents, or the residents are what the columns are for. They have no fixed body. In the first hour of light they stand at the pans, thin and pale and upright, with a suggestion of shoulders and a lean of attention, and the tree ferns behind them show through faintly. Then they walk. Nothing in the mist changes except that each column is now a little farther from its pan than it was, and the moss along its path fills with small round prints, bare and narrow, which rise and sink again in a minute. These are the same prints that surface in the floor of the egg-house. You have seen where they go.

They walk the terrace the way a person walks a garden they have kept for so long they no longer look at it, only touch it. They pause at each rain-key. They do not press a hand to it, or if they do, the hand is only a denser place in the mist. But the key answers. Its slow pulse changes by a beat, and something below the shelf, which had been drifting, is brought back into step. This is the work. The keys are not tools, or not only tools. They are more like the places where a large sleeping thing can be spoken to, and the residents are the ones who know how to speak.

They are not many. The three bowls on the shelf are the best evidence: three, each always exactly half full. The residents drink half, and they leave half, and the half they leave is not for the visitor. It is for the others of their number who are not here yet, or not here now, who are somewhere on the long slope of the mountain on a walk that takes a century, and who will come in out of the cloud at the end of it thirsty and find the water waiting. The bowls are a promise that someone will be back. The residents do not think of themselves as gone. They think of themselves as spread out.

By midday they have thinned into nothing you could point to. The heat takes them apart. Their water goes into the general steam, and it would be truer to say they have gone into the terrace than that they have left it. In the afternoon the pans lift their glittering threads into the low cloud and the keys begin to hum. That is the residents at their most fully present, and also at their most invisible: the whole cloud is them, arranged. When the rain comes down warm on the moss and the frogs and your own shoulders, you are being rained on by the household. It is not a metaphor they would recognize. They would say only that everyone is home.

## The one beneath

Below the residents, and below the terrace, and lending the ground its animal warmth, there is a larger occupant, and it is the reason for all of it.

No one has seen it, and the residents do not describe it, though everything they do is directed at it. What the palm feels against a rain-key is its breath. It is slow, and very large, and asleep, or something close to sleep, and it is holding a great deal of water. The whole mountain is its body, or a fold of its body, and the terrace is a small pocket where the water it carries comes near enough to the surface to be tended. Its sleep depends on the water staying in circulation and not being lost to the cloud or the sea. The residents keep the circuit closed. Every drop that falls off the lip comes back by afternoon because they call it back. That is their whole occupation, and they give it the entire slow span of their lives.

They are not its owners or its captors. They are closer to what a body keeps near its skin: attendants of the pulse, the small warm shapes the sleeper makes so that something can be awake beside it.

## What they make of you

A visitor who stays is treated without unkindness and without much notice. The sundews turn toward you. The frogs go on singing. The moss fills your prints and then, if you linger, does something it does not do for the bird: it leaves them a little longer, a minute, two, as though considering them.

Sit in the egg-house through the hot hours and you may notice that the floor, remembering your feet, has begun to remember them beside the other prints, bare and narrow, and that yours are no longer set apart. The bench is a little warmer at one end. One of the bowls, if you are thirsty, will let you drink from it, and it will still be half full afterward. The bowl does not seem to mind. It is used to giving up half.

This is as near as the residents come to a welcome. They have no other. They are made of the same water as you are, in a shape that stays slightly longer, and they have never had much reason to be surprised that the water, in passing, sometimes takes an interest in itself.
