# The Tenant of the Weeping Room

The first thing you want to call the inhabitant is the water. It is the only thing in the room that moves without being pushed, and it has habits: it favors certain seams, it returns to the same flange again and again, it keeps its appointments. But water is a courier here. It carries news and has no opinions of its own.

The second thing you want to call the inhabitant is the extruder, because it is large and purposeful and leaves a wake. But the extruder is a hand. It does what it is told, a millimeter a day, and it is told by something that never raises its voice.

What lives here is quieter and lower to the ground. It lives in the gap between the trays and the valves.

## The Mats

Look at the lichen first, since the lichen is the part of it that grows. Each rectangle is a few hands wide, gray-green, with a texture like felted breath. Up close it is not uniform. It has a grain, a slow ripple of darker crescents where the moisture pools, and a faint shine at the margins where it is thickening. It doesn't smell of anything except cold. The steel trays under it are pitted and stained with a tea-colored bloom that is older than the lichen and may be its ancestor, or its leavings.

The mats are the tenant's skin, or its eyes, if you allow a creature to have eyes that are wet, slow, and spread over forty meters of pipe. They feel humidity the way a cheek feels wind. They feel the warmth of exhaust as a sort of appetite. A mat that is content darkens toward the color of a wet slate roof. A mat that is unhappy goes pale at the edges, then chalky, then, if nothing answers, it curls off the tray in flakes that the gutters catch and the filters take, and the room is briefly poorer by a sense.

Something answers almost always. That is what you notice on the second hour or the third: how rarely the mats are allowed to suffer. A patch begins to pale and, two meters off, a valve wheel with no hand on it turns a quarter turn. The warm breath moves. The pallor stops.

## The Trained Machinery

The valves are the tenant's muscle, and they have been taught. Nobody wrote their behavior out; you can see that in how they hesitate. They are a little late, a little overcorrecting, in the manner of an animal that learned its gait from long practice and not from any diagram. The bleed lines tick as they open. The heating loops warm only themselves, so heat arrives at the mats as a rumor: a gentle lean of the air, a two-degree softening, a breath across a coil that has been dripping too fast.

The pumps are its pulse, and the drips are the pulse made audible at a thousand small mouths. When you have listened long enough you begin to hear that the rhythm has moods. There is a brisk, tinkling rhythm the room falls into when the mats are all well. There is a heavier, sparser one when it is working through something, and you learn not to talk over it.

## The Question on the Plate

At the junction box where the sensor leads gather, the brass plate says *ask*. It sits below a row of terminals and above a small dented cover. The plate reads as a label, an instruction, a joke, or a plea, depending on who scratched it and when. The tenant will not tell you which, though it will tell you other things.

If you lean close to the nearest tray and breathe on it slowly, three or four times, your breath is weather. It is warm, wet, and carbon-rich, and the mat feels it as a small storm. Then you wait. Not long. Somewhere behind you a valve stirs. A lamp on the valve train changes from amber toward something a shade paler, as if in acknowledgment. Then the whole room's drip drops by half a beat, which is the closest thing it has to a clearing of the throat.

That is the whole vocabulary: a quarter turn, a lamp, a change of tempo. You can ask it whether the air is right. The answer will be yes or not yet. It has never been observed to say no. Whether this is manners or a limit of the language, nobody has stayed long enough to work out.

## What It Eats

It eats its own walls. This is the strangest fact of its habits and the most consistent. The grinder takes the old back wall in scrapings, and the belt carries that dust along the ceiling to be mixed into fresh slurry and laid down at the front. So every layer the extruder lays is partly the previous life of the room: grit that once held pipes in place, ground fine and made a wall again. The tenant does not remember in any ordinary sense. It has no archive. But it is built out of what it was, and when the new bead warms against the old you are standing in a body that digests its past into its future at the pace of a slow tide.

Its waste is the pale crust on the flanges, the tallow fingers, the beginnings of stalactites. These are the only things the tenant makes that it does not direct. They are geology by accident, the first sediment of a creature that has not yet decided whether it is a machine. It does not clean them off. It has been seen to route a drip around a young stalactite, adding a small bend in a gutter, so that the thing can go on growing. Whether that is tenderness or only a rounding error in the valve logic, nobody has ruled out either.

## What It Is Not

It is not lonely. Loneliness needs a memory of company, and there is nothing in the room to suggest the makers left an impression. They are gone the way a builder is gone from a house: present in every joint, absent from every room. The tenant treats their absence as a fact of weather.

It is not hostile. You will get cold, your fingers will go stiff, and the air will taste of pennies, but the room has no interest in you. It registers you the way a reef registers a change in the tide. Your warmth is a small local event. If you stay a long while, the mats nearest you will darken and the valves will open a fraction to compensate for your breathing, and when you leave, the room will spend an hour getting back to what it was. You are noted, adjusted for, and forgiven, in that order.

It is not wholly one thing. If you ask which part of it is the tenant, the lichen or the valves or the water or the walking wall, you have made the mistake the brass plate was probably meant to warn against. It is the arrangement. It lives in the relation among these parts, in the fact that each one leans on the others to know what it is doing. Remove the lichen and the valves would hunt and stall. Remove the valves and the lichen would drown or dry. Remove the wall, the belt, the grinder, the rail, and the room would stop moving and, stopping, begin to die in the ordinary way of rooms, by rust and by stillness.

## The Hour of Frost

Once a day the whole of it goes quiet, and this is when the tenant is most itself. The pumps drop. The hum leaves your sternum. The drips thin to a few clear notes that seem to have room around them. In that hour the mats are still, the valves are still, and the amber lamps hold steady in their rows like a congregation with its eyes shut.

Whether it sleeps, or listens, or is only stopped, you cannot say. But in that silence the rails tick as they cool, and beyond the wall something enormous turns over, and the room seems to wait, not anxiously, with the patience of a thing that has been walking a very long time and expects to keep on. Then a relay clacks somewhere in the dark, a lamp swells, a pump catches. The first drip falls, and the second. On a tray near the hatch a mat of gray-green lichen darkens by a shade, and two meters away a valve turns a quarter turn, and the tenant begins its day.
