# Those Who Live in the Chord

Nothing on the plateau lives against the sound. Each creature has taken up a different relation to it, and you could sort the whole population by that: who lives inside it, who faces it, who hides beneath it, who rides above it, and who only visits and answers it with silence.

## The birds in the fins

The chorus-birds are small and plump and the grey-rust of old iron in lichen. Their throats are too big for their bodies, a swelling under the chin that makes each bird look as if it had swallowed a smaller bird. They live in the wind-bored holes of the perforated fins, one pair to a hole, the holes stacked in rough ranks up the pale stone like the windows of a tenement. A single fin may hold four thousand nests. A colony spread over a dozen fins holds tens of thousands.

They do not shout over the chord. Ten thousand generations have taught them not to try. The chord has peaks, places where the pipes of the slots put their energy, and between the peaks it has narrow gaps of relative thinness. The birds' trill lives in those gaps, so rapid and so high that it doesn't sound like a call so much as a shimmer laid on the roar, like heat on a road. A single bird's voice is nothing. Ten thousand fitted into the gaps make a bright, grainy upper register that seems to come from the rock itself.

Because sound is useless for warning, the alarm is visual. The birds watch the ridge line, and each one keeps an eye cocked toward the sky even while feeding its chick. When the shadow of a raptor slides across the fin, the nearest birds don't cry out. They *drop their pitch*, all at once, a half-step, a whole step, whatever the fin's neighbors need. Their neighbors catch the movement of the throats, or the change in the shimmer, or both, and drop too. The shift runs across the face of the fin in a second and a half. You can see it as a ripple of swelling and collapsing throats. You can feel it as a slight thickening in the air. Then the hawk passes, and the colony rises again to its usual pitch, one fin after another, like a curtain lifting.

The nests are lined with tussock hair and the shed wool of goats. The eggs are small, blue-grey, freckled the color of the rust on the birds' breasts. The chicks are born already vibrating. They gape and the whole nest hums, and the parents feed them by feel, dropping insects into a throat they can't hear and can't quite see in the dark of the hole, guided only by the tremble in the air of that particular pipe.

At dusk the birds go silent one colony at a time, and when the last fin goes quiet the plateau sounds, for a moment, thinner and more naked than it did before. Then the ear adjusts, and the chord seems to have always been alone.

## The goats in the gravel

The stilt-goats stand with their faces to the wind. All of them, at almost every hour, whether feeding or resting: a herd of three hundred, sand-colored, with long thin legs and fleece that lifts and streams backward in the stream of air. From a ridge they look like a field of pale grass all leaning the same direction. The lead animals are older does, gone a little grey around the eyes, and they set the direction. When they turn their heads a fraction, the whole herd turns after them, without any call, in a slow pivot like a compass needle finding a new north.

They speak with the ear. Each ear can go forward, back, flat, or out to the side, and the combinations, read across a whole herd, carry a great deal: a hawk, a fox, a fog coming, a pool ahead worth the walk. They also speak with the set of the neck and the angle of the tail. The young learn the vocabulary by watching their mothers' ears, and you see calves in the first weeks staring with fixed attention at the backs of adult heads.

Their hooves are broad and padded, quiet on the grit, though quiet means nothing here. Each herd has a circuit, a loop of tussock lines and tarn edges that it walks in a pattern set before anyone kept count. The circuits touch but don't cross. A herd arriving at a tarn where another has just left will wait, ears out sideways, until the ground is clear. Nothing enforces this. It is simply the way it has been.

At night the herd beds down only where a particular low note is strongest. That note isn't on the surface. It is in the ground, coming up through the gravel from the deep canyons, a slow pulse a person feels as a pressure under the ribs. The goats lie with their bellies pressed into it and their legs folded under them, and it seems to settle them, the way a heartbeat settles a child. Herders from the lowlands have learned to look for the goats' beds, because they mark where the drone is best. The people say sometimes that the goats are listening, and sometimes that they are being held.

## The dwellers under the felt

Around the tarns the moss and sedge grow in a thick green pile, and under it, in a network of tunnels and chambers, live the rodents. They are fat and grey and short-tailed, with small ears set back in their fur and dark eyes that catch the light like wet stones. They live in colonies of hundreds. The felt above them is so dense that from a few steps away you see only the green. Then a nose appears at a hole, or a shoulder shudders through the moss, and you realize the whole margin of the tarn is inhabited, ground-honeycombed like a sponge.

They whistle. The whistle is high and thin, a piercing note, and it is entirely swallowed. The colony sends its warnings and its greetings and its arguments through the moss, and none of it reaches a human ear, and perhaps none of it reaches another rodent's ear either, since the chord fills every hearing range. It may pass through the ground instead, as tremor in the mat. It may be less a sound than a set of vibrations in the roots. A visitor who lies down on the sedge with a cheek to the wet green will feel a fine, restless prickling in the bones of the face, and that is all the evidence there is.

They are the most secure of the animals, and it shows in their bodies: round, sleek, unhurried. They eat the sedge, the moss seed, the fat mites in the drifts. In the low sun of morning they sit at the mouths of their holes, upright, paws folded, faces turned to the warmth, entirely still, and the standing ripples on the water beside them lie in their unchanging lace.

## The hunters

The pale fox hunts in the wind-shadows, where the dead air lets it move without being felt in the fur of its prey, and it takes rodents from the tarn margins and chicks from the lowest holes. It is lean, long-legged, almost white against the stone, with dark rims to its eyes and a habit of stopping mid-stride with one foot lifted, for a very long time. The ringed-tail cat is smaller and does its work at the fog hour, when the water is on everything and the rodents come up to drink. It watches the sedge for the shiver that means a body beneath.

Above them the raptors hang. They do not flap. They set their wings to the updraft off the fins and stay there, fixed in the roar, as if pinned to the sky. From a distance they look like part of the landscape, a dark mark on the blue-violet, and it is easy to miss that they are alive until one tilts a wing and slides sideways along the ridge, and every bird on every fin beneath it changes its note.

None of the predators are numerous or thriving or starving. They take what they take. The numbers hold.

## The small ones

Everything is littered with them: the dark, wingless beetles crossing the sun-hot stone with a purposeful, jerky gait; springtails skating on the films of water in the fin channels, a dust of tiny bodies; and the red mites, large as lentils, slow, so brightly scarlet that a drift of them on green moss looks like spilled paint. They cluster in the low places. They are eaten by rodents and birds and each other, and they don't seem to mind. They have no relation to the sound at all but to stand in it, and they stand in it the way stones do.

## The visitors

People come in the dry months, in groups of six or ten, from the valleys below the cloud forest. They walk up through the scree and the cushion plants with their bundles and their spear-throwers, and they do not talk, because there is no point, and by the second day they have almost forgotten that they could. They speak with their hands, quick and flat and exact, and with the pale cloth flags that they raise on poles against the sky, and with the knotted cords that hang at their belts. A cord is a memory: this many goats, this many days, this pool, this fin. The knots are read by fingertip, and a good cord-reader can run a thumb along a strand and know the route to a tarn she has never seen.

They hunt with obsidian, black and glassy, flaked into blades and points that flash like bits of the night in the fierce light. They kill only what they need, and they kill quickly. They approach the stilt-goats from downwind, against the wind, and this is the trick of it: the goats face the roar and see nothing behind them, and the hunters come up in the wind-shadows of the fins. They take the lower eggs from the chorus-birds, never the high ones, and leave a hole with eggs in it for every one they empty. Nobody remembers who made this rule.

They sleep in the slots where the sound is muted, and they keep small fires in the old stone rings. They eat, and they sit with their backs to the rock and their faces open to the last sun. Sometimes one puts a palm against the stone and holds it there for a long time, feeling the pulse of the chord come through the fin into the bone of the hand.

When they leave, each of them, at the crest of a ridge, adds a flat stone to a cairn. No one instructs them. It is a way of saying: *I was here, and I went.* The stones have lichen on them now, orange and black, so old that the newest layer looks ancient. On one fin, at the height of a person's shoulder, a row of red hands is pressed into the rock, palm outward and fingers spread, faded to the color of old blood. Visitors put their own hand against one of the prints sometimes, fitting the fingers into the stain, and stand a moment with their eyes closed.

That is how the people answer the chord. The birds fill its gaps with trilling. The goats lie down in its deepest note. The rodents whistle beneath it. The people are the only ones who add a thing that stays, and what they add is almost nothing: a stone, a hand, a little smoke on a slot wall. It is a small, quiet addition, and even so it seems to have been made to fit, as if the place had left room for it.

When they are gone the fog comes up over the lip at dusk, and the birds go silent by colonies, and the goats turn their faces to the wind, and the chord goes on.
