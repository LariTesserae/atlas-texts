# Those Who Stand In

You will hear them before you see them, and then, a few seconds later, you will hear them again.

It is a single clean sound: a bark plate dropped on a root, ringing faintly, and gone into the moss. Then the person who dropped it steps out of the fog with empty hands, as though the sound had gone ahead to announce them and they had followed, a little embarrassed at being late. If you stay, the ring comes a third time from somewhere else, from nothing.

They have no name for themselves that isn't a sound the hill makes. If you must call them something, *the Understudies* is close. They are what the plateau puts in when a part goes missing.

## The Body

They are not tall, about the height of a ten-year-old child, but nothing about them is childlike. They are long-armed and narrow through the ribs, with the settled, slightly stooped posture of people who have spent their lives passing under fronds. Their skin runs a dusky grey-green like the underside of a leaf and is always faintly oiled, from the plants that touch them from all sides at once. The oil beads the mist rather than absorbing it, so they move inside a light halo of droplets. They are warmer than the air, but only barely, and they do not fog. The warmth of the ground has been in them so long that it has become the temperature of thinking.

Their faces are broad and flat and still. Expression wastes heat, and heat is the one thing here that arrives free, so they do not waste it. What moves is the eyes, which are very large and dark and have a phosphor-blue shine at the back, like the undersides of leaves. In the brown dusk of noon you can see them coming by their eyes, two low blue coins passing at the height of your hip, perfectly level, because they walk in the two-beat gait of the long-legged herbivores and no one in ten thousand years has taught them a different one. Their ears are large, folded, and mobile. They are the ears of people who live among sounds so rare that each one has to be caught.

Their nails are the blue-black of iron slag. Their lips and gums are tinged verdigris. The metal in the young rock has passed through the plants and the plants have passed it through them, and they carry it visibly, the way a long-time smoker carries smoke.

And there is the hair.

## The Hair

The bacterial mats do not stop at the edge of the slope. They have colonized the Understudies as they colonize everything that holds still long enough, and the people permit it. On the scalp, in the beards of the old, and along the backs of the forearms grows a thin orange-black mat, and out of the mat come fine bright wires of pure metal, curling in the mist like fiddleheads. They are never cut. A child has a few, a young adult a soft bright frizz, an elder a whole chiming mane that beads with water and tinkles faintly when the head turns. The oldest, sitting still in the warm dark of a valve-hill, look like something the mountain has been slowly knitting.

Whether the wires are hair, or the people are a kind of mat that has learned to walk, or the two are simply a long partnership between a body and its tenants, is not a question they've ever felt a need to answer.

## Speech

They talk little, and what they say comes out one clear piece at a time. Conversation is mostly a matter of dropped plates. The bark of the ringing tree flakes off in curved pieces that each sound a slightly different note, and an Understudy picks one up on the path and carries it until the moment arrives, then lets it fall. This is a word. It is not a made thing, since it was only picked up, and it will lie where it fell and be grown over by evening. Nothing is kept. Nobody owns a vocabulary.

Their spoken language, when they use it, is built around the fact that sound leads cause. It is a grammar in which the noise comes first. They say the pop, and then the frog swells. They say *yes* before the question has finished being asked. A conversation between two of them can sound like each is reciting half of a poem they both already know, each answer landing just ahead of its question, all of it fitting together perfectly and neither seeming to lead. Visitors find it uncanny. The Understudies find nothing strange in it. They find *your* way, cause first and the sound trailing after, a little tragic, like watching someone follow their own footprints.

Grief works the other way. When someone dies, the sound of it may not come for days, and then it arrives from nowhere, a low soft blow in the air like a changed altitude. They call this the *late thunder*. They do not comfort each other before it arrives. They sit together in the warm dark and wait for it, hands open, and when it comes they let it pass through them without a sound, and they are then finished.

## The Work

They have no houses, no tools, no fires, and no stores. They have a job.

Across the plateau the production loop runs on: wire from the mats, coils from the beetles, sheets from the weavers, stacks at the crater rims, pressing by the steam, eating, pelleting, and back to the mats. Nobody runs it and each stage is blind to the others. And it stalls. A slope goes silent and still for days, with a stage missing or a link broken, and the whole hillside waits with its valves half open.

That is when the Understudies come. They do not know what is wrong and could not tell you what the loop is. They simply feel a slope go quiet the way you feel a missing tooth, and they go to it, in ones and twos, walking in step with the breath of the hill. There they do whatever the stalled stage would have done, and do it badly. They gather wire with their fingers, not with beetle mandibles. They carry a coil to the wrong rim. They lie on a stack to press it flat with their own weight, then get up again, having pressed nothing. They eat a pellet, spit it out, and try another. It is all clumsy and approximate, the work of understudies who never saw the play. And the clumsiness is what does it. The blunder catches on some unrelated part of the loop, a beetle startles, a valve takes the wrong pressure, and the whole slope starts again.

They are the accident that restarts it. They don't know this. They think of it as the thing they do.

## The Chip

There is one thing they do understand, and they understand it with a depth the rest of their lives lacks.

When a slope of ferns is stamped from a single die, so that every frond is identical to the chip in the third leaflet, they go to it and sit among the fronds and trace the chip with a finger. They do not admire the ferns. The chip is the thing. Ten thousand perfect fronds are only a frame for the one flaw, and the flaw is what is being manufactured, and they *are* the flaw, or its kin. They are the little irregularity in a very large regularity, the place where the stamp did not quite take.

When the die changes, when the chip disappears or shifts to another leaflet, they move. Nobody decides to. One morning the whole community is simply somewhere else, walking a slightly different line toward a slightly different slope, following the flaw as it migrates.

## Where They Sleep

They sleep in the valve-hills, curled in the warm mineral chambers where the steam exhales in twelve-second cycles. The stone there is smooth, with a body-heat sheen, and the breath of the hill pushes past the sleepers with a soft roar and draws back. They sleep in step with it, chests rising as the valve opens, so that a chamber full of Understudies seems to be one animal breathing. Babies are born here. They are grey and fibrous at first, still half mat, and they do not cry until the first fog touches them. Then they do, once, a clean single note.

## The Seams

Because they live at the edge of what is fully in force, they meet themselves sometimes.

At the horizon lines where old moss meets raw glass and the ground cools on one side and is still hot on the other, an Understudy will sometimes find another, sitting, waiting. It is the same elder, the same wire mane, but one has all ten fingers and one has a joint missing, and neither is the copy. They sit down together without alarm. They have no word for *original*. They stay a while in the warm side of the seam, two of the same old person, one whole and one chipped, and then one of them gets up and walks the way it was going, and the other stays.

The same goes for the places where their own shadows fall toward the light, or the mist rises off their shoulders instead of settling. They notice and do not remark on it. A place that is still deciding what it is will decide it on you as well.

## When They Die

They walk to a crater rim, to one of the stacks, and lie down among the pressed plates. They do this without ceremony, at a time that seems to be understood. The steam comes. What happens after belongs to the loop: something eats, something excretes, and the pellets fall on the mats. The wires grow a little brighter on that slope for a season.

They do not regard this as an ending. They regard it as continued employment, in a stage they have never been able to watch.

## The Others Who Aren't Workers

They are not alone in being neither quite animal nor quite part of the works. They walk beside the armored boilers for the warmth, matching their slow trailing steam, and the boilers permit it. In the black round tarns at the bottom of dead vents lives something the Understudies do not go near and do not speak of, seen only as a roundness in the water that is very slightly rounder than it should be. And in the seams, where the mountain is still deciding, other things sit that do not have the patience to be described.

But the Understudies are the ones who stand in. Ask them what the product is, and who it is for, and whether it is finished, and they will not answer. They will hold out their hands, palms up, wet with the fog and beaded with oil, and the water will gather at the fingertips and drop into the moss, one clean ring of sound a few seconds before it lands.
