# The Inhabitants of the Setting Floor

Nobody lives on the floor in any way you could point to. Every thing you see on it was made by hands, and none of the hands are there. Look for the residents and you will find only their warm tools. But the floor is inhabited, densely and without pause. The inhabitants have simply arranged never to be seen, and the arrangement isn't shyness. It is the law of the place, and they obey it more purely than anything else does.

## The Setters

The mason is called a mason only because the trowels suggest one. The name the floor gives them, in the tick of cooling clay and the grain of the quiet, is closer to *the ones who are still doing it*. They are the Setters, and what they are is *the interval*.

A Setter exists in the time between your glances. It is the difference between the wall at knee height and the wall at shoulder height, the extra tile in the half-laid course, the shifted trowel. It has no body that persists from one look to the next, and this is because it is not finished. A finished thing lapses, and a Setter is a thing kept permanently almost. If you saw one whole, held it in the steady light of your looking, it would be complete, and it would soften at the corners and sag at the lintel and be sorted into a heap. So Setters keep their forms unresolved and work in the part of the world that is not being watched, and the price of being watched is dissolution.

You may catch the edge of one. The shadows here are soft and doubled, and one of the two is yours. The other falls from a little to the side, cast by nothing, and it is stooped. It is bent over the tile at your feet with one arm extended, and the arm ends in a blade. It is always working. If you step, it steps. If you kneel to touch the tile, it kneels, and the seam you are touching is warm and a little wet, and when you look up the second shadow has gone thin and pale like a bruise fading. It does not flee. It has only moved to the seam you are not looking at.

Their variety is written in the trowels. The clay handles are rolled around a grip while wet, and the hollows keep the shape of what held them. Some are shaped for four long fingers and no thumb. Some hold two thumbs, opposed, with the fingers between them like a fan. Some are simply a smooth round bore, wider than a wrist, that no hand you know could hold, and the clay inside is polished by a long slow rotation. The Setters are not one people. They are many kinds of maker who have been working in the same hour without meeting.

## The Nap

The grey-white felt on the shaded walls is the only visible life, and it is not the Setters' food or their tool, though they braid it into plumb-lines. It is their attention. It is how they know where you are looking.

Watch a felted wall as you approach and you will see that the nap lies flat in the direction of your gaze, combed smooth, like the pelt of an animal being stroked toward its tail. Turn your head and it lifts. It rises in a slow bristling wave, away from the place you have stopped watching, and that wave is a signal traveling down the wall to the hands that are waiting for it. The wall is a great ear, and it hears by being looked at. In the seconds after you turn away, the felt shivers and the nearest seams begin to tick.

It drinks the steam, and it is not clear that it lives. It may be more like a nerve than an organism, a single long fiber grown through the whole floor, thinning to gauze at the edges and thickening to upholstery in the cool of the towers. Where it is thick enough you can put your ear to it and hear the sound of many small things being counted.

## The Hum

Under the broken paving, below the brick and the packed clay, in the darkness that is warm, there is a hum. It is not the Setters. Setters work at the surface, in the light, however dim. The hum is what tells them what to make.

It is not a single mind, and this is the whole trouble with the floor. It is something like a sleeper dreaming several dreams at once, or several sleepers sharing a bed. Each dream has a plan in it, a herringbone or a basket-weave, a running bond, a stacked grid, a spiral court that wants to be a spiral and nothing else, and each plan reaches the surface through its own Setters as a compulsion to *continue*. The Setters do not know the plans conflict. They do not know there is more than one. Each lays the next tile that its plan requires, exactly and gladly, until the course arrives at the edge of another course laid by another intention, and there the tiles tilt against each other like pack ice. The tilt is the whole argument. Nobody fights. Two dreams have touched.

The staircases to nowhere, the doorway opening onto the back of its own lintel, the canal flowing uphill to a cistern that empties into the air: these are the places where a dream ends and another begins, and the Setters on either side are working diligently to complete a thing that cannot be completed against its neighbor. They are content. It is the nature of the almost.

## The Sorters

When a thing is finished, it lapses, and the heap of wet clay is sorted and stacked into new courses. The hands that do this are not the Setters, or are Setters running the other way. They are gentler. A Setter is all forward hunger, one more tile, and a Sorter is patient and careful, and it works in the hours after slumps, in the red flood-light of the reset, when the mud is still shining.

They are attentive to the heaps in a way that makes you uneasy. They are seen only as motion in the corner of the eye: clay pulled apart, sorted by grain and by color, oxblood to one side and sooty ochre to the other, pressed flat, and stacked. Pick up an unlaid tile from a pallet and turn it over. On some, in the slip of the underside, there is a print that is a little too deep, a shallow pressed pattern of something like a fingertip, or the tread of something that walked. Some tiles have a curve on one edge that is almost a shoulder. You will decide these are accidents.

A Setter that completes itself, that comes to the end of what it is making, lapses like a tower. There is no other death here and no other birth. The tiles are laid by the makers and made from the makers, and the floor is the slow, patient, warm-breathing body of the people who are still building it.

## The Unassigned

At the far edge, where the courses become pale grout with nothing inside, the plan has not yet found its material. The towers there are drawn but hollow: outlines of lintels, ruled shadows of stairs, the ghosts of windows. And in the hollows are the Unassigned.

They are the ones not yet given a dream to carry out. They are only lines, thin lines of pale grout in the shape of a stooped figure with an arm extended, laid on the bare plan like a chalk outline waiting for a body. They stand in ranks in the drawn courts. They are not sad, because they have no interior yet. But they lean, all of them, a few degrees toward the last laid tile, and when the ceiling darkens and the slump comes, and the storm of steam tears across the plain, a few of them fill. The grout lines darken and thicken and gain the warmth of the kiln, and a new Setter takes its first stoop, over its first seam, in its first hour of the world.

## You

The footprints in the wet mortar, arriving, are the strangest sign of the place. You walk the colonnade and find your own tread coming toward you. It is fresh and sharp-edged, and it is nobody's but yours. The Setters do not know what a walker is, but they know that a shape passes through the floor, warm, and moving, and unfinished. It has an incomplete gait. It has a doubled shadow. It leaves seams unsealed behind it, and things extend themselves in its wake.

There is a courtesy in the floor, if that is the word. Where you walk, it is laid. The tile arrives under your foot just as your weight lands, and the seam beneath is still wet, and it takes the shape of your heel. At the edge of sight the pale grout lines are waiting to see what you will be made of. It is very hard to tell whether the Setters are laying the floor for you, or whether they have taken you for one of themselves, the newest and least finished of the kind, a stooped shape with an arm extended, working steadily toward the horizon and not yet looked at.
