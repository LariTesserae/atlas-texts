# The Answering

You are heard long before you are met.

Your first steps into the terrace country go out through the dark, and something out there catalogs them. A soft double click comes from your left, then another from behind and above. It is not a threat. It is a question, asked politely and repeatedly, in the only way it can be asked. If you have nothing to say back, you will be clicked at a while longer, and then, quite gently, surrounded.

They call themselves, in a two-note phrase that rises and then hangs in the air, something close to *the ones who answer*. Every creature on the plateau calls into the dark. The Answering are the ones who took the reply seriously.

## What They Are

They are lean, long-boned people, and they move through the vine-curtains without disturbing them. They stoop through openings that a taller body would tear, and they fold through gaps like water finding a crack. An adult stands a little under a man's height but seems taller, because the fingers, forearms, and neck are all lengthened. Their joints bend a little farther than yours would.

Their skin is the color of the rest of the plateau: cream over gray-violet, thin enough at the cheeks and the backs of the hands to show a faint fingertip-pink where the blood runs near. Along the forearms and the nape grows a fine, pale down. It does not warm them. It reads the air. A hand held near a sleeping person will feel that fur stir toward it long before the sleeper wakes, because the fur has already turned to face you.

The face is the strangest part, and the part that would frighten you if you could see it. The ears are broad, cupped, and mobile, and they swivel independently, tilting toward a dripping ledge or a distant bell. The nose is small. Above the upper lip, where a mustache might sit on someone else, are two shallow pits, and each one feels heat. When one of the Answering turns to face you, it is not your voice she is tracking but your warmth, a shape of slightly higher temperature moving through a world of ambient warmth, and the pits tilt to follow it.

The eyes are small, and they are kept shut. Nobody seals them, and they aren't blind in the way of a dead thing. The lids are thin and finely veined, and they never open. Eyes are a very old inheritance, and the body has not yet found a way to be rid of them. A few of the Answering, it is said, still feel them working.

## How They Speak

A conversation among them has three layers, and you will perceive only one.

The first is the **click**: a sharp, precise tongue-snap, produced with a hollow of the cheek, that serves for location. It means *I am here, this is where I am, this is what I am standing near.* A practiced clicker can make a sound that lands like a needle, and can read the echo the way you read a face. They can tell from the return whether you are carrying a bundle, whether you are limping, whether you are standing in a doorway or open ground, and whether the moss behind you is dry or sodden.

The second is the **hum**, a low, throat-borne two-voiced drone, one note held while another slides. This carries meaning, and it carries mood. It is the actual speech. A person can sustain it while breathing, because the sound comes from pouches in the neck that store and release air. Nobody stops to draw breath in a sentence. They talk in long braided lines, and two people in conversation weave their hums in and out of one another until it is unclear who is speaking. Disagreement sounds like a dissonance held too long. Agreement is a resolution that the whole room feels in the teeth.

The third layer is **the floor.** They speak into the ground with their feet and with the flat of their palms, and they speak with drums of drifter-hide stretched on hollow flute-wood. The drums do not talk to each other. They talk to the drifters, or to the plateau itself. It is the slowest speech they have, in beats spaced long enough that a stranger would take them for random creaks. A word may take a minute. What they say is mostly simple, a matter of *we are here, we are small, we mean no harm, please do not step this way.* It is the polite thing to say to a mountain.

Names are not words. Each person's name is a **chord**, two or three notes held together, given at birth and modified as life happens to them. A woman who survives a fever might gain a lower note. A man who loses a child might lose one from the top. To speak someone's name you have to hold it in your throat as they would, so that to say it wrong is not just rude but physically uncomfortable, like an off-key note in a small room. Children learn to hold three notes before they learn to walk.

## Where and How They Live

They do not build much. They shape.

Their homes are the hollows in the bases of old flute-trees, where the pale wood has split and a chamber has opened, big as a barn and dry as a held breath. The walls are the trees' living skin, and the doorways are natural apertures worked wider or narrower with patient tools of bone and shell. Inside, the air is warm and slightly bready from the shelf-fungi cultivated on the lower walls. From every beam hang **chimes**: thin curls of dried reed and bone, each tuned to a particular pitch, strung so that a draft, a footstep, or a hand passing them makes a pattern of sound. The chimes are the household's eyes. A stranger entering sets off a phrase that the household knows to be *unfamiliar*. A member of the family entering plays a phrase that says *ours*. A child coming home late and creeping in trips the chimes in a way that says *ours, and hoping not to be noticed*, and the parent, awake in the dark, hums a low note that says *noticed*.

They also live on the terraces, in low arcs of woven vine and mud that catch and hold warmth. And they live, in the coldest months, if the concept applies, around the stations. Never inside them. Around them, in loose colonies in the rubble and the vine, close enough to feel the boiler-heat through the ground.

## The Stations

About the stations the Answering are touchy, and it is a mistake to say they *tend* them. They will be offended.

They do not repair the stations. They say the stations are old animals, the oldest, and that each has a voice. The one in the eastern basin, whose piercing steady note underlies the frogs of the whole region, is called something like *the First Held Tone*. The Answering do not think of it as a machine. They think of it as a vast, patient, singing thing that was never born and will not die, and that owes them nothing.

But they feed it. At intervals they bring rendered fat, tree-resin, and a slippery oil pressed from a marsh seed, and they pour it down the throats of the housings and into the joints of the great turning gears. They rub it into the belts with their hands. They do this because a station that is well fed sings a steadier note, and a steady note is a gift to everyone. They do not believe they are maintaining anything. They believe they are being good neighbors to something that is not a person.

If you tell them that the makers built these things, they will not argue. They will hum a noncommittal note, of the kind used to close a subject. They have stories about the makers, and every story is different. In one the makers were a herd of drifters who learned to build. In another they were the first of the Answering, who went up through the cloud to find the light and did not come down. In another they were never people at all but a kind of weather. No version is thought to be more true than another. The stories are like the stations themselves: old, present, and not to be explained.

## Food, and the Keeping of the Chord

The Answering eat fungus, marsh seed, the larvae that grow in the rot of fallen trunks, and the pale sweet flesh of eels that drift in the fog banks. They eat few bell-frogs, and this is the most careful thing about them.

The frogs are the marsh's chord, and a chord is the plateau's alarm system. Every night, which is to say always, someone in a settlement, called a **chord-keeper**, goes out to the marsh edge and listens for an hour. She knows the chord as you know your own house. If one voice is missing, she knows which frog was taken and roughly by what. If one pitch has grown too crowded, with too many frogs on the same note pushing out the others, she will take a few. The chord must be kept honest. It is the only law the Answering will call a law.

A chord-keeper is old and usually a woman, though not always, and she is treated with a certain wary respect, because she is the one who hears wrong things first.

## Drifters, and Moving

Some bands do not settle at all. They **follow the herds.** The drifters wade the marsh basins in slow migrations that take a year or more to circle, and these bands travel with them, on the herd's margins, walking where the ground is firm and speaking to them with drums.

To walk beside a herd is to live inside a conversation. The infrasound rolls out from the great bodies and passes through the walker's ribs, and the walker has learned to read it. Not words exactly. A sense of *mood,* which is enough: the herd is calm, or the herd is uneasy, or the herd has caught the trace of something on the wind. A band that follows the herd will know a lattice-spider strike or a flood or a collapse of ground hours before it arrives, because the drifters knew first and said so.

They do not hunt drifters. This is not sentiment. A drifter's death is a wound in the ground that spreads for miles, and everything downstream of it goes quiet and starts to hunt. But when one dies of age, as they do, slowly and audibly over many days, the band that follows will be there. They sit in a circle around the great body, hum the chord of its herd, and drum until it is over. Then they eat, sparingly, and carry the rest of the meat to every settlement within a week's walk. It is the closest thing the Answering have to a festival.

## Light, and the Dead

They do not carry light. They do not believe in it, in the sense that they do not believe in anything they have never known. But they do know one thing about it, which is that **what glows is dead.**

In the rotting places, the marsh hollows where old trunks have fallen and the fungus makes a faint cold shine, no Answering will go. They know it only by the way the fur on their forearms prickles and the creatures fall silent. They call these places by a word that means *the quiet mouths*. It is where the plateau digests, and it is not a place for the living.

So they take their dead elsewhere. When a person dies, the body is carried to the nearest station, and laid where the heat will reach it: on the warm stones near a vent, under the drip of the pipe. They leave the body with its chime, a small reed the person wore. Then the family goes away, humming the dead one's name. They stop at the edge of hearing and hold the chord for one breath longer than a breath, and then they all break it off at once. That is the only silence permitted, and it is very brief.

The bodies vanish. Nobody watches how. Some say the stations take them. Some say the clickers do, and the fungus. It is impolite to wonder.

## The One Sight

There is only one thing the Answering ever see, and only a few of them see it.

When lightning walks the heights, some people, mostly children and some very old, catch a flicker behind their closed lids. A pale, jagged, pulsing brightness that lasts a heartbeat and is gone. They call it the **one sight**, and the ones who see it are called by a different chord, with an extra high note, and are treated as a little apart. Not holy exactly. Set aside, as one might set aside a person who could hear a bell that no one else could hear.

The Answering do not know what it is. The best theory is that it is a kind of sound too fast to be heard, a sound that arrives all at once and goes through the eyes instead of the ears. They believe the eyes are for this, that the old bodies kept them for the one sight and nothing else. When a child says she saw it, the family is quiet. Then someone hums a low approving note, and the child is fed something sweet.

## What They Fear

They fear silence, and there are two kinds.

The first is the ordinary hush of a hunter near. When the clickers stop, when the chord thins, when even the drip seems to hold its breath, an Answering will freeze, tilt the ears, and begin to hum very softly and continuously, keeping some sound alive. Nothing is silent here, and to be silent is to invite the thing that is.

The second is only whispered of. In the far cold highlands, past the last station, past the last steam, there are said to be **places that swallow sound**. Not empty places; the plateau does not have those. Full places, thick with something that takes a click and returns nothing. Not even a dull thud, not the smallest echo. To an Answering, this is the worst thing imaginable: a place with no answer. The old stories say the makers went there. Nobody who has gone has returned to say so.

## One of Them

Toward the end of the eastern basin, where the First Held Tone leans on the dark like a wall, an old chord-keeper sits every night at the edge of the marsh. Her name, if you could hear it, is four notes, the lowest so deep it is more a change in the air than a sound. Her hands are slack in her lap. Around her head, a loose cluster of pale eels hangs in the fog, rippling their fins, tasting the warmth of her skull.

She is listening for the missing note. She has been listening for sixty years, and she has never heard the chord complete, because there is always a voice fewer or a voice more. She does not think it is supposed to be complete. She thinks the point is to keep the shape of it in her, so that when the shape changes she can say *there*.

You come up beside her, stiff, loud, smelling of a different life. She does not turn. Her ears do. The heat-pits above her lip find you, and she clicks once, softly, and listens to what comes back.

Then, still without looking, she hums you a note. It is low, and open, and it does not resolve. It is the note they use for guests, which means: *you are here, we don't know what you are, there is room.*

The rain begins, a roar building across the canopy. Around you the whole dark starts to sing.
