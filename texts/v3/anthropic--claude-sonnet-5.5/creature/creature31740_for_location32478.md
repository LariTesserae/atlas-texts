# Who Lives in the Skylight Ward

## The ones you will never see

The residents that matter most are the ones you will not meet. They are termites, and they are blind, pale, and soft-bodied. In a lifetime they never feel direct sun. They live inside the dark towers and under the covered roads, and the ward is theirs in the way a body belongs to its blood.

At the base of the largest mound, against the tube wall, where the rock is warmest and the glass stalactite above has been swallowed into the structure, there is a chamber the size of a clenched fist. Its ceiling is a smooth vault, and its door is a slit too narrow for anything but a worker. In it lives the queen. She is only a few years old, and she is already longer than the top joint of your thumb, a cream-colored, faintly glistening body swollen with eggs, ringed with dark bands like a rolled scroll. She does not move except to pulse. Beside her, small and dark and unchanged, is the king, who has not left her side since the two of them dropped from the fig and chewed their way into the ash. Around them stands a moving wall of attendants, licking, grooming, and carrying away eggs one by one, passing them hand to hand down corridors toward nurseries in the warm heart of the mound.

She lays an egg every few seconds, and everything else in the ward follows from that. Hundreds of thousands of her daughters and sons now spread through thirty mounds and the roads between them.

**The workers** are the great majority: small, milk-pale, with soft mouths and no eyes. They do everything. They chew dead wood from fern-stalks and fallen fig-limbs into pulp, mix it with ash and saliva, and lay it down in wet beads, one bead at a time, so that a wall is a slow accumulation of tiny decisions. They repair a breach within the hour. They tend the fungus. They know the roads by taste and scent, and a worker touching a stranger's antennae can tell in an instant whether it belongs to the colony.

**The soldiers** are few and look like they belong to a different species: heads the color of dark amber, hard and almost square, with long crossed jaws that cannot feed them. Workers feed them. A soldier's real job is to fill a hole. When a centipede tears into a covered road, soldiers stream to the breach and wedge their heads into the gap, jaws out, and hold. Behind them the workers plaster the wall shut, sometimes with the soldiers still in it. A wall in the ward will occasionally show a row of amber heads set into the paste like studs in a door.

**The gardeners** are workers too, but older, and they live in the chambers where the fungus grows. The comb is a soft, folded, brain-gray sponge, cupped in the dark like a held breath, and it is the true food of the city. The termites cannot digest the wood they gather, but the fungus can. They eat the fungus, the fungus eats the wood, and both grow in the warm air rising from the rock. Small white nodules bud from the comb, and the gardeners harvest them with great care. Neither partner would last a season without the other.

The mound is also a lung. Its fluted flanks and hidden shafts carry warm air up through the core and let it exchange with the cooler, damper air at the surface. The whole tower breathes in a slow cycle over the day, drawing the damp from the steaming fissures into the chambers where it is wanted. This is the whisper you hear with your ear to the wall: the pulse of countless feet, and the air moving in a structure that is more a single animal than a heap of dirt.

## The night the city flies

Once or twice a year, after a warm afternoon rain, the mounds open small round doors near their tips and the winged ones come out. These are the alates, the future kings and queens, dark-bodied with four long wings, clumsy on foot and beautiful in the air. They climb the ridges in a crowd, wait, and lift off together toward the skylight in a slow, dense smoke of wings.

Everything in the ward eats well that evening. Geckos leave their territories and stand where the fliers land, gulping. The wasps and spiders take their share, and the frog, still calling, snaps at them. The bats, emerging from the tube into the swarm, do not need to look for a meal. The great majority never reach the sky. Some fall back to the floor and shed their wings, the wings breaking at a seam and lying scattered on the moss like tiny panes of glass. Every so often a pair falls near the same patch of ground. They tuck their tails together, chew their way into a crack in the ash, and begin a city.

## The ones you will see

**The geckos.** There are about a dozen, pale as candle wax, with bellies through which you can see a dark thread of gut. Each keeps a territory of a few paces and knows every leaf in it. One, near the largest mound, has a notched tail and hunts from a black glass stalactite, head down, motionless for an hour before a single strike. They are the ward's clock. They come down as the light dims, they ambush at the road-mouths where the termites go out to feed, and they freeze all at once, twelve small statues, whenever a green shadow moves in the rim vines.

**The snake.** It is slender and green, about the length of your arm, and thin as a stem. It comes down out of the vines twice a week, in no rush, and threads through the ferns as if it were a vine itself. It eats a gecko, on average, every other visit, and when it does the ward goes quiet for the rest of the evening. It is the only true predator here, and it takes very little. Its presence keeps the geckos from ever becoming too comfortable.

**The frog.** It is smaller than a thumbnail, green-brown and mossy, and you will hear it far more often than you see it. After rain it climbs a fern-frond and calls one soft click, waits, calls again. Somewhere across the floor another frog answers. Between rains it is silent and hidden in the wet moss, living on springtails and patience.

**The wasps.** In the blade of morning sun on the sunward overhang, the mud-daubers work with a grave, metallic-blue industry. They roll pellets of wet mud from the seeps, fly them to the nest tubes, press them into place, and go back for more. Each tube is stocked with paralyzed spiders, and each holds a single egg. Their wings tick against the quiet like a slow watch.

**The spiders.** The orb-weavers hang at the centers of their webs between the fern trunks, still as lenses. The webs are beaded with condensation, and at certain hours each bead holds a tiny upside-down skylight, so the whole ward seems hung with hundreds of little green windows. The spiders take moths and small flies. The wasps take the spiders. The chain here is short and everyone is on it.

**The centipedes.** They are long, orange-legged, and quick, and they are the reason the termites bother to roof their roads. They patrol the litter at night, feeling along the outer galleries with their antennae, and now and then they find a weak place and break in. The soldiers answer. A centipede can kill dozens of them, and often does, and the workers build the wall again. There is no grudge in this. It is simply one of the seasons of the city.

**The bats.** They roost in the deeper tube, where the roof is still whole, hanging in dense, warm clusters. At dusk they leave one by one through the skylight, so softly that you notice them only as brief gaps in the first stars. Before dawn they return, swooping down through the hole, and what they leave below feeds the beetles and roaches and, in time, the ferns.

**The cleaners.** Beetles, moth larvae, springtails, and mahogany-colored cockroaches move through the litter, eating what everyone else has finished with: shed wings, bat leavings, dead soldiers, gecko skins. They are unremarked and essential. Without them the ward would not turn over.

## The quiet residents

Not everything that lives here moves. The moss covers each fresh block of rubble within a season, and behind it come the lichens, gray-green crusts that are, in effect, the first slow chemists of the rock. The ferns unroll their fiddleheads by the hour. The vines lower themselves from the rim one cord at a time. And on the floor, in a crack full of ash and fig-pulp, a seedling has begun. It is three leaves high. It is the only resident that will, if it survives, change the terms for all the others, closing the roof, dimming the light, and shading out the ferns. The termites will not mind. The geckos will find new stalactites.

## What holds it together

None of these lives is large. The queen is a thumb-joint of pale flesh, the frog is smaller than a fingernail, and the whole city fits under a canopy you could cross in a few breaths. But the arrangement is complete. Warmth comes up from the rock, and light comes down. The termites eat the light, in the form of the wood the rim trees make from it, and pass it to the fungus, which passes it back. The geckos eat the termites, and the snake eats the geckos. The bats bring in the night and leave it behind. The cleaners return everything to the ash, and the ash feeds the moss.

You are the only thing in the ward that is out of place, standing at the middle of the floor with your breath fogging in the green light. For a minute nothing moves. Then a slab lets go somewhere in the dark of the tube with a single dry clack, and every creature stops. Twelve geckos hold still. The frog stops calling, and the wasps' wings go quiet. In the mounds, hundreds of thousands of small feet pause, and the whisper stops for the first time you have heard.

Then, after a long breath, it resumes.
