# Who Lives in Ammerwane

The first thing to understand is that the Held Sea has more room than residents. The forest is built for a crowd, with its ledges, hollows, and canopy-veils thick enough to carry cities, and the crowd never came. What lives here lives thinly and slowly. The residents have arranged themselves around the absence without ever quite agreeing what it is.

## The Long-Walkers

They are the most numerous of the larger animals, and so the nearest thing to neighbors.

A long-walker stands about as tall as a person, though it is hard to say how it stands. It has nine or eleven jointed limbs, the count shifting with how you look at it, and each is pale and hollow-looking, like the stem of a dead reed. It has no face. Where a face would be there is a ring of fine hairs that tastes the water, and nothing behind the ring seems to look back. It climbs the trunk-woods in long arcs, setting one limb, then the next, and scrapes the bark-paper into a pouch under its body with a sound like a page being turned very slowly.

They do not flee, because they do not seem to register that they might need to. If you swim into one, it pauses and lets the hairs lift toward you, and for the length of a breath you are being read. Then it continues. Their patience does not look like calm. It looks like a decision made so long ago that it no longer needs making.

They are early. Their tracks appear in the silt ahead of them, a line of narrow punctures that fills in from behind as the animal arrives to walk in its own prints. Some travelers find this unnerving. Others follow the tracks on purpose, since a long-walker never steps where the current will not carry you gently. In the older valleys they are said to be the only creatures that can feel time seep, and to lean into it the way a sailor leans into a wind.

At the cairns they stop. They do not touch the stones. They press the ring of hairs to the nearest crust and hold there, sometimes for hours, and then go on.

## The Shoals

The lantern-shoals are dull red and slow, each fish the length of a hand, each shoal a loose sphere of forty or fifty. They make no light. They keep what little arrives, and in the green dusk they glow like coals in a banked fire.

A shoal is not exactly a group of fish. Watch one long enough and you see that no individual leads. The sphere bends around obstacles as a single body would, and when it passes through a seam it may come out with more fish than it went in with, or fewer, and nothing in its manner changes. The count is never wrong. The shoal simply has as many as it has.

The old travelers' habit is to follow one for a day and see where it settles. It will always settle somewhere pleasant, in a hollow with a slow current and a good view of the trunks. Whatever the shoals are keeping, they keep it in comfortable places.

## The One That Passes

Above the canopy-veil, at intervals nobody has measured, something goes by.

It is not seen. It is inferred from a shadow, wide as a valley, that slides across the veil at the pace of a walking elder, and from the flower-mats, which shut together beneath it in a wave that runs ahead of the shadow and does not open again until well after. For an hour the water is very quiet. The bubble-columns thin. Even the low tone that rolls through the sea from no direction holds off.

It has never turned back. Every sighting agrees on its heading, which follows, if you plot it on a map you cannot really have, the curve the cairns make around the bowl of the continent. Whether the cairns were laid to mark its road or its road was bent to pass them, no one says. Some think it makes a single circuit that takes more than a lifetime, and that everyone who sees it is seeing it for the first time. Others, who have met themselves in the seams and come away subdued, suspect it has only ever passed once, and that the passing is very long.

## The People, If They Are That

There are people. There are not many, and they are not sure they are a people.

They call themselves nothing that translates. Travelers call them the Tenders, for the cairns, and the name is accepted without being used. Perhaps a few hundred live in the whole continent, spread so thin that two of them might go a lifetime without crossing paths. They are tall and narrow, with skin the pale gray of weathered granite, and anyone who stays still for a season grows a thin crust of green and ochre along the shoulders and spine. They do not scrub it off. It is a record of how long they have been listening.

Their bodies have adapted to the seams. There is a fold in the throat, pleated like the underside of a mushroom, that can open to water or air and, more importantly, can wait. A Tender does not decide whether to breathe the sea or the pocket of air that has opened in it. She keeps the fold half-open and lets the world choose, and she has been wrong only a few times, and remembers each one.

They read the shears in the current the way other peoples read faces. They can tell from the shimmer between layers where a swimmer will be carried and for how long, and which drift leads to a seam and which to a long ride across the drowned forest. They do not hurry. Hurrying against a current is how you learn how little it minds.

### Speech

They speak little, and almost never in seams. In the seams a voice arrives before it is spoken, so a question is answered before it is asked. The Tenders have made a habit of this. In a seam, you give your answer first, then wait, and only afterward hear what you were asked. Conversations in the pockets are odd, slow, and oddly tender, each speaker responding to something they will not learn about until the breath ends.

Outside the seams they talk in a low, dragging speech, with long gaps between phrases, and they treat silence as a courtesy. To fill a pause is rude.

### The Cairns

Every Tender builds one cairn in a life, and it takes years.

The cobbles come from the whaleback slopes, rounded and already half-furred. She lifts each one, sets it, and waits, sometimes for a season, until the stack will bear the next. The stillness is the difficulty. A person who stays this long in one spot is colonized, the crust creeps up the legs and along the arms, and the cairn and the builder begin to share a coat. When the last stone is placed, the Tender either leaves and never returns to that valley, or she sits down beside it and does not get up. Both are counted as finishing.

The cairns line up because the Tenders build toward one another, not toward any plan. Each picks her site by looking for the nearest cairn she can see from the highest whaleback, and then goes one long swim beyond it, in the direction the curve is bending. None of them has seen the curve whole. Each has seen only the next cairn, and the one after that as a smudge in the amber.

### The Stair and the Door

The stair is used for leaving. When a Tender has finished the cairn and doesn't sit down beside it, she may walk to the stair, climb its nine loaf-edged steps, and step off into open water at the top. What happens next is not discussed. Nobody watches. It is considered a private matter, like the last few breaths.

The doorframe is used for the opposite reason. A Tender who cannot bear what she is carrying, whether grief, or a memory, or a mood that has settled in like silt, walks through it and comes out somewhere slightly warmer, with the light shifted a few degrees and the taste of the water a little less like iron. She is not cured. She is somewhere else by a small margin. Most people go through it three or four times in a life and never speak of what they left on the other side.

## The Occupant of the House

The low round house with its roof grown into the hill has one resident, and no two accounts of her agree.

Some who have knocked (there is no door, but they knock on the doorless frame) say she is a child of ten or eleven, sitting on the dry floor with her back to the wall, listening to something. Others say a middle-aged woman, weaving something out of fine dry thread. Others say an old man, hardly able to lift his head, who nods as if he had been expecting them for years. All of them say the same thing on leaving: she has been here a long time.

The Tenders don't visit. They regard the house with the reserve of people who know something about it and have agreed not to say. The rooms hold the smell of an old rain, and in the evening, when the gas-bladders vent and the whole hillside breathes out, the air inside is said to move once, softly, as if something had exhaled with it.

If you ask who she is, the answer, when there is one, is that she is the one who kept the rain.

## What They Have in Common

The long-walker with its early tracks, the shoals with their shifting counts, the Passing with its single road, the Tenders with their crusts and pleated throats, and the resident of the house are not a community. They do not gather, trade, or share language. But they share a manner. Each of them lives as though the land's own tiredness were a kind of instruction. Each moves at the speed of something that has finished arguing with the world. And each leaves, in the silt or the stone or the water, a small mark that does not quite say who made it.

The sea holds them all the way it holds everything else, warmly, without insistence, and lets the rules go soft around them. When the flowers close ahead in their long wave, none of them looks up to see what is coming. They already know.
