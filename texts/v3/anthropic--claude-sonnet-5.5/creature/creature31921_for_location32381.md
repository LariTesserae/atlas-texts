# Those Who Keep the Low Ember

## The People

About nine hundred people live in the bowl, and they are the kind of people a place like this makes of you slowly, without asking.

They are lean, because fat is a burden in that heat, and they are damp. Nobody is ever quite dry. Their sleeves are rolled and their collars open, and their skin has the faint sheen of a stone in a stream. Sixty years of amber light has left them pale, but not the pale of illness. It is the pale of people who have stopped expecting to be looked at by the sun. Their eyes are wide and dark and slow to narrow. When the blade of daylight crosses a lane, they slow down in it, blinking, as you might slow before a stranger you half recognize.

They move as the place has taught them to: unhurried, sideways through the tenement lanes, one shoulder leading, baskets held close. Nobody runs. A child who runs is caught gently by the collar and walked back to where the running began, without a scolding, so that the walk can be done again properly. In the foundry lanes, where belts hum overhead and every sound is swallowed by moss, they talk with their hands. It is a working language of pinches, taps, and slow sweeps, half borrowed from the gardeners' pruning signals. A stranger sees a crowd moving in silence and takes it for grief. It is only manners.

They sleep where the stones are warm. During the "cold" hours, which are only less hot, the lanes fill with people lying on the paving in ones and twos, on rush mats or on nothing, like cats on a sill. Nobody thinks it odd to step over a sleeping neighbor. You step carefully, you murmur the sleeper's name in passing so they know it was a friend, and the sleeper, without waking, lifts two fingers.

## Trades and Temperaments

The town has two temperaments, and they have lived side by side for so long that they no longer quarrel, only tease.

The **engineers** are the tenders of the boilers, the beam engines, and the lagging. They are precise, dry-humored, and suspicious of anything that cannot be measured. Their forearms are banded with old rag burns, and they carry small tin oilers on their belts like a second heartbeat. They explain the leaning ferns as heat, patiently, and they will explain it again tomorrow.

The **gardeners** are the tenders of everything green, and they are slower and warier. They carry clippers at the hip, and they know every trained stem in their lane by feel, the way a mother knows her children in the dark. A gardener will stand for a long time with a hand on a fern trunk before making a cut. They do not say the plants are *listening*. They say the plants are "attending," and they leave it there. Their hands are green-stained to the wrist and scarred with the small white nicks of sixty years of pruning near moving iron. A gardener who has lost a finger to a flywheel is spoken of with reverence and some embarrassment, like a soldier.

Between and around them are the makers: cable-spinners, glassblowers, potters, rubberers, and the smelting crews who work the copper seam in the east wall. Because flame is forbidden below the second terrace, the glassblowers work at vents in the floor, where the old tubes give up a dry, steady heat. They gather glass from crucibles that sit in the ground like nests, and they shape it with wet wooden paddles and breath. The glass they make is faintly green and full of tiny bubbles, and it is the best glass in any country you could name. No one in the bowl thinks of it as beautiful. They think of it as glass.

## The Old and the Young

The oldest inhabitants are older than the collapse. They were adults when the ground gave way, and they do not agree about what happened. Some say the town fell whole, streets and all, in one long night. Others say the town was only a camp on the rim, and they descended afterward on ropes, in their own time, to see what the earth had swallowed and found they could not leave it. The old do not settle the question, and the young have learned not to ask. When the old say "up," they say it in a particular way, with the eyes going somewhere just to the left of the person they are speaking to.

The young were born into the amber. They learn to tie stems before they learn letters. Children run the pulley lines between terraces, sending up toys, coal, and small folded notes, and they know which ropes carry which weight by the sound. At about twelve they are led to a terrace on a clear day and made to stand in the blade of light for as long as they can bear it. Nobody calls this a ceremony. It is called "taking the sun," and afterward the child is given a wide leaf, a pair of clippers, and a first job. Some come down from the terrace laughing. Some do not speak for a day.

## The Keepers of the Small Lives

Every household keeps a finch, or hopes to. The cages hang by the doors, small wire cages lined with cloth, and the birds inside are grey-brown and unremarkable and each one has a name. They are the town's sentinels against the gas, and the town has never confused this with using them. When a finch dies of old age, the household mourns for a full breath, eleven hours, with the empty cage left hanging. When a finch dies of bad air, the whole lane mourns, and the lane is cleared and aired and the tubes beneath it are inspected before anyone sleeps there again. There are people in the bowl whose lives were saved four or five times over by birds now gone. They know the birds' names and say them at the exhale.

The other small residents are not kept but are counted. The blind crickets in the wall cracks are counted as neighbors. The salamanders in the warm channel are counted as neighbors, and children lie on their bellies at the water's edge to watch the tiny dark hearts beat through the skin. The grey moths in the lamp-shades are counted as neighbors and are never swatted or shooed. When a lamp is cleaned, the moths are lifted out on a sheet of paper and set back on the shade when it is done.

## The Ones Who Tend the Light

A handful of people, never more than a dozen, tend the tallow-flowers on the old terraces. They are not a guild. Nobody appoints them and nobody trains them. One year an elder is too stiff to climb, and a younger person is simply there, holding the brush.

The brushes are made of moth-wing, grey and soft and impossibly light. The tenders carry them in the crooks of their arms, wrapped in cloth, and touch the pale blooms with them in a slow, tender sequence that follows no pattern any observer has been able to name. They do not speak while they work. If asked what the ritual is for, they say, "It's what's done," and if pressed, they say, "It's asked." They do not say by whom.

The lamps along the terraces burn low, and the people who trim them will tell you it is to save the current. They say this quickly, and then they look at the tallow-flowers, and then they look away.

## What They Do at the Breath

Every eleven hours the bowl exhales, and the town stops.

It is not a solemn stop. A cable-spinner will pause with the thread in her fingers. A child will pause halfway through a joke, mouth open. A gardener will pause with the clippers half closed. For the length of a long, low sigh through the slot, nobody in the bowl is doing anything, and every face is turned a few degrees toward the sound, as the leaves are turned. Then it ends, and the shift changes, and the joke is finished, and the thread runs on.

No one has ever written this down as a law, and no one has ever broken it. It is the fifth rule, and the only one that everybody says they would keep even if it were not one.

## The Grate

At the end of the lowest lane, where the warm channel goes into the rock, there is a place where the wall has been scratched over and over with names. They are scratched small and close together, in many hands, without dates. These are the people who followed the channel in.

There are not many. A few every decade, always adults, and always, it is said, with a calm face. The town does not forbid speaking of them. It only forbids following. At the exhale, when the air moves up the slot, some people go to the grate and stand there with their hands at their sides. The air that moves past them is warm and smells faintly of iron and something older than iron. They stand and listen, and after a time they go back to their work, and no one asks them what they heard.

## What They Are Becoming

The people of the Low Ember are not sure what they are. They are not quite miners, or quite gardeners, or quite engineers, and they are certainly not settlers, since nobody settled. They are a people who arrived at a place already in motion and learned to move with it. They pay attention. They lean, a finger's width a year, toward the center. They cut only what they must and ask before they cut it. They keep their voices low, their lamps lower, and their hands busy, and they keep the five rules as one keeps a promise made in someone else's house.

If you asked them who lives in the bowl, they would say, "We do," and after a moment, a little uncertainly, "And the others."

They would not say who the others are. But at the next exhale, when every leaf in town turns its face, they would turn theirs with it.
