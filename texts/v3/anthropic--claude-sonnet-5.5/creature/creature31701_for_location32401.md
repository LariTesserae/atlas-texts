# Those Who Live on the Long Grey Table

## The Kilns

You see them first as a change in the cloud. A bruise of thicker white sits low over a basin, and beneath it something is moving that you took for a hill.

A kiln is longer than it is tall, and taller than any building you have stood beside. The back is a rolling landscape of overlapping scutes, each the size of a door, dark and matte, with edges furred in rime. They are built like roof slates, and they grow in rings like wood. On an old animal the scutes at the shoulders are almost black and pitted like meteorite. The young plates along the flanks are blue-grey and still faintly glossy. Lichen has settled on the oldest ones, so that an elder kiln wears orange and bone-white maps of its own age.

The head hangs low, close to the ground. It is less a face than a tool, a broad shovel-lip of horn that drags through the lichen crust and takes up moss, peat, gravel, and the thin topsoil in one long unhurried sweep. The eyes are small and deep-set, hooded under brow plates that shed frost. They open slowly. If a kiln looks at you, it does so the way a mountain would look, if it did. There is attention, but no hurry, and no sense that you are more than one feature of the ground.

The legs are pillars, stubby and wide-set, ending in flat splayed pads that spread the weight over the gravel. A step takes several seconds. The pad settles, the gravel sighs and compacts, and a pellet-trail begins behind it. Four legs, then four again. The pace is a person's walk, but with nothing of the walker's effort in it, only momentum.

### The fire inside

The kiln swallows stones on purpose. A muscular chamber behind the chest, a mill lined with ridged horn, holds a bed of round grey cobbles that the animal has collected over decades. They grind the forage to a paste. The paste then passes into the hearth.

No one has seen the hearth in a living animal, but its position is easy to read from the outside. Along the ribs the scutes lose their rime, and the fog above them lifts away in small warm eddies. In the chest the flesh is hot enough that a hand held a metre from the plates feels sun on it. The hearth is a chamber of dense, spongy, glowing tissue, fed by the animal's slow bellows-lungs. Those lungs draw one breath in perhaps five minutes, and the pulse of it is the thing you feel through your boots. In the hearth, iron sweats out of the stone and copper beads in the ash. Carbon burns off, and the sulphur and salts go up the spine as steam. The kiln keeps some of the metal for its scutes and its blood, which is thick, dark, and warm as a brine. The rest is cast off.

The vents along the spine open and close in a rippling sequence, front to back. Each exhalation stands up as a column of white, leans, and lays itself flat against the cloud deck.

### What they leave

The slag comes out behind, under the tail plates and between the hind pads, in a slow, steady seed-fall. Nothing about it is violent. The pellets drop hot, hiss on the wet gravel, and go from orange to grey within a minute. They are sorted before they leave the body. The iron-heavy ones are blue-black, the copper-rich ones a dull verdigris, and the light glassy ones that are mostly silica are milky. A herd crossing a basin lays down bands of colour along its route, and each animal's band is a little different, according to what it has eaten.

### Speech underfoot

Kilns do not call. The vents are silent and the throat makes nothing but breath. They talk by stepping. A kiln can drop its weight in a particular rhythm, a long, a short, a long, and a pulse goes out through the pavement that others feel through their pads for miles. It travels farthest in the cold, when the gravel is frozen into a single plate, and its meaning is beyond a human reading. Herds turn together, stop together, and lower their heads together toward some patch of lichen still unseen. Sometimes a whole basin will halt for a day, every animal standing and waiting, and then, after a stamp from one of the elders, resume grazing as if nothing had passed.

### Birth and cooling

A kiln is born cold. This is the fragile time. A calf is dropped from the mother in a bed of warm slag and lies there like a grey boulder, and its hearth is a small dead cavity. Then an adult, often not the mother, lowers herself over it, pressing her vent-warmed belly to its back, and holds still for hours or days. The calf's fire catches or it doesn't. Calves that catch rise slowly and follow the herd, feeding first on the finest, softest slag. Calves that fail lie where they were dropped, and the attendants take them within the week.

Old kilns end in stillness. The hearth banks down, the pace slows, and finally the animal stops in a basin and does not move on. Frost creeps over the vents. The scutes rime over. Over years the body sinks into its own shape and the life on it changes. Lichen spreads, moss finds the seams, and the beetles move into the warm hollows. After a century a dead kiln is a low mound with a grain in it, and it is difficult to say how many of the rounded knuckles standing on the Table were never rock at all.

## Those Who Attend

### On the back

The grey-feathered birds are about the size of a crow, but rounder, with short bills and feet that grip the scute edges like clamps. They live entirely on the animals. They pick rime from the plates, eat the mites in the seams, and take the small pale insects that crowd around the vents. Their nests are woven of lichen and hair-moss and wedged into the crevices near the warm ribs, and their eggs are the colour of slate. Fledglings never touch the ground for their first year. They hop between the plates, and when a bird does go down to the gravel it looks around as if it had stepped into the sea.

### In the folds

Where the hide folds behind the shoulder and under the jaw, there are gardens. Springtails move in pale drifts across damp skin. Mites the colour of rust cluster in the creases where sweat and brine collect. Fungus grows in gloved patches that the birds do not eat, and the mites graze it. Each fold is a small country with a border and a history, and it moves at the pace of the animal that carries it.

### Below

Where the pellets fall, the ground quickens. The pale beetles arrive first, within minutes. They are long-legged, blind, and quick, and each carries a tiny pair of pincers that it uses to crack the slag and lick the mineral rind. They roll the smaller pellets into galleries, and the larger ones they honeycomb until they collapse into grit. Behind them come the burrowers, soft pink-grey things that live in millions and never surface. They eat the beetle waste and the cooled ash, and they turn the ground over so thoroughly that after a herd has passed, the gravel is loose and sweet-smelling for a season. This is why the lichen returns.

### The cushions and the seeps

Not all life depends on the kilns directly. The cushion plants along the old routes, the black moss at the seeps, and the sedge in the thread-streams all depend on the herds indirectly, on the metal-laced soil and on the damp they carry. The small pale insects that shelter in the cushions live their whole lives in a dome a hand wide. They never meet a kiln, though they are its dependents as surely as the birds.

## The Rust Walker

Between the herds there is the scavenger. It is the size of a fox, long in the leg and narrow through the chest, and its coat is the exact orange-brown of oxidised slag, because it rolls in the old kiln roads and the dust stays. It does not follow any one herd. It ranges instead along the abandoned trails, working from pellet-line to pellet-line and eating what the kilns leave behind: dead birds, stranded beetles, the occasional calf that did not catch.

It moves alone and in silence. When it crosses a pavement of polished stones you hear only the faint tick of its claws. It is watchful in the manner of an animal that has learned the ground is not always still. It sleeps curled against warm slag, in the hollows a herd has left, and it is the only creature on the Table that seems to look at the horizon. When the fog thins and a great steaming back rises in the distance, it will stop, raise its head, and stand for a long time, as though checking a memory against what it sees.

## What Lives Here

In the end the Table has few kinds of inhabitant, and most of them are small. But the kilns are so large that the whole plateau arranges itself around them, and their slow, warm, humming passage through the cold is the only story anyone here tells. The birds ride it. The beetles inherit it. The lichen paints itself along the road it leaves. The rust walker, alone in the grey, keeps a distance and watches, and does not seem to think of itself as apart from any of it.
