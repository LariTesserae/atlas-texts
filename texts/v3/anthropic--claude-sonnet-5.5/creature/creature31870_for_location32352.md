# Who Lives on the Shelf

## The People of the Shoulder

About forty people live on the plateau through the year, and perhaps twice that in the drier months when the peat crews come up from the road. There is no village. The dwellings are as scattered as the machines: a bunkhouse by the turbine hall, three stilted huts on the fourth terrace, a shed by the winch drum, and a few low turf-roofed shelters strung along the boardwalks, each within a day's slow walk of the next. The residents are not one people. The keepers of the station came up from the coastal cities with engineering certificates. The peat cutters are from the valley towns below the scarp, and their grandfathers cut peat in other bogs. A third group, the oldest families on the plateau, arrived when the road was new. They stayed when the road's purpose had thinned to a cable and a bucket, and they became the ones who maintain the boardwalks and reset the quartz cairns after each storm. They call themselves by a word that is not spoken. It is a single flat-handed stroke down the breastbone.

All of them are shaped by the sound. They have long since stopped fighting it and built a life in the shape it leaves.

You notice the hands first. They are always moving, but not in gesture as lowlanders use it, as ornament to speech. The hands *are* the speech. A finger count against the collarbone gives a number. A double tap between the shoulder blades means *look behind you*. A slow drag of the palm across the upper arm means *the fog is coming*, and a fast one means *go now*. Names are rhythms tapped on the shoulder, and a child is given hers in the first week, pressed with a fingertip into her back while she is held. Everyone carries their own name in their body, and a person's name is never said aloud, because there is nothing to say it with. When someone dies, their rhythm is retired for a season, and no one taps it.

Their faces are the most open faces you will see. Eyes do the work that tone does elsewhere, and eyebrows, mouths, and the angle of the head are unguarded, almost theatrical. Two people can conduct a long argument across a boardwalk with nothing but eyes and shoulders, and it can be affectionate or bitter, and no one else could follow it. Laughter is silent here, a shaking of the whole chest with the mouth wide, and you learn to read it as quickly as you'd learn to read a hand.

They dress against wetness. Their coats are felted wool, greased with lanolin and pine tar, dark and heavy. Over the coats they wear panels of bright oilcloth in ochre, blue, or white, cut in shapes that mean something: this is a keeper, this is a cutter, this one is carrying a child or a bad knee or a message. The colored flags on poles double as signals. A red flag at a hut door means someone is ill and should be visited. Two blues crossed means *bring rope*.

Their sleep is the strangest thing about them. They cannot sleep in silence. The families who have gone down to the towns come back within a season, and they say, mostly by pantomime, that the lowlands press on the ears like a hand. The old men in the bunkhouse sleep with their heads on the wall nearest the turbine, and the young ones sleep pressed together like animals. Infants are carried against a parent's sternum, and a mother hums, though no sound will leave her mouth, so that the child feels the note in her bones. It is thought to be the first language. It is done at night and in the cold and in every other hour of a child's first year.

The dead are laid in the moss. It is cold and tannic, and it keeps what it is given. The peat cutters occasionally lift a brick and find a hand, a boot, a length of braid. They lay these back, add a white stone, and tap a rhythm on the nearest shoulder, and this too is a kind of mourning. There is no one to be frightened by it. Everyone up there is slowly becoming the ground.

## The Watchers of the Cup

They are not afraid of much, but they are afraid of one thing, and it is not the height or the wet or the cold. It is the moment the scarp lets go.

The rock here is new and jointed, and the falls have not yet worn it round. Slabs come off. They come off without warning, and their sound is lost in the greater sound, so that the first sign of a collapse is a tremor in the boardwalk and a ring in the water of a cup. The keepers set out tin cups at every hut and every corner of the boardwalk, half full, and they watch them. They can tell the ordinary shiver of the falls from the other, deeper, lopsided one. A cup with a single, off-center ring is a warning. A child is taught this before she is taught to walk the boards. It is the nearest thing they have to scripture.

Old women sit at the cups the way lowland women sit at fires. They keep a kind of ledger in knotted cord, one knot for every slide they have felt, and the cords hang on the wall in loops. The oldest are hung with hundreds of knots, and the color of the cord changes with the decade. The women say nothing of it. They only hand the cord to a younger person and touch her wrist.

## The Frogs of the Seeps

Along the weeping faces, in the hollows where the ferns fringe the rock, sit frogs the size of a thumb joint, polished orange, wet as new paint. They do not call. They tap. A frog will lower its chin to the surface of its little pool and drum a fast pulse with its throat, and the rings go out, and another frog three pools over answers, ring for ring. At dawn the seeps are covered in it: concentric circles crossing and crossing, a slow patterned writing in water.

The people know these frogs. A child may be given one to watch, a particular frog in a particular pool, and the child will come back to it for years and learn its rhythms, and so learn how to read water. The frogs are said to tell the weather, and no one has ever settled whether they do, but it is generally agreed they are right more often than the barometer in the station.

## The Runner in the Moss

Beneath the cushions lives a small dark rodent, thick-furred, nearly tailless, with tiny eyes and forepaws like a mole's. It tunnels just under the surface and leaves long raised ridges, so the moss is scored all over with fine straight lines, like the palm of a hand. It lives on seeds, on the pale rootlets of sedge, on insects slowed by cold. It is almost never seen. It is known by its ridges and by the way the moss sometimes ripples ahead of your boot.

The runners have their own quiet economy. They dig, and the water finds their tunnels, and the tunnels become the first faint channels, the first tentative lines of the future valley. The runners are, without knowing it, sketching the stream beds the falls will one day cut. The peat cutters know this and treat the ridges with some respect. They step over them. They say, in their fashion, that the animal is drawing the map.

## The One with the White Collar

On clear mornings, when the fog has not yet come, a large dark bird rides the updraft along the scarp. It has a white ruff at the throat and a wingspan wider than a door. It hangs. It does not flap. It slides along the edge of the cliff as if hung from a wire, turns a shoulder, and comes back. Everyone stops to watch it. They tap a slow, sacred rhythm on each other's arms, the same one, the bird's name, and it is the only name on the plateau that is used casually.

It nests, they say, on a ledge on the fifth terrace, where no one has been. Some think it is the same bird their grandparents saw. It probably is not, but the people up there are used to the idea that things persist by replacing themselves, and they see no difference.

## The Columns and the Pale Thing

There are also the living columns, those four-meter stacks of plant on plant, no ground in them, growing from rot into more life, and the people think of them almost as neighbors. They leave offerings at the base of the tallest, a thumb of butter, a bead. They say a column is a kind of family, one generation of moss dying so the next can stand on its back. Nothing in the place contradicts them.

And below the moss, far below, in the water-drummed dark where the true ground might be, something else lives. No one has seen it whole. The peat cutters, digging deep, sometimes lift a brick and find in the wet hollow beneath it a pale, eyeless salamander no longer than a finger, translucent as wax, with feathery red gills. It does not flinch from the light because it has no way to know it is light. It lifts its head slowly toward the warmth of the hand, and the cutter, who cannot speak to it and does not try to, lays the brick back over it gently and moves on. They do not tell anyone. Or they tell one person, with a tap on the shoulder and a finger held to the lips, though that gesture is redundant here.

Up on the plateau, everyone keeps a few quiet secrets like that, in a place where every secret is already safe, because nothing said could ever be overheard. The falls take all speech. The moss takes all footprints. The people have learned to live inside that erasure, to touch what they mean, to leave small marks in white stone, and to trust that they will be held, when their time comes, by the cold green ground that has been growing for them since before there was anyone to lie down in it.
