# Those Who Live in the Hum

The room has no people in it, but it is not uninhabited. Its residents are the kind that ask nothing of it and have long since stopped expecting anything back. They are slow, and each has settled into one habit and kept it.

## The Patroller

In the warm channel along the north wall lives a long fish, eel-shaped but not quite an eel. It is olive on the back, the color of the moss it passes under, and the belly is the pale yellow of old piano keys. It is as long as your forearm and a little thicker. Its eyes are small and clear. There is light enough in the room, and nothing here is dark, so it never had to give up sight for something stranger.

It swims a twenty-pace stretch of channel, from the place where the water leaves the wall to the place where it tilts toward the sump grate. It goes down and back, down and back, with a slow lateral ripple that never hurries and never rests. It does not feed, or you never see it feed. You suspect it takes in the warm water the way the ferns do.

There is always exactly one. Whether it is the same fish or a long chain of them, no one has ever learned. No small ones are seen, and no carcass drifts into the grate. Sometime, perhaps, one ends and another begins, and the patrol goes on across the gap, because the patrol is the thing and the fish is only what carries it.

It is not deaf. It has spent its whole life inside one sound and has nothing to compare it to, so the hum is not something it hears. It is what the water is made of.

## The Snails

Tall-spired snails, the color of weak tea, are spaced across the wet stone about one every few paces, like pieces set out on a board. They graze the film of algae and liverwort on the walls, and they are so slow that their movement can only be told by their trails. Each has worked the same route for so long that the concrete is worn into a faint, glossy track, a shallow groove the width of a foot, looping between a drip-basin and a seam of moss and back. Crossing the room, you cross dozens of these small roads, and none of them meets another. Each snail has its own circuit, and each circuit was long ago agreed on.

If you lift one from the wall, which is a rude thing to do, you feel the shell buzzing against your fingertip. The spiral resonates. It is the only part of the room that answers the turbine in a voice you can feel, and for a moment the small thing in your hand sounds like the whole chamber in miniature. Put it back where it was and it goes on.

## The Frogs

The small green frogs sit on the fern fronds with their throats pulsing, and no sound comes out that you could ever hear. This is not silence, because they are speaking. The trick is in the ferns.

Every frond in the room shivers with the bass. A frog sitting on one is riding the tremor like a rider on a horse. When a frog calls, the throat's pulse sends a small irregularity into the frond, a knot in the shiver, a hitch in the rhythm. The knot runs down the stem and into the crowded neighboring fronds, and from there to the next frog, who feels it in the pads of its feet. They have not called in air for generations. They talk through the plants, the way news moves through a crowd by touch. A stranger to the place, watching, sees only frogs sitting still on shaking leaves. But listen with your hand on a frond and you may feel, under the general hum, a small stutter travel past and then go on somewhere else.

In the marsh above, the same species calls loudly at dusk. The room's frogs are the same animal, but they have learned a different language. In the room they never quite grew up to the sound.

## The Small Ones

On the standing rings in the puddles live specks. Pale springtails, no bigger than grains of rice, hop between the fixed ripples and always land on the same ring they left from. Some have lived on a single puddle for their whole lineage. They graze the film there and know it the way you might know a room in the dark.

In the moss, in the sodden fur on the handrails, there are mites, worms, threads of things, a whole slow economy passing through the cushions. It is unseen and unnoticed, and it is the reason the moss is so green.

## The Heron

Now and then, a tall gray bird stands in the column of light. It looks like a heron, but heavier in the chest and darker in the wing, as though it has been standing in mist for too long. Nobody knows if it lives on the roof and drops through the oculus, or if it is only one of many that pass. It stands on one leg on the turbine dome, with the vibration running up through it so that its feathers tremble faintly, like grass.

It does not notice you. You can walk within reach of it, wave, stamp, and it will only turn one eye to you slowly, without alarm, and turn it back. Nothing has ever startled it here, because there are no sudden sounds in this room and never have been. It has forgotten fear. It is the calmest thing you will ever look at, and if you stand near it long enough your own shoulders come down and your own breath slows to match.

## What Lives Here

Take all of them together and you see what really dwells in the room. Beneath the eel and the snails and the frogs and the bird there is a way of being, in which nothing hurries, nothing is startled, everything keeps to a circuit, and the world is one steady pressure to lean against. The creatures are just the places where that steadiness has taken on bodies.

You will feel it in yourself after an hour. Your hands begin to talk more slowly. Your gaze rests on one thing. You begin, without deciding to, to walk a route through the room that you do not vary. Then you look up and see the frogs on their shaking leaves, the fish at the far end of its patrol, the heron in its light, and you understand that you have started to belong to the place. When you climb the worn stair and cross the marsh, the ground will still be humming beneath your boots, and you will go on feeling it for a long time after you have stopped being able to tell that you do.
