# Those Who Live in the Full Hollow

## The Ones You See First

The salamanders come first, because they are on the rock at eye level and the rock is where you put your hands. They are the length of a finger or a little more, black-backed, with bellies the orange of a struck match. Each holds still inside its own sheen of water. They do not flinch when a hand approaches, only slowly rotate one gold-rimmed eye. Their skin is always slightly open to the air, always drinking. They breathe through it, and the fog is the only thing they have ever needed. Some are so still that moss has begun on their backs, a green dust that they do not seem to mind. If you count them along an arm's length of wall, you get a number, and it will be the same number on the next arm's length, and the next.

Then the frogs, pale as candle wax, each the size of a thumbnail, each on a leaf of its own. Their throats pulse in a slow, private rhythm. In the moss-glow their eyes take a point of green light and hold it, so that the leaves seem strung with tiny lamps. They are spaced with a courtesy no one arranged. Where one sits, another sits an arm's length off, and if one is gone, the space is filled by the time you look again. Nobody has seen a frog arrive.

Snails climb the fern fronds in translucent shells through which you can see the coil of the organ inside, dark and slowly working. They leave silver lines. Over years the lines have layered, so the fronds wear a fine lacquer of old passage, and the snails follow these tracks the way you would follow a road.

## The Ones Who Move

The millipedes are the largest things that move in the open. A forearm long, glossy black-brown, they go through the litter in processions of five or six, one behind another, though none leads. Their legs ripple in waves that begin at the tail and run forward, and the sound of it is almost nothing, a dry-wet whisper like a page turned underwater. They eat the fallen and the rotting, and what they leave behind is the dark crumb that the ferns root in. They are the hollow's slow hands, turning the dead into soil.

The moths sit on the bark in ranks so close they overlap like slate on a roof. Gray, brown, and dust-white, they are indistinguishable from the trunk until one shifts. Then a whole hand's width of tree ripples and settles. They do not fly, or they fly so rarely that no one has learned when. Their wings are heavy with fog and beaded like everything else.

The spiders hang in every gap. Their webs are not the neat wheels of drier places but sagging hammocks, tented and layered, loaded with water until every strand is a rope of small glass beads. The spiders sit in the middle, pale-bodied, long-legged, and patient in a way that seems to have no interior, no waiting, only being. They take moths and small flies, and when they take one, the web barely trembles. The whole transaction is silent, and something is fed.

Other things live in the litter, and you feel more than see them: beetles like wet seeds, worms as thin as thread, and a many-jointed pale creature about the length of a thumb that flexes and vanishes under a leaf. Under the peat there is a whole further population, and it is the largest, and it has never once been counted.

## The Ones in the Pool

In the black pool on the lowest terrace, small pale fish hang suspended in a cloud. They are eyeless or nearly so, with two dimples where eyes were meant to be and a faint pink flush where the blood shows through the skin. They face the same way, into a current that is not there, and they move only by the smallest quiver of a fin. Now and then the whole cloud turns, all at once, without a signal, and settles facing another direction. Perhaps they feel something in the water that you cannot. The pool never rises or falls, and the fish never grow more or fewer.

Below the fish, in the black silt, there are certainly others. When the surface shivers for no reason, something has moved down there, something larger than a fish, that does not come up.

## The Ones You Hear

The soft, wet, rubbery *tk* comes from somewhere in the ferns and is made by no animal you can find. Perhaps it is a tongue leaving a wall. Perhaps it is a small creature clicking its jaw shut on a smaller one. It comes from a different place each time, and it is never answered.

And there is the note. It rises from the deepest part of the bowl, low and round, like a wet finger drawn once around the rim of a glass, and it fades without an echo, because there is nothing hard enough in the hollow to send one back. The salamanders do not stir when it sounds. The frogs' throats go on pulsing. Whatever makes the note is known to the place and is not remarkable to it. Nobody has seen the singer. It may be a very large amphibian, older than the terraces, lying in the mud beneath the pool with its throat sac slowly filling. It may be the bowl itself, drawing breath through its stone, and the animals no more notice it than you notice your own pulse. It comes at no interval you can count, and this is the one irregularity in the whole place. It is possible that the note is what keeps the count.

## The Ones Who Came and Went

Someone once came here. The hand-axe in the tree fern and the cord strung between two trunks are all that remain, and they have been so absorbed that they hardly read as marks at all. You cannot tell how many there were, or whether they were hunters, pilgrims, or lost. They left no bones, no fire-scars, no cairn.

It is tempting, in the dark, to look at the moss-cushioned shapes along the terraces, the humped forms furred green and hung with lichen, and think of them as more than boulders. It is tempting to see in a certain fern-crowned hummock the curve of a shoulder, and in the small bell-flowers, bruise-colored and nodding, something that has learned to be patient. This is probably only the mind's habit. But the hollow is full, and it has always found a place for what enters it, and the ones who came and did not leave may simply have been fitted in, one more quiet form among the rest, gently held and slowly covered.

## What They Are Together

Taken singly, these are salamanders, frogs, snails, moths, and spiders. Taken together, they are less a population than a single tenant with a great many small bodies. Each animal keeps its own place, its own arm's length, its own film of water, and the arrangement is so exact that it begins to look like one thing thinking slowly. The eaten are replaced as fast as they are taken. The moss-glow holds its one green. The drip counts nothing and misses nothing. Whatever lives here lives as a pattern, and the pattern is what has persisted, while every individual in it is only a bead of water on a leaf-tip, let go, and replaced by the next.

If you stay, you will be counted. A thousand small eyes will settle into the arrangement of you, unalarmed, and the moss will begin at the edges of your boots. Then, somewhere a little way off, a frog will sit an arm's length from where you are standing, and it will feel to you like the gap you left was already filled.
