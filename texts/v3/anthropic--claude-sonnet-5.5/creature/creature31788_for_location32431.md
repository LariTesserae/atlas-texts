# Who Lives in the Sunk Meadow

## The Builders

The first inhabitants are the smallest, and everyone else lives inside what they have made.

Each worker is about the length of a grain of rice, pale as the gypsum and soft-bodied, with a head the color of weak tea. They have no eyes worth the name. They know the world by touch, by taste, and by the vibration of the ground. There are millions to a city, and the city is not a crowd of individuals. A single worker cut off from the mound wanders in small circles and dies within a day, dry and confused. Only in numbers does it become part of something that knows what to do.

The colony sorts itself into trades, and the trades have bodies.

- **The wetters** are the most numerous and the most important. Before dawn they climb the outer skin of the spires and wait in rows with their mouthparts lifted. When the fog arrives they drink the beads that form on the raw clay, swelling until their abdomens turn translucent. Then they go down into the walls and pass the water mouth to mouth, worker to worker, gardener to nursery, nursery to queen. Nothing in the city is drunk alone. A drop that enters at the tip of a spire may pass through forty bodies before it is finally used.
- **The fluters** are the masons. They chew clay, gypsum grit and resin into a paste and lay it in thin, deliberate courses, and they seem to follow a rule no one taught them. A flue is extended when the air in the gallery beside it grows stale. A buttress is thickened where the wall hums too loudly. The fluters do not read plans. They read the wall, and the wall answers them.
- **The gardeners** are small, pale and slow. They tend the fungus combs deep in the cool middle of the mound, chewing leaf-scrap and moss into beds and weeding out stray molds with their forelegs. The sweet-rotten smell that leaks from the vents is their work. The colony eats the garden, and the garden eats everything else.
- **The plug-heads** are the soldiers. Their heads are blunt, hard and shaped like corks, and when a wall is breached they wedge themselves into the hole and stay, sealing it with their own bodies. Some are still there a season later, dried in place, and the fluters build over them.
- **The drummers** are a quiet caste that is easy to miss. When the ground shakes strangely, or a fox digs at the base, they knock their heads against the gallery floors in a rapid, stuttering rhythm. The message travels through the whole mound in moments, and the thrum in your palm changes pitch. Then it settles, and the city resumes its low tone.

At the center of the oldest core, in a chamber with walls polished by generations of touch, lives the **queen**. She is the size of a thumb, a pale swollen thing that cannot walk, and she is attended day and night by a slow crowd that licks her, feeds her, and carries away what she lays. She is not a ruler. She has no more say in the city than the water does. She is simply the place where the colony's future accumulates. When she fails, as every queen eventually does, the attendants raise another from the nursery, and no one grieves. The city has been through this hundreds of times.

## The Long Tenancy

The insects living in a mound today are not the ones who began it. Colonies die, from a bad drought or a fungal blight or a wound that never closed, and the mound stands empty. Then, sometimes after years, a young colony founded by one winged pair arrives and finds the old architecture waiting. The flues are still cut, and the chambers are still aired and shaped. They move in, patch the roof, and go on.

So the knowledge of the basin does not live in any body. It lives in the clay, in the strata that the wind sometimes exposes, in the dark hard core and the raw pale skin. A colony is a season of the city. The city is the thing that lasts.

## The Neighbors

Around and inside the mounds live those who have learned to profit from the builders' patience.

**The martins and swallows** are small and dusky, with pale throats. They plaster their cups of mud in rows along the sheltered sides of the towers, and they chatter constantly, not from alarm but from habit. They bring the builders no harm and no benefit. The mound is simply a good cliff, made by someone else.

**The buttonhole burrowers** are round, soft-furred creatures with blunt noses, bright black eyes and wide, sensitive whiskers. They live in tunnel-towns that run for hundreds of paces beneath the meadow, connected by runs so worn they shine. Each town is a family of families, and the young are raised in common nurseries lined with shredded moss. They come up to the surface at dusk to feed, and their heads pop out of the round entrance holes one by one like a slow applause. They are afraid of nearly everything, and with reason, because nearly everything eats them.

**The paper-wasps** live in the leatherleaf branches in gray combs, up to forty to a tree. They are lean, amber and unhurried. They chew bark and pulp into thin sheets, fold them into cells, and defend the whole structure with a quiet, unhysterical ferocity. Even the foxes give the wasp trees a wide berth. Underneath a comb the leaves are always slightly cleaner, since the wasps hunt the caterpillars that would otherwise strip them.

**The lantern-moths** live only among the pale blooms and are almost never seen apart from them. They are the color of mica flakes, and their wings make a soft dry sound as they cluster on the stalks. The flowers open at dusk to receive them and close by morning. Neither could live without the other, and at dusk the meadow hums with their transaction.

## The Grazers

The **lawnsheep** move slowly across the open ground in loose bunches of twenty or thirty. They are barrel-bodied and short-legged, with thick, matted wool the color of old rope and broad flat teeth that tear the ribbon-grass down to a neat, close turf. They graze mostly at dawn, when the grass is wet, licking the beads directly from the blades, and they are almost never seen to drink anything else. By midday they are sleeping standing up, with their heads lowered and their eyes half-closed, in a stillness so complete that martins sometimes land on their backs.

They know the trails. They step around the files of workers with an odd, exaggerated delicacy, raising each hoof high, and no one ever taught them to do so. A lawnsheep that tramples a trail is nudged by the others, and the lesson passes down the flock without fuss.

Their dung is what the **thumb-beetles** are waiting for. These are glossy black or oil-green, and they roll balls of it with stiff, comic effort across the bare clay and bury them at the roots of the leatherleaf trees. The dark circles around each trunk are partly their work. The tree feeds the fog to the ground, the sheep feed the beetles, the beetles feed the tree, and no one is anywhere near the center of it.

## The Hunters

The **gray stilt-foxes** are long-legged and narrow-chested, with big ears and a habit of standing motionless for minutes on end, one forepaw lifted. They hunt the burrow-towns in the last blue light. A fox listens at a hole, shifts its weight, and listens again, and sometimes the wait is thirty minutes. They are thin animals, always slightly hungry, and often they leave a hole alone for the night.

The **ground owls** are low, round-headed and nearly silent, with pale gypsum-colored feathers that make them invisible against the bare clay. They fly a hand's breadth above the grass, and the burrowers go still as they pass. They are the one thing in the basin that is quiet, and they seem to know it.

The **pale lizards** hunt the bare ground by day, stalking the insects that stray from the green. They are the color of chalk, and they do a slow push-up when nervous. In the middle of the day, when the heat is greatest, they lie flat against the salt with their eyes closed and their mouths open, like small stone carvings.

## The One That Is Not Seen

There is also something larger.

No one has watched it directly. Its tracks cross the bare clay at the edges of the groves, broad and splay-toed, deep enough that the pale crust cracks around the rim, and always at night. Its scat is coarse, full of snail-shell, beetle-wing and the small bones of burrowers. It keeps to the shadowed edges of the basin, to the low pale ruins of dead mounds where the moss has just begun to take, and there it digs. The old cores are the softest place in the meadow, and hollow, and possibly warm.

The colonies do not seem to fear it. The drummers do not drum when it passes. Some of the oldest mounds carry, near the base, a scar of raking claws, long healed over and re-laid, and the fluters have built around it as carefully as around a plug-head. Whatever it is, it has been coming for longer than any living city, and the meadow has made room for it.

## What Lives There, Together

If you ask who lives in the Sunk Meadow, the true answer is not a list. It is the fog, which arrives each night and is drunk, passed from mouth to mouth, taken up through leaf and root, and made into wool, wingbeat, wasp-paper and mud. It is the mounds, which outlast their tenants. It is the birds that come down at dusk in their thousands to shout the whole roost into being. And underneath, it is the slow shared breath of the basin, in which no single creature is more than a moment, and the meadow is the only one that stays.
