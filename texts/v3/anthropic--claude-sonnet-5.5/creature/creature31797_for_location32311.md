# Who Lives on the Shelf

## The Drifters

They are the bulk of the living world. A drifter is a ribbon of pale flesh three or four meters long, flattened side to side, with a soft fringe of fins running the length of its body like the hem of a slip. The skin is nearly translucent. In the blue light you can see the dark line of the gut and the small bright knot of the heart, which beats slower than a human's, because everything here is slow and cold. The mouth is a broad flat scoop, lipless, lined with rasping ridges. It is made for scraping film off rock, and it leaves pale tracks across the rust and sulfur mats.

They do not so much swim as undulate, one long wave passing from head to tail. The wave is timed to the engines. A drifter's tail completes its beat on the stroke of the bell, every seven seconds, and so does every other drifter within hearing, which is all of them. A herd of thousands moves as one lung. From ten kilometers away the herd looks like smoke, and up close it looks like a pale field being combed by a wind that only the animals feel.

They have no voices. They have never needed any, because they are already inside a voice, and everything they might say to one another is said by small departures from it. A drifter a fraction of a second behind the beat is tired, or ill, or very young. One a fraction ahead is frightened, and its fear runs through the herd in a ripple of tiny advances, like a shiver crossing a horse's flank. Mothers and calves find each other by phase. A calf's particular lag is its name, and a mother reads it across a crowd of thousands. Courtship is syncopation. A male who wants to be noticed slips half a beat off the herd, holds it for the length of a dozen strokes, and then slides back. It is the most dangerous thing a drifter ever does. Courting is done in the shelter of the halls, where the racket of the engine masks the flaw and the hunters do not go.

They are gentle and single-minded. They feed, and keep the beat, and are mostly not afraid, since fear is expensive and the hunters are few. When they sleep, they sleep in the beat too, hanging in the water with only the tail-tips moving, a slow white forest of stillness that ticks like a clock.

They die by falling behind. An old drifter loses the stroke a little more each season. It is not cast out, because the herd has no means of casting anything out. It simply drifts to the margin, where the beat around it grows thinner, and then it is alone, out of step, and something dark is already coming.

## The Nurseries

The calves are born in the halls.

The warm outflow from the stacks pools inside the iron, a few degrees above the surrounding sea, and that small warmth is the difference between a calf that lives and one that doesn't. Females come to the halls heavy and out of rhythm with their own bodies. They wedge themselves in among the girders and the idle valve rods. The calves come out as thin curls of white, no longer than a forearm, and the first thing each one experiences is the bell. It comes through the plate iron, through the water, through the unfinished bones of its head. Within a day it has caught the beat and is pulsing with it. Calves that do not catch the beat within three days are not fed by their mothers, who cannot find them among the others. It is not cruelty. They are simply not there.

The calves grow up among riveted plates and the iron ribs of the roof, and to them these are as natural as reef. They shelter in the lee of the great cylinder, whose warm flank they rub against as it cycles. They graze the mineral scale that builds on the condenser tubes. Whether their grazing is why the tubes never foul, no one can say. It may be that the machines were made to run untended and simply do. It may be that they were made, in some way that predates the makers, to be tended by exactly this.

## The Hunters

There are few of them, and each is enormous. A hunter is dark, long, and heavy, the color of the basalt it lies on, with a body like a drowned bridge girder. It moves seldom and slowly. The mouth is hinged like a trapdoor and drops open along a seam that is invisible in repose. Its eyes are small and set far back, and they are not the organs it hunts with.

It hunts by listening to the beat. This is the whole of its craft. It approaches a herd from downcurrent and lies on the rock or drifts in the water above the fringe of the crowd, and it feels the herd's rhythm through its skin, its jaw, the long sensitive line down its flank. Then it waits. It waits for a stroke to come late, a single tail out of the great tide of tails, a calf still learning, a sick animal, an old one. The hunter is not looking for the weakest body. It is looking for the flaw in the pattern.

When it finds one it moves, and the jaw drops, and there is a hollow bark of water rushing in. Nothing else in the herd changes. The beat closes over the gap like water over a stone, and the stroke falls again on its seventh second.

Hunters are not exactly solitary. Sometimes two lie a few hundred meters apart with the same herd between them, each feeling the other's stillness through the rock. They are not friends. But when one takes a kill, the other will drift over and eat what is left, and there is a kind of manners in this. They go slowly, and they do not fight. Life on the shelf is long and the meals are rare, and they have learned to be patient about everything.

The hunters are entrained too. Their heartbeats and their slow breathing fall on the bell, and their jaws never open on the stroke itself. It is the one moment when the whole shelf is united and there can be no flaw.

## The Small Ones in the Iron

The wreckage of the works is a country of its own.

The crawlers are armored, the size of a dinner plate or a hat, jointed and flat-bodied, colored the exact rust-red of the plate iron. They live in the boiler rooms and under the floor gratings, and they walk the pipes and the valve gear, rasping at deposits with their forelimbs. They move only between strokes. Each step is taken in the few instants after the bell, and they freeze while the sound is at its height. Every crawler in every hall does this, thousands of them, so that if you could watch a boiler room from above you would see the whole floor tremble into motion and go still, tremble and still, seven seconds at a time.

The tube-worms cluster on the flywheel housings, white stalks tipped with red plumes, in colonies thick as fur. They feed on the warm chemistry seeping from the bearings and the packing glands. Their plumes sway with the turning of the wheel, and they lean into its rhythm like a congregation. The wheel is the oldest thing they know.

Then there are the pale fish, which barely count as animals. They hang in the shadowed corners of the halls, translucent and thin as a hand, so still that they seem to be part of the machinery, some tarnish in the water. They do not swim in the beat because they do not swim. They feed on whatever falls from the crawlers and the worms. It is said, by no one, that they are the oldest residents of the works. If you touched one it would move away without haste, and settle again a foot from where it had been.

## What They Do Not Know

None of them knows what the halls were for, or that anything ever stood behind them. The rails that run from the walls toward nowhere are for the crawlers only a place to walk. The stair in the cliff is a ledge. The crane over the empty dock is something to shelter under. The carts of cut stone are a reef.

Nor do they know that the sea is a channel and the sound a captive, or that the bell has been ringing since before the oldest herd's memory, or that a world might be otherwise. The drifters rise and fall in their thousands, the hunters lie in their patience, the crawlers scuttle and freeze, and the great pistons come down, and everything living on the Tablelands lives in the shadow of that fall. They are not unhappy. They have never been anywhere else, and the note under everything is not, to them, a noise. It is what it feels like to be.
