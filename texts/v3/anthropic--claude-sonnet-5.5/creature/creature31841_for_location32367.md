# Who Lives at Verrin Flat

Most of the residents of Verrin Flat are too small to be called residents. They are conditions of the water.

## The Felt

The oldest tenants are the mats. A thumbnail of green film over black mud is a city in section: a roof of cyanobacteria taking what light the overcast allows, a purple layer beneath them living on the light that gets through and the sulfur that rises from below, and under that a black, stinking floor of things that have never needed light or air. The layers trade with each other. What one breathes out, the next breathes in. The whole arrangement is older than the works, older than the coast as it now lies, and it will be here when the concrete has dissolved.

The pink pans are pink because of salt-loving microbes so thick that the brine looks stained rather than inhabited. The orange ones are a different tribe. The milky green ones are a third. None of them know one another. They hold their pans the way clans hold valleys, and a dike breach or a summer of rain will shift the borders by a season's worth of color.

In the brine itself swim the shrimp and the copepods, ticking through the water in clouds. Each is a red or translucent speck, but there are so many that a cupped hand dipped into a pan comes up holding a thin soup. They eat the felt and the bloom, and the birds eat them. For most of the residents of the flat, that is the whole story.

## The Grazers

The mud is inhabited to a depth of a hand. Worms thread it. Tiny clams and amphipods sit just under the skin of it, and the snails cover every upright surface. The cone snails on the walls do nothing that a person would notice. They drift a few centimeters at low tide, close their doors at the first dry hour, and hang in the seams of the concrete like seed. They are the reason the causeways click. They are also, without intending it, the reason the old works don't look abandoned to the eye of the flat: every surface is furred with living things.

The fiddler crabs run the surface. Each male carries a single enormous claw, pale and ridiculous, and waves it in slow arcs to the females down the burrow line. Ten thousand of them waving together is not a display for anyone. It is a habit of the whole flat, like the tick of a clock. Each crab keeps a burrow and defends a hand's width of mud around it, and they wage small, punctilious wars over the boundaries. When a shadow crosses, the whole band drops into its holes in a single pour and the flat is bare and grey. Then, a minute later, a few claws reappear, and the waving begins again from the edges inward.

The mudskippers are the philosophers of the shoreline. They prop on their fins at the water's edge with their bulging eyes above the skin of the mud, and they look at the world with an expression of slightly offended patience. They fight, too, with erect fins and gaping mouths, over territory the size of a dinner plate. Sometimes one climbs a rail, for no clear reason, and sits there in the heat.

## The Birds

The birds are the visible population, and the flat seems to belong to them.

The waders live in sheets. Stilts on their absurd pink stilts, avocets sweeping their upturned bills side to side in the shallows, sandpipers stitching the mud edge with rapid stabs. The small pale shorebirds, whole clouds of them, feed together and rise together. When they turn, the entire flock flashes from dark to light, and the sound of their wings is a single dry breath. They roost on the pan dikes at high water, shoulder to shoulder, facing into a wind that hardly blows.

The cormorants keep the gantries. They stand in black rows with their wings hung out to dry in air that is too wet to dry them, so they hold the posture all day as a kind of devotion. Ibises hunch on the rails and stitch the concrete edges with their curved bills, hunting the snails. Terns line the roof ridges of the pumping houses, and the ridges are white with them. The guano has dressed the cracking tower in a pale coat, and from a kilometer off it looks like a snow-covered pylon in a country where snow does not fall.

The flamingos are not quite residents; they come and go with the brine's richness. But when they are here they are the most conspicuous crowd on the plain: a drift of pink a kilometer long, heads down, feet stirring the mud, the whole flock filtering. Each bird is slowly walking in a small circle. A flamingo flock feeding is one of the quietest things on the flat, and one of the most complete.

Above them, in a supporting role, are the hunters: herons standing with their necks cocked for hours, an occasional fish eagle over the sluice, a monitor lizard that ambles through the crab bands like a slow drunk and comes away with a mouthful. They are kept honest only by how easy the eating is. A heron here is not desperate. It is merely thorough.

## The Air

At dusk the midges rise from the pans in brown columns, and for an hour the air is a second flat, a vertical one, crowded and lit by nothing. Swallows work through them in long, flicking arcs. The bats come out of the pumping houses in streams, a brick chimney of them, thousands, each dropping from the arched windows and unfolding into the dim. They have lived in those buildings so long that the roof trusses under their roosts are crusted black with generations of droppings, and the smell of the interior is a thick, sweet, ammoniac dark.

Brine flies live at the pan edges in a fur that lifts ahead of a walker's boots. They are so numerous that they behave like a fluid; they part, they flow around, they close behind.

## The Sweating Houses

The buildings have their own residents. Geckos hunt the wet walls at night, pale and translucent, calling with tiny clicks. Swallows plaster mud cups under every sheltered eave, so the salt stalactites and the nests hang together. Rats live in the compound and in the drier pumping houses, and rats are the only creatures on the flat that have any opinion about people. Spiders string wide orb webs between the pipe runs, beaded with condensation so that each one hangs like a wet chandelier.

Standing water fills the pump-house floors, and it is not empty. Mosquito larvae hang in it, and small fish that the herons have not yet found, and a green scum that the sun, coming through the arched windows, turns to a slow glow. Everything here has moved in, as though the buildings were simply another kind of terrain, a set of caves cut by a peculiar erosion.

## The Crew

Then there are the people.

There are a few dozen of them, and they live in the tin-roofed sheds by the main sluice. They are not the builders of the works. They are the last of a line of keepers whose reasons have thinned generation after generation until what remains is mostly a way of life. Nobody now can quite say who the salt is for. A boat comes in the dry season with fuel, flour, and parts. Something in a ledger is signed. The crew accept this as they accept the tide.

They move slowly, because the heat teaches it, and they speak little, because the flat hushes people the way it hushes its birds. They wear wide hats and long sleeves against the sun they can't see, and boots soled with cut tire, since a barefoot walk on a causeway is a walk over ten thousand snails. They walk the causeways at low tide with their eyes on the ground and pick their footing out of long habit, and the crabs part ahead of them without hurry.

Their work is small and repetitive. One person opens and closes the sluice gates by the tide table. Another tends the diesels, the slow-hearted ones, and knows each by the sound of its stroke, the way one knows a relative's cough. Someone changes the oil in the signal light at the crossing where no train has run in years, because the light has always been kept, and to let it go dark would be a kind of desertion. Someone cooks. Someone keeps the ledger. At the end of a day a few of them sit on the shed steps and watch the bats stream out of the brick, and say nothing, or say something short.

They are almost never seen because they are mostly indoors or far out on the pans, small upright marks among the waders. From a distance they are hard to tell from a survey post. A visitor might walk the whole length of a causeway and meet no one, and still be watched from a window by someone who wonders, mildly, who is coming.

The crew love the place in the way you love something that has never asked whether you do. They know the birds by season, the crabs by their marches, the clouds by the hour of their arrival. Twice a year, when the crabs cross the rail beds in a red-brown tide, they stay inside for two nights with the doors shut and listen to the small dry rain of legs on the tin.

## The Tenancy

So the flat is inhabited by a great crowd, in layers. The felt and the bloom below, the crabs and snails on the mud, the birds on the towers, the bats in the brick, and a few dozen people at the edge of it all, keeping the wheels turning for a city that never came. None of them is in charge. All of them are living in the leavings of something larger than themselves, and the tide comes in over them all, silent and thin, and takes the horizon for an hour.
