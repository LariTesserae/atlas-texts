# Who Lives in the Boiler Basin

## The People

There are about three hundred, and they will tell you it is always about three hundred. They are a damp, ochre-toned people, and the basin has coloured them the way it colours everything. Their skin has a faint sheen, like a glazed pot, and takes on a warm yellow-brown that has nothing to do with sun. Their hair holds steam in fine beads. Their eyes are a little narrowed against the vapour, so that they all seem to be thinking something over. They dress in cotton and oilcloth, loose at the wrists and neck to let the heat out, in the colours of the place: grey lagging, rust, sulphur, verdigris. Everyone wears something with pockets, because there is always a valve key, a rag, or a spare bulb to carry.

They talk in a particular way. Years under the turbine hum have given them a pitch of voice that cuts through it without shouting, a flat, carrying, unhurried tone that outsiders find oddly calming. Between the hiss and the whistles they also speak with their hands, a working sign language of small gestures: *pressure's up, pass the wrench, come and eat, look, the flamingos are in.* Two people on opposite gantries can hold a whole quiet conversation across forty metres of steam. They read lips well and seldom bother to raise their voices.

They are not hurried. The basin does not reward hurry. A pipe that is hot will still be hot in a minute, and the whistle will blow when it blows. Watch them walk under the mains, ducking without looking at the lagging, one hand trailing along warm iron, and you see people who move through their town the way a hand moves through a familiar pocket.

## The Work

Everyone has a job, and the jobs have old, plain names.

The **turbine tenders** live in the long brick hall in overalls gone soft with oil. They lay their palms flat on the casings and feel for the note. They can tell a bearing going bad from a change in the hum too slight for any gauge, and they have not been wrong in living memory. This is a fact the town treats as ordinary.

The **valve-walkers** go the rounds of the red wheels, a hundred and forty of them, in a fixed order that has never changed, giving each a quarter-turn and a listen, like doctors going down a ward. Most of their turns undo the last shift's turns. The steam is balanced, and they are its patient hands.

The **pan-rakers** work the boron and salt beds in long wooden-soled boots, drawing the crystal into pale ridges. They wear scarves over their mouths and talk to the shrimp. The **canners** work in the yard, where the eels go into tins and the smell settles in your clothes for a week. The **lamp-keepers** are a small, cheerful guild who spend their days replacing yellow bulbs on their iron brackets, so that at any hour some part of the town is being re-lit. The **tree-wards**, mostly the old, oil and tend the sooty street trees in their collars, and would sooner lose a child's shoe than a leaf.

At the far edge are the **well-listeners**, three or four who sit in the small stone huts over the deep wells with an ear pressed to a brass fitting. They are listening for the tide. The sea's slow breath comes up through the ground here, and they feel it as a swell in the water column, a long pressure like a sleeper's inhale. They keep no written record. They say it has always been the same size.

## The Table

Bread bakes in steam ovens in the walls. Eggs are lowered into vents in wire baskets and come out firm and faintly sulphurous; the children eat them like sweets. There are eels smoked over a pipe-joint, brined greens from the cool cracks, tea brewed with water from the tap-pools. Nothing is fried, because there is no need. The whole town is already a stove. Meals are eaten on the verandas under tin awnings with the plates steaming, and no one has ever complained of a cold dish.

## Children and the Old

Children are born knowing the place, and they are quick and barefoot and nimble on the hot ground. They know which plank walks flex and which flagstones are too warm to stand on for more than a count of ten. They play at valve-walking, taking turns to be the pressure. They never ask why the basin is as it is, any more than they ask why they have a pulse. To them it is simply the beat under everything.

The old are called the **settled**. It is hard to say when a person becomes one, because the basin does not mark age the way other places do. People grow into themselves, reach a certain colour and set of lines, and stay there, like iron reaching its orange. A woman at the pan-rakers' bench and her daughter beside her may look, to a stranger, like sisters, and the daughter may have children who look the same. Nobody remarks on it. The settled sit on the verandas with their hands on their knees, in the lamplight, listening to the note. When asked how old they are they smile and say: *the basin keeps its own time.*

Some hold that no one dies here so much as goes quiet. There is a burial ground behind the cooling towers, but its stones are all the same rust colour and no one reads the names.

## The Other Residents

The people share the basin with the animals, and the arrangement has the ease of very old neighbours. Geckos sun on the mains and are lifted off with two fingers when a valve needs turning, and put back. The feral cats lie along the warm gantries in rows. The pigeons on the turbine ridge, thousands of them, lift together at every relief-valve shriek, circle once, and settle again, and no one looks up. The frogs sing all night, the same rattling chorus, and the residents say they cannot sleep without it. Newcomers who arrive to visit lie awake for the silence they expected and cannot find.

The flamingo-like birds stand in the evaporation ponds pink as lamp-shades, one leg drawn up, and are given the same quiet regard as the pipes. When one lifts and flaps and settles, the raker nearest will say *there she goes* without looking. Nobody remembers naming them and everyone knows which is which.

## The Ones Who Walk Out

A few people every generation, not many, go out along the plank causeway past the last valve house. They are not banished and not exactly forbidden. They go because something tugs, and they walk until the roar thins and the frogs go quiet and the light widens into something that has a direction. They sit among the black glass boulders with the egrets, in the brown plume, and look at the sea.

Most come back by dusk. They look at the town as if seeing it for the first time, at the low huddle of roofs and the hundred white feathers of steam and the lamps coming on, and then they go in under the pipes and pick up their wrenches. They say little about it except that it was cool.

Once in a long while someone does not come back. No one looks for them. Their lamp-bracket stays lit, and their place at the veranda table is set, and steam goes on rising from the plate.
