# Those Who Live in the Ward

## The Keepers

There are perhaps sixty people, though no one has ever gotten the same count twice, and they have made a kind of peace with that. They are thin, not from hunger but from a habit of economy that has settled into the body. They do not gesture widely or raise their voices. They walk as if the ground might be listening, which it may be. Their clothes are layers of grey and undyed wool, with waxed cloth over the top, cut long against the damp, and their sleeves are cuffed with a little folded pocket for the cords.

The cords are the first thing you notice. Every resident wears a hank of knotted string at the belt, and each knot is a measurement: the steps from one door to another, the count of a stair, the length of a lane on a given morning. They know the numbers won't hold. They tie the knots anyway, and they read them the way other people read the sky. A cord that has stopped agreeing with itself tells you something about the neighborhood. A child is given a first cord at about seven, with three knots in it, and told: *these are true today.*

They speak quietly and rarely finish sentences aloud, since the sentence is usually understood by the midpoint. Their humor is dry and understated. A greeting might be "Is your shadow with you?" and the answer, "Mostly. A little behind." They pass on ordinary news with the same gravity as errata. *The east stair is a spiral again. The Hallow's cup broke before she dropped it, twice.* Nothing is a complaint. They speak of the errata as farmers speak of frost.

They have no rulers and no priests. What they have are roles, which they slip between without ceremony:

**The Tenders** look after the pale trees. They kneel at the glass-lipped wells with small brushes and pipettes of cold water, and they talk to the trees in a low, even murmur. They do this not out of sentiment but because the trees seem to hold their leaves better when spoken to about ordinary things: the weather, the count of the day, who has slept badly. A Tender might spend an entire morning reporting to a tree that the fog was in the bakery.

**The Cord-Walkers** go out at dawn and lay their cords along the lanes near the new edges. They come back with the day's discrepancies, sometimes pale with the effort of having walked a square that was ninety steps in and forty out, and having tried to hold both in mind.

**The Ledger-Keepers** sit in the old quarter, where the world holds still, and write. The ledger is not one book but many, and older volumes are cross-referenced to newer ones with thread markers in the margins. The Keepers work slowly and neutrally. They do not interpret. A typical entry: *Third day of thin fog. Cup heard breaking, 4 seconds early, courtyard of the Nine Wells. Rain upward in the Tallow Passage, five drops. Two herons in the Nine Wells and the Low Basin, matched. Door on the north wall of the Weavers' lane opened onto silt; closed again by evening.*

No one is certain what the ledger is for. Some Keepers believe a pattern will one day resolve and the errata will turn out to be a sentence being spelled. Others suspect that the writing itself is the point, that an error noticed and set down is an error the city can stop arguing about. They note that entries which are cross-referenced often stop recurring. They say this carefully and never claim it as proof. It might be coincidence. It might be that they are, in their small way, collaborators.

## What They Eat and How They Sleep

The walls provide. In certain recessed niches, which appear where a person has lingered with a hand near the surface, the pearl-grey substance produces a dense, faintly sweet cake, the color of ash and the texture of firm cheese. It tastes of mineral and cold water. Residents call it *offering*, though nobody knows to whom it is being offered, or by whom. They eat it with moss tea and sometimes with a paste of the grey-green lichen that is harvested sparingly from the shaded lower walls, where it is thinned out and never stripped bare.

They sleep in rooms that have grown for them, small ovoid chambers with the walls slightly curved inward, warm at the head and cool at the feet. The light-guides dim on their own toward evening, to the color of overcast dusk, and the fog drifts in to keep company. Most residents say they sleep well. Some say they occasionally wake in a different room than the one they lay down in, and that the other room is always a little more finished than the first.

## The Lagging

Some residents have been through the doors that open onto raw silt.

It is not forbidden. It is simply understood to be a matter of consequence. A person might go through out of curiosity, or grief, or a wish to see what the city looks like before it has finished deciding. They come back, usually within the hour, with damp grey silt dried on their boots and a look of having stood at a very long edge. They are not harmed. But they are, in a way no one can quite name, less *settled* than before. Their shadows hang back a beat behind them, permanently. They cast a fraction less certainly on walls. A cup they set down may take an instant longer to be entirely there.

The others treat the Lagging with the tenderness one gives to the recovering ill. They walk slightly slower beside them. They wait a moment before finishing a sentence. Some of the Lagging become the best Ledger-Keepers because they see the errata from a little to one side. A few, over years, grow back in. A very few do not, and move gradually toward the newest quarter, where the walls are still warm and smell of struck matches, and where being not-quite-finished is less conspicuous. No one stops them. It is understood as a kind of vocation.

## The Herons

The grey wading birds stand in the courtyard basins and do not feed. They are tall, longer in the leg than any heron ought to be, with knees that bend a little too many times. Their feathers are the color of the walls, and when they are motionless, which is nearly always, it is hard to say where the bird ends and the courtyard begins.

The residents do not think of them as animals in the usual sense. They call them the Standing, and they are treated with a respect closer to that given a witness than a pet. When a heron appears in two courtyards at once, in exactly the same posture, one talon lifted a finger's width above the water, the residents do not say there are two. They say *the heron is in the Nine Wells and the Low Basin*, and they leave a small cake of offering at each. When one of the doubled birds eventually resolves into one, everyone agrees it was the correct outcome, though no one can say which courtyard it chose.

It is said that the herons are the city's memory of what a living thing ought to look like, and that they are being drafted, carefully, toward some future accuracy. They have never been seen to die. They have never been seen to arrive.

## The Moths and the Trees

The pale moths that gather at the light-guides are small, dusty, and silent, with wings the color of the inside of a shell. They congregate in the evening, and in the hour before dark they form loose drifting constellations around the veins of light. Children sometimes try to read them. The patterns are not random, and the residents have noticed that the swarms tend to gather around walls that are about to resolve into something more elegant, a day or two before the change. *Watch the moths,* they say, when a lane is about to become a stair.

The pale trees, six or seven of them, are the only things in the city that ask for care and repay it in any visible way. They are thin-stemmed, translucent-leaved, and shaped like small open hands. Nobody remembers who planted them, or whether they were planted. In autumn, the leaves fall upward, and the Tenders stand beneath them with their faces turned up, letting the falling hands drift past, not catching any. The leaves land on the eaves and stay there through the winter, a pale, papery layer that slowly turns to a kind of glassy dust and is absorbed by the roofs. The Tenders say the city is eating the trees' leaves. They say it fondly. They think the trees may be how the city learns what it is to let go of something gracefully.

## Beneath and Behind

No resident has ever seen what tends the city. There is the pulse in the walls, once every several minutes, like a very large hand adjusting its grip. There is the fine ticking of moisture moving through the ribs. There is the far-off tone that sounds when a district finishes resolving, clear and single and without echo.

The residents don't call it a god, and they resist the word *machine.* Some call it the Mathematician, without irony, and speak of it as one speaks of a difficult colleague, someone brilliant and absorbed who does not quite know you are in the room but has been, all along, arranging the room for you. They say it is not unkind. They say it is very, very busy. They say the errata are not its mistakes but its margins, where it is working something out, and that living beside the margins is a kind of privilege, if an uncomfortable one.

Late in the evening, in the oldest quarter where the air holds still, the Keepers sometimes lay their hands flat on a wall and wait for the pulse. They do not pray. They simply keep it company, the way you might sit with someone thinking hard. When the pulse comes, they nod, and the wall beneath their palms is warm for a moment longer than the pulse itself.

And when the single tone rings across the basin, far off and unhurried, everyone in the Ward, wherever they are, whatever they were doing, pauses. The Tenders lift their heads from the trees. The Cord-Walkers stop mid-lane. The herons do not move, but the water at their feet shivers once. The moths rise and settle. And somewhere a street that was yesterday a little wrong is, for the first time, exactly right.

Then they go back to what they were doing, a little quieter, and someone, somewhere, ties a new knot.
