# Those Who Live in the Brine Gardens

Nothing here has a face, and yet the garden is not empty. It is inhabited the way a body is inhabited, by many kinds of tissue that have agreed to be one thing. If you ask who lives here, the truthful answer is a list, and then a correction to the list.

## The Mill-Weed

The mill-weed is the most numerous, and it holds itself like a congregation. Ten meters tall, wrist-thick at the base, each plant stands in its rank on its steel lattice, and each leans with the others into the day's long turn. They are young. Nothing in the basin is older than a few years, but the mill-weed carries itself as if it were ancient, because it has been *used* from the first day of its life, and use gives a body a kind of gravity.

A mill-weed does not seem to grow toward light. It grows toward being harvested. Its blades are bottle-green above and silver below, and when the current turns them over, the whole rank flashes from dark to bright like a shoal of something that isn't there. The white film gathers along the blade edges in a fine bloom, and every blade seems to know when it is ready. The pumps do not so much take the ribbon as receive it. A blade near its moment stiffens slightly, and its edge takes on a faint chalky glow.

The individuals differ, though you must look for it. The oldest plants, in the bays nearest the central spool-house, have thickened at the base into fluted columns, and their stems have swallowed the cast-iron eyelets that once merely guided them. Where you would expect a seam between metal and stem, there is only a rust-stained collar of bark-like tissue, warm to the touch, with the iron ring visible inside it like a coin inside a tree. These plants stand a little straighter than the rest. Their blades are broader. They give the most ribbon, and the younger plants around them lean a fraction toward them, as if they could tell.

## The Coin-Mats

Beneath the mill-weed, in the cool of its shadow, live the rust-colored mats, and they are the garden's patient ones. Each is a stack of flat plates, round as coins, overlapping like shingles. They do not sway. They do not lean. They simply sit in their trays and drink the poison the old pipes give up, and they fold it into themselves, layer by layer, until they are heavy with it.

Their dying is orderly and even tender. The outermost plates darken first, to violet and then to a soot-black. When the blackness reaches the center, the mat is cut out whole by the machines and carried away, and a fresh one is set in its place, small and bright as a new penny. There is no grief in this. But the neighboring mats seem to *spread* a little in the days after, as if to take up the space, and their rust color deepens toward the empty tray.

They are the ones who know the pipes best. If a joint is failing, the mats beneath it blacken early and in a ring, precisely under the leak, like a stain on a tablecloth. The machines read this. It may be the closest thing the garden has to speech.

## The Lantern-Vines

Along the pipes, and hanging in loops from the catwalks, the water-leaf vines make their translucent lives. Their leaves are pale bladders, clear as blown glass, each full of water slightly different from the water outside it. Light passes through them and comes out altered, so a vine in the afternoon throws small wandering lenses of color onto the terrazzo, jade and lemon and, rarely, a deep clean gold.

The vines are the garden's touch. Their tendrils cross every gap, and where two pipes run close they have knotted the space between them into a soft web of green cord. They trail over railings and take on the shape of a hand that has gripped there. If a person came, the vines would find them first. They would loop a wrist without harm and hold on a moment longer than expected, in the manner of something that has not yet learned that holding is optional.

## The Dome-Heads

On the valve housings, over the intake grates, sit the dome-headed growths. They are the garden's mouths, though they take in only what would otherwise choke the works: fibers, seed-dust, the pale drift of the water's own debris. Each is a bulb the size of a cask, glossy, faintly veined, ringed with a fringe of fine filaments that open and close in slow sequence, like breathing, or like a very deliberate blink.

Their timing is not quite synchronized with the turbines' hum, and that small disagreement is one of the few things in the garden that feels like personality. Each dome-head keeps its own rhythm, a shade ahead or behind the beat. You could learn to tell them apart by their lateness. There is one, in the third bay of the eastern terrace, that opens always a little after the others, and it seems, absurdly, to be thinking about it.

## The Crown at the Open Pipe

In one bay, a pipe ends in nothing. It was once, presumably, meant to join something that was never built, or that was removed. At its open mouth a full crown of leaves has grown, healthy, glossy, unlike any other plant in the basin: a rosette of round, cupped leaves, pale at the edges, dark at the heart. It has no tray, no substrate, no batch code. It lives on whatever the pipe gives it, and the pipe gives it enough.

Of all the inhabitants, this one comes closest to seeming *chosen*. The other plants are planted, but this one was permitted. The mills lean toward it slightly in the turn of the day. The vines have wound about its pipe without touching the crown itself. Nobody has cut it back. It may be the only thing in the garden that no one meant, and the garden appears to have taken this as a kind of sacred instruction.

## The Turbines

The turbines are the sleepers. Each housing the size of a cottage, green-bronze with old verdigris, turns at the pace of slow breath, and the hum in your sternum is theirs. They do not live in any sense you could prove. But the plants have made a home of them. Moss-fine filaments coat their casings. Roots are threaded into their seams. The warm water pouring off them supports gardens of its own, tiny colonies of that same flat coin-plant, clustering along the housings' shoulders as if warming their hands.

You could believe the turbines feel this. You could believe they slow, very slightly, in the places where the growth is thickest. It would be an easy thing to imagine, and the garden seems to have imagined it first.

## The Ones You Don't See

There are replanters. They move at night, or in the dim hour after the lamps come on, along tracks laid so low in the terrazzo that they are only faint seams. They carry seedlings in racks and drop them into the blackened places with a precision that suggests memory. They do not seem to have been designed to be seen, and no one has seen them. What is seen is the result: an empty tray at dusk, a small bright mat at dawn.

Whoever wrote *Grow, and be drawn out* on the bay plates is also here, in the way a hand is present in a worn railing. The grooves in the oldest catwalks are the record of a body that steadied itself in the same way, again and again, and then stopped. The plants have grown into the grooves. The vines fill them now.

## What Is Actually Living Here

Taken together, they make something that no single part could be. The veining of the garden, the great branching leaf-pattern visible from the rim, is not a plan the inhabitants follow. It is what they *are*. The pipes are the veins, the mill-weed the blade-tissue, the coin-mats the filters in the root, the dome-heads the pores, the turbines the slow heart, and the spools at the center the seed, or the fruit, or the message. The whole basin is one broad leaf laid flat in a cup of warm water and held up to the sun.

Sometimes, at the top of the turn, the current slackens and every blade stands upright. The dome-heads close together for once. The vines go still. The turbines drop a note. In that moment the inhabitants stop being a list. They become a single attention, turned upward, toward the glass and the sky and the cable vanishing into cloud, toward whatever is winding in the spools.

Then the pumps catch, and the leaning begins, and they are many again.
