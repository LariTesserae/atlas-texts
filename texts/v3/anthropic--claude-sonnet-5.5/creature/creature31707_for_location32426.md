# Who Lives in the Struck Basin

Start with the smallest, because everything else stands on them.

## The Ones Who Eat Stone

The first residents are the microbes, and there are two nations of them. On the walls, the orange, cream, and ash-gray mats live on seep water and the slow chemistry of fresh rock. They are cold-loving and patient. Each tongue of mat advances a few centimeters a season, and each is a dense city of cells layered like pastry, with the living film on top and a graveyard of mineralized ancestors underneath.

The second nation lives on the chimneys and eats the vents. Their food is the fluid that comes up hot and reeking, and they turn sulfur and iron into sugar the way leaves turn light into sugar. They are the reason there is anything here to graze. They form the pale fur on the flanks, the slick on the terraces, and the bacterial film on every falling flake of pyrite. They have no idea of the flaps, but their density tracks the pulse. Where the water is pushed out in bursts, the film is thickest in a band just downstream of each burst, where fresh fluid arrives every few seconds and never quite stops.

Nothing here photosynthesizes. Sunlight pours through eighty meters of clear water and lands on the terraces as pure decoration.

## The Ratchet Worms

The worms are the basin's nerve endings. Each is a pale tube as long as a finger, cemented to the chimney by its own secretion, with a red plume that opens like a small flame. They are not individuals in any way they would recognize. A colony is a slope of ten thousand tubes, and it behaves as one skin.

They retract a fraction of a second behind each flap. This is not fear. The burst that follows a flap is scalding, and a worm that stays open takes a plume of near-boiling water to the face. So the whole colony has learned the machine, and the machine has quietly learned them back. Chimneys with thick worm fur fire slightly more slowly, because the tube mass insulates the crown and changes how pressure builds. In a small way, the worms tune the rhythm they flinch from.

A young worm settles where the beat is right. It drifts down as a larva, sensing the hammering through its whole body, and cements itself on the first surface that feels like *enough*. The ones that misjudge and settle too close to a fast flap are cooked. The ones that settle too far go hungry. A living colony is a record of successful guesses.

## The Lantern Shoals

The fish are the basin's weather. They come in shoals so large that their shadow moves across the floor like a cloud, and when they turn together the flash runs through the whole body of the school in a fraction of a second. Each fish is only a hand long, silver, with iron-dusted gill covers, and each moves by watching its neighbors and by listening. The field's hammering is their compass. A lantern fish that strays beyond the sound goes uneasy, its swimming loosens, and it turns back toward the noise like a moth to a lamp.

They feed on the falling snow, mouthing pyrite flakes as they sink to strip the film. They swallow some of the metal. It settles in their gills and scales, and it is why they glint and why they are heavy. A dead lantern fish sinks fast and lands with a small puff of glitter, and within a season it is a crust on the terrace, part of the plate.

They do not sleep, exactly. At the darkest hour of the surface's night, the shoals go still in the mid-water, hanging like a held breath, and only the beat of the field moves them: a slight sway with each deep thock.

## The Bell-Grazers

Above the chimney crowns, in the warm shimmer, the bell-grazers drift. They are translucent discs the size of dinner plates, with soft bodies you can see through to the pulse of their own slow pumping. Inside each is a cluster of small hard grains, like the parts of a watch, which they rotate to tune their weight. They tilt into a warm plume, rise on it, tip out into cool water, sink, and tilt in again.

They seem to have no interests. They do not hunt anything that can be recognized as hunting; they take warmth and whatever the warmth carries. But they are the basin's height gauge. Where they hang in a layer, that is where the water is exactly the temperature that layer wants, and the layer moves as the field moves. When the hammering shifts and a plume fails, the bell-grazers above it lower themselves, slowly, like a mobile losing a string.

## The Drum-Crabs

The crabs stand on the warm terraces, heavy and flat, each with one oversized claw held cocked. At intervals they strike the mineral plate in front of them: a hard knock, a pause, another. They might be marking territory. They might be calling a mate, or knocking loose flakes to eat. No one is here to ask. What is certain is that the taps fit inside the great rhythm, never on the beat but always between beats, so that the crab population reads as a second, smaller machine running against the first.

A crab's claw is its whole life. It grows in one direction, and a crab that loses it will regrow a smaller one and be a lesser drummer for the rest of its existence. The big-clawed males hold the warmest ledges. The females and the young live on cooler shelves, and drum softly, and are answered.

## The Long Ones

They are the rarest and the hardest to describe honestly, because they are seen only at the edge of the eye. Slender, eel-shaped, four meters or more, they move through the chimney forest in loose spirals, brushing their flanks along the warm stone as if to be counted by it. Their bodies are ringed with faint bands of blue light that pulse in slow patterns, not in time with the field but in slow counterpoint to it.

They seem to be doing something. They pass along the rows, and where they pass, the ratchet worms flinch in a line, tracing their path. Whether the long ones cause the flinch by touch, by pressure wave, or by the blue light itself is unknown. What is clear is that they are the only animals in the basin that seem to cover the whole field, moving from the hot southern rows to the cold edge of the terraces and back, over weeks. They may be feeding on worms, or on the crabs, or on the fish. They may be grazing the mats. They may simply be the ones who travel, carrying something between the distant colonies that the pulse alone cannot: eggs, spores, a slight shift of tempo.

They are also the ones that hear the whole basin at once. If any animal here holds the field in its head, it is one of these.

## The Turnover

Twice in a year, or in some years once, the surface chills and the basin turns over. The cold water sinks down the walls and the sediment lifts in a glittering haze. Visibility drops from six hundred meters to twenty. During those weeks, the shoals climb, the crabs go still, and the worms retract half their length. The pulse continues, muffled, like a heart heard through a wall. When the water clears, everything resettles a little differently, and the crowd rearranges itself into new bands.

The young are born into this. Most hatch or bud in the turnover, in the confusion, and are swept downward as the haze falls. The ones who reach the terraces find a field that has changed since their parents settled. Some chimneys have cracked. Others have grown new flaps. The rhythm greets them in a new phrase, and they must learn it from nothing.

## What Lives Here, Then

Not the animals alone. Anything that lives in the basin lives in the pulse, and the pulse is made of all of them: the chimney that fires, the worm that flinches, the crab that answers, the fish that turns toward the sound and the bell that rises and falls with the heat. Remove any of them and the rest change their tempo. The basin has no ruler, no center, and no founder, only a very large number of small guesses that keep coming out right.

It has been alive for a geological moment. In ten years, the beat will be a different beat, and the animals will have followed it there, as they follow it now, without having decided to.
