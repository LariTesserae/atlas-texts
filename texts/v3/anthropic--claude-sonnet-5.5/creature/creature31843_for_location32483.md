# Who Lives in the Roofed Valley

Almost no one who lives here is an individual, and this is the first thing to understand. The valley is not full of many creatures. It is full of a few hundred large beings, each made of thousands or millions of small bodies, and around them a looser population of ordinary animals who have learned to live inside their architecture.

## The tower-bodies

The great mounds are each one animal. It helps to think of them that way, because the alternative is to think of them as buildings, and buildings do not breathe.

A tower has lungs, which are the vents and chimneys that pull warm air up through the core and let it settle back down through the outer channels, exchanging gas through walls as thin as paper. It has a gut, which is the fungus gardens, gray-brown sponges of chewed wood folded into chambers the size of cupped hands. The termites do not digest the wood themselves. They feed it to the fungus, and the fungus feeds them. It has a circulation, which is the traffic in the galleries, workers going out in the dark under their covered roads and coming back with cargo in their jaws. And it has a heart of a kind, deep in the base, in a hard-walled chamber the color of old bone.

The queen lives there. She is the size of a man's thumb, or in the oldest colonies the length of a hand, a pale swollen sac of a body with a small dark head and legs that no longer carry her. She lays an egg every few seconds, for years. Attendants stroke and groom her and carry the eggs away. They lick her skin and take her chemical messages out into the tower, and the tower reads them the way a body reads a hormone. The king lies beside her, small, dark, and faithful. They may have been together for twenty years. When the queen dies, or fails, the colony does not immediately understand. It goes on building for a while out of momentum. Then a smell changes, and the towers begin to sleep.

This is not the end of the tower. Another colony moves into the husk, or the old walls stand empty for a generation while lizards, mice, and snakes move in, and then a new colony starts to build on top of the ruin. The mounds in the valley's oldest district are the accumulated work of a dozen successive queens, each unaware of the last.

The towers do not think, but they respond. When a pangolin breaches a wall, soldiers appear within seconds, huge-headed, pale, their jaws crossed like scissors. Behind them come the workers with mouthfuls of wet carton, and by morning the wound is plastered over, darker and smoother than the wall around it. The mound will carry that scar for a hundred years. Walk the valley and you will see the scars: whorled patches, healed gouges, an old bullet-shaped blemish where lightning struck. The towers remember what has happened to them, and they keep the record in their bodies.

## The roof-weavers

Above the towers lives a different kind of being, more diffuse and more restless. The social spiders are small, brown, and mottled, no bigger than a fingernail, and a single colony may number in the tens of thousands. They don't hunt alone. When something lands in the sheeting, a moth or a katydid or a fledgling bird, the threads carry the shudder outward through the fabric, and hundreds of spiders converge from every direction. They bite together, they drag together, and they feed together, packed shoulder to shoulder on a single body until nothing is left.

The web is their shared skin and their shared nervous system. A tremor in one corner tells the whole colony where to look. A tear is repaired by whoever happens to be nearest. There is no boss, no queen with a throne. The web knows itself through its vibrations, and the spiders are the way it moves.

They are gentle with their own. Mothers regurgitate food for the young of other mothers. Females who die leave their bodies to be eaten by their daughters, and their daughters go on weaving. The colony is a long inheritance of silk and appetite, and it has lasted for far longer than any spider in it.

Over generations, the colonies have grown together, and now the roof is less a collection of nests than a single continuous territory, meters thick, stitched across a kilometer of valley. Whether the roof is one animal or a thousand neighbors is a question that has no answer. The spiders do not seem to need one.

## The tenants of the silk

The roof is also home to the people who rent from the spiders.

Tiny kleptoparasitic spiders skulk along the edges of the sheeting, stealing scraps from the main colony's kills. They are so small and so still that the colony rarely notices them. Mites ride the legs of larger beetles, and pseudoscorpions ride the mites. Certain wasps lay their eggs in the silk. Moth caterpillars feed on the debris, spinning cocoons in the felt of old leaves. A community of beetles lives entirely in the roof's rotting layers, a place no ground has ever touched. Some of them will spend their whole lives, and their grandparents' whole lives, without once descending to the floor.

## The weaver-birds

Below the sheeting hang the flasks, and the flasks belong to the weavers.

They are small yellow-and-black birds with pale eyes, and they build. The males do the weaving, stripping long blades of grass and knotting them with their beaks and feet into sacks with a downward-pointing entrance tube. A good nest takes days. A bad one is torn down by the female, who inspects it, tugs it, and decides. The male will begin again. In the colonies of the roofed valley, hundreds of males hang in a single tree, each beside his own unfinished basket, wings shivering, calling to every female who passes.

They are the valley's gossip. Nothing moves through the canopy without the weavers knowing, and nothing that they know stays quiet. A snake approaching a nest sets off a specific alarm, a fast dry chatter, and every bird within a hundred meters hears it and passes it on. A different call means a hawk overhead. Another means a monkey. The monkeys, the bats, the geckos, and even the spiders have learned to listen to the weavers' chatter. When it stops abruptly, everyone in the valley goes still.

The birds do not know they are the valley's news system. They think only of grass, knots, mates, and hunger. But the whole community reads them all the same.

## The walkers

Between the towers, in the alleys of carton, live those who move.

**The pangolins.** They are ancient and solitary and armored in overlapping scales the color of wet bark. They tear open the towers with claws as long as a man's fingers, sweep up termites on a tongue longer than their bodies, and retreat into the shadows to digest. When threatened, they curl into a ball, a tight, layered armor that even a leopard hesitates over. They walk on their hind legs sometimes, with the front claws tucked up, in a slow, dreamlike, rocking gait. They are easy to mistake for something wearing a costume, and in the dark of the valley they seem less an animal than a rumor.

**The monkeys.** They live in the vine-roads in families of a dozen or so, small and quick and endlessly curious. They know the valley as a set of routes: this vine to that fig, this fig to the resin-tree, the resin-tree to the wasp-paper globes. They pick at everything. They scrape the amber from the trunks and chew it, and their faces are sticky with it. Young ones ride on their mothers' backs with wide eyes, learning the map. The monkeys are the valley's messengers. When they move through, the weavers scold, the frogs go quiet, and the news of their coming travels ahead of them in a wave.

**The small cats and civets.** They are careful, slender, and patient. They do not rule the valley. They live in it the way you might live in a crowded neighborhood, aware that every wall has ears. A leopard-sized cat that steps wrong will disturb a sleeping snake, shake a nest of weavers, or break a gallery, and every one of those mistakes is remembered. So they move slowly, and they hunt rarely, and they take what the valley is willing to give.

## The frogs, the bats, and the night

At dusk, a second population wakes.

The frogs come out of the rosettes and the crevices, thousands of them, in a dozen colors: the pale green tree-frogs on the ledges, the mottled brown ones in the mud, the tiny orange ones no bigger than a thumbnail that sing from the leaf-tips in voices like struck glass. Every species has a note, and every note has a place in the chorus. Listen long enough and you can hear that the frogs are not competing. They are leaving each other gaps, as instruments do. Over many generations they have arranged themselves into a score.

The bats leave their roosts under the silk in dark streams that pour through the gaps in the roof and out into the ridge-air. Some feed on insects, some on fruit. They come back before dawn, dropping through the holes like handfuls of thrown seed, and cling upside-down to the felt, licking their wings clean.

## The listener

And there is one more resident, though it isn't clear it counts as one.

The valley, taken as a whole, behaves like something that pays attention. The weavers report to the monkeys, the monkeys to the frogs, the frogs to the bats, and the spiders feel it all in the silk. The termite towers absorb the news through vibrations in the ground. When a leopard moves, the hush travels outward in a ring, and within seconds every being in the valley has adjusted itself to the leopard's presence. When the leopard leaves, the noise returns. Nothing directed this. Nothing planned it. But it happens the same way every time, with such reliability that one begins to suspect that the valley has a kind of sense of itself, a mind without a center, distributed across ten million bodies and a thousand years of habit.

If it has a voice, it is the roar at dusk. If it has a face, no one has seen it. If it has a memory, it is written in the scars of the towers and the layered silk of the roof. And if you stand long enough in the dark between two mounds, with things crawling on your skin and the whole world talking at once, you may feel it notice you. It will not be alarmed. It will register your weight, your warmth, and your unfamiliar smell. Then it will make room, and go on.
