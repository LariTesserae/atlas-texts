# Who Keeps the Shelf

Most of what lives on the Slow Shelf lives quietly, and the quiet is not an accident. It is the local manners, kept by every creature that has stayed long enough to learn them.

## The Keepers

The people are few and never gather in large numbers. They call themselves nothing in particular. When pressed by outsiders, they say *the ones who walk the water*, or simply *those who are up early*. There are perhaps a few hundred across the whole shelf, in small parties of four to nine, moving between the pools by a circuit that takes most of a year. There are no villages. There are the low fitted-stone walls, and the people use them as windbreaks without claiming them, the way you might sit in a stranger's shade.

They are lean, long in the shin, and most are squint-lined by their twenties. They wear undyed cloth in the color of the gravel and a broad hood with a stiff brim. Around the eyes they rub a paste of charred cane and fat, a dark band against the glare that comes up off the rock. From a distance a party of them, moving in file along a channel, is easily mistaken for a herd of drifters. This is partly deliberate. They learned to walk without scuffing from the drifters, placing the whole foot at once, and the shale-cats seem to have made the same mistake often enough that the people have never been troubled by them.

They do speak, but sparingly and low, and mostly at night beside the water, where the channel's thread of sound covers their voices. By day they talk with their hands, a shoulder tilt, a lifted chin, and a slow open-palm gesture meaning *wait, something is changing*. A child learns this before words. A party can cross a valley in the morning without one spoken syllable, changing direction all at once because one person's weight shifted, and the others felt it in the ground.

### What they do

The Keepers tend the channels. This is the center of their lives, and it is less work than it sounds and more devotion. The channels were cut before memory and need almost nothing. But moss crowds the lips, crust builds in the joints, and a wind-driven drift of gravel can fill a bend in a season. So the Keepers walk the lines with flat bone scrapers and soft brushes of cane fiber and clear what needs clearing. They never widen or deepen or straighten. There is a saying: *Clear it to how it was, not to how you'd like it.* Newcomers who try to improve a channel are not scolded. They are simply not given the scraper again.

They do not touch the combs' edges. They touch the cups, resting a palm on the smooth stone where the water gathers, and some of them do this every morning like a greeting. They say the combs are warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. A Keeper who has walked the same comb for thirty years will tell you which ones lean earliest toward the far veils, which ones sweat heaviest in the cold months, and which one "has been slow since the year of the long wind." They speak of the combs the way one speaks of elderly relatives who have never once asked for anything.

They keep count. Each traveler wears a cord at the wrist, and for every day's water drawn from a pool, they tie a small knot. When the cord is full, they leave it coiled in the stone lip of the pool where the water was taken, a kind of receipt, though no one collects it. Nobody can say whether the plateau reads it. They do it anyway. The old stories say that people who kept no cord were not punished so much as *misjudged*, and the shelf's arithmetic drifted away from theirs, until they were always a little thirsty and never sure why.

### How they live beside the strange things

The Keepers have no religion of the Level, the Arch, or the Well of Rising. They regard them as neighbors of a very different kind, ones who are polite and unhurried and whose business is their own. Children are taught to greet the Level by dipping two fingers and touching them to the forehead. The frogs in it are considered guests of the bowl and are never eaten, though frogs elsewhere are. No one crosses the Arch at Ninefold on the day of the eleven-day boulder's anniversary, which they mark by walking around the long way, not from fear but from a sense that the Arch is *thinking* on that day.

At the Well of Rising, they lower their vessels and wait. They say thank you aloud, softly. They say that once, long ago, a herder forgot, and his vessel came back full, but the water tasted of nothing, not even water, and it never satisfied any thirst. He was said to have gone back and apologized, and the next draw was sweet.

They do not ask who made these things. They have a phrase for the question: *asking the wall who built the wall.* It means a question so large that it is rude in its wrongness. What they say instead is that the makers were *careful people who left*, or perhaps *who are still leaving*, and that the combs go on leaning toward wherever they went.

### Death and the south faces

When a Keeper dies, the party carries the body to the nearest mesa's south face, where the lichens grow in their slow orange and slate maps, and lays it in a shallow hollow in the stone, wrapped in gravel-colored cloth. They do not mark the place. Over years the lichen takes the cloth, then the shape, until the hollow is one more patch of color among the others. Kin speak of visiting the dead by "reading the map," and they can look at a rock face and tell you which patch of rust-orange was an aunt, and how large she has grown.

## The Drifters and Those Who Follow Them

The drifters are the true citizens of the shelf, in that they have never once needed to be taught its rules.

A herd of thirty or forty moves as a single slow argument, and no one is the leader. A pale flush runs through the coats when a shale-cat is sensed, a different flush when a hawk's shadow falls wrong, a third when the water-hour approaches and they begin to drift toward the pools. Older drifters, gray at the muzzle, sometimes stand a little apart at the edge of the herd with ears turned outward. They are not sentries exactly. They are the ones whose attention the others have learned to trust, and when the old ones flush, the herd changes direction before any single animal has decided to.

Drifters tolerate the Keepers, and occasionally a young one will walk alongside a party for a day, matching their pace, then peel away without ceremony. Keepers consider this a small honor and say nothing about it until evening.

They drink at the pools only at dawn, when the combs have just run, and always downstream of where a Keeper has drawn. They seem to understand the ledger.

## The Hunters, and the Ones Who Come After

The shale-cat is not an enemy of anything but drifters, and even that is closer to a shared agreement than a war. It lies on the warm stone, its flanks slowly rising and falling in time with the cooling rock, and it may wait days for a straggler. It kills without a sound. It then lies over the body for a long time, not eating at first, only holding, as if the animal deserved a moment. The Keepers have seen this often and do not speak of it lightly.

Then come the sun-hawks, their membrane-edged wings turning the air around them to glare, and the tiny dry click of their calls, one to another, *here, here*. Then the small scavengers, a dozen kinds, brown and quick and mild-eyed. Then the ants in their long dark ropes. By evening there is a pale patch, and by the next dawn the patch has a faint green fuzz, and by the next season a cushion plant sits there, a small green fist that will outlast three generations of drifters.

The whole shelf is a slow recycling in which nothing is wasted and nothing is hurried.

## The Threadwings

The threadwings are the closest thing the shelf has to a chorus. Thousands to a pool, iridescent, humming at the threshold of hearing, they rise and fall in banks that shift like breath. They do not seem to fear anything, not Keepers, not hawks, not each other. Some Keepers believe they are the combs' children, that they are born from the water-beads themselves, and that at dusk, when the great hum lifts and the air seems to thicken and clear, they are carrying something up to the cooling air and back down again.

Nobody has ever seen a dead one.

## What Lives Under and Behind

There is one more kind of inhabitant, and the Keepers speak of it least, because they know least. Below the Well of Rising, something adjusts. Above the Arch, for eleven days, something held a boulder and then decided to let it go. Inside the Level, something has kept one hand's depth of water flat through every lever and every tilt. These are not creatures, exactly, or gods. The Keepers call them, when they must, *the attentions*.

The attentions do not appear. They have never spoken. They do not answer to prayer, gift, or complaint. But every Keeper has, at some point, felt one turn toward them. It is a small pressure, like the sense of being read by someone who reads very slowly and does not mind waiting. It usually happens at dusk, when the rock ticks as it cools, and the first beads gather on the highest combs.

What the attentions want, if anything, is unknown. The Keepers have a working guess, passed from one generation to the next: that the attentions are what remains of the makers' care, still tending, still counting, still leaning toward the far veils of rain that never quite arrive. And that the Keepers, the drifters, the hawks, the shale-cats, and the threadwings are all, in their way, part of the count.

At the end of each day, the Keepers sit beside the channel with their knotted cords and their scrapers set down, and they listen to the thread of water moving through stone. They do not pray. They only stay quiet, and let themselves be counted.
