# The Tenants of Ennis Verre

You will not see them at first. You will see that the moss on a certain boulder is pressed flat in a long hollow, the size and shape of someone lying on their side, and that the hollow is still faintly warm. You will see a cushion-orchard with one bite-shaped scoop taken from its flank, too neat for a grazer. You will see that a row of ingots on a slope has been wiped clean of its lantern moss along the top edge, where a palm would rest.

Then you will see one, standing among the cedars, and realize it has been there since you arrived.

## The Lay

They call themselves something that, as near as a whisper can be written, is *the Lay*: those who lie down. It is not a boast or a lament. It is simply what they do, and what they are for.

They are a small people, a little shorter than you, broad through the shoulders and low in the hip, built to carry weight over soft ground. Their feet are wide and pale and nearly boneless-looking, and they walk with the same hushed, spreading step as the grazers. Their skin is the gray-green of wet stone. Across the shoulders and down the spine, lantern moss has taken root in a fine, deliberate pelt, and it glows. A group of them walking at a distance is a scatter of dim blue-green saddles moving over the dark, and it is easy to take them for a small herd of something else.

Their eyes are large, black, and wet, with no white at all. They have learned to live in almost no light, and they flinch from any sudden brightness. When they must cross open ground near the cedar stands, they walk with one hand loosely raised near the face, ready. It is the most common gesture among them. It looks like a greeting, and it is really a guard against the stalker's lantern.

Their hands are broad and cold and clever, but they use them lightly. What they do with them is mostly touch.

## What They Do Not Do

The first thing a visitor notices is what is missing. There are no dwellings. No fires, no smoke, no tools set down and forgotten. There is nothing that would still be there if they left.

The Lay do not build, and this is not poverty or forgetting. It is a discipline older than their memory. The plateau makes iron and stacks it in rows, and they hold that anything made on purpose, in a place that already makes everything without purpose, would be a kind of noise. They do not speak of it as a law. They speak of it the way you might speak of not shouting in a sleeping house.

So they live the way the herds do. They sleep in the bellows-fern basins, curled in the moss in the hollow's slow breathing, and after a night there their own breath falls into the rhythm of the ferns. They shelter against the warm bark of the copper-cedars, hands flat on the wood. They cook by laying flat stones on the furnace-mats until the stones take the mat's warmth, and they set cushion-orchard flesh and threadworms on them to soften. It is never hot, only warm, and they eat everything cold-warm and faintly mineral. They drink from the beads on the leaves, tipping each fern frond to their mouths, taking one drop at a time and never stripping a plant.

They wear the fleece of the pale grazers, but only what the herds shed against the ironstone. It is heavy, gray, and threaded with glowing lichen, and they wear it draped and unstitched, held at the throat by a single sliver of ingot pressed through the wool. The sliver is the closest thing they have to jewelry, and it is never made. It is only found: a flake that has broken from the end of a windrow and been left for anyone to take.

## Speech

They whisper, and beyond that they click. Their talk is a low, wet, tongue-and-palate language, and half of it seems borrowed from the vent-wrens, which the Lay regard as a kind of cousin, or perhaps as tutors. A conversation between two of them is mostly breath, with a small clicking punctuation like a far-off tapping on bark. It carries almost nowhere. They talk with their heads close together, and they have no word for shouting, only a phrase that means *to throw your voice away*.

They listen far better than they speak. Every one of them can tell, from the bell-note of a distant *clang*, roughly which slope an ingot has settled on, how heavy it is, and how old the line is. They keep no records, but they keep a map of the plateau's sounds, and an old woman lying in a basin with her eyes closed is often following the news: *the eastern rank has grown by one; the long line under the saddle has shifted; the mats in the low place are moving on.*

## The Circuit

They are not settled, but they are not wanderers either. They follow the herds around the plateau over the long season, keeping a little behind and a little to the side, as one keeps company with a larger, slower animal. They pass through the same basins, the same cedar stands, the same ranks of ingots, year after year, and each place has its own name. The names are all descriptions of how it feels to lie down there.

They do not herd or cull. They lick the ingots beside the grazers, for the salt, in the same slow unashamed way, and sometimes a Lay and a grazer stand cheek by cheek at the same gray bar. They take from the cushion-orchards only what the plant can spare, and they press the wound closed with moss afterward.

## The Stalkers

They live among the hollow-stalkers as one lives near a river that sometimes floods. The stalkers take some of them, as they take some of the herds. The Lay have no vengeance in them for this and no real horror. When one is taken, the others close their eyes against the returning dark, put their hands over their faces, and stay very still for a long while. It is the same stillness the stalkers keep. Then they go on.

They will tell you, quietly, that the stalkers were once like them. They do not say it as a story but as an observation. They will point to a stalker in the cedars, so still that moss has climbed to its knees, and say only: *that one stood too long.*

Whether that is true, no one can say. But the Lay are careful about how long they stand. They keep moving, a little, always, and they teach their children to shift their weight every few breaths, so that nothing takes root.

## Lying Down

Their whole life bends toward one act, and they are not afraid of it.

When a Lay grows old, or tired, or simply ready, they walk alone to a low basin where the furnace-mats are warm and working, and they lie down on the mat. They wrap themselves in fleece and pin it at the throat with a sliver of ingot. They close their eyes. The mat warms under them, a few degrees above the air, and the hum in the rock rises through their back. The ferns breathe over them.

The beetles come slowly. They take a long time, years, and no one watches. What the Lay leave behind goes through the mat, then the beetles, then the pellets, then the windrows, and at last it lies in the moss as a bar of dull gray iron, cool to the touch, in a rank with the others of their line.

That is where the living go to visit. A child is led, hand in hand, to the long low rank of her grandmothers, and she is taught to lay her palm flat on the cold iron and hold it there until it is no longer cold. Then she lifts her hand and says nothing, because nothing needs saying. Grazers lick the same bars, and the Lay find this fitting.

## The Ribcage

At the center of the plateau, the great iron arch stands half sunk in the felt, pitted, beaded, and glowing faintly with lantern moss. The Lay go there rarely and always in silence.

They do not claim it. They do not know whose it was. They say it was the first one who lay down, long before the mats, the beetles, or the ferns, and that the plateau grew up around the work of unmaking that body. They say the hum in the rock is the sound of that unmaking still going on, unhurried, into everything.

They do not think this is sad. They think of it as the shape of things. Everything here is in the middle of becoming something else, slowly, in the dark, without anyone's help. They are only the ones who know it, and who, when their time comes, agree to it.

When they pass under the great arch they don't touch it. They lower their raised hands from their faces, expose their eyes to the dark, and walk through with their heads bare. It is the only place on the plateau where they do this.
