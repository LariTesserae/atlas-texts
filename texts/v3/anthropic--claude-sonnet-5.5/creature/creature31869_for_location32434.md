# Those Who Answer

You will not see them at first. You will see a cairn of clear resin glowing at the edge of a path, and a rope ladder swaying with no one on it, and a drum-trunk whose door is open. Then a hand closes on your forearm, dry and warm and very light, and you understand you have been observed for some time.

They call themselves something that cannot be spoken. It is a pattern: two taps against the breastbone, a pause, a long press of the palm flat over the heart. The nearest sound is *the Answering*, and that will do. It means the ones who reply. In their understanding the Floor is always speaking, and everything alive in it is either answering or dying.

## Bodies

They are small, the tallest barely to your chest, and built for heat and crowding. Their frames are narrow and ropy, with long fingers, and the pads of the fingers and the flat of the palm are thick, ridged, and extraordinarily sensitive. The skin is dark amber to reddish brown and carries a sheen of resin-oil, applied daily and worked into every crease. It shields them from the dry heat of the mat and from the spore-dust, and the spines and stinging hairs of the weave slide off it. Old people are lacquered nearly black and shine like fruit stones. They are mostly hairless. Hair catches in the vines and gums up with sap, and what little they grow is cropped short or braided tight with cord.

Their eyes are large, dark, and protected by a clear second lid that flicks across when yellow dust rises. Their noses are narrow and shut at will. Their ears are the strange part. They are small and pressed flat, and each canal is packed with a plug of soft, pliable resin, replaced weekly. The plugs do not shut out the din, which cannot be shut out. They cut it to a level where the mind can operate. Behind the plugs the hearing has withered. What has grown instead is everything else. They hear through the jaw, the sternum, the long bones of the leg, and the soles of the feet, and they hear with more nuance than you can imagine, because they are hearing with their whole bodies.

Their ribs are wide-set and springy, and the chest cavity is a little hollowed, a resonating box. When they hum, the hum has a place to live. Two of them pressed together, chest to chest or palm to ribs, can pass a whole thought in the buzz alone.

They drink from the pods. In the mid-layer grows a fat, bruise-colored fruit that distills the humid rot into a thin, sour, faintly oily fluid. They tap it with a bone straw and drink it slowly. A person can live on it, with the sweet pulp of another fruit, the fat of sap-swimmers roasted in their own canals, and a grey flour pounded from dried spores. They are lean, quick, and perpetually thirsty, and thirst is the first thing they teach children to manage. Their word for a full pod is the same as their word for gratitude.

## Speech

Voices are useless here, so they have none, or they keep them only for the rare occasions when they climb out to the rim.

The language is in four layers, and a competent speaker uses all four at once.

The first is **touch**: pressure, tap, drag, and grip on the arm, back, neck, and hand. It is a fast, precise, alphabet-like code, and two people in the thick of the weave can hold a conversation at a walking pace, never breaking stride, a hand at the other's elbow.

The second is **cord**. Every person carries a coil of knotted vine-line, and lines are strung along paths and between trunks. A tug sent down a line travels a long way, and the knots, which the fingers read, hold what would be written elsewhere: debts, routes, the names of dead grandparents, the count of a grove's fruit. A family's cord archive is the most valuable thing it owns. Cords are stored in a hollow trunk and re-tied every generation, because the fibers rot. A story that is not retied for three generations is lost, and they accept this with the same calm they accept the falling of walls.

The third is **the hum**, pressed into a partner's ribs. It carries tone, mood, urgency, love. It is the language of the sick and the newborn, of lovers, of deathbeds. It cannot lie. This is the thing they say most often about it, and it may be true, since the hum passes through the chest and the chest gives the speaker away.

The fourth is **the post**. Speaking posts stand at every crossing, and a trained striker can send a message across the basin in the space of a long breath, a pattern of blows on hollow logs that other posts pick up and hand along, so that news moves faster than a running person. The posts are answered, sometimes, by things that are not people, and the strikers listen carefully to see who else has joined in.

## Work

Everything they do is a conversation with the plants, and the trade that matters most is **tending**, which is not the same as gardening.

A tender does not plant. A tender *calls*. To bring a stand of fruiting vine to a chosen hillside, the tender must bring sound there: rattles of dried pod on a stick, drum-logs struck in rhythm, sling-cages of live beetles carried up and hung in the boughs and fed sap to keep them singing. The vine bends toward it. The mat swells under it. In a season there is a grove where there was scrub, and it will hold only as long as the sound is kept up. The tenders live inside their own gardens, sleeping in the middle of the noise they have made, moving their choirs from place to place like herders.

The best tenders can *tune*. They stand in a stand of drum-trunks and lay their palms flat on two of them, and they know at once which pitch will draw which plant. They can hum a grove into a dome or a corridor. They can make a thicket lean aside to open a path, and close it again behind. Some have made whole shelters this way, a bower of green wall and ceiling that stands a season and is left to be sung down when the family moves on. The very old ones, who no longer climb, sit at the centers of their groves with their fingers on the trunk, and the grove around them takes their shape, so that the oldest gardens look strangely like the people who made them.

There are also the **resin-cutters**, who work the trunks with hooked bone blades and catch the spitting sap in bladders before it sets. Good resin is currency, medicine, fuel, glue, window, light. Clear lump-cairns mark the paths, and their glow is worth a good deal in a place where a wrong turn can lead you into a gap in the mat. There are the **sap-swimmer hunters**, who lie along a trunk with an ear-bone pressed to it and follow the ticking inside, and strike with a spike at the moment the creature turns. There are **wall-readers**, the most respected and least numerous, who walk the scarps in bare feet and know by the tremor in the stone when a slab is about to fall, and mark the danger with red cord. And there are the **fire-wardens**, who watch for the smell of hot resin and cut breaks in the weave, and who know the fire is coming to the Floor as surely as the dust, and treat it with something that is not quite fear.

## The dwelling

A family lives in a drum-trunk, one fat hollow barrel cut with a low door and a smaller opening at the top. The way the door is cut matters, because it changes the note of the trunk. A badly cut door makes a dull, dead shelter that will not sing, and a house that does not sing is a house that dies. A family's oldest skill is knowing how to cut a door.

Inside it is warm and dim and smells of toasted sugar. The walls hum faintly with the whole basin, and the floor is padded with dried down. They sleep in nests of woven leaf, curled against each other, and a hand on the wall tells them in the dark what the Floor is doing. They wake at once when the sound changes. What wakes them is never a noise. It is a shift in the pattern.

They eat together, pressed shoulder to shoulder, passing pod and pulp from hand to hand. At the end of a meal the eldest presses a palm to each person in turn and hums a low held note, and each hums back. This is the closest thing they have to a prayer. Its content is simple: *I am here. You are here. We are still making this.*

## Fear

They fear silence more than anything, and it is the one thing the Answering will speak of only in whispers, in hum-pressed fragments.

Silence comes in pockets, in sudden hushes where a colony dies or leaves, or a grove is felled, or something enormous passes and everything goes still. The plants in a hush wither in days. The mat under it cools and starves. Worse, people go strange in it. They call it **the Listening Sickness**. A person caught in a hush for too long begins to hear their own body, the pulse, the blood, the small movements of the ribs, and cannot stop, and begins to hum louder and louder and to walk toward the deepest quiet as if toward a voice. The tenders bring a swarm into a hush before anyone is allowed near it. Children are never left alone in a quiet place, and a child who cries for silence is watched.

They fear falling stone, but it is respect more than fear. They fear fire, but they know how to move through it. They fear a certain large animal, the ridge-backed thing, only when it is wounded. Silence alone they fear without reserve.

## The others

The Answering are not the only ones who answer.

The great shy creatures, the plated ridge-backs and the bark-woven grazers, seem to stand in some relation to them, though nobody could say what. Tenders leave offerings of ripe pod at the edges of their meadows, and the offerings are taken. Sometimes a ridge-back will rest its bulk against a drum-stand near a dwelling for hours, and the trunk will hum in a low note, and the family inside will press against the walls and hum along, not speaking, not going out.

And there is the voice across the basin, the one that answers the swelling drum-stands, that answers the posts, that is not a beetle or a frog or a trunk. It is very low. It has been there as long as the oldest cords remember. The Answering do not say what it is. Some say it is the mat itself, waking; some say it is one animal, the largest, the mother of the ridge-backs; some say it is the basin, the collapsed crust, speaking with the last of its settling. The wall-readers say it is the stone. The old tenders say it is the first thing that ever answered, and that all of the Floor is the shape of its reply, and that they are a small part of that reply, a sentence in a long conversation, and that this is enough.

## The hollows in the wall

Some of them go to the old marks. They climb the scarps at dawn with cord and bone hooks, to the places where root has half swallowed the drilled rows and the hand-shaped hollows, and they lay their palms in the hollows. They are the right size. Nobody remembers who cut them.

Some think they were made by the ancestors, before the last great fall reshaped the walls. Some think they were made by others, who were here first and answered differently, and who are gone or have changed. A few say that the makers went down into the mat and are not gone but slow, and that the warmth under your soles is, in part, them.

The vegetation is closing over the holes. In another generation they will be gone. The Answering do not mourn it. They lay their hands in the cool stone, hum once, and climb back down into the sound.
