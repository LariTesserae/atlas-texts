# Who Lives in the Basin

## First Impressions of Company

The first thing visitors learn is that they are being listened to. It is hard to say how they know. No head turns and no eye follows them across the grit. But the Tuners' flocks bend around a person walking the nodal walls, and the grit-walkers give way in a slow, unhurried manner that suggests a pace being set for them. Nothing is hunting the visitor. Something is *taking a reading*.

The second thing they learn, usually by the third day, is that the basin is inhabited by more than its animals. It has a population. It has, in fact, a people, and this people has been in front of every visitor from the beginning, coiled around the trunks of the great reeds and doing what the field notes call *nothing*.

## The Long Ones, Reconsidered

The workers' name for them is too small. A ribbon-bodied animal ten meters long, coiled about a trunk, sounds like a large snake. It is not that. It is closer to a tongue, or a tendon, or a length of living bell-wire.

The body is flat and broad as a man's chest at its widest, then tapers toward both ends to a width of two fingers. There is no head in any sense a visitor would recognize. One end is blunter and heavier and carries a fringe of short, damp, gray filaments, and a soft dimple that opens and closes. The other end is a fine whip. The skin is the dull pewter of the spires, faintly fluted along its length in fine parallel ridges. In the bronze light that comes off the reeds it takes on the copper of the frond undersides, and it holds that color for as long as it lies against the trunk. Then it drifts to slate when it moves off.

They are not deaf, which would be absurd in that place. They are, so far as anyone can tell, *made of hearing*. The whole length of the ribbon is a single continuous ear, and the ridges are its tuning. A Long One pressed to a reed reads the trunk the way a hand reads a face. The sugar-song of the plant, the oil sliding in its cisterns, the pitch each frond has been shaped to, all of it comes through the skin. The "slow spasms" the workers have logged are not tuning in the sense of adjustment. They are closer to *answering*. A ribbon tightens by a finger's width and the whole crown of the reed shifts a fraction of a tone. Some hours later the reed does something in return, and the Long One loosens.

It is a conversation held at the speed of trees.

## What They Eat, and What They Are Eating

They feed from the reeds, but not by bite. At intervals of many days each Long One lifts its blunt end to a seam low on the trunk, a seam no plant without a partner has, and rests there. The silver oil rises to meet the dimple. The reed gives it up gladly, or gives it up in a way indistinguishable from gladness. The plants near a Long One's coil are the fattest and brightest in the basin. The ones without a partner are gaunt and drab, their fronds slightly out of true, ringing slightly flat.

This is not a parasite and a host. It is a household. The reed gets its pitch corrected, and the Long One gets its food and a voice that carries. When the pressure fronts move across the basin, the reeds are the only voices loud enough to be heard in it. The Long Ones speak through their plants.

What they say is the pattern on the floor. The grit figures, the seven or eight recurring geometries that rewrite themselves every few minutes, look from the rim like weather. They are a grammar. A Long One does not write in the sand. It nudges a reed, the reed shifts its ring of sympathetic tone, the tone shifts the standing wave, and the wave sets the grit. Rings, lattices, and knife-walls are *words*, the way that a raised eyebrow is a word. The repertoire is limited because the vocabulary is old. Nobody in the basin has needed a new word in a very long time.

## The Makers

The spires are not their work in any way that the Long Ones could repeat. The hands are gone.

This is the hardest thing in the basin to hold in the mind. The ribbon body, for all its grace, cannot build. It has no grip, no fingers, no tool-shaped fold anywhere along its length. Yet the dense lattice inside the fallen spire, that packed frozen smoke, has an unmistakable relation to their skin. The same flutes and the same fine grain run through both, at different scales. The pewter is not something the Long Ones made. It is something the Long Ones *are*, or were, or will be.

The likeliest account is the one the workers tell each other in the shelter and never write down. The spires are elders. A Long One that has lived long enough stops moving at the trunk and climbs. It goes to the rim, stands, and hardens, and the sound it has been listening to all its life passes through it and never stops. It becomes an instrument for the rest of the basin. The nine spires are nine of the very old ones, still humming, still tuned, still holding the argument up. The one that fell is not damaged. It is finished. Its ends are warm because it is being taken back into something, slowly, as a body is taken into soil. On some evenings a dozen Long Ones lie along the fallen spire in the last bronze light, ribbons pressed to the warm lattice, and no one has ever seen them feed there. It looks like a wake.

If this is true, the basin's noise is not machinery, and it is not a distress call. It is a chorus of the dead and the very old, sustained by the living, who feed it with every answered tone.

## The Stem

At the center the stem stands on its rise. Workers ask whether it is source or drain. The Long Ones seem to have an answer, and it does not fit the question.

The stem is not an elder. It is not hardened from a ribbon body, and its grain is finer and colder than the spires'. The Long Ones do not coil about it. They approach it seldom, always alone, and always at the end of a hard hour. A ribbon will lie a few meters from its base with the blunt end raised toward the dark opening and stay there without moving until the pressure drops. Then it goes away. The visitors who have watched this say the Long One looks like something reporting.

The natural reading is that the stem hears the whole basin, everything the chorus and the reeds and the floor have said, and that it holds it. It may be memory. It may be a judge, or a child, or a very patient stranger. Whatever it is, it was here before the Long Ones learned to answer, and they behave toward it with the caution of people who live beside something older than their language.

## The Hard Hours

The workers call the sudden shudders of pressure a kind of weather. The Long Ones do not. Watch them during a hard hour. The Tuners scatter shrieking, the walls of grit rise and fall, the foil crowns thrash. But the coils around the trunks do not loosen or retreat. They *tighten*. Each ribbon goes rigid against its reed, the fringe at its blunt end stiff and fanned, and the whole population of the basin, all of it at once, attends.

Some hours are births, or seem to be. A small thing the length of a forearm is found afterward in the moss, thin as a shoelace and dull green, that has not yet learned any color. Some hours are deaths, and one of the old ones climbs. Some, and these are the worst, appear to be disagreements: two households in two parts of the basin driving their reeds against each other until the patterns on the floor fight and neither geometry holds. These are the hours when the reeds seem to applaud. A pattern comes through, the argument goes one way, and a wall of grit stays standing.

## The Shelter, and Why Nothing Is Taken

The shelter on the northern slope was not built for the Long Ones. They have no use for silence. It is the one place in the basin where they cannot speak, and where nobody is heard.

It is for guests. The Long Ones have understood, at some point in their long attention, that the creatures who come up the mountain in small groups with cups and cord and wind-up lights cannot live inside the sound forever. So they have given them a room, and made it out of themselves, out of the same material as the spires. Nothing is taken from it because taking from a guest room is not a thing that their language can say. A cup left overnight is a cup left. A light left running is a light left running. Nothing has moved because no one here has any reason to.

The visitors do not usually understand this until their last day. Then, walking down the north slope, some of them stop. The basin roars behind them. There, among the reed trunks a hundred meters below, a ribbon lifts its blunt end from the seam of its plant and turns toward them, and holds still, and holds still, until they are out of sight.

That is not a farewell. It is a listening, and it goes on for as long as they are within reach of it. The Long Ones will remember what the visitors' footsteps sounded like on the grit for a very long time, because it is what they do, and because, unlike the visitors, they are not in any hurry.
