# The Lodgers of Marrow-Under-Leaf

## What You See First

You see no one. You can walk an arcade for a quarter of an hour, cross a plaza the size of a cathedral nave, and meet only the pale bird standing in it. The town looks uninhabited because its people live in the thickness of it.

They are called Lodgers, by outsiders and, with a shrug, by themselves. Their own name for themselves is a hand-shape: the fingers of one palm laid flat against a wall, slightly cupped. It can't be spoken, and the only way to say it is to touch something. So the Lodgers are named for what they do all day, which is to lean on the town and listen.

## The Body

They are a narrow, small people, built to fit the pleats. The shoulders are set low and forward, the ribs are flexible, and the joints turn in ways that let a grown adult slide into a root-fold the width of a bookshelf. Their skin is dark and always faintly damp, with a green bloom in the creases of the elbows, the neck, and the backs of the knees. They don't scrub it off, since the bloom is a living thing and considered a kind of family.

The hands are the remarkable part. They are broad, with thick pads on the fingertips and the heel of the palm, and the skin there is so callused and so sensitive that a Lodger can lay a hand on a column and tell you which cicadas are singing in it, whether the sap is rising, and whether someone is walking on the far side. Elders can feel the loud wave coming round the town a full minute before it arrives.

The ears are small and folded flat against the head, and are packed from childhood with a plug of the amber resin that mortars the walls. It is warmed, shaped, and replaced every few weeks. The plugs do not shut out the roar, since nothing could. They cut it to a level at which the body can bear to be a body. Lodgers hear through the skull and the palm, and they think of hearing with bare ears as something like swallowing a mouthful of raw salt. Visitors are offered plugs at the edge of town. Those who refuse have usually left by nightfall.

The eyes are large-lidded and slow. They blink rarely and look for a long time at very small things. Because the open halls are empty and the life is at the edges, a Lodger's gaze goes to the margin of everything: the join of wall and floor, the rim of a cup, the border of a face.

## Where They Live

The dwellings are called *lodgings*, and they are not built. A family finds a buttress root with a hollow in its heart and asks it, over several seasons, to open. Asking means trimming a little here, tying a vine there, feeding the right kind of moss to the inner wall. What results is a set of rooms shaped like the inside of a seed pod. The floors are sprung, and the walls are warm and sweat gently. The doors are curtains of fine root that part when leaned on and close behind you with a sound like a long exhale.

Every lodging has one rule, and it is the same in every household: **the center of the room is left empty.** The beds are niches in the wall. The hearths, where there are any, are shelves. Food is eaten from ledges, and the tools hang along the edges on pegs of hardened resin. The floor in the middle is bare and swept. A family of nine will sleep, eat, argue, and grow old with their backs to the walls and a clear circle of nothing in the middle of their home.

Ask why and you will get several answers, all offered with the same gentleness. *It's for the room. A room needs somewhere to be. It's for company. It's where the answer will stand.* The last is the oldest, and it is said quietly.

## Speech and the Hush

A Lodger's voice is not the voice of someone who lives in a quiet place. It is the voice of someone who lives in a loud place and has learned to speak *under* it. They talk in a low, dry, throat-bound murmur that hardly uses breath, and much of what they say is not said at all but tapped: two fingers on a wall in cadences that the wall carries across a quarter of the town. A conversation between neighbors is a hand on the same column, patient, with each of them waiting for the tap to arrive.

The reason is the distance. The town measures you by how loudly you are heard, and the streets stretch for shouters. So the Lodgers are a quiet people, trained to it from the cradle, and the highest virtue among them is to be *small-sounding*: to move through the town leaving hardly any wake.

Two trades depend on it.

**The hush-runners** are couriers. Silent-shod in moss-felt, breathing through the nose, not speaking from the moment they step out, they cross the whole town in the time it takes a kettle to boil, because the streets contract around them. They carry news, medicine, seed, and love letters. They are thin, calm, and slightly unnerving. It is said that a good runner can pass through a room and leave the dust undisturbed.

**The long-heard** are the opposite. These are people whom the town cannot help but hear: a voice that carries, a heavy step, a laugh. Around them the streets swell. A long-heard woman going to market may walk an hour to a stall that her neighbors reach in ten minutes. They are not shunned. They are given the outer quarters, where the streets are broadest and there is more room to stretch, and they tend to become the town's gatekeepers and guides, the ones who go out to meet the strangers, since a stranger is usually loud too. It is an odd fate, being a person the town makes room for by pushing you away, and many of the long-heard are gentle to the point of tenderness.

Lovers court in whispers. It is understood that if two people can keep each other in earshot at a murmur, the town will bring them close, and that if it won't, it won't.

## Time, Work, and Bread

They do not keep hours. They keep the wave. The loudest point of the frog-song circles the town slowly, like a lighthouse beam, and each quarter has its time when the crest passes over. The greeting in the morning is *Has it come round?* and the answer is *Not yet* or *It's on the far side* or, at the moment it passes, only a hand pressed flat to the wall.

The work is mostly **tuning.** The town is a resonator, and its resonance has to be maintained by centuries of small adjustments. Tuners carry hooked knives of silica-cane and go from trunk to trunk, shaving a hair off a hollow's throat or packing a crack with resin to shift a bell-cicada column by a quarter tone. They take an apprentice's whole youth to train, and their hands are the most sensitive of all. The tuners will say, when pressed, that they are not making the sound. They are keeping the sound from tearing the place apart.

The others tend. Some tend the cisterns, walking the drip-channels beside the water-carriers and learning from them, since no one knows where the animals take the water and the Lodgers have come to feel that this is their business too. Some tend the ants, cutting away the trails that would strip a young root. Some tend the walls, coaxing new vines across gaps and sealing old gates before the autumn does it for them. A few, the *cup-keepers*, tend the Street of Cups, where the rain rises. They set out bowls and read the way the drops leave them. They have kept a ledger for nine hundred years of every drop they have seen rise, and the ledger has never told them where any went.

They eat fig-fruit, ant-paste pressed into cakes, the roe of wall-frogs, roasted seed from the high canopy, and a grey, sour bread made from a fungus that grows on the cistern walls. They do not eat slow-cats, wall-frogs (only the roe), or the pale birds. They drink cistern water through reed straws, always from the edge, never from the middle.

## The Dead

The dead are put back in the walls. A body is wrapped in leaf-felt, carried by hush-runners to a hollow trunk that has fallen silent or a root-column that is thinning, and placed in the hollow's throat. The cicadas take it in a season. Within a few years it has become part of the timber, and the family comes to lay a hand on the column and feel, if they are old enough and patient enough, a new pitch in the note. *That's my mother*, they say. *She's a little flat.*

This is not thought of as sad. The town is one thing, and the Lodgers are, in a sense, the parts of it that walk around. What is sad is dying in an unsettled way, in a place without wall, without a trunk to be returned to. That is the worst fate a Lodger can imagine, worse than the circle: to die in the open middle of a room.

## The Stilled

Once in a while, someone steps into the silent plaza and comes out.

This happens by accident, by dare, by grief, and sometimes by choice. The circle is thirty paces across and entirely quiet. The people who enter it are there for a few minutes, and when they step out they are the same person, with the same name and the same scars, and *changed*, in a way they cannot report.

The Lodgers call them **the Stilled.** They do not speak of what changed. It can be small: a woman whose shadow is now a fraction of a second late, a man who cannot be surprised, a girl who says a word nobody has ever said and then cannot remember saying it. It can be large. One of the Stilled, they say, has not blinked in forty years.

The Stilled live in the quarter that faces the plaza, called the Ear, and they are not shunned or worshipped but *consulted*, in the way one might consult a barometer. They can tell, better than the tuners, what mood the town is in. *It's leaning*, they say, or *It's holding*, or, once in living memory, *It's almost about to*, and the whole town stopped talking for a day. Nothing happened. The Stilled will not say what they were waiting for.

## The Answer

Beneath the tuning and the tending, the Lodgers are engaged in one long work.

They do not call it a project, because it has no end. The elders call it **the Answer.** It is a phrase, a long one, built up by centuries of accretion, and each quarter of the town contributes a passage. It is not spoken. It is tapped, hummed, and pressed into walls in rhythm and pressure, and no living person knows all of it. The tuners keep the quarter-tones. The runners keep the pace. The cup-keepers keep the silences between phrases, and the silences are said to be the hardest part.

Nobody knows the question.

There are two schools. The older holds that the town is listening for a *voice*: that the plaza is an ear and has been waiting since the river left, and that the river is what it wants. The Old Channel, the cold black thread under the town, is kept clean and unblocked for this reason. Small pale people live along it. They pole flat boats a few dozen yards between root-banks, on a stream that goes nowhere, and they are the river's last habit: they carry cargo that is never delivered, sing ferry-songs to nobody, and wait. They say the river is coming back. The Lodgers do not quite believe them, and do not contradict them, and leave bowls of grey bread at the water's edge.

The younger school says the town is listening for *them*. That the Answer is not a message but a rehearsal, and one day someone will walk into the circle who is ready, and the whole thing, the roar, the pressure, the falling rain, will finally hush, and be heard. Some of the younger tuners have taken to sitting in the dead quarters, where the sound is thin, and practicing the silences. A few of the Stilled are said to disapprove. One of them, when asked, said only: *It has been answered before.* Then she put her palm to the wall and would say no more.

## Others Who Live Here

The Lodgers are not alone in the walls.

There are the **dust-tenders** of the dead quarters, gaunt gray people who live among silvered wood and rot-dust and keep the recolonizing going, seeding fungus into dead columns and handing the ruin back to the green. They are not quite Lodgers. They are quiet even by Lodger standards and their skin has the color of old bark.

There is the **pale bird** in the great plaza, which stands without moving and is not, the Lodgers believe, a bird. They leave it alone. It has been seen to turn its head once, when a child laughed.

There are the **water-carriers**, whom the Lodgers regard as senior citizens.

## A Visit

If you arrive as a stranger, a long-heard woman will meet you at the edge, will give you plugs, will look at your boots, and will ask you, gently, not to shout. She will lead you along the outer ring, keeping a hand on the wall. She will show you the lodgings from the outside and will not ask you into any of them. If you insist, you will be admitted, and shown to a ledge, and given grey bread, and you will sit with your back to the wall like everyone else, and you will find yourself looking, over and over, at the bare circle of floor in the middle of the room.

It will be very clean. It will be warm. If you stay long enough, at dusk when the sound climbs, you may see a child of the house cross to it, put one bare palm on the floor exactly in the center, and hum the low rolling *tock* that is the base note of everything. She will wait, head tilted, as if for a reply.

Sometimes, the family will tell you, the floor hums back. It is always a half-second early.
