**Who lives in the Sweat Room**

Start with the smallest, because it is the one that will find you.

**The cup-walkers**

In nearly every water cup on the stalagmites, and along the rims of the flowstone pools, lives a pale animal about the size of a barley grain. You will not see it. You feel it, a touch like a loose hair drawn across the back of your knuckle. It has too many legs to count by feel, each as fine as spider silk, and two feelers longer than its body that tap ahead of it in a slow alternating rhythm, left and right, as though the animal were reading the world one syllable at a time.

It is blind in the complete way of things whose ancestors stopped needing eyes so long ago that the place where eyes would go has closed over smooth. It has no fear of you. Nothing in its lineage has ever been large, warm, and hungry in the same moment. When your hand comes down on its rim it stops, taps twice, walks onto the hand, walks off, and goes back to grazing the film. It eats the thin slime of the mineral wet and the beads at the tips of the fungal stalks. It drinks the cups. Each cup seems to have its own resident, and the residents rarely visit one another. A cup-walker may live its whole life in a bowl of water the size of a thimble, listening to one drip fall in it, that drip and no other. The drips do not keep time together, so each animal has a private clock that beats a little differently from its neighbor's. Some part of it must know this, the way you know the sound of your own house at night.

They lay their eggs in the film at the cup's edge, single, clear, each a bead of slightly denser water. The eggs are held there by surface tension alone. You will never see one. If you exhale too hard near a cup, you may blow one off.

**The mat**

The pale pads on the ledges are the room's largest living thing. They may be the room's only large living thing, and there is a possibility you should take seriously: that there is only one of them.

The lobes you find on separate ledges are joined. Thread-thin strands, white and unbranched, run through the rock's hairline cracks from one mat to the next. The mountain is full of these seams, and the mat has been slowly following the wet along each one for longer than any lamp-maker's people have had names. It doesn't grow the way a plant grows. It advances at the pace a stain advances. A lobe that reaches the width of a palm may have taken a lifetime to do it. In the deep of the rock, where you'll never go, the same organism may run for a hundred paces, thin as a thought, and every ledge you touch is where it comes up to breathe.

It is cool to the touch in a room that is not cool anywhere else. It doesn't produce the cold. It steals warmth from the water it drinks and keeps only what it needs. When you press your hand into it, it holds your print for a few breaths, then fills the print back in from the edges, so the mat is a kind of slow memory that forgets you politely. The single stalks that rise from it, each topped with a bead, are not reproduction as we would mean it. They are how it feeds its neighbors. The beads are sweet. The cup-walkers climb the stalks in the long dark, drink, and go down again. Some of their droppings fall back into the mat. The arrangement is old and neither party has ever considered it.

The mat gives off almost no smell. On the ledges where it grows thickest there is a faint mushroom-cellar sweetness that gets in the back of your throat, and you notice it only when you leave and its absence is like a missing tooth.

**The one that clicks**

High in the crystal curtains, where the needles grow longest and thickest and the air is at its wettest, something lives that you do not meet.

You know it by the click. It is a single dry tick, the sound of one fingernail struck against another, and it comes every few minutes or every few hours, in no pattern anyone has managed to note. It is not the drips. The drips are wet and this is dry. When you click back, it does not answer. That is the first thing every visitor learns, and the second thing, learned later, is that this is not a refusal. The click was never a call. It is an animal listening to a room by making a small sound in it and attending to the shape of what comes back. It is looking, the way you would look with a lamp. The room is its lamp, and the crystal needles, each one a little mirror for sound, are its lenses.

When you come in, it knows. It has known since you entered the throat, from the change in the way the room gives its sound back, the small dull patch that is your body. It knows how many of you there are. It knows the seven pebbles, the four lamps, the tally cord, the handprints in the clay, and their exact positions, and if you were to move one of them by a finger's width it would feel the room change. Perhaps that is why they have not been moved. Nothing forbids it. But the sense is strong in there that someone would notice.

No one has described it, because no one has touched it. What is known is only traces. Along one stretch of the curtains a body's width of crystal has been rubbed smooth, the only worn surface in the room, the needles ground to nubs and polished by a slow shifting weight over uncounted years. It sits there. It sits there for very long spans without moving, the way the mat advances, the way the water stands in its cups. Once in a great while a needle snaps somewhere high up with a tiny bright sound and a few splinters of crystal come down through the veils and chime against the others going by. Then the click follows, and you can feel it checking, as you might glance up at a noise in your own house.

Whether it is one animal or a line of them, or whether it has ever been anything more than the mat's way of hearing, no one who has crawled in has been able to say.

**The hum**

Some who have stayed long enough claim the hum is a resident too. This is not a scientific claim. It comes from a feeling. The pitch does not waver, and yet it responds. If you speak in the same note, the room seems to lean into you, to hold your voice a fraction longer before flattening it and handing it back. If you move too quickly, the hum goes thin for a moment, as if it had been holding something and you'd jostled it.

The hum is a physical fact, the meeting of the standing water and the tuned crystals. But the mat lies along every ledge and every seam, and a body that large, made of water and threads, must lie in the vibration like a hand in a stream. The cup-walkers walk in it. The clicker hears the room through it. It may be that the hum is simply the sound of the three of them being in one place, each unaware of the others' names, and that the room itself is the thing that lives here, with the animals as its slow habits.

**And you**

You are the only fast thing in the Sweat Room.

Everything else is slow: the mat, the drip, the crystal, the animal that sits on its polished shoulder of needles for a season at a time. You come in breathing, sweating, sliding, cutting your palms, and for the time you are there, your heart is the loudest thing in the room, a small violent engine in a place that has never hurried. The cup-walker walks on your knuckle and considers you and leaves. It is not unkind. It is the calm of a creature that has never known an enemy, meeting an animal that briefly, terribly wishes it were one of them.

The people who came before you understood this, or felt it. That is why the pebbles are counted, why the handprints face the wall, why the lamps are full of oil and dry of flame. They brought light up a mountain and set it down in the dark and left it there, a gift they did not spend. They came to a house where everyone was blind and had the manners not to bring a candle.
