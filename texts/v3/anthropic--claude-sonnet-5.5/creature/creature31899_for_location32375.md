# The People of Vellum Row

They call themselves the Kept, or sometimes the Held, depending on the level. The word means the same in both cases: *what the wall is holding up.* It is not a complaint. It is the first fact of their lives, the way weather is for other people.

## Bodies

They are lean and long in the hand. The heat has taught them to carry no more flesh than they need, and most have the dry, corded look of people who sweat constantly and drink constantly and are never quite done doing either. Their skin is pale under a brown that comes from dust, not sun. Their eyes are wide and dark for the dusk they live in, and the whites have a faint amber tint, as if the lamps had soaked in. Those who work near the floor lose their eyebrows and the fine hair on their forearms, and they have a slight permanent squint from the red glow.

They dress in undyed linen, cut loose and wrapped in layers, and wet down at the cisterns before every shift. A person coming up a stair leaves a dark trail of drips on the plate. Hands are always covered: felt mitts, cloth-wound palms, sleeves pulled over the fingers. Bare hands are for intimacy and for the rock. Nearly everyone has a pale scar somewhere, in the shape of a rail or a rung.

## How They Speak

They do not speak much, and never carelessly. A voice in the lanes is a small violation, because everyone is listening. They listen for the rim, for the dry tick that comes before a slab lets go, for a change in the pitch of the engines, for the soft slump of the lower walls. A child learns to stop mid-word when the grit begins to run. An adult will pause in the middle of a sentence, head tilted, and the person opposite will pause too, and both wait for the sound to resolve into nothing. Then they go on as if there had been no break.

Most talk is done through the iron. The rails, pins, and shaft-hoods carry sound the length of the town, and the Kept have a language of taps, a rapid finger-knuckle patter on the cool underside of a handrail, or a spoon against a pipe. Every level has its own dialect, and every stair has its own accent. A stranger's taps can be told at once by their rhythm. Bereavements, greetings, and jokes are all tapped. The jokes are said to be very good and to lose everything in translation. When the whole town is tapping at once, in the slow hours, the pins hum faintly with it, and the walls seem to be murmuring in their sleep.

The bell, struck once and left to fade, is the only public voice. It is not rung for hours or alarms. It marks a person leaving the count, whether by a fall, by the heat, by old age, or by the baskets. Everyone within hearing stops for the length of the ring and goes on. Nobody says who it was. They will know by evening from the tapping.

## Names and Kin

A child is named for the place where they were born: the stair, the landing, the number of bridges crossed to reach the room. *Ninth-Twin-Under.* *Copper Landing Two.* It sounds cold, but it is the reverse. To bear a place's name is to be promised a place. Through the years the names wear down into something like ordinary ones. Nintwin. Coplan. Vessa, from *Vessel Row, the last*. A person who moves levels takes a second name and keeps the first, and old people carry strings of them, like a route memorized.

Families are loose and many-roomed. Three or four households share a landing, and children run to whichever door is nearest when the grit begins to fall. The rule of the twin stair is taught before walking: *where is the other way?* A toddler will point at it without looking, the way another child might point at the moon.

## Work

Nearly everyone has a hand in the engines, even those who never touch one. There are the **drawers**, who haul the wrapped pipes out of the fissure on short shifts measured by sand-glass, and whom the rest of the town treats with quiet deference, though no one would say so. There are the **rack-wardens**, who walk the condenser sheds day and night, feeling the glass with the backs of their fingers for the first hint of dryness. There are the **scrapers**, who cut the white blooms from the lower walls with flat copper blades and carry the crumbs away in buckets to be pressed into something useful. There are the wire-hall spinners, the tile-firers, the cable-lift keepers who oil the ratchets by ear, and the **pin-checkers**, who go along the iron pins with a hammer, tapping each one and listening to its ring. A dull pin is marked in chalk, and by evening a crew has come to drive a new one.

Nobody is idle, and nobody looks hurried. The pace of the engines sets the pace of the people. They move in a long, patient gait, without waste, and rest in shade with their eyes half closed and their hands slack and wet.

## Food and the Pools

What they eat comes partly from below and partly from above. The rust-orange mats from the drip-lines are scraped, boiled, and pressed into dense, faintly sour cakes, which are the bread of the town. The feathery ferns are picked only when they curl, and only a frond at a time, and are eaten raw as a bitter relish. From the fenced pools come a thin red paste, which is skimmed by the **pool-tenders**, solitary people in cloth masks who work alone, speak seldom, and are said to talk to the water. The water does not answer, but the tenders swear it changes color when they are troubled.

The rest comes down from the rim in baskets: grain in sacks, lamp-oil, salt, bolts of linen, and sometimes a small, wrapped thing that nobody has ever agreed how to explain. They never see who sends it. The baskets go up full and come down full, and it has been that way since the founding. The Kept do not discuss it. A child who asks is told, "Someone is up there," which is the entire theology, and it seems to be enough.

## The Tile-Painters

The oldest art in the town is the tile. The ceramic is glazed in blood-red and mustard, and the painters incise creatures into it: long-necked things, winged things, things with many legs, and things that swim. These are the only animals the Kept know, and they know them only from tiles that were copied from tiles that were copied from something further back. Over the generations the creatures have drifted. A bird has become a sort of feathered stair. A fish has grown hands. The painters keep pattern books, and they argue in taps over correctness, though no one alive has seen a living thing that could settle it. To the Kept these are not fantasies. They are memories held at a great distance.

## The Keeper of the Tree

In the largest cistern hall, under a copper spout, stands the pale tree, and one person, always one, is its keeper. The office passes quietly from hand to hand. The keeper sleeps in a niche beside the roots and measures the trickle with a cupped palm. They are not a priest, and no one bows to them. But when a new pair walks together through the cistern hall, they stop at the tree and touch its trunk once, lightly, the way you might touch a sleeping child's shoulder.

Nobody knows what the tree is. It has never flowered. The keeper says only that it is *very patient*, and that this is the thing to learn from it.

## The Showers

When the steam rises, meets the cooler rock, and the haze begins to darken, a certain attention passes through the town. Work does not stop, but it slows. Then the warm rain comes down, tasting of chalk, and the Kept turn their faces up, one by one, wherever they are: on stairs, in doorways, on the bridges. They stand with their eyes closed and their mouths a little open, letting it run over their lips. It lasts an hour or two. No one speaks. The gutters run brown, the troughs fill, and the long wet whisper passes over everything.

Afterward there is a quiet, almost happy stir, the closest the town comes to festival. The tapping picks up. Cakes are shared. Somebody, usually an old person, will strike a spoon on a pipe in the rhythm that means *again, soon*, and the answer comes back from three levels away, *yes.*

## What They Are Not Asked

They are not asked why they stay. The question has no hold on them. The walls are young and the floor is hot and the sky is a small pale coin, and yet they speak of the Sink with the low, unshowy tenderness one uses for a difficult relative who has never once let them fall. They know it is collapsing. They say so plainly: *it's still coming down.* They mean it as a description of the world, and also, in some way, as a form of love. They tend a wound that has become a home, and it keeps them warm, and in return they listen to it, every hour of every day, for the moment it changes its mind.
