# Who Keeps the Long Green Works

## The mill-hands

There are people, though not many, and they do not think of themselves as many. They think of themselves as enough. Perhaps a few thousand are spread across a country of leaf, in crews of five or six who move between mills on a circuit so old that no one remembers choosing it. They are called mill-hands, and they answer to it, though the mills need them less than the mills let on.

They are lean, dry people, with skin like good paper and a fine chalky bloom on the backs of their hands that they never wash, because there is nothing to wash with. Their eyes are large and pale and used to glare, and they squint through the light in a way that gives them a look of permanent, mild doubt. They dress in felt carded at the mills, in dull greens and the gray-white of old frost, cut long and layered against the cool. At the wrist and throat they wear rings of brass lichen scraped from the lubricant seeps, which they believe, without much conviction, keeps the belts kind to them.

Each carries a vial. It is a small glass thing stoppered with wax, worn on a cord against the breastbone, and it holds water, a few drops or a finger's depth. It is not for drinking. A mill-hand drinks the thin sweet spirit from the trunks, cut with a little ash-salt, and it keeps them level and unhurried and slightly forgetful of the time. The vial is for knowing what wet is. When two crews meet in a gallery, they do not shake hands. Each unstoppers a vial and holds it out, and the other looks at the light in it and nods. Then they talk, low, because voices carry wrong here.

They talk carefully because of the labels. A mill-hand learns early that what is written may become true, and most go their whole lives writing almost nothing. They speak in short sentences and prefer to point. Names are the hardest matter. A name written on a beam might grow a plant toward it, or turn a person toward the name, so nobody has a written name. A child is called by a pattern of taps, two fingers on a trunk, and the pattern is theirs and no one else's. When they must speak of someone absent, they use the taps and not the words. On a still evening in a colonnade you can hear crews calling to each other across the courses, a dry small knocking on the stems, and the trunks carry it a long way.

They count once. This is the first thing they will tell a stranger, and they say it kindly, as a courtesy. Count a colonnade walking out and trust that number. Never count it coming back. They carry a knotted cord and tie a knot for each trunk on the way out, and on the return they leave the cord in their pocket and walk with their eyes half-lowered, taking the aisle as it comes. It is not superstition, or not only. They have all seen the second number. They keep the first because a person has to keep something.

They live in the pod-racks. Along the great branches hang pods in housings that fit them like sockets, each the size of a sleeping body, lined with felt and warm from the sap-loops behind. A mill-hand chooses a rack for a season and sleeps in whatever pod is nearest empty, and no one has ever found one too large or too small. Old hands wonder about this privately. If the pods were grown for fruit, why do they fit a shoulder so well? If they were grown for sleepers, who was the first to sleep in them? These questions do not trouble anyone much. They ask them the way one probes a tooth.

They mend and do not build. They know the mills as a shepherd knows a flock, by sound. A mill-hand will stand in a hall with eyes closed and say, that piston is a hair late, the governor on the third belt is sticking, and then go and press a thumb of felt into the right bearing, and the note will settle. They do not know how the mills were made, or who set the maker's marks in the floor. They keep the glyphs clean of moss where they can, out of something like manners, and they do not try to read them. Two crews, in two generations, are said to have tried. Nobody speaks of what happened, only that the marks they were reading are now a little different, and the crews went on to other work.

## The pruners

Then there are the pruners, and it is harder to say whether they live in the works or are among its tools.

A pruner is tall and slow. Its body is a folded pair of shears in dull green-black chitin, blades closed along the spine, with long jointed legs that fold and unfold with the deliberate rhythm of a carpenter's rule. Where a head might be there is a smooth ridged dome and a pair of small bright sensors like drops of amber. Its forelimbs are the cutters, a pair of long curved blades that part and meet with a sound too small to hear until it is very near. They walk the galleries at the pace of shadows, stop at a trunk, and consider it. There may be a long pause, perhaps a quarter of an hour. Then they take one stem, one shoot, one leaf-hinge, and cut it.

The cut is so exact that it heals over in a few breaths, the bark closing like a lid, the scar smoothing to nothing. A mill-hand who watches this usually looks away. The stem does not look cut. It looks as though it had always ended there, and the tree around it seems to have been built to end there, and the pruner moves on.

Pruners do not eat. They do not sleep, or nobody has seen it. They do not fear, and they do not respond when spoken to, though they do sometimes pause when a name is tapped near them, and it has been noticed that the name-tappers are sometimes gone a season later, or sometimes the reverse: a crew of six becomes five, and no one grieves, because no one can remember there being six. The mill-hands do not draw conclusions. They leave a pruner a wide berth, and they never count when one is near.

The mill-hands' own theory, held loosely, is that the pruners do not cut the works. They cut the record of the works, and the trees follow. But this is the sort of thing said late at night, in a pod, in a low voice, with the vial in hand.

## The small movers

The moths live on the frost. They are pale as candle-wax, with wings like torn tissue and bodies no larger than a thumb-joint, and they drift in slow flocks under the high panes, feeding on the dried crystals of spirit as they fall. They do not seem to have eyes. They find their way by the ticking of the leaf-panes, and when the wave of clicks rolls over a field the moths wheel together as if the sound were a wind, then settle again, and the whole flock glows faintly in the shaft-light. Mill-hands say that where moths are thick the counts hold. They pin small hopes on moths.

The beetles are bright as enamel, red and blue and a green that only appears in glass, and about the size of a fist. They live in the joints of the machines. They walk the drive-shafts and gear-teeth and pistons, cleaning the seeps with small industrious jaws, and when they land after a flight they ring, a faint sweet note like a fingernail on crystal. Each beetle has its own note. A mill full of them can produce, at the turn of a shift, a slow scattered chord, and mill-hands who have worked one hall for years can tell by that chord whether the hall is well. Beetles will sit on a hand without alarm. They are the one creature the works permits to be known.

## The leaners

In the few aisles where the shadows fall toward the light and thicken, there are others.

They are not many. The mill-hands do not enter those aisles unless they must, and some who have entered did not come out the same. The leaners are what the aisle has made of the ones who stayed. They are thin and pale, long in the limb and slow in the joint, and they lean, always, into the dark side of the aisle, as though it were a fire on a cold night. Their skin has gone the color of the etiolated stems. Their eyes are open and quite calm. They do not speak. When a mill-hand passes through carrying a lantern of glowing felt, the leaners turn their faces a little toward the light, the way a plant turns from a wall, and then away.

They may be dead. They may be something that has learned to grow. Mill-hands who cross those aisles sometimes leave a drop of water on a leaf, from the vial, not as an offering exactly, but as a way of saying: I know what this is, and you did once too. The drop is usually gone when they return.

## What is at the center

At the oldest halls, in the middle of the basin where the land tilts, the weave of the floor shows its patterns across the seams of the world, and something lives there that no crew has described the same way twice.

It is not seen. It is inferred. It is the hand that set the maker's marks, or the reader of them, or the thing the marks were written to. It is present in the way a vein runs from a frond on the ceiling down a wall, across the floor, and into your shadow, where it goes on without you. Mill-hands who have stood in that hall say that the vein continues, and that for a moment they were a place the vein passed through, and that it was not unpleasant, and that they did not want to go on. They say it the way one says something about an old kindness.

Nobody there has a name and nobody there needs one. The mills turn. The panes tick their slow wave overhead. A mill-hand knocks twice on a trunk to say I am here, and somewhere out in the long green dimness another knock answers, from a distance that will turn out to be wrong, and the knock is the right one. Frost lets go of a pane with a glassy chime. A pruner stands at a trunk, considering, and the blades part.
