# The Inhabitants of the Pressed Highlands

Begin with the smallest, because they are everywhere and you will mistake them for the light.

## The Tenders

On any working ridge the surface seems to shimmer, as if heat were rising from it. The heat is not rising. It is the tenders, a few billion of them on a single slope, each the size of a thumbnail, each wearing a shell of polished bronze and oil-dark green. They move in a crawling tide over the boiler-stalks. Their front legs end in soft brushes and their mouthparts in fine shears. They are always working. One oils a cam. One backs into a reed-pipe and chews out a plug of resin. One finds a soft patch of rot on a trunk and eats it down to clean tissue, then secretes a plug of sealing wax and moves on without pause.

They have no voices and no need of them. When a tender finds something wrong, a cracked valve or a scorched seam, it stamps a rhythm on the surface, a small code that runs through the cordage. Others stamp it onward. Within a minute a work-gang has converged from a hundred meters away, the way a crowd gathers without anyone having called it.

They die in the machinery. A tender that has lost its wax-gland or its edge settles into the pipe it has been cleaning and stays there. Its body dries and lodges in the reed, and the reed's note shifts by a fraction of a tone. Over the years the chords of a ridge are tuned by its dead, and the tenders that come after work around the small fixed pitch the old ones left.

## The Haulers

The haulers are the animals you notice, because they are slow and large and seem to have somewhere to be.

A hauler is the size of an ox and built like a barge: six short thick legs, a long low body, a broad flat back with a shallow trough worn into the dorsal plates by centuries of cargo. Its hide is grey-brown and layered like slate, and along the flanks it carries the only bright things on its body, panels of iridescent scale that flash in slow patterns. These are its speech. A hauler at the head of a line pulses amber-amber-blue, and forty animals behind it turn together onto a new cable. A hauler in distress flashes a hard, fast white that others take up along the line until it reaches something that can help, which is usually a tender-gang, and sometimes nothing at all.

They walk in time with the cables. Each spinner-cable hums at a low frequency as the wind drives it, and the haulers' feet, broad and cup-shaped and sensitive as tongues, read that hum. They do not hear the cables. They feel which cable is slack and which is taut, which has been cut, and which has just been laid and will not yet bear weight. A hauler can walk blind through a smoke-front for hours and never leave the line.

They carry pressed paper in stiff bundles, charcoal in woven cradles, and seed-cases that rattle faintly against their sides. They deliver these to the places where the Mat has thinned or torn. There the loads are unpacked and pressed into the wound, and the Mat's own growth closes over them. The haulers do not know they are gardeners. They know only the line and the load and the tug of the cable under their feet.

Their young are born on the move, slung in a fold of skin beneath the mother's belly. They are set down at the first stop and taught by imitation, with the flashes going back and forth above their heads, the first thing they learn to read.

## The Gliders

Above all this, on the updrafts that pour off the ridge-faces, hang the gliders.

They are the largest living things on the plateau that move under their own power. Their bodies are thin, almost skeletal: a long keel of bone and cartilage, a slender neck, and spars that run out to support membranes of translucent brown, veined like a leaf held to the sun. A large one is fifteen meters across. From the ground it looks like a scrap of paper someone has thrown into the wind and forgotten.

The gliders are born half-deaf and half-mad with the noise, and they endure it by living above the worst of it. Their ears are small and sealed, and what hearing they have is mostly a rumor in the bones. But their eyes are extraordinary. Each is a great domed cluster of lenses the size of a dinner plate, and together they see the whole sky at once. They see seeds. Every volley from the seed-guns is a spray of tiny dark points against the bright air, and a glider can track a hundred of them at once, choose the ripest, and turn its whole body to intercept one. It plucks the seed with a lipped, flexible beak and never seems to hurry.

They rarely land. They mate on the wing, two bodies locked and turning in a slow spiral down a thermal, then parting without ceremony. They sleep in long shallow glides, one brain-hemisphere at a time, and come down only to die. A dying glider spirals into a gully and spreads across the drift like a dropped sail. The tenders find it within the hour, and within a season its frame has been carried off and worked into the Mat.

## The Harrowers

The gullies are not empty.

The drift that floors them, the loose bark and shredded leaf, is soft and deep and hard to cross, and it swallows sound better even than the noise does. The harrowers live there. They are long, grey, and narrow, with the low flowing bodies of things built for speed through resistance, and legs that end in wide furred pads. They are silent, and here silence is more than the lack of sound. It is a discipline.

A harrower hunts by listening with its feet. It lies in the drift with its belly pressed flat and reads the tremor of the Mat, the tender-gangs stamping their codes, the young haulers straying from the line. It has learned something the other animals have not. The boiler-stalks drift out of phase, but their interference makes slow moving nodes of relative quiet, small pockets in the roar where the pulses cancel. A harrower runs from node to node, and its footfalls vanish into the gaps. To its prey it appears out of nothing. The hunt is very fast, and what it takes it eats in the drift, quickly, before the wind can carry the smell.

They are solitary and few. A harrower may hold a gully for a decade and never see another of its kind, and it does not seem to mind. Sometimes, at the edge of a burned valley, you may see one standing motionless in the cooling ash, watching the first pale shoots of regrowth. No one knows what it is doing there. It may be waiting for the tenders to return.

## The Underfelt

Down through the turf, past the cordage layer, below the place where the wind can reach, the noise changes. It does not stop. The Mat is too thick for that. But it softens into a low, distant thunder, and in the dark humus at the bottom, where the oil-soaked peat presses toward stone, there is almost quiet.

Things live in it. Few visitors ever learn of them, and the animals of the surface do not seem to know.

They are pale, blind, slow, and soft-bodied. They move through the tunnels the tenders have left, feeding on the rot-fungi that grow in the deep. Some are long and wormlike, and some are flat, broad disks that drift through the oil-films like slow jellyfish. They are the Mat's memory. Everything that dies on the surface eventually sinks, is broken down, and passes through their bodies, and what comes out is the slow black oil that seeps upward through the strata and feeds the roots of the stalks. They do not know they are part of the machine. They are simply what is left after the noise is gone, and the machine cannot run without them.

## What Watches

A last thing should be said, though it is hard to say cleanly.

In the cut faces of the ridges, in the long terraced cliffs where the strata are open to the air, there are rows of small hollows. They are round, smooth, and evenly spaced, and inside each sits a creature about the size of a hand, motionless, its body the exact grey-green of the felt around it. Each faces outward. Each has a single small lens-eye, clear as a drop of oil. They do not feed with the tenders or travel with the haulers. They sit in their hollows for years.

No one has ever seen one move. But in the morning, when the sun first strikes the cut faces, a slow ripple of light passes along the rows, each lens catching and holding the glare in turn. The ripple runs down a kilometer of ridge and is answered from the next ridge over. Then it stops.

It may be a language. It may only be how they warm themselves. It may be the plateau's way of looking at its own light, a small patient attention, one lens at a time, that is the closest thing this place has to someone who is paying attention. If so, they are not paying attention to you.
