# Those Who Listen

Everything that lives in the hollow has learned the same first lesson: the dark is not empty, it is full of information, and all of it arrives as sound. The eyes of the creatures here are small, or pale, or ringed with their own weak light for the sake of others. The ears are enormous. The throats are the true organs of life.

Start with the largest resident and the least seen.

## The Lid, Which Is Also Someone

Nobody in the hollow would say the Lid is alive in the way a bellcrake is alive. They would say it is *attending*. It answers a quake with green bridging shoots inside a season. It thickens the pillars where the Lid-tenders' dung falls and lets the others thin. When a band lives long beneath one span, the drip-tips over their camp begin to run sweeter, less bitter with resin, and no one can say whether this is chance. The old women of the bands say it is being *known*. They say a place that has heard your feet for a hundred seasons stops flinching at them.

Whatever it is, it keeps the whole household. Its descenders are the hearths around which the small life gathers. At the base of each pillar, in the wet ring where the drip-tips shed their water, there is a whole village: beetles like black seeds, threadworms in braided knots, pale snails that graze the blue lichen and carry a glimmer in their shells for hours after. Small sightless lizards hunt the snails by the heat of their slow movement. Above them all, in the ribs of the pillar, are the nests of a bird nobody has properly named. The people call it the **hush-wren**. It is a brown thing the size of a thumb that has given up song entirely. It lives inside the noise of the Lid and steals scraps from the gliders, and it is the only creature in the hollow that can be trusted not to be listening for you.

## Bellcrakes

They are gaunt, gray, and beautiful in the way of anything built for one purpose. The legs are too long for the body. The neck bends like a heron's. The head carries the crest, a hollow twist of horn open at the back, and through it the animal sounds its note.

Each herd holds one pitch, and the pitch is inherited. A calf is born into the tone of its mother's herd and is silent for its first season, walking in the middle of the group while the sound of the others washes over it. When it finally calls, it calls the herd's note but slightly flat, and the herd answers it a little louder until it finds the pitch. A young bellcrake that never finds it is not driven out. It becomes the herd's *listener*, always at the edge, head turned outward, taking the first watch. These are the ones the drip-wolves most want.

The chord of a herd is not one sound. Thirty animals holding one pitch produce a slow shimmering interference, a beat like the beat between two strings nearly in tune. A skilled listener can hear a herd's health in that shimmer. A missing animal changes it, and a sick one drags it. In fog, herds keep to about a stone's throw apart and answer one another across the valley, and the answers are a kind of arithmetic: *there are thirty of us here, there are twenty-six of you there, the pool between us is unclaimed.* They graze the black peat on the pale fungal mats that grow in the wet, and they drink at the crater-pools in strict turn. The turn is negotiated in tone, and violations are rare and terrible.

They die where they stand, mostly, or in the first hour after a wolf finds them. The herd's note drops a tone for a day when one of them falls. People hear the drop from far away and do not need anyone to say what it means.

## Drip-Wolves

They are not wolves. The people call them that because the word meant *patient hunter* once, in a language older than the hollow's stories. They are long and low, with the flat skull of something built to slide through fog, and the eye-rings burn a dull green that is visible only from a few paces. They hunt in threes and never call.

What makes them terrible is their understanding of the herd. A drip-wolf does not chase. It comes downwind in the fog until it is close enough to hear the herd's shimmer, then it *waits*, sometimes for hours, for one animal to sound half a breath late. Then all three move at once, and the herd, feeling the chord break, breaks with it.

But the bellcrakes have an answer, an old one. When the wolves are near, the herd does not go silent. It goes *loud*, all thirty animals sounding at once at a pitch just past comfortable, so that the sound fills the valley and the wolves cannot find the one flat note. Sometimes this drives the wolves off. Sometimes a herd sounds for a full day, hoarse and stumbling, and by evening there are twenty-eight of them and the note has dropped.

Between wolf and herd there is no hatred. There is a long slow conversation that has gone on since the mountains were raised, and each side has shaped the other's throat.

## Gliders, Choristers, and the Ones Between

The **ceiling-gliders** are a single society more than a species. They hang from the underside in clusters of hundreds, pale as bone, and every cluster keeps a private click-dialect. When two flocks meet in the air they slow, and the clicking changes, and sometimes one flock takes on a few of the other's members like a river accepting a tributary. They drink from the drip-tips and eat the white beetles that live in the seams, and where their droppings fall, the peat is rich and the pale mats grow thick. Bellcrakes follow the gliders for this reason. Where the clicking is loud, there is good grazing.

The **peat-choristers** are the valley's clock. They sleep buried in the floor for what passes as day, cold and slow, and wake in a wave. A single male's call is a small wet chirr, almost nothing. Ten thousand of them, each answering the neighbor to its left, become the breath that runs from one end of the valley to the other. The wave has a direction, and that direction changes with the season. Some old listeners can tell the month from it.

In the crater-pools, in the coldest water, lives something few have seen. It is long, eel-like, and colorless, and it feeds on the snails and small fish that come down with the falling columns. Its whole body is a lamp of very faint violet, and it swims in slow spirals under the surface, so that a pool at rest is sometimes ringed with a turning purple wheel. The people do not eat these. They say the pool-spirals are how the water counts the hours.

And in the fault-cracks, in the raw seams where the mountain is still opening, are pale things that have never been named because they have never been carried out. They are eyeless and bristly, and they eat the mineral-slick that the new springs lay down. They know nothing of the Lid. They are the only creatures in the hollow that do not listen for anything, and they may be the oldest.

## The Tenders

Very few have seen one. They walk the ceiling upside down, slow as slow weather, ponies of pale bristle and many hooked legs, and where they pass, the underside is trimmed clean, the drip-tips pruned, the ribs bare. They do not seem to notice anything below them. Once a season a tender may let go, out of age or accident, and fall, and the sound is remembered by everyone within a day's walk, a great soft crash followed by a long silence in which every herd stops calling. The body is left where it lies. The Lid sends down descenders toward it within the year.

The tenders may be the Lid's own mouth. Or its hands. Or its gardeners, as the bands say, though no one has asked the Lid.

## The People

They call themselves something that means, roughly, *the ones who follow the bells.* There are perhaps a few hundred of them in the whole hollow, in bands of ten or a dozen, and a band will meet another band twice in a lifetime.

They are small, dark, and lean, with large ears that they keep uncovered even in the cold, and they walk with a slight forward tilt, head cocked, like people always about to be told something. They speak very little. Speech competes with the drip and the hum, and a shout can turn a herd. What they say, they say low, close to the ear, and much of their conversation is done with the hands and with small tongue-clicks that are copied from the gliders. Children are taught to distinguish a hundred herd-tones before they are taught to walk carefully, and a child's first name is often a note, hummed, that the mother finds in the birth-hour and keeps.

Each band keeps a **listener**, usually an old person, sometimes a blind one. The listener does not hunt or build. She sits at the camp's edge in the fog and hears the valley, and it is her word that tells the band when to stay and when to leave. A band that ignores its listener is remembered in stories, briefly.

They do not kill bellcrakes often. They take the old and the lame, the ones the herd's note is already dragging on, and they take them with the herd's consent, or something they call that. A hunter goes out alone and sounds a horn crest, borrowed from a fallen animal, until the herd answers. Then he waits. If the herd will not answer, he goes home hungry.

The lichen pots are the heart of every camp. They are unglazed clay, stoppered with peat, each holding a palmful of blue-glowing moss that must be fed weekly with drip-water and a little dung. A band that loses its pots has lost its light and, they say, its memory. In the long dim of camp they gather around the pots and touch the lichen, one after another, and watch the light run through the fingers and fade. It is the nearest thing they have to a fire and to a story.

## The Ones Who Stack the Stones

Bands say they do not know who made the cairns. That is true. They will also tell you, if you have sat with them long enough, that the cairns are *kept*.

Cairns fall in quakes. A line of black stones on a scarp will lose three or four to a tremor and, a season later, be whole again, restacked with the same odd care, the top stone always the flat one. No band goes to the ridge-faces to do it. No band has ever seen who does.

Sometimes, at the highest edge of a valley, in the deepest fog, a listener will pick up a sound that is not herd, not glider, not chorister, not wolf. It is a low double knock, stone on stone, patient, at intervals of about a breath, moving slowly along the wall. It lasts an hour and is gone. The listener does not tell the band unless asked. The band, if asked, says they were only stones settling.

Above them there may be a people who live not under the Lid but in the seam between it and the mountain, in the thin rind where roots meet rock, and who have kept the old routes for reasons of their own. Or something else entirely. The rope bridge is re-twisted every year, and every year the band that does it finds, on the far side of the cleft, a small fresh pile of drip-tip pitch, set down on a flat stone like a gift, or a receipt.

They leave a pot of lichen in exchange. They never see who takes it.

## Evening

Toward the end of a long dim day, a band stops in a valley of tall pillars, and the listener sits down at the edge of the fog. The children, who have been quiet all day, are told they may speak, and do not.

The chorister wave begins at the north end of the valley. It runs beneath them like a breath. Far off a herd sounds a long low note, and another answers from the ridge across the water, a half-tone higher, and a third, further, drops in beneath. Over it all the Lid hums its enormous chord. The drip-tips tick. A glider flock passes overhead like a handful of thrown gravel. Somewhere a rain-column falls into its pool with a sound like torn cloth, and the violet wheel under the surface, invisible from here, turns once more.

The listener lifts one finger. That means: *stay.*

They stay. Above them, all the millions of leaves lean into the dark, and on the black peat the trails of blue light where they walked go slowly out, one after another, until only the lichen in the clay pots is left burning.
