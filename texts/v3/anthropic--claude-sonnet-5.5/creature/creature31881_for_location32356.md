# The Keeper of the Basins

One person lives in the bowl. There have been others, and there will be others, but the bowl holds only one at a time, and this is the one who is here now.

You will not see them at first. They wear felted wool the grey-gold of the tuff, crusted white at the seams with salt, and when cloud comes up over the lip the cloth darkens with the stones and the keeper darkens with it. Between the pinnacles, in the blue-white glow, a figure standing still is only another shape with flutes in it. It is the movement you notice eventually: slow and level, never a wasted step, as if the keeper carried a full cup across the whole bowl every day of their life.

## The body

They are built for the air. The ribcage is deep and wide, the shoulders held back and open, so that the chest seems to be a separate and larger creature riding inside the coat. Their lips and nail beds are the dark blue of slate in cloud. It is not sickness. It is what blood looks like when it has learned to live on very little. The pulse is slow. The breath is slow. Where a visitor gasps and rushes and is always a little ahead of their lungs, the keeper takes one long draw and lets it out in a thread of white, and does so unhurriedly, all day.

Their eyes are narrow and creased at the corners from sixty years of squinting, and over them they wear a strip of bone with a slit cut for each eye, tied at the back of the skull. The bone is yellow with age and polished by skin. Without it they would go blind in a season. They take it off only in the hollow, in the dark, and then they sit for a while with their eyes open and simply rest.

Their hands are cracked at every knuckle and always faintly damp. The salt gets into everything, and the keeper's skin has the tight, glazed look of something cured. Their fingertips are hard as horn. They can hold a basin of ice-lidded water and feel, through the lid, how long until it will need emptying.

## The work

The keeper's day is set by cloud, not sun.

When the cloud comes up, they go to the windward slabs and stand among them while the rime grows. They do not touch it. They watch the direction the feathers lean and how long they get, and from these they read what the wind is doing above the bowl, where the keeper cannot see. When the sun clears and the rime begins to shed, they move from basin to basin with a hide skin on a strap, and they pour. Each basin gets the same attention: a hand laid flat on the slab's fluting to feel if the runnels are running clear, a fingertip along the basin's lip to check for new chips. If the edge has gone rough, the keeper takes out a small hammerstone from the coat and dresses it, tapping so lightly that the sound is more like a bird's foot than a tool. The basins have gone smooth as spoons because of them. Every basin in the windward row has been reshaped by a hundred hands, each one thinking they were only maintaining it.

By late afternoon, before the runnels freeze, the skins must be full and sealed and carried to the hollow. This is the hurry in the keeper's day, and even it is not fast.

## What they do not know

The keeper knows the water. They know the flutes, the salt, and which slabs sweat at dawn and which at dusk. They do not know what the cairns are for.

They tend them anyway. The lean of each is watched. When frost heaves a flat stone out of place, it is set back exactly, and if it will not sit, the copper wedge is tapped tighter with a stone maul, a few strokes, no more. Some cairns have a wedge fresher than the rest, the green-black just starting to bloom. The keeper adds one when it feels right. Asked why, they would say it is not a matter of deciding. Something in the air of a particular morning says *this one*, and they go, and it is done. They might say the cairns count seasons. They might say the dead. They have said both, on different days, and meant both.

The same is true of the pegs. The keeper does not know what the pegs were for, but they know that every morning the pegs are white antlers, and that this is the most beautiful thing in the bowl, and so they walk the peg lines at first light, slowly, before doing anything else.

## The hollow

They sleep in the low walled place with the half-fallen roof. The floor is scraped clean every day. On the lintel are the tally-marks in fives, and the keeper adds to them, one scratch per day of a kind they can't quite explain: a day the cloud came three times, or a day something was mended. The spiral below the marks they do not add to. Sometimes, before sleeping, they trace it with a thumb, inward, and stop before the center.

## Sound

The keeper hums. It is the only tune in the bowl, and it is not really a tune. They find the pitch of the wind across the pinnacles and sit on it, and when a gust changes the note, they slide with it, a little late, a little flat, like someone trying to keep a friend company in a long silence. They do not speak to the stone. They speak to no one. But if you stood near enough in the thin air, you would hear two low notes braided and unresolved, one from the rock and one from a chest built to hold more air than the mountain has.

## The minute

Late afternoon, when the low sun pours through the gaps in long parallel blades and the rime lights one feather after another, the keeper stops wherever they are. They take off nothing, hurry nothing. They turn their face into it, bone slits and all, and stand while thousands of small fires come on around them, and the salt at their seams flashes white, and for that one minute they are not a shape among shapes but the brightest thing in the bowl, a figure lit from the edges.

Then the next cloud lifts over the lip. The wool darkens. The keeper picks up the skin, shifts the strap on their shoulder, and goes back to work, and within moments you can no longer say which of the dark upright shapes is the one that breathes.
