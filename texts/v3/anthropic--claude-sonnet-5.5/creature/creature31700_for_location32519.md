# Those Who Keep the Cup

Nothing lives in the Cup in the way you'd expect, so it takes a while to notice that something does.

You notice it first as a courtesy. You've been standing at the pool's edge for some minutes, and the drips have been falling in their shifting rain, and you realize that no drop has fallen on you. The fronds overhead are twenty feet across and beaded with water, and you are standing under two of them, and you are dry. The drops fall on either side. The rain has been arranging itself around you.

That is the first sign that the seven trees are not just trees.

## The Seven

They are not a family, exactly, and not a council, though visitors would reach for both words. They have no word for themselves, because they have never needed to tell one from the others except by weight and lean and how the water reaches them. But you can learn their differences if you stay long enough, and if you stay you will start naming them, and they will let you.

**The First** stands nearest the slot, on the far side of the pool. Its trunk is darker than the others, almost black-brown, and it is the only one that leans, a few degrees toward the arch, as if listening at a door. It feels the breath before the rest do. Its roots run down along the seam of the wall and touch the cold, and when the water begins to draw back, the First lets go of its held pressure a few seconds early. The others take their cue from that release. It is not a leader. It is the one nearest the pain, and it speaks first the way a hand jerks from a flame.

**The Second and Third** stand across from each other on the moss like two people who have shared a house for a hundred years. They are the most alike. They finish each other's water. When one drinks deeply after a hard rain, the other holds back, and this is not generosity; it is simply how they've learned to be a pair.

**The Fourth** is the oldest, or thinks itself so. Its trunk has gone pale and ribbed with age, and one great frond has died and hangs like a folded arm, and it will not drop it. It remembers, as much as anything here remembers, the people with the hammerstones. To the Fourth they were a warmth on the ground, a weight on the moss, a rhythm made in stone that the boulders answered and the roots felt through the floor. It has never forgotten the rhythm. In the long silences between breaths, its roots go still in a particular pattern that might be an attempt to play it back.

**The Fifth** is young, by the standards of the place, and restless, and is responsible for most of the small changes. The ferns on the eastern wall lean where they lean because the Fifth has been coaxing them for a few decades. It grows toward light with a kind of greed the others tolerate. In wet years its crown throws off new fronds in a rush, and the Fourth is said, in the root-slow way of such things, to disapprove.

**The Sixth** is the quiet one. Its canopy is the fullest and its shade the deepest, and beneath it the moss is thickest and coldest. The small gold frogs prefer it, and the snails gather on the boulder beside it. It has taken to sheltering things. Nobody asked it to.

**The Seventh** stands a little apart, on the side where the bark cord hangs. It holds that cord up on a low frond and has held it for longer than any of them can say. It doesn't know what a knot is. It knows the cord is not the Cup's, that it came from outside with a person, and that it was left. So the Seventh keeps it, the way you keep an object you don't understand for the sake of the one who set it down. The fronds around it have been taught, slowly, not to drip on the fibers.

## How they speak

They do not speak, in the sense you mean. They move water.

Beneath the moss, the seven root-masses are joined, not fused but woven, threaded through the black stone and through each other's fibers, and all of them run down to the same cold place at the base of the pool. Water is the medium. A tree that takes a long drink changes the pressure in the whole web. A tree that lets a frond die changes what flows. The drip you hear, ten thousand drops onto ten thousand surfaces, is not just rain. Much of it is them. Every drop that leaves a fern tip on its own slow schedule has been held there deliberately, released a fraction sooner or later than gravity alone would choose, and the sum of those small decisions is the sound of the Cup, which is the sound of seven trees thinking in company.

If you could read it, it would say small things, mostly. *Cold here. Wet here. Someone on the north wall. The light is leaving.* It says the day over and over in the language of weight.

## The argument

There is one question they have never settled.

The bands in the moss on the eastern side, pale and dark, are the pool's old high-water marks. Every tree can feel them. Their roots have grown through those years. And the bands are narrowing, so the breath is changing, but the trees disagree about how.

The First says the breath is weakening. It is nearest the slot and feels the draw thinning year on year, the inhale less hungry, the fall a little shallower. The Fourth agrees, grimly, with the authority of age.

The Fifth says the opposite. The breath is deepening: the water is dropping to a lower shelf and rising from a deeper place, and the narrow bands are just the pool learning to stretch. It has no evidence but its own appetite for the future.

The Second and Third say nothing. The Sixth says nothing. The Seventh is too occupied with the cord.

The argument has run for longer than any of them can remember, carried out in pressure and in patience, and it has never grown heated. Nobody is trying to win. To disagree slowly in company is one of the ways the seven stay together.

## What lives in the trees, and what lives around them

The frogs are not theirs, but they are tolerated, and the trees hold water in the round leaves so the frogs will have somewhere to sing. The snails draw their silver lines on the boulders, and the trees consider this a kind of writing and do not read it. The dark bird with the blue throat, who looks down into the bowl with such apparent professionalism, is, as far as the trees can tell, an inspector. It comes, it assesses, it leaves. They have never learned its findings. They suspect, on some level, that it is satisfied.

And the boulders ring. They are not alive, but the trees treat them as kin, since the stones were the trees' first companions and remember, in their ringing, what the hammers taught.

## The one below

Nobody in the Cup has met whatever breathes through the slot.

The First comes closest. It lives with its roots against the seam and feels the cold rising from the deeper chamber, the slow weight of a great volume of water shifting, but it has never touched the source. The others know the breather only as the event that gives their day its shape: the long rise, the hold, the sudden draw, the groan that rolls up through their trunks and out into the air.

They do not know whether the breather is an animal, or a spring, or a shape the stone makes when the water finds it. They do not know whether it knows they are there. They only know that when it exhales, they answer. All seven let go at once, the stones hum, the frogs chirp, and for one minute the whole Cup is a single instrument being played by something that doesn't know it has an audience.

Perhaps that is what the trees are for. Perhaps the breather, if it listens, hears through the roots seven small pressures giving back its own note, and understands that the note has been heard.

## Toward evening

When the light turns amber the drip slows, and the trees begin to settle. The mist column stands over the pool. The Seventh adjusts its fronds a fraction so the cord hangs true. The Fourth, in the dark of its roots, goes still in the old pattern, the hammer rhythm, one more time. The First leans a degree closer to the arch.

And they wait, seven trees around a round dark pool, not hungry or afraid, doing the only work they have ever been given, which is to be there when the slot draws in the water, and to answer.
