# Who Lives on the Floor

## The Stitching

The first residents are the smallest, and they moved in before the concrete had finished curing.

Each tie-hole in the slab faces, the round stub-holes left where the formwork was bolted through, has become a house. A blenny sits in most of them, a fringed, frowning, pop-eyed fish no longer than a finger, with its head just out of the hole and its body wedged behind. Along a wall of rows, a hundred, then a thousand of them hold the same pose, a stitched line of small faces watching the current. They are absurdly territorial. Each defends a hole the width of a coin against neighbors it could touch with a fin, flaring a crest of red frills and darting out to bite the air. The slabs have the look of a tenement in the middle of an argument.

The black seams between the slabs hold something larger. Conger eels lie in them, gray and thick as a man's thigh, most of their length tucked out of sight and the blunt head held at the seam's lip. They are older than the slabs' first winter, and they came before the barnacles, before the diatoms had made the concrete slick. Nobody knows how a conger finds a new crack in the world. But every seam wide enough has an occupant, and the occupants know one another by scent and by the vibration of one another's swallowing. They come out at dusk, when the light ropes fade, and slide across the floor in long pale-bellied ribbons to hunt the silt. In the morning each returns to its own seam without error.

Beside the congers, and under them, and in the narrowest gaps, live the lobsters. The antennae you see waving from the dark belong to them, blue-black animals with one claw larger than the other, standing sentry in doorways they have modified by dragging out grit. Squat lobsters cluster behind them like children hiding. Shore crabs, edible crabs, and spider crabs fill the seams that are left, and the whole seam-system is a slow, crowded, clicking city that no one on the surface has yet thought to count.

## The Shoal-Towns

Around every turbine tower, a town.

The herring are the citizens, and they behave less like animals than like a weather system. At rest they hang in the lee of the tower, a silver wall thirty meters tall, so tightly packed that the sun ropes flash from them in a single pulse when they turn. They turn constantly. The instant a porbeagle drifts in or a seal arcs down, the wall folds, splits around the attacker, and rejoins on the far side, having lost a few dozen and none of its shape. The individuals have no idea what the shoal is doing. Each fish tracks its neighbors' flanks and nothing else, and the flight pattern falls out of that.

Cod hold the base. They hang head-down, mottled brown and green, chins whiskered with a single barbel, patient in the shadow of the tower's foot where the dropped herring come spiraling down. They are the tower's old residents and the most conservative. A cod will occupy the same square meter of slab for a season, grunting at intruders, and will not be moved even by a passing dredger, only shuffle aside a body-length and settle again when the noise has gone. Pollock hover higher, in the middle of the water, restless and quick. Haddock work the silt below. Together they form strata, each level a rung on the same ladder, and the ladder stands in a field of hundreds of towers, each tower a village that knows its own herring.

Wolffish live among them, though few ever see one. They are heavy, blunt-headed, grim-faced fish with front teeth like a row of pegs, which they use to crack whelks and sea urchins. The gate walls' ribbed faces give them deep horizontal shelves, and there they sit for years, in pairs, mated for life, eyes fixed on the current. They are the most solemn residents of the Basin, and probably the most at home.

## The Hunters

The porbeagle sharks patrol in loose lines between the towers, and the fields are their beat. They are stocky, gray-blue, warm-bodied, faster than anything else in the water, with a pale crescent tail and a black unblinking eye. They read the rotors the way a gardener reads rows. The *whump* of a blade passing its tower makes a small pressure pulse, and the herring flinch with it, and the sharks time their approach to the flinch. A porbeagle will hold station for an hour, drifting through the pulses, and then take a fish in the fraction of a second between two blades. They are patient, but they are also, plainly, curious. Several have begun to follow the survey crews' lamps.

The seals came in through the outflow channels, adventurous juveniles that found the shoals and stayed, and they now form a resident population of several hundred. They are lazy, clever, and utterly unafraid of the noise. They hunt in the towers' lee, ride the ebb currents like children on a slide, and know the gate schedule to the minute. Before the gates close, they rise to breathe at the surface, trusting the still water that follows, and it is possible, on a quiet day, to see the whole colony ascend in a loose spiral of dark bodies and bubbles.

Rays lie in the silt at the foot of the gates, thornback and small-eyed, flat and mottled and almost invisible. Only their eyes show, two raised beads turning to follow anything that moves. Flatfish share the silt with them, plaice with their orange spots and dabs the color of sand, and the whole bottom layer seems to be watching you back.

## The Visitors

In the spring the plankton blooms around the nutrient outfalls, and the Basin fills with something enormous.

The **basking sharks** come in slowly. They are the largest fish in these waters, ten meters long, with mouths a meter wide held open in a permanent gape as they cruise, and they take in the blooms like a floating net. They pass through the tower fields without hurry, and the herring hardly bother to move. For a few weeks they drift along the gate walls and the long empty stretches between the slabs, a dozen at a time or more, gray and speckled, casting shadows the size of buses. Nothing hunts them. They do nothing to anyone. And it is in those weeks, with the great slow shapes moving across the emptiness, that the Basin looks almost as though it were built for what it holds.

Some of the crews say the size of the place was planned for them. Nobody in the offices confirms it.

## The Crews

People come and go. They live above, in the floating rigs and tenders, and descend in bells or on umbilicals to work in shifts. Down here they are visitors, in bright helmets, bubbling and clanking, hauling tools along the slabs. The fish have adjusted. The blennies retreat into their holes and glare. The cod pay no attention. The herring split around the divers like they split around the towers.

Some machines have stayed so long they've stopped being machines. A survey ROV, snagged on a seam edge months ago and never recovered, sits at the foot of a gate wall wearing a beard of hydroids and a crust of barnacles, with a lobster in its thruster housing. The cod treat it as one more tower.

The dredger crews rarely see the animals. They operate their machines from control rooms miles away and watch the floor on screens. But they know the inhabitants by the way the images change. They know the pale flicker of the herring, the sudden blossoming of a silt cloud when a ray bolts, the shadow of a conger's head withdrawn as the wheel comes near. They have given the seams names.

## The Garden

The kelp lattices are the nearest thing to a forest. In the brown light beneath the fronds live the small, secretive things: pipefish, thin as pencils and colored exactly like the blades, lumpsuckers clamped on the stipes by their sucker discs, sea hares, tiny crustaceans in thousands. Wrasse work through the curtains, nibbling parasites and shrimp, and young cod use them as nurseries. The kelp is planted, but its inhabitants are not. They arrived on their own, by larvae and drift and swimming, and made the curtains a place worth living in. Where kelp has seeded itself along the seams in thin dark tufts, the same small creatures have followed it, one step ahead of anyone's plan.

## The Frontier

Past the last survey marker, where the slabs are still wet-gray and rebar bristles from unpoured edges, the fish are already waiting. Small ones first, the blennies inspecting each new tie-hole days after the forms come off. Then the crabs, pushing out of the last seam into the new one. Then the cod, arriving to sit beneath the floating rig and its shadow, as if the shadow itself were a structure.

That is the character of everything that lives on the Floor. It makes no distinction between the wild and the built, and it does not wait for permission. The concrete is poured, and the tie-holes are occupied. The tower turns, and the shoals gather. The gates cycle, and the seals learn the hour. The residents of the Long Basin are the ones who were not invited, arriving faster than the builders can work, settling each new edge and crack and shadow into a place that is theirs.
