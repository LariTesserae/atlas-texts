# Who Lives in the Wringing

Six hundred people live here, and they are the smallest voice in it. They know this and have arranged their manners accordingly.

## The people

They are short and broad through the thigh and calf, built by stairs. Their feet are wide, with toes that spread and grip, and the soles are thick as boot leather and permanently pruned. Their skin is never quite dry, and there is a sheen on the forehead and the backs of the hands that visitors take for sweat. It is only the air. Their hands are the most telling part: soft from constant damp, but ridged across the palms and finger-joints with cord calluses, pale grooves where a lifetime of lashing has sat. They talk with their hands a great deal. At dawn, when the birds turn the air solid, whole conversations pass across a lane in gestures: *bring the hook, the north gutter's slow, is your grandmother up.*

They wear wool dyed in the colors of the place, moss-green, bark-brown, a rusty red from a lichen, and over it the reed rain cape, layered like a wren's nest, which they never fully take off indoors. Hair is worn braided tight and often wound with a thread of red cord, so a person can be found by it in fog. Their voices have a flat, carrying quality, pitched low and forward against the water. They laugh loudly and without embarrassment, having never known a quiet room in which to be embarrassed.

They are not a solemn people, though the town could have made them so. Living against a tide that never stops has given them a cheerful, faintly ironic patience. They say *it will grow back* about a cut vine, a fallen wall, and a grief, in exactly the same tone, and no one considers this cold.

## What they call themselves

*The Wrung.* It began as an insult from the salt-carriers, who said you had to wring the people out before you could speak to them. The town took it up. Wringing is what they do to cloud, to cape, to cord, to the mountain's damp breath. A child learns to wring a cloth before learning to write her name, and can twist a cord from three strands of fiber before she can count past twenty. There is a saying that a person is what they can hold water in, and another, more common, that a person is what stays when the water has been squeezed out.

## Names

A child is named for the sound the town was making when it was born. A baby delivered under the hollow bonk of the barrel-frog is *Bonk*, or more properly *Hollowknock*. One born in a downpour is a Roar. A child born in the rinsed minute of sun is a Glare, which is the most prized name and a mild burden. There are Tinks and Whistles, a pair of grandmothers both called Trill, and a stern, respected cordwright named Drip. Adults take a second name from their work, so that Drip Hookhand, Roar of the Kiln, and Whistle Lashwright are clear enough to any neighbor.

## The work, and the ones who do it

Nobody is idle, and nobody is exactly employed. Everyone carries a tool at the belt, a hook or a knife or a coil of cord, and everyone answers to the rules. But some have made a life of one thing.

**The Hookers** keep the gutters. They walk the lanes with long hooked poles, lifting the drowned leaves out of the channels, and they can read a blockage from the sound of the water alone, a stammer in the flow. They are honored in a rough way: no one pays them, and they are never refused a meal.

**The Roof-keepers** are mostly old women, and they are terrifying about moss. They go up on the ridges in reed capes with curved knives and decide, house by house, how much green a roof may keep. They can look at a thatch and say when it will rot and which fig seed will split it. They are often right. They are also the town's best gossips, since a roof is the one place from which every lane can be overheard.

**The Cordwrights** twist the vine cord for the bridges. They say a cord has a voice, and the whole town half-believes them. Every child learns the sound of a failing strand, a tiny dry ticking like a beetle chewing, and will freeze on a bridge at it, and shout.

**The Webwalkers** are children, roughly six to ten. Every dawn they go ahead of the adults down the lanes with long sticks, gathering the night's spider-webs onto the tips like spun sugar. They are proud of the job and very solemn about it. Each web is carried to the midden and dropped, and the spiders, who have nothing against anyone, start over that evening.

**The Horn-keeper** sleeps in the room below the great horn and has done so, in an unbroken line, for as long as anyone has counted. The post passes to whoever wakes fastest. Two blasts empty the town toward whatever wall is falling, and the keeper has been known to sound it from a dead sleep with the reflexes of a struck bell.

**The Kiln-scheduler** holds the one flat dry place in town and is therefore the most negotiated-with person alive. It is a thankless post. It is also a position of great quiet power, and its holder is treated with the careful courtesy one shows to weather.

## The others who live here

The town does not think of itself as separate from what surrounds it, and neither should the account.

**The frogs** have standing. A frog on a doorpost is not moved. A frog in a gutter is stepped around, and the Hookers lift them out with the leaves, gently, and set them on the nearest wet stone. Every household knows which frog is theirs, meaning the one that has lived in their cistern longest. The barrel-frog in the lower basins is called the Cooper and is spoken of fondly. When the Cooper falls silent for a night, people ask after it, and when it starts up again there is a small general relief.

**The hummingbirds** are thought of as an angry relative's temper, always about, always somewhere just past the ear. **The wrens** in the walls are taken for the town's real critics. **The parakeets** are the news, since everything is shrieked past everyone twice a day.

**The guinea pigs** are the domestic class. They live under the floors and whistle for greens at the sound of a knife on a cutting-board, and every family speaks to them as to a small, greedy, over-trusted committee. **The llamas** on the upper terraces are the town's only aristocracy, and they behave like it.

**The bats** are accepted at dusk as a tide, pouring from the eaves in a dark stream. **The marsupials** on the beams at night are merely known, and left alone, unless one gets in the flour.

## What they believe

Not much that could be called a creed. There is a strong, practical religion of *moving*. Water must move, cord must be renewed, the horn must be answered, the walls must be rebuilt. To stop is a sin only in the sense that it is a mistake with consequences. They do not fear the green tide. They regard it the way a swimmer regards a river, as something you can do a great deal with as long as you never expect it to stop.

The dead are laid in the rock terraces below the shelf and given to the ferns within a season. No one marks the place. It is understood that the mountain will keep them the way it keeps everything, by making them into more of itself.

## At night

When the mist thickens and the lamps blur amber in every window, they gather in the doorways without quite deciding to. Someone is always drumming lightly on a hollow log to call a child in. Someone is always coughing from the smoke. The frogs come up to full chorus, and the people fall into a habit older than speech, which is to listen. Nobody talks. Six hundred people sit in the wet dark with their hands in their laps and let the Wringing say what it has to say, and it says it in a thousand voices at once, none of them human, and all of them somehow addressed to the ones who stayed.

Then someone laughs at something, low. Someone else answers. The lamp behind them gutters and holds. The town closes over the small sound the way the forest closes over a lane, and by morning, the Webwalkers will need their sticks.
