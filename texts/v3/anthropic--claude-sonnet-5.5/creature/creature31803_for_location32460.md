# Those Who Attend

You will not see them first. You will see the pillar-rosettes standing apart, the hut small at the edge of sight, the cairns and the chiming poles. You will think the shelf is empty and that someone has just left. Then a patch of moss on the ridge, which you took for a rust-colored hummock, lifts a hand.

They have been watching you since you crested the scree, and probably before that, because the hyrax sentinel changed its whistle when you were still an hour down the slope. They read that whistle the way you would read a bell.

## Who They Are

There are twenty-three in the warm months and three in the cold. They call themselves something that means *the ones who attend*, though the word has the shape of a whistle more than a word: two rising notes and a held one, pitched to cut through the falls. In any voice you could make, it comes out as a sigh.

They are short and broad through the chest, built for thin air. The men and women look alike from a distance: deep ribcages, thick calves, and large quick hands, held always a little out from the body, ready to speak. Their skin is the color of wet bark, roughened and reddened across the cheekbones by wind and by the constant fine spray. Their eyelids are heavy and their eyes narrow against the wet, so they seem to be squinting even in fog. They watch mouths and hands more than eyes. A conversation among them looks like two people trying to catch each other's small falling things.

They wear cloaks of woven blue tussock, shaggy and gray-green, lined on the inside with the silver fur of pillar-rosette leaves, which sheds water and holds warmth. Seen lying in the moss, they are nearly invisible. Their boots are hide stuffed with dry moss, changed each morning. They grease their hair into thin ropes with hyrax fat and tie the ends with bits of shell. A few wear a single bone bead at the throat. Each bead is a whistle, with a hole bored in it, and each has its own pitch.

## How They Speak

They use four languages, and which one they use depends on the distance and on the hour.

**Hand-speech** is for anything within arm's reach, and for anything within sight and out of earshot. It is fast and full of weather: a flat palm tilted means *the cloud is thin*, fingers fluttering downward means *the swifts are dropping*, and there are eleven distinct ways to say *water*. They do most of their talking this way. A stranger passing through would hear almost nothing from them and might think them a silent people.

**Whistle-speech** is for the distance. It is pitched above the roar, high and piercing, made with a fingertip against the lip or with the bone bead held in the teeth. It travels across the entire shelf. It has grammar. It also borrows heavily from the animals, and the hyrax sentinel's calls have been folded into it: the long falling note for *hawk*, the double chirp for *mustelid in the moss*. Children begin whistling before they can walk, and the first thing they learn to say is a warning.

**Stone-speech** is for the far end of the shelf and for the night. Each person carries a pair of river-cobbles, smooth and dense, which they knock together in rhythms. Names are rhythms. Someone's name is knocked to call them, and a knocked name carries through the dark and through the falls' hollow undertone in a way whistling does not. A person can tell who is knocking from the sound of the stone alone, the way you know a friend's step.

**The white speech** comes once a day, and it is the one they love.

## The Hour of the Cloud

When the swifts spiral down and the frogs go silent, the people stop whatever they are doing. They do not hurry. They walk out from wherever they are and sit or kneel in the open moss, spaced apart, facing east. They wait. The cloud pours up over the lip, and the roar softens to a muffled rush, and the shelf becomes a small, white, close room.

Then, and only then, they use their voices.

It is low, ordinary speech, murmured, as if nobody wants to break something. Because the roar is gone, they can finally be heard without effort. This is the hour for everything that cannot be whistled or gestured: stories, teaching, jokes, arguments, requests for forgiveness, the naming of newborns, the talk of the dead. They say nothing that matters outside it if they can help it.

Quarrels are settled here. In the cloud you cannot see the other person's hands, so you have only their voice, and neither of you can storm off, because you would walk off the edge. A grievance carried into the white is aired to the end. Then the cloud drains and the roar returns, and the matter is finished. It is bad manners to raise it again in the noise.

They believe, or say they believe, that whatever is said in the cloud belongs to the cloud. It is taken down with the drained white and not to be repeated. Children learn this early, and it is the reason the adults are always a little strange with each other in the hour after: gentle, a bit shy, avoiding eye contact, as though they had all been briefly naked.

## What They Do All Day

Most of the day is work, and most of it is keeping.

They re-lay the stones of the channels where frost has heaved them, lifting each one, scraping the peat from beneath, and setting it back with a small grunt. They dip the clay basin clean. They thin the moss from the runnels so the water does not back up and drown a cushion-mound. They restack the cairns, and each time they do, they take the quartz cap in both hands, turn it to the light, and set it back. The route across the shelf is a line of memory, and they walk it daily so that it never becomes a route someone else could find.

They re-thatch the pillar-rosettes. Thatch is what the plants wear, but the people help it along, combing dead leaves down into a tight skirt and picking out the beetles. Some of the older trunks are hollow, and in the coldest weeks a person will sleep in one, curled in the warm dead-leaf dark, with the silver crown closed over them like a lid. The rosettes do not object. The small brown birds with the pipe-ringing voices nest in the same trunks and seem to regard the people as another kind of bird.

They gather the yellow-flowered mat plant, which is bitter and sharp and eaten raw, and they dig the fat roots of the blue tussock. They take the eggs of the swifts from a certain ledge in a certain week and no other. They trap frogs sparingly, leaving the population for the mustelid, because they say the mustelid is the shelf's tax collector and must be paid. They take a little milk from the shaggy long-legged goats, who tolerate this from a certain few people and bolt from anyone else. They do not hunt the hyraxes, or almost never; the hyraxes are the shelf's sentries, and the sentinel's whistle is too valuable.

They also do something harder to describe. Every morning, before the sun crosses the far ridge, they go out onto the frozen moss while the streams are still slow and the rime is white on everything. They put their palms on the largest boulders and wait. They are feeling for the hum. If it is strong, the day is good. If it is thin and uneven, they say the shelf is troubled, and everyone is a bit quieter and stays near the hut. When the sun finally strikes and the whole shelf thaws in a wave of dripping and popping and trickling, they lift their hands off the stone, as if the shelf had just finished a sentence.

## The Chimes and the Carving

The poles on the windward ridge are not a signal, though they are sometimes mistaken for one. Each strand of hollowed bone and shell belongs to one person, and each person makes their own from the day they are named. When a person is alive and on the shelf, their strand hangs on the near side of the ridge. When they are away, the strand is moved to the far pole. When someone dies, the strand is taken up to the last pole, at the very end, where the wind is strongest.

So the chimes are a census. Any of the attending can stand among them and listen, and know who is here, who is gone, and who is only a sound. The wind plays the dead in the same breath as the living. The old ones say this is the point: the shelf is loud, and nobody should ever be quiet in it.

The spiral carving near the lip was there before any of them. They don't know who pecked it. Every year at the turn of the season they carry water from the basin in cupped hands and pour it into the grooves, one full handful for each person alive. The spiral fills and shines. Then they leave it to the falls.

## The Hut and the Blade

The hut is the keepers' house, not the whole people's. Most of them sleep out of doors in hollows or trunks or in shelters of turf, and come to the hearth in the evening. The hearth is never allowed to go fully out. Someone always feeds it, even in summer, and this is why the ashes are never old. It is not for warmth. It is because the first fire is said to be the same fire, passed hand to hand, and none of them wants to be the one who lets it die.

The small crawl-door is not an accident. You must go in on your knees and elbows, with your head down. Anyone who comes in must arrive humbled and quiet. It also keeps out the wind.

The copper blade in the wall is older than the hut's present roof. No one remembers how it came. The story they tell in the cloud-hour is that a stranger climbed up out of the cloud one autumn, so thin and cold he could barely stand, and was fed and warmed at the fire. He stayed the winter, learned some hand-speech, and never spoke of where he came from. In spring he went back down and did not return. The blade was in the wall the morning he left. They have never taken it out. Some say it was a gift, some that he forgot it. One old woman says it is a question he left and it is still waiting for the answer.

Once every few years, one of the young people goes down to see if there is anything at the bottom of the descent. They take cured hyrax pelts, dried yellow flowers, and a rope of twisted grass. They are gone for a season. Most return with a small, incomprehensible object, such as a scrap of cloth or a glass bead, and tell the whole shelf about it in the white. Some do not return. Their strands are moved to the far pole. The people do not blame them.

## What They Fear

They do not fear the mustelid or the hawk. Those are the shelf's ordinary business, and death is loud and quick and closes over.

They fear the day the cloud does not come. They say it has happened once, long ago. The swifts dropped and the frogs went silent, and they all knelt and waited. The cloud did not rise. The silence held and held, until the swifts began to fall out of the sky. The old story ends there, and nobody knows how it resolved, and nobody likes to be asked.

They also fear, a little, that the hum will one day stop.

## How the Dead Are Kept

When one of them dies, they carry the body to one of the old hollow pillar-rosettes, and lay it in the dry dark of the trunk, in the warmth of the thatch. At dawn the silver crown, which closed in the night, opens with its faint creak, and closes again at dusk. Over time the thatch thickens, and the trunk takes the person in. Small brown birds nest above. The people say the pillar is then a person standing still, and they walk past it with their hands lowered, palms out, as one does to an elder.

There are eleven such pillars, and the living know each by name.

## To Meet Them

If you come up out of the scree and you are patient, someone will eventually stand. They will lift both hands, palms out, fingers spread, and hold them there without moving. This means *we see you; we do not yet know you.* If you answer by lifting your own, they will approach, slowly, by the route of the cairns and not straight across the moss.

They will not speak. They will touch your sleeve, your wrist, the back of your hand, and look at your mouth. They will offer water from the clay basin in a cupped palm. Then they will lead you toward the hut, knocking a stone as they go, a rhythm that means a stranger, no harm.

If it is nearly afternoon, they will stop and look at the swifts. And if the swifts are dropping, they will gently, without a word, put you down in the moss among them, and wait with you, facing east, for the white to come in and for the shelf, at last, to be quiet enough to talk.
