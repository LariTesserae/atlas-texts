# Those Who Keep the Table

Most of the plateau is empty of them. This is the first thing to understand about the people of the Tabletop, if *people* is the word: they are rare, and they are everywhere their work is. You cross an acre of bare, felt-slicked pavement and meet nothing. Then the stone rises in a ragged stand of pale chimneys, and the water around it is thick with small bodies, the way a single lit window can mean a whole house.

## The Wrights

A wright is a soft thing that has decided to be a hard thing, one drop of secretion at a time.

The body is a pale ribbon, translucent enough that you can see the dark thread of the gut pulsing inside it, a slow peristalsis that carries food down and leaves the body's reckoning of the sea behind. The animal never leaves its tube. It lives head-up, tail-down, braced in a sleeve of its own making. Along its flanks run bands of glands, each band laying a different substance: a sticky organic mucus, a paste of dissolved mineral, a thin sealing varnish. Together they harden into a wall no thicker than a fingernail. A wright adds to the rim of its tube as it grows, and it grows only upward, because that is where the food is. The tail end crumbles away below. Each wright rises on the ruin of its own past.

Look closely at a tube wall and you will find it banded, thin ivory against thin ochre, one pair for each turning of the tide. It is the floor in miniature, the same record written a thousand times faster. A wright cannot read it. But when a surge tears a tube in half, and the survivor stretches out of the broken end to begin the rim again, it does so at the exact height where the last tide's band ended, as though it knew where it had left off.

Their crowns are their faces. When the current turns, and only then, each wright thrusts out a fan of feathered threads, a hand's breadth across, transparent as breath. Falling matter sticks to the filaments: flakes of dead plankton, the fine dust of unmaker mud, the sweet dropped crumbs of the world above. The wright combs the fan through its mouth one thread at a time. A stand of a few hundred, all fanning together in a steady flow, looks like frost growing upward. The pale wall of stems hums low and uneven. Each length of tube is a note, and the wrights do not hear it, but they feel it in the base of the tube. A wright that grows out of tune with its neighbors, too tall or too short, tends to be the one the surge takes first. Whether this is chance or the current's judgment, no wright can say.

They reproduce in a slow cloud. On the slack water after a great surge, the survivors release clouds of eggs and sperm from the open crowns. The larvae drift and swim upward, then sink, tasting the stone. What they are looking for is mud. The pale ribbons the unmakers leave carry a chemical sign, and larvae settle wherever it is thickest. So each new colony begins in the wake of an animal that was eating the last one. The wrights build on what devoured them, and they do not seem to mind.

## The Weavers

The sheet-weavers are humbler and stranger. Each is a flat, oval animal the size of a dinner plate, mottled gray and cream, with no eyes and no crown. Its whole margin is a sensing edge. A fringe of tiny hairs tells it which way the current flows, how strong, and how much silt rides in it. It moves by a wave of muscle beneath the body, so slowly that watching one is like watching a stain spread.

Where it goes, it builds. A gland along the front edge extrudes a film of clear slime. The weaver drags itself across the pavement, drawing the film out in a sheet, and the sheet cures behind it, going from wet to glassy in a few minutes. Overlapping passes make a roof. Where the roof meets a wright-stump or a ridge of old stone, the weaver seals it with a bead of harder secretion. The result is a gallery: a low tunnel a body-length wide and a hand tall, lit only by whatever glows within it.

The weavers do not live in their galleries so much as leave them behind. Other things move in. Blind crustaceans hang from the ceiling by their hooked legs. Small silver fish rest in the dead-air pockets. Shrimp tick along the walls, grazing the felt that grows on the underside of the roof, where seeps of chemical water come through the cracks. The weaver has made a house without meaning to, and the house makes a village.

When the surge comes, the roofs rise off the stone like a lid, crack along their longest stresses, and drift apart in plates. The weavers, clamped low, are mostly spared. They wait out the silt. When the water clears, they go on laying film across the ruin, as unhurried as ever, not seeming to grieve. Whether a weaver remembers what it has built is not known. It is possible that the fringe of hairs remembers only the flow, and that every gallery is the first gallery.

## The Unmakers

The unmakers are the only individuals on the plateau.

Each is the size of a draft horse, a low, ridged mass under overlapping plates, with a broad muscular foot that grips the stone and a mouth on the underside like a rasp or a plow. They move so slowly that they appear to have been placed. You know they are alive by what follows them: a pale, fine, endless ribbon of excreted mud, coiled in their wake like paste from a tube.

They eat the works. A wright colony, a weaver's roof, a fluted stump that has stood for a hundred years, all go under the rasp. The stone passes through the animal and comes out finer. Nothing else is digested but the thin organic glue that held it, the residue of the builders' bodies, and the felt that grew on the surface. The mud is the plateau's soil. The unmakers are its earth-movers and its gardeners, and because they live for many decades, they carry the plateau's history on their backs.

Their plates are scarred. Old surges have cracked them and they have healed in ridges. Barnacle-like animals, bristle-worms, and small anemones colonize the plates, and on some old bulls these communities are thick as a coral head. The felt grows in patches. When one unmaker meets another, they stop and touch, plate to plate, for a long time, though no one has learned what passes between them. Sometimes they walk together for a season. Sometimes one dies, and the rest of the herd feeds on its plates, and the stone from the dead animal's own body becomes the mud that seeds the next colony.

When the surge comes, they do the only sensible thing. They turn to face it, lower the plates to the stone, and clamp the foot. The wall of silt breaks over them and rolls on. Afterward they lift, shaking off grit, and look no different. They have the patience of things that outlast what they eat.

And they will not eat the seams.

The oldest seams are the places where the floor's laminae surface as a dark, glossy vein, no wider than a hand. The unmakers graze right up to the edge of one, then lift their mouths and steer around. The felt grows thicker there, in a deep dark green. The weavers will not lay film across a seam. The wright larvae, tasting the stone, drift over it and settle elsewhere. The animals do not seem to fear it. They seem, instead, to defer, the way a crowd will leave a space around something it cannot name. And sometimes, when the water is quiet, the seams give off a faint pulse, a warmth against the cold, a trace of a hum. It may be only the leak of chemical water. The animals steer around it anyway.

## The Small and the Swarming

Around these three, the small lives crowd.

**Amphipods** drift in clouds like blown ash. They graze the felt and the tube-walls and the dropped fall of dead matter from above, and they are the plateau's principal food. When a surge strikes, they rise in a single flickering cloud, and for a moment the whole water fills with tiny pale bodies. Then they settle, and the fish come to feed.

**The silver fish** are eyeless and shoal in hundreds, sensing each other by pressure along a line down the flank. They turn together with a sound like a tearing sheet, so sudden that the water seems to rip. They graze the amphipods and hide in the galleries, and they are the main prey of the eel-things.

**The eel-things** are long, colorless, nearly all mouth, and they thread through the tubes like needles through cloth. They lie in wait in the hollows of wright colonnades with their jaws slightly open, and the silver fish, sensing nothing, drift by. A strike is a small white click.

**The translucent shrimp** live everywhere: in the tunnels, on the tubes, in the felt. They crackle constantly, a fine dry sound like frying, and the crackle is the plateau's background noise.

**The jellies** hang in the twilight above, ribbed and slow, their trailing tentacles gleaming with a faint cold fire. They fish the drifting water. Occasionally one comes down too low, and the surge takes it, and it is torn apart in the silt. Its pieces fall to the felt and feed it.

## What They Share

Nothing here has eyes worth the name. The dominant senses are touch and pressure, the taste of water, the feel of current against skin. Light is something the animals make for each other. A shrimp flashes at another shrimp. A weaver's margin glimmers faintly when disturbed. After a surge, when silt hangs thick in the water, these lights catch on every particle, and for a few minutes the plateau is a field of low stars, and everything alive on it is visible to everything else.

They have no history except the floor. They have no future except the next surge. They live in a time measured by the tide, and each of them, from wright to weaver to unmaker, spends its days making what will be eaten.

## The Groan

Sometimes, from beyond the edge, comes the note.

It begins below hearing, in the sternum, in the plates. The shrimp stop crackling. The silver fish drop from the water into the gallery floors. The wrights withdraw their crowns and the whole stand hums a half-tone lower as the tubes lose their fans. The weavers press flat. The unmakers, in their slow herds, turn to face the sound, plates down, feet locked. The jellies sink. For a long while nothing moves.

No one has seen what makes it. It comes from below the lip of the plateau, from the falling dark, and it could be a living thing or a shifting of the seamount or the sea itself groaning against its own weight. The animals do not care to know. They know only that within the day, the water will lean, and the wall of silt will rise off the dunes, and the colonnades will break.

And when it has passed, the survivors will lift from the stone and unfold their fans into the cloudy dark. The unmakers will resume their grazing. The weavers will begin to lay their film. The wright larvae will drift downward in the settling silt, tasting for mud, and the first pale tube of the next colony will rise from the stone, one ring at a time, in the exact place where the last one fell.
