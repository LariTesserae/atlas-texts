# Who Lives on the Green Floor

## The Crowd Beneath Notice

The first inhabitants are too many to count and too small to meet. A single cushion of moss the size of a cottage holds more lives than the walkers have ever numbered among all their peoples. Stilt-beetles pick across the plate-ferns as though testing each frond for a lie. Lacework spiders sit in the centers of their chandeliers, and when the noon rain comes they fold themselves into knots the size of seeds and let the water go through their webs like a hand through hair. The gliding frogs are flat and green, and are so much a part of the cistern-lilies that a lily with a resident frog looks like a lily with a second, darker heart. The coal-birds run in their thousands with a sound like a handful of gravel thrown across a drum, and the rope-grass closes behind them.

These creatures do not know they live on a floor made of their ancestors. The floor is simply what there is.

## The Walkers

The people have a name for themselves, but it is not a word. It is a sign made with the flat of one hand moving over the back of the other, a palm passing across a surface. Outsiders, if there were any, would gloss it as *those who go on top*. In speech they use no name at all, since the sound would not carry.

They are lean, long-legged, and deep in the chest, built for three kilometers of altitude and days of carrying. Their feet are broad, with toes that spread and grip, and the soles are thick but not callused, kept sensitive on purpose. A walker who cannot feel the mat through the foot is considered half-blind. Their skin is dark and shiny with fern-wax, which they rub in each dawn against the brightness. Their eyes are creased at the corners from squinting into a glare that the humid air makes soft but never dims. Most of them carry the slight, permanent lean of people who spend their lives listening with the whole body.

They are partly deaf, all of them, by middle age. This is not thought of as a misfortune. The noise takes something from every ear eventually, and the old are not diminished by it. They have simply moved further into the other senses. An old walker will stop mid-stride, lay a palm flat on the rope-grass, and know that a grazing mound is a day's walk east, that a bell-thicket has begun to flower, that a child two trees over has been born, all from the taps and tremors and breathing of the floor.

### How They Walk

Children learn to walk twice. First on their own legs, and then again on the mat, which is a different skill. The ground breathes in and out with a slow cycle, and a walker steps *with* it, planting the foot as the mat exhales and lifting as it draws in. Done properly, the ground carries the walker forward. Done badly, each step fights the floor and the legs are spent in a morning. Small children are carried in slings on the backs of anyone available, and are set down to practice only on the firm, dense mats near the trees, with the whole band watching and tapping the rhythm on their own thighs.

Adults walking in a group fall into the same pace without deciding to. Seen from a distance, a band crossing the rope-grass has a look of one animal with many legs.

### Water

The central morality of the people is about water. Each walker carries a measure in a tightly woven basket sealed with resin, slung low on the back where the weight rides with the hips. To let another walker go dry is the only truly unforgivable act. There are no laws otherwise, only habits. But a band that arrives at a spire-tree at the end of a hard day does not drink first. They wait at the edge of the moss moat while the eldest kneels and lets the first cupped handful run back into the water. Then everyone drinks. Nobody can say when the custom began. The spire-trees are older than the custom, and older than the memory of the custom.

They also collect dew in the early morning by dragging cords of fiber across the leaves and wringing them out into shells, and they know which plants hold water and which only look as though they do. There is a small, false-looking cistern-lily that is poisonous, and children are taught to recognize it by a song of hand signs that has no sound at all.

### The Trees as Homes

Every band belongs to a small group of spire-trees, three or four, on a circuit that takes a year or a little more. The walking between them is the life. The stays are the rests. At each tree there is a hanging hut, or the remains of one, high in the lower branches where the insects thin out and the air moves. The huts are lashed with cord and roofed with woven leaf-plate, and each band maintains its own, and by custom leaves every hut stocked with dry fiber, a coil of cord, and a few days of food for whoever arrives next, even if that is a rival band, even if that is a stranger.

The stairways in the bark, worn to dishes by generations of feet, are the nearest thing the people have to written history. A walker climbing a stair puts a foot where ten thousand feet have gone. The stone stacks on the root-knots are prayers, or warnings, or names, and even within the band no two people agree on what a given stack means, only that it should not be knocked down.

### Speech

They speak with their hands mostly. The hand-language is fluid, with a grammar that lives in the motion of the wrist and the angle of the forearm, and when two walkers talk across a distance they can be seen from a kilometer away, small figures shaping the air. Spoken words exist, short and low, used when close. Songs exist too, but they are sung in a chest-voice so deep it is more felt than heard, to match the drum-reeds.

The long-distance speech is the tapping. A stick on a hollow stem, or the butt of a digging staff on the mat, sends a rhythm through the floor. The rhythms are code, but also something more personal. Each walker's tapping has a hand, as handwriting would, and the old can name a distant speaker from three rhythms. Bands leave taps behind them like a wake as they travel, so that anyone on the same circuit can read who passed, how many, how tired, whether anyone was ill.

### The Dead

When someone dies, the band carries the body to a seam where the rope-grass meets the plate-fern, and lays it down without wrapping. By evening the beetles have come. Within a few weeks, a garden. The band returns on the next circuit, and the garden is the person. Who grows there is read carefully. A dense little cushion of blue moss, a cistern-lily unfolding in a place where no lily has grown, a line of red coal-birds nesting in the new tussock. The band sits with it. They name plants for the dead, quietly, and use the names.

It is understood that the floor is made of everyone who came before. No one finds this sad.

## The Ones Below

Beneath the walkers' feet is another population, and the walkers know it, without ever having seen it. The blind pale creatures in the stem galleries are the nearest of these, long and soft and quick, the color of peeled root. They are heard as much as anything else on the plateau, a sliding and pouring beneath the ground, water that is alive. The walkers do not hunt them. Some bands leave scraps at particular soft places in the mat, and the scraps are gone by morning.

Deeper, in the root-tunnels where the dark rivers run, there are things that no one has described, because no one has gone down far enough to describe them and returned. The mat is four hundred meters thick in places. There are pale fish in the rivers, spoken of by the few who have lowered baskets through sinkholes and drawn them up with something wriggling in the weave. There are colonies of glowing fungus, seen as a faint bluish stain in the sinkhole depths, and walkers say that at night some of the sinkholes are lit from below like the mouths of lanterns.

Beyond that, no one knows.

## The Answering Taps

There is one thing the walkers rarely say to outsiders, but say quietly to their own children. Sometimes, when a walker taps a call across the mat, the answering rhythm comes back from a direction where no band is known to be. The rhythm is not a walker's rhythm. It is too slow, or too even, or it has too many beats between the beats. It answers correctly, in the sense that it acknowledges the call, and then it stops, and nothing further comes.

Some say it is the deep creatures, learning the code. Some say it is the mat itself, carrying the taps and returning them changed by the depth. Some say it is the thing that booms in the night, once every few minutes, from a distance that never changes no matter which way the band is walking. That sound is the only one on the plateau the walkers do not imitate, and do not name, and do not tap. When it comes, in the low deep of the night, they stop whatever they are doing, and hold still, and breathe with the floor, in and out, together, until it passes.

Then the frogs start again, and the drum-reeds hum, and the whole enormous chorus resumes as though nothing had been said. The band sleeps in the branches of the spire-tree, above the flood of insects, on a floor that is alive and does not stop growing, a little, under everyone.
