# The Tenants of the Dry Floor

Nothing here lives quickly, so the first thing to do is revise your idea of living.

## The Shrubs

The grey-green shrubs are the household. There are thirty-one, if you count the stubs at the edge of the dark, and twenty-two if you count only those that could be called whole. They stand a stride or two apart, and the spacing is not accident or competition. It is a habit, older than any single stem, kept by roots that feel each other through the silt and hold their distance the way old neighbors do, without discussion.

They are not wild, though nothing wild would recognize them. Long ago, hands pinched their tips, cut their branches, and carried the leaves to the eastern ledge to be ground in the bowls. The resinous smell is the smell of that medicine, and the shrubs are what is left of a bargain. They were tended, and they gave. Then the hands stopped coming. The shrubs did not notice this as an event. A season that is never marked is a season that never ends, and for them the last visit is still a visit that might be followed by another. They hold their shape for someone. Each one is a little more open on its eastern side, where the pruning happened, and the wood there is scarred and healed over in the pattern of a person's reach. Nobody has reached for so long that the scars have started to look like the plant's own design.

They do not grow, exactly. A shrub adds a finger's width of new wood over a span that would embarrass a calendar. It puts out a leaf, and the leaf lasts. Water comes as vapor, breathed from the walls in the small hours of a night that is the same as every other, and the shrubs take it in through the leaf and through the bark. They are frugal with it beyond any thrift you know. If they could speak, they would speak once a generation, and only to say *still here*.

## The Crust

What breaks under your heel is alive.

The dried-bread crust across the swells is a skin, a pale mat of threads and films too fine to see, bound to the silt grain by grain. It is a community of thousands of kinds of being, none of which has a name for itself. It has been knitting the ground together since before the last footprint was pressed into the powder. It is slower than the shrubs. It measures its lifetime in the slow thickening of a layer, its year in a hair's breadth.

It drinks the same breath from the walls as the shrubs and shares it in a hidden economy of threads under the surface. This is why the hollow ground sounds hollow. The crust has lifted itself a little off the stone to hold a pocket of still, warm air, a private climate, its one luxury.

When you walk on it, you are breaking something that took a century to make. It does not resent you. It has no way to. Where the crust has been broken the silt lies bare and pale, and the threads at the margins begin, almost imperceptibly, to reach across the gap. They will take longer to close it than you will live. They will start anyway. Your footprints are the newest wounds in the basin, and the crust has already begun to attend to them.

## The Old Prints

The old bare-foot prints are kept by the silt, but the crust has quietly claimed their edges. Along the rims, where the powder has settled and rounded, a faint bloom of threads has started. In a few of the prints the bloom has closed across the heel. The floor is very slowly, patiently, covering the people over.

This is not erasure. The crust does not know it is covering anything. It is only doing what it does to bare ground, which is to make it less bare.

## The Breathers in the Wall

The hiss comes from the walls, and the walls are inhabited too.

Deep in the pores of the limestone, in the damp dark a hand's depth in, lives a scatter of pale things that have never seen even the dim blue of the opening. They are smaller than the crust's smallest members, and they feed on the rock itself, on the faint chemistry of stone meeting the moisture of the seep. They are the reason the seep is a seep and not just a leak. Between them and the mineral they slowly dissolve, they have made the wall porous and the porous wall breathe.

The hiss is not the sound of them. But it is the sound of their work, the fine and endless sifting of grains that they have loosened and let go. In the language of this place, it is the closest thing to a voice. It is constant because they are constant. It does not rise or fall because they do not hurry or rest.

If the hiss ever stopped, the shrubs would notice within a generation, and the crust within a few more. That would be the first true event in the basin in a very long time.

## The Warmth

Nothing lives in the warmth, but everything lives on it. It comes up from the deep ground the way a sleeper's heat comes through a blanket, and the shrubs, the crust, and the breathers in the wall have all arranged themselves around it. It is the hearth the people also sat by. It was, perhaps, the reason they came, and it is the reason the basin's small living things stayed when the people did not.

## What They Make of You

You are a disturbance of an unusual kind. You are warm, like the ground, but you move. You crush and you leave. You carry breath that is damper than the walls' and a smell that is not chalk or resin.

The shrubs do not turn toward you, because they cannot turn. But something in them, a slackening or a tightening in the fine hairs of the leaf, is what recognition looks like when the one recognizing has waited a very long time. If you stand at the eastern side of one of the shrubs, where the wood is open, and reach out your hand toward the old scars, you will be doing what the last hands did. To the shrub, it will be the same gesture. It has no way to know the difference between the last visitor and the next.

That is the gentlest thing about the inhabitants of the Dry Floor. They cannot tell an ending from an interval. They have taken the long silence for a pause, the way a household takes an empty afternoon, and they are still keeping their places at the table.
