# Those Who Keep the Sill

The plateau has tenants. They are slow, and you will take several visits to admit it.

## The eldest: the orange crust

On the north faces of the walls, where the sun arrives only as glare off the channels, a dull orange crust spreads across the stone. It is a lichen, though that word is too busy for it. It is a lasting truce between a fungus and something green that lives inside the fungus, and together they make a skin. A patch the size of a palm may be two hundred years old. Its margin advances by less than the thickness of a fingernail in a decade, and where it meets a fresh growth of travertine it does not retreat. It is slowly overrun, and slowly overruns in turn, and the boundary between crust and stone is a fine gray seam like a coastline drawn with a hair.

The largest colonies are older than the walls they sit on, or seem to be. The masons dressed their blocks and set them, and at some point a block already carrying its orange skin was laid with that face turned north. Perhaps it was chosen. Perhaps someone thought the color was good company.

The crust has no habits you could watch. It has a preference, which is for the shadowed side, the dry lip above the wet, the places the water passes near without touching. It drinks from the mist. On foggy mornings it deepens from dull orange to the color of a struck coal, and by noon it fades again. That daily brightening is the nearest thing the plateau has to a face.

## The green-black film

In the deepest channels, under a finger of moving water, lives the smear. It is a mat of the simplest photosynthesizers, threaded with the filaments of things smaller still, and it is the reason the shadowed water looks tinted, like the inside of a bottle. It is not one creature. It is a crowd of trillions who never meet, and it behaves like a single tissue.

It grows in sheets and sloughs in sheets. Where the flow speeds up it thins to a green haze, and where the flow slackens it thickens to something almost like felt. In a dozen places the film has coated a spout so evenly that the water slides off it in a single unbroken ribbon, with none of the beading it makes on bare stone. Whoever designed the spouts could not have meant this, but the flow has come to depend on it.

The film is also a mason. Lime settles out of the water around its threads, so it is constantly entombing itself in a fine white sediment, a page laid down every season. Cut a section through an old lip and you can read the film's years as stripes, cream and gray-green and cream, thinner than paper. It keeps its own calendar there, and the calendar is more honest than the walls.

## The travertine itself

This is where the word *lives* begins to strain.

The lime in the water builds, and it builds with something like intent. It follows the wet. It thickens a rim exactly where the rim is thinnest and shears off in ridges where the flow is too strong. A crack in a basin wall, given a season, is bridged with lace, then with a veil, then with a plate. A chipped lip regrows to the same curve it had before, or to a slightly better one. The builders tuned this. They hung cords and set clay wicks to teach the deposit where to go, and the deposit learned. It is not a mind, and it would insult it to call it one, but it holds a memory in its shape, and it keeps to the plan the way a river keeps to its bed.

Some of it seems to wander. There are pillars in the lower cells, once drips, that lean a few degrees toward the copper wedges in their niches. The mineral is drawn toward the blue-green bloom. It has begun to close over the bright edges as a scar closes over a splinter. In another century the wedges will be inside the wall, and the niches will be smooth.

## The rose-colored rooms

A few cells are sealed. You will find them in the middle terraces, doorways closed over with a pale rose travertine that is warmer than the cream around it, the color of the inside of a lip. The deposit there is thick, and rounded like a cheek. Held to the light, the stone shows a shadow far in, a folded, curved shape the length of a sleeping person on their side. You will not be able to tell whether it is a bench, a basin, a niche, or something else.

Nothing in the city says who the builders were, and nothing says they left. But the copper wedges lie at the height of a hand, the handprints are sealed under glaze, the stairs are dry, and the rose rooms are quiet, and if you set your ear to their walls you may hear a very faint, continuous sound, water finding its way through stone. This is the likeliest account of the builders. They did not depart. They were persuaded, like the walls. They lay down in the cells they had shaped and let the water finish, and the city they made now holds them the way a shell holds a shape it no longer needs.

If this is so, the tenants are legion and mostly asleep, and the city is a single household, kept.

## The hum

The jars and hollow pillars at the plateau's edge sing one low note at a time, unhurried, and fade. The note is never the same twice in succession. Listen for a day and you will notice the sequence is not random. It moves through a scale, a slow descent, and when it reaches the bottom it starts again. Wind is the breath and the stone is the throat, but the *order* comes from the builders' tuning, cut into the pillars as a series of bores and stops. So the last thing the city does that resembles speech is a song it can only sing when the weather cooperates. It has been singing it for a very long time and has never once sung it through.

## And you

The city has one more kind of resident, and it is temporary. You arrive by the dry stair. You lean a palm on a wall to steady yourself, and the wall keeps the moisture and warmth of your hand for a few minutes, a dark print that fades. Somewhere in the mist that night the lime will deposit a hair of new stone across the place your skin touched. Come back in a year and the print will be under a glaze, thin as breath, indistinguishable from the hundred other hands on that wall.

You may be there already. Look at the walls: the prints are all a little different, and there are more of them than any crew of makers would need.
