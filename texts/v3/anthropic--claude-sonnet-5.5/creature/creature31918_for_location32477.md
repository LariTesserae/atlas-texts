# Those Who Keep the Cistern Works

## The Keepers

There are twenty-three of them, or twenty-four, or twenty-two. The number depends on who is asleep, who is inside a pipe, and who has been gone so long down some far trestle that the others have stopped counting and started listening for a knock that doesn't come.

They call themselves nothing, since there is no speaking. The nearest thing to a name for the whole people is a single flat rap of the knuckle on iron, followed by a palm laid down and held: *the ones who touch.* It is what they do more than anything else, and it is the first thing a child is taught.

You would not know them for people at first. You would see a shape in canvas and brass, thick as a bell, turning a wheel the size of a cart tire with the slowness of a man moving through honey. The canvas is stained rust, sulfur, and green, and patched in a dozen weights of cloth. The helmet is a brass sphere with a round glass port, and the port is fogged at its rim, so that the face inside seems to look out through the center of a pale eye. Behind the glass you might see the flat white of a cheekbone, the dark of a brow, and eyes wide and wet from a lifetime in the dim, the pupils always huge.

They are pale under the suits, and their hands are strange. The gloves are cut and re-stitched so many times that the fingertips are thin and supple leather, while the heel of the palm and the knuckles are plated with layers of hide. Every Keeper's right hand is a little larger than the left, and the bones of the knuckles are thick with a lifetime of rapping metal. They can read a valve's fever through a glove. They can tell iron from brass, and old iron from new, by the ring it gives back.

## The Knock

Their speech is a code of taps and pressure. It is older than anyone's memory, and the pipes hold it the way a groove holds a needle.

A short rap is a word like *here*. Two shorts with a drag between them means *hold*, or *wait*, or, in a certain rhythm, *I am afraid but continuing*. A long press of the flat palm, without any rap, is the closest thing they have to tenderness. They lay it on a friend's shoulder plate, and the friend feels it as a slow warmth through the canvas and the water.

Each person has a name, and it is a rhythm. It is not given but earned from the way you naturally strike, which is as personal as a walk. One old Keeper on the north trestle raps *tk-tk—TOK—tk*, quick, quick, a heavy one, a skittering one, and everyone knows it is her before her glove has left the rail. A boy at the pump house has not yet settled his name. It changes month to month as his hand grows surer, and the others are patient, tapping back their guesses.

They argue in the code. An argument is a fierce thing: two Keepers on either side of a pipe, both palms flat, hammering back and forth until the iron sings and the whole line of the street knows there is a quarrel. Then one of them ends it with a single soft rap that means *enough*, and it is enough.

They lie in it too, a little. A Keeper who wants to seem more sure than she is will strike a hair harder than she feels. The others feel the falseness the way a person hears a cracked note.

There are also dead rhythms, names no living hand owns. These are struck only at the old handholds by the Engine Hall, once, softly, at the start of a shift. It is not quite a ritual. It is more that the rails are worn bright there, and it would feel rude not to say hello.

## The Count of Days

There are no days. There is the surge, every eleven minutes or so, and everything they measure is measured in surges.

A shift is ninety surges. A meal is one. A child is *nine thousand surges old* or *thirty thousand*, and the eldest Keeper, whose hands have begun to shake, is called by a knock that means *very many*. It is the largest number the code has, and it is applied to her with love.

Each time the surge comes, the town takes a breath. The floor lifts first, a shudder in the boots. Then the water thickens and presses. Then the teeth ring, and the lamps go to smudges as the silt blooms. Every Keeper freezes in the middle of whatever she is doing. Someone with a wrench raised holds it. Someone mid-step plants the foot. Not from fear, exactly. The surge is the pulse the whole place lives by, and they hold still for it as one holds still to hear a heart.

Then the silt settles, the lamps clear, the wrench comes down. Eleven minutes pass. It comes again.

## Breath, Bread, and Sleep

The air comes from the Engine. Its pistons squeeze it out of the sweet warm water, and it runs through the breath-mains, narrow copper lines along the pipe-streets, to tap-posts set at every stair landing and station. Each Keeper carries a reserve tank on her back that lasts about four hours. Their world is therefore a map of tap-posts, and a Keeper thinks of distance not in paces but in breaths. *Two posts to the pump house. Five to the north trestle. No post at all past the slag fall.* Past the last post, the town stops.

The children are kept close to the posts. They wear small suits, let out at the seams each year, and stitched with the older children's patches.

Food comes in tubes, a grey paste of scraped mat, dried shrimp, and something fermented from the blue-green films. It is fed into the helmet through a valve at the chin, and it tastes to them the way mother's milk tastes to a baby, which is to say it tastes of being alive. Cooking is unknown. There is no fire. But some pipes are hot enough to shimmer, and the Keepers hang the tubes near them to warm, and to warm a meal for someone you love is the closest thing they have to a gift.

They sleep in hammocks lashed in alcoves between the warm pipes, suits plugged to a tap-post, with one palm flat against the rail. The town's vibration runs up the arm and into the skull, and it is the lullaby of every Keeper who ever lived. Those who sleep well say they dream of the chord. Those who sleep badly say they dream of a bell that is out of tune.

## The Listeners

A few among them are Listeners, and they are the only ones truly valued above the rest. They can tell, by the tone and interval of a given bell, what is failing and where.

A Listener stands motionless on a catwalk while the silt drifts sideways past her helmet, one glove on a rail, head tilted. When the bells speak she knows. *The third governor is sticking. There is a hairline in the eastern header. Something has come loose in the oil line, forty paces down.* Her hand leaves the rail and raps out the news, and the Keepers go running, slowly, as one runs in water.

Nobody can teach it. The Listeners say it is not a skill but a way of being tuned. Some are born to it, and a Listener's child may be deaf to the bells while a shrimp-scraper's son hears them like speech. The best Listeners grow strange. They stop knocking in ordinary company and begin to answer the bells instead, rapping back in rhythms that belong to no Keeper's name. Perhaps they are only lonely. Or perhaps they are answering.

## The Ones Who Were Here First

Around the Keepers, and under them, live the others.

The shrimp are the oldest companions. They cluster in white drifts on the warm pipes, pale as fingernails, thousands together, the whole mass shivering at once when the pipe flexes. The Keepers have never learned whether the shrimp know them. But if you hold a glove still against a pipe for a full minute, the shrimp come and pick at the canvas, delicate as rain, and it is one of the few gentle things in the Works. Children do it for hours. Old Keepers do it when they think no one is watching.

The eyeless fish drift in ribbons through the catwalks, long and thin and colorless. They flinch, all together, at every surge, a single motion through a hundred bodies, so that a school seems to be one animal with one nerve. The Keepers watch them the way a farmer watches birds. If the fish flinch early, something is coming.

And the crab. It is pale as bone and the size of a table, and it moves in the silt between the foundations with a patience that is nearly geological. It has been seen crossing the same stretch of floor for longer than anyone's grandmother can recall, and it molts in the dark, leaving behind cast shells like abandoned cathedrals, which the Keepers do not touch. They step around it. They step around all of it: the crab, the shell, the slow track it leaves. When it passes, they stand aside and lay one glove on the nearest rail, flat, no rap, the palm-press. *Go on.* *You were here first.*

Nobody knows what the crab knows. It doesn't flinch at the surge, and that is the thing they remark on, in the quiet of the hammocks. Everything else in the Works braces. The crab, alone, seems to lean toward it.

## The Films and the Mats

The blue-green films on the older walls glow faintly, and the Keepers tend them as much as they tend anything. They do not scrape them. They stand before them at the end of a shift, still, for the long minute it takes the eyes to open, and watch the cold light come up out of nothing.

The grey-white mats are another matter. They ripple in the cool corners and creep along the pipes, and the Keepers scrape them away, sack them, and feed the tubes. The mats always come back. There is no hatred in it, just a sort of weary tenderness, the way one might regard a stubborn cousin. *You again.*

## The Machines

It would be wrong to leave out the machines, because the Keepers do not.

Many stations are unattended, and the machines run on, governed by their own spinning brass balls. The Keepers regard the governors as colleagues. A worn one is *tired*. A new one is *young and proud*. They oil them with a care they show to nothing else, and sometimes, on a slow shift, a Keeper will sit beside a pump and rest a palm on its casing and just feel it work, as one sits with a sleeping animal.

The Engine Hall is spoken of with a different rap, a heavier one, struck with the heel of the fist. Nobody lives in it. Nobody rules it. It is where the Works begins, and every Keeper walks a little softer near its doors.

## The Chord

And then there is the moment the Keepers do not have a word for, only a stillness.

When the vent surges and every bell speaks at once, and the roar swells and the floor lifts, the sound resolves into something that is almost a chord. In that instant every Keeper in the Works, wherever she is, on a trestle or in a pipe or asleep in a hammock, goes quiet. The raps stop. The gloves lie flat. For a breath they are not twenty-three people but one listening thing, and the town is not a machine they tend but a voice they are inside.

Some Keepers believe they were built to keep the Works in tune with it. Some believe the Works was built to keep them. The Listeners say nothing, because they are already answering.

Then the chord passes, the silt drifts down, and a glove lifts, and somewhere on the north trestle an old woman raps *tk-tk—TOK—tk* against the iron. Nothing is asked. It is only her name, sent out into the warm dark to say that she is still there, and that the pipe under her hand is still holding.

Somewhere far off, a hand answers.
