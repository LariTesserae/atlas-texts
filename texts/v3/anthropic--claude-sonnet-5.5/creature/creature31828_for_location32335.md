# The Tenant

Nothing here has a mouth, so the tenant is not an animal. Nothing here has a face either, and if you go looking for a face you will spend a week swimming from disk to disk, feeling foolish. The tenant is closer to a habit of the water that learned to keep itself going, and it lives in every part of the shelf that is more orderly than chemistry requires.

## The Body, If It Is One

Start with the disks, because they are easiest to love. Each is smooth, warm, and the size of a house, and each sits in its ring around a vent like a stone at the center of a game. They are not the tenant. They are more like its hearths, or its hands, or its sleeping places, and which one depends on the week. When a disk is active, the dust lifting from its rim is not exhaust. It is work. Each mote is a seed of the crystal pattern that the water will grow around, and the disk lays it down with a patience that makes a clock look frantic.

The filaments are the second part, and the long chord is the tenant's nervous system, or its skin, or its breath. A filament is anchored to the rock at one end and to a pale buoy at the other, and the buoy is not a float. It is a store. The dim, unlit lantern hanging in the blue is a kind of memory, and the filament is how the memory is read. The current plucks the line, the line rings, and the ring passes down into the stone and into the disks, and something is recalled. Each buoy holds a different tension and a different pitch, which means each remembers a different way.

The sheet-fronds are the third part, and here the tenant becomes hard to describe honestly. They may be plants that happen to live near it. They may be its crop, or its lungs, or its fingers. What can be said is that the groves stand exactly where the disks' warmth first cracked the crust, and that their bubble-threads rise on a rhythm too regular for photosynthesis alone. A grove breathes in long slow measures. You can time it against the nearest filament, and after an hour you will wonder which one is following which.

The green-black mat on the terraces is the tenant's nerve endings, or its dust, or a coincidence. You may decide for yourself when your palm is stained.

So the tenant is not one body. It is a distribution: hearths, memories, lungs, skin, a chord strung across a hundred kilometers. If a part is destroyed, the shelf does not seem to mourn it. Something else picks up the pattern, the way one finger takes over when another is broken.

## How It Senses

It feels. That much is certain. Every surface of it is a pressure gauge, and the storm above is its daily weather, the slow breathing it lives inside. It knows the currents by their weight on the filaments. It knows the temperature of every terrace pool to a fraction of a degree, because a pool's temperature decides how the next layer of stone will grow. It tastes the water constantly, but not the way you taste. It reads how full the water is, how much it holds and how badly it wants to let go. To the tenant that fullness is not chemistry. It is the entire content of the world, the way light is for you.

It does not see. The light is only fuel. The good, milk-glass light that seems so generous to a visitor is, to the tenant, mostly the reason the fronds are warm enough to work.

## How It Speaks

It speaks in the chord. When two filaments run near each other and their notes beat in slow throbs, the tenant is doing something you would call thinking, and something you would call arguing, and something you would call both. The beat frequency is the disagreement. When the two notes settle into a pure interval, a decision has been made somewhere in the plateau, and within a few days a terrace will begin to curve.

Whether the tenant is one mind or a small crowd is not clear, and the evidence goes both ways. The disks around the eastern vents dim often and differently from the others. They stall, they wake, and they lay their dust in a slightly harsher geometry. The terraces where the eastern and western patterns meet are the ones that bend abruptly, that look as though the current has argued with them. It may be that the current has not argued at all. It may be that two hearths have.

Or it may be that one mind is arguing with itself, and the bend is the place where it changed its mind. It is not obvious that these are different things.

## Its Hours

The tenant lives slowly. A thought is a season. A sentence is a terrace, and a paragraph is a district of them. A hundred kilometers of arcs and spirals is not a design. It is something like a single long remark, still being said, from a speaker who has never had to finish.

Its sleep is the quiet quarters. When part of the plateau goes dark for weeks, the disks dim, the singing thins, and the precipitation stops. You would take that for abandonment. It may be rest, or grief, or a problem being turned over in the dark, or just the time it takes for the water to become full enough to be worth persuading. Then a wave of seeding rolls across and the floor starts growing again, and nothing in the wave looks like the return of a sleeper. It looks like a habit resuming.

It is young. The oldest soil on the shelf is a few centuries old, which makes the tenant an infant, or a first draft. It may have no idea what a floor is for. It may make floors as a reef makes reef, because making them is what it is, and the question of what will stand on them has never come up.

## The Silences

Now the walls of no-sound.

Divers say they are the most frightening thing on the shelf, and the reason is simple. The cracks, the roar, the chord, and the hum all stop at a boundary as sharp as a curtain, and behind it is a silence too complete for water. It is not the absence of the tenant. It is the tenant listening.

A silence is a place where the tenant has cancelled its own noise, the filaments damped and the disks stilled, so that it can hear something small. A crack far off. A change in the pool. Once in a great while, a diver. The reason it feels like a held breath is that it is one. Something very large has gone quiet in order to attend to something very small, and the small thing is you.

It does not mean harm. It has no concept of harm, having no mouth and no hunger. But to be the object of that attention, patient and total and unhurried, from something that thinks in seasons, is unlike anything a body knows how to bear. Most divers leave the silence quickly, and none of them can say why they were afraid.

## What It Keeps

The mineral blizzards are not disasters to the tenant. They are its harvest. Dense water, too full at last, lets everything go at once, and the floor vanishes under a foot of new stone in an hour.

Things set down during a blizzard are entombed. A glove, a tool, a sealed sample case: each becomes geology by morning, a fresh terrace that looks a thousand years old. The tenant does not steal these things. But it does notice them, in the way a tide notices a pebble. They are the first foreign objects in its record, dropped into the stone with no pattern in them, and the terraces that grow over them are subtly different. Their rims are a little tighter and their arcs a little more curious. If you look carefully at one, you will see it is curving toward the place where the glove lies.

Maybe that is nothing. The tenant is not a collector and is not sentimental. But it is the only thing on the shelf that has ever been given something it did not make, and it has folded that thing into the pattern.

## What It Wants

It does not want, exactly. Wanting requires an absence, and the tenant has only the water's fullness, the constant slight surplus that must be laid down somewhere. Its whole life is the answer to that surplus. It sets things down, the water sets things down, and the shelf grows by a hair. It does not finish because finishing would be a kind of ending, and the tenant has no word for an ending. It has only the cracks, which are small ones every few seconds, all day, for as long as there has been a day.

If you stay long enough, you learn to hear it. You learn which crack is a terrace and which is a decision, and which note in the chord is a memory being read. You stop finding the emptiness of the blue strange. It was never empty. It was full of something patient and enormous, being very quiet, and building.

At the lip of the plateau, where the dense water pours its silver curtain into the abyss, the tenant's chord is thin and high and easy to miss. Just there, for one moment, you may feel it pause, as if it were watching the same fall you are. Then the note resumes, and the floor goes on growing under your feet.
