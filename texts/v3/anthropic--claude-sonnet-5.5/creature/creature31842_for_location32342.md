# Those Who Live in the Drum

Nothing here is quiet, so everything that lives here has made some arrangement with the noise. The inhabitants are best understood by how they have come to terms with it.

## The Ones Who Keep Time

The pale filaments on the vent-warm stone are the nearest thing to a single creature the chamber has. They are thousands of hair-fine strands rooted in the same seep, and they bend together with each eleven-second pulse. They bend in the same direction at the same instant, then straighten in a ripple that runs outward from the fissure like a breath. No strand leads. If you cut a tuft in two, the halves keep the same time for a while, then slowly drift apart, as though the pulse were a shared thought that has to be re-learned when it's interrupted.

The ribbed, translucent reeds beside them do something similar, but slower. Their gold seed cases rattle only on the loudest concussions, when the rock lets go. A reed does not sow its seed in calm. It waits for the mountain to shake it, and seed is thrown on the tremor into whatever new cracks the slab has opened. These stands are always younger near recent rockfalls. They are the chamber's memory of its own violence.

## The Ones Who Listen

**Ghost-eels** lie in the dead zones between the pillars, colorless as wet paper, with eyes that are only faint dimples under the skin. Their whole length is the sense organ. A ghost-eel threaded into a stone flute is not hiding. It is reading. Each flute passes the chamber's sound through its own small geometry, and the eel lies inside it like a finger on a string. When the pulse peaks and the water goes hard as a fist, they draw out of the flutes and press themselves into the seams of the floor, and the terraces look empty. When the pulse eases they come back, one by one, and the pillars fill with pale bodies swaying almost imperceptibly to a rhythm slower than the water's.

They eat what the vents kill: small things scalded in the plumes and drifting down. They are patient about it. A ghost-eel may lie in the same flute for a year. Their only known fear is the crack of the ice, which sends them into the stone at once, all together, without hesitation, as if they had heard it coming before it broke.

**Lantern shrimp** live in the loud middle water, where nothing else will stay. They hang in clouds the size of rooms, each animal no bigger than a thumbnail, each with a cold blue point at the base of its tail. When a crack goes off overhead, the cloud flares from the near edge to the far, a wave of light crossing the cloud faster than the sound could. The wave is always in the same direction, away from the crack. It is a warning, or a shout, or a way of saying *there* to every animal in the cloud. It may be a language too simple to be called one. Still, the shrimp answer each other. After a flare there is a scatter of smaller flashes, uneven, like people murmuring after a bell.

## The Sitter

Twice a decade someone sees it, and always the same way: a pale shape on a terrace, unmoving, face to the stone.

It is the size of a large dog and heavier than it looks, built low and broad, with skin the color of tallow and no scales. The head is a wide flat plate, almost a spade, with small eyes set far to the sides. They are open but seem to be used for little. Its forelimbs are wide and fin-like, with four stiff ridges at each tip that spread into fingers when it needs them. It breathes slowly. Its gills open and close about once per pulse, a rhythm no one has ever seen it break.

When it rests, it presses the whole underside of the head against the rock and stays like that for days. Whatever it is doing, it is doing it with its skull. The flat plate is a resonator. It is listening the way the ghost-eels listen, but with a much larger instrument and much longer attention. It seems to be following the floor's growth: the crawl of a ridge, the slow hollowing of a pillar tip, the shift in the hum when a stone somewhere gives way.

Then it moves a stone. It rises, takes a flat piece of rock in the ridged tips of its fins, and carries it a body's length or less. It sets the stone down at a node, on top of two others already there, and settles back. The stone is never dropped. It is set, turned a little, then checked, with the head lowered again to the ground beside it. Sometimes it reverses the placement a hand's width and listens again. It is tuning something.

What it is tuning seems to be silence. A cairn set at a node does more than mark it. The added mass nudges the standing wave, deepening and steadying the quiet there. The Sitter is a gardener of still places. The ghost-eels gather around the cairns. The sleeping filaments lean toward them. Small things that would be shaken apart elsewhere hold on there and live. It is not clear the Sitter does this for anyone. It may only need a quiet place to rest, and have discovered that a quiet place, once made, is used by others.

It is not alone in this work, or was not once. The cairns are the same as the ones the tally-keeper built: three or four flat stones, stacked in the still places. The Sitter has either learned the habit from human hands or taught it to them. The bell on the ledge sixty meters down has never been disturbed. The Sitter does not touch the hide dome, the flute, or the tally stone. It has been seen resting near the ledge, head down, feeling something in the rock beneath the lashed weights. It may be listening to the old tallies as if they were another kind of pillar, a slow pattern laid down by a creature that counted the same pulse.

The stone shows eleven marks to a group: one for each second of the pulse, or one group for each beat, thousands upon thousands. Someone lived long enough in the quiet to count the drum. The Sitter has no counting that anyone can see. But it breathes once per pulse, and it has done so for as long as anyone has watched.

Where it goes between sightings is unknown. Warm fissures run deep beneath the vents and no light has ever gone down them. It seems to come up after the floor has been rewritten, after a slab falls and the map of loud and still is redrawn. Then it works: it walks the new terraces, presses its head to the stone, finds the new nodes, and stacks the new cairns. Then it goes, and the chamber holds the marks of its passing like a tidied room.

## The Pillars

Not living, though the eels will not leave them, and the shrimp will not cross them. The hollow-tipped stone pillars, thin as wrists, hum when the water moves a certain way. Their notes are not random. Each field is tuned to the pulse: the pillars in it are the same height, and they sing the same overtone, and when they sing together the sound they make is the only sound in the chamber that is not violent. The eels are drawn to it. The filaments bend toward it. The Sitter, when it hears it, stops moving.

When the wind crosses the ice above and a shaft of light falls through the lens onto one pillar, the hum changes, warms perhaps, rises a little in pitch. For a moment every ghost-eel in the chamber turns toward it, all blind, all facing the same direction. Nothing else in the place ever makes them do that.

Then the shaft moves on, the pillar settles into its old note, and the drum goes on beating under the snowfield, once every eleven seconds, for anyone who is listening.
