# Who Lives on the Bellows Plateau

## The Foundry-Beasts

Start with the largest, since nothing else can be understood until you have accepted it.

A foundry-beast does not walk so much as proceed. Its legs are columns, each ringed with collars of horn that ratchet against one another and give the gait its rhythm: a long shifting groan, a pause, then a stride that lands with a report you feel in your teeth. Its plates overlap like the scales of a fish, but each is the size of a threshing floor and pitted with the scars of ten thousand years of heat. The seams glow dull orange when the beast is working hard and go black when it rests. In the hollows between plates live colonies of small things that would not survive anywhere else. The beast does not seem to mind them, or to know they are there.

No two are alike, and the people who watch them (there are a few, and I will come to them) tell them apart by voice. Each has a note: one a deep, round bronze, one a cracked tone like a split bell, one a rising whistle at the end of each exhalation, like a kettle that never boils over. The names people give are all descriptions of sound, and the sound can change over a lifetime. A beast that has eaten well for a century mellows. A beast eating sulfur-rich ground goes sharp and bright for a few years, then settles.

They are not stupid, exactly. They choose their routes. They stop at certain places for a season and lean into the ground with their whole front, and the stopping places are always rich in ore, though no one has ever seen a beast test the rock. They avoid some pans entirely. They sleep, and sleeping is the strangest thing to witness: the vents close, the glow drains out of the seams, the beast cools over days, and the plates tick and contract until it looks like a ruin. Then, weeks later, a first faint exhalation, and the ruin is alive again. Some of the mesas are certainly beasts that did not wake. No one agrees which ones. The old people say the difference is in the ticking, that a truly dead thing goes silent and a sleeper never does. The old people also say they have been wrong.

They breed, or something like it. Young ones are never seen suckling or following. Instead, in the deepest part of a slip, a lump of white-hot metal is sometimes found sitting on the pavement, too large to be an ingot, glowing, and it does not cool. Over decades it darkens and begins to crack along a pattern of fine lines. The lines are plates. The retinue gathers round it, the pounders hammering in time with something inside, and one day it stands up. That is what the watchers say, having seen it once or twice in a lifetime, at a distance, through heat shimmer.

## The Retinue

A great beast is a district, and like any district it has tenants.

**The hammerjaws** are the nearest thing to workers. Cart-sized, low-slung, with forelegs like drop-forges and a jaw that is a solid block of black horn, they follow the beast's droppings and stamp the still-soft metal and slag into sheets. They work in threes, sometimes in teams of twelve, all landing together with a sound like a door slammed in an empty cathedral. Between strikes they rest with their jaws open, and the heat pours out of them in visible sheets. They are territorial about slag and about nothing else. If you stand on a sheet they are flattening, they will go around you.

**The weavers** are wire-legged crawlers, all joints and tension. Each spins its thread from a spinneret at the base of its abdomen, a fine silver filament that cools instantly in the air, and they anchor it across the rock and pull it taut until the whole web sings. A weaver field is a harp the size of a town. The wind plays it. Certain notes, the watchers say, are what the weavers are actually working for, and a web that falls out of tune is unpicked and rewoven that same day. In the mornings the strands collect a fine dust of hematite and glow faintly red, so the webs seem drawn on the air in blood.

**The sparks** are the smallest and the most numerous, and possibly the most important. They are bright motes, some no larger than a seed, that drift in clouds around the vents and feed on the heat of each exhalation. When a beast breathes out, they flare and swarm into the plume. When it stops, they settle into the seams of its plates and dim. They may be the beast's blood or its parasites or its sense organs. When a beast is dying, the sparks leave it first, and the exodus is visible for kilometers, a river of drifting light going somewhere else.

**The rustlings** are the ones no one expects. They are low, soft-bodied things, the color of the oxidized ingots they graze, and they are the only creatures on the plateau that seem gentle. They move in slow flocks across the red lumps that lie in the open, and they eat them, or eat the rust from them, and leave a fine pale powder behind that the wind takes. Without them the plateau would be paved in metal. They are why it is not. They are easy to kill and nobody does, because the gleaners know what they are for.

## The Gleaners

There are people here, though fewer than the plateau's size would suggest and fewer than they themselves would like.

They are called gleaners, or listeners, or tally-walkers, depending on who is talking. They are lean and dark and dry-looking, wrapped in layers of pale cloth against the light, and they carry water in sealed flasks of volcanic glass, tinted and thick-walled, which they must bring up from the lowland edge on journeys that take a month each way. The plateau gives them nothing to drink. It gives them metal. A gleaner's whole life is a trade between the metal that lies free on the pavement and the water that does not.

They live in the lee of the herds. They camp against a resting beast's cooling flank for warmth at night, and they know the tempo of a sleeping beast the way a mother knows a child's breathing. They walk the circuits, taking ingots that are sound and leaving the rest, and they never take anything from a slip. They carry sounding-stones, and when they cross a stretch of ground where an echo returns early, they build a cairn and note it. Their maps are not maps of land. They are maps of where the land has stopped agreeing with itself, and they update them by the year, because the slips creep.

They are not sentimental about the beasts, but they are not indifferent either. They speak of them the way sailors speak of weather. They believe, most of them, that the beasts are making something. They do not agree on what.

## What Lives in the Slips

The gleaners will not talk about this much, and what they say is not consistent.

In the worst places there are things that appear to be the herd, seen twice, at two distances, moving in step. The nearer one is a beast. Of the farther one, the gleaners say only that it is *a little too silent*. It has no vents that breathe. Its shadow falls the right way. It walks exactly as the near one walks, with a beat's delay, and its tread raises no dust.

Some think it is a memory the ground keeps of the herd. Some think it is what the herd will be after it is finished. A few, the ones who have stood closest, say that the silent one is not following the beast but leading it, and that the beast is the one that is the echo.

Other things live only in slips and are seen only sideways: a lattice of fine bright threads that seems to grow out of the rock and is gone when you look at it directly; a low creature like a hammerjaw made of glass, which strikes without sound; a smell of warm pennies with no source. The ingots that come out of these places are hollow, or uneven, or stamped with a pattern that shifts. The gleaners bring none of them home, except, they say, once in a generation, one of them, the one that changes, and then not to sell.

## At the End

You could walk for a day and meet nothing. That is not because the plateau is empty. It is because everything here is patient. The mesa on the horizon is a mesa. The mesa you passed at noon may not be. The stack of rock that stood on the edge of the world an hour ago has, very slowly, turned its head, and a pale plume is rising from the top of it into a sky that has no clouds, and after a long moment, across all that bare distance, comes a single low note, like a bell struck once and left to ring.
