# Those Who Keep the Held Sea

The inhabitants of the basin do not think of themselves as living *in* a place. They think of themselves as living *inside a sound*. Ask any creature there where it is from, if a creature could be asked, and it would answer with a note. Home is a pitch, and kin are those who hear the same one.

## The Tenders

The Tenders are the ones an outsider notices first, though only because they are the ones who look busy.

A Tender is about the size of a large hound, low and broad, carried on eight jointed legs. Ahead of these it holds four slender limbs ending in hands of astonishing delicacy, each finger tipped with a comb of fine hairs. The shell is the color of wet slate, thick as a shield across the back and thin and flexible at the joints. Over the centuries it grows a pelt of pale, threadlike growths, and a very old Tender looks furred, almost mossy, so that it is hard to say where the animal ends and the hall begins. Their eyes are small, milky, and mostly ornamental. They do not need them. What they read the world with are the combs on their fingers, which feel the vibration of every surface, and a pair of pits along the jaw that taste the water for resin, sap, rot, and the slightest sourness of a failing fiber.

Their central skill is listening. A Tender approaches a tether and taps it once with a claw, then holds still with its whole body pressed to the stem, and *reads the note*. The pitch tells it how thick the wall has grown, how much gas is in the hollow, whether the inner fibers are tight or slack, and whether some hidden weakness has begun. The Tenders do not build the halls so much as tune them. The permanent chord of the basin is not an accident of length and thickness. It is the thing they exist to preserve. When a tether drifts a hair flat, they brace it, bind it, thicken it, and coax it back until it sings true again. To a Tender, a hall in tune is a healthy body, and a sour note is a wound.

They are extraordinarily slow. A Tender may live four or five hundred years, on a metabolism so unhurried that an hour of its labor would look to a human like a pause in the middle of a gesture. It sets a fiber, waits, tests, adjusts by a hair, and waits again. It does not hurry because it has no reason to. Its work will not be finished in its lifetime, and this does not trouble it, because no Tender begins anything. Each one inherits a task already in progress, and each hands the task on still in progress.

Memory lives in the work rather than in the Tenders. Every lineage has a *hand*, a characteristic way of knotting a binding or angling a graft, and the old parts of a hall are legible to those who know how to feel them. A young Tender running its fingers along a joint knows who made it, roughly when, and in what mood: hurried, careful, grieving, or content. A colonnade is a library written in knots. The Tenders read it constantly, not out of scholarship but in the way you might glance at a familiar face.

They speak little, and slowly. Their language is a tapping, a sequence of light claw-strikes on resin, and each colonnade's tapping has drifted over the ages into its own dialect. A Tender from one hall meeting a Tender from another, if they ever met, would find the meaning arriving slowly, like an image surfacing through silt. Mostly they do not speak at all. They work side by side on the same arch for years, understanding each other entirely through the small adjustments each makes to the other's work.

When a Tender dies, and they do die, they know beforehand, and they walk. They descend the pillars to the terraces of the Understory and settle into a bowl of the hall they served. The crawlers find them there. In time the shell is pressed into the floor and the body dissolves into the pale garden, and the next generation of bottom-plants grows from what the Tender was. The floor of every bowl is, in a real sense, the old ones. The living Tenders walk on them daily and do not call it sad.

## The Boomers

The grazers are the largest animals in the basin and possibly the oldest individuals. A boomer is a soft, immense, sail-finned thing, forty or fifty meters from blunt snout to trailing tail. Its back is the color of wet ash, and it browses with a broad, lipped mouth, taking the Raft's underside in long, patient sweeps. Its sight is poor. Its hearing is not. And its mind, whatever it is, has been running at the pace of continents for a very long time.

Boomers keep the commons. They drift between the halls in herds so slow that a single circuit of the basin may take a lifetime, and they carry the long news, which is not really news at all. Their calls, those descending minute-long groans, are answered by other boomers kilometers away, and a conversation may take an hour to complete a single exchange. What they discuss is unclear. Perhaps it is only *here I am* and *yes, here I am too*, repeated over centuries until it becomes something like scripture. But the pattern is more complicated than that. Each boomer's call bends subtly around the note of the hall nearest to it. They know the whole chord, every tether in every colonnade. If a hall were to go out of tune, the boomers would be the first to say so.

Many carry lights, cold blue-green points along the flank, arranged in constellations that are unique to each animal. These are how they know one another, and how the smaller creatures know them. A young shoaler, asked to point out a boomer, would not describe its shape. It would describe its stars.

## The Others

**Shoalers** are the color of the basin: silver-grey on the back, faintly luminous along the belly, small as a hand. They move in tight, breathing masses through the colonnades, turning together like a single thought. They eat what drifts, seed-husks, shed scales, the fine fall of the dead Raft, and they keep the pillars clean simply by passing over them. They live short lives, a few years, and are perhaps the only creatures in the basin to whom time feels like it is going somewhere. They are anxious, quick, and gossipy, and every colonnade is thick with their rustle.

**Lurkers** hang in the galleries with their jaws open, and they have hung there for so long that algae has grown across the inside of their mouths. They are enormous-headed, thin-bodied, and pale as tallow. They are patient beyond even the Tenders, and they eat almost nothing, perhaps one meal a season, a shoaler that miscalculates or a snapper that wanders. But the galleries are theirs, and the still water inside is theirs, and the Tenders have come to depend on them. A gallery with a lurker in it stays clean and clear of intruders, and the Tenders repay them by keeping the resin lining smooth and warm against their skin. Neither party would call this friendship. Neither would call it anything.

**Snappers** are everywhere in the galleries, tiny, blind, and pink-translucent, and their crackling is the sound of the galleries breathing. They graze the resin lightly, and their grazing polishes it. A gallery is glossy because a million small mouths have been working it since before there was a word for glossy.

**Knockers** are the ones who talk. They have hard, bony heads and no other obvious use for them, and they station themselves along the walls of the colonnades, knocking out slow patterns in the dialect of their hall. What they say is not entirely clear even to the halls themselves. It may be a running census: who is here, who has come, who has gone. It may be a kind of song sung to no one, the way a wall ticks in the cold.

**Ribbon-swimmers** are long, flat, and faintly iridescent. They move through the tether-forest in slow S-curves, threading between the pillars with no visible effort. They are messengers of a sort, carrying seeds, spores, and now and then news, from one hall to the next. They are the only creatures who cross the commons for the pleasure of it.

**Crawlers** live in the Understory, in the burrows older than any hall. They are blind, jointed, and gray, the size of a thumb up to the size of a door. They are the undertakers, turning the dead into floor and the floor into food, and they are the reason nothing ever accumulates. They rarely rise above the silt. Most have never seen the ceiling-glow and would not know what to make of it.

## What They Share

There is much the inhabitants do not have. They have no seasons, no dawn, no dusk, and no storms. Their world has never once been rearranged by something outside itself. They have no word for *surprise*, and if one were coined it would probably describe a pitch changing by a hair.

What they do have is the sound, and a bone-deep relationship to it. The drone is not background to them. It is the thing that tells them they exist. A Tender pressing its body against a pillar is doing something like what a person does laying a hand on the chest of someone they love, feeling the steady, reassuring proof that the thing is alive and in tune. Silence, in the basin, would not be peace. It would mean the pillars had stopped, and that everything they were built to hold up had begun to fall.

So they keep the note. Tenders tune it, boomers carry it, knockers count it, snappers polish it, and shoalers hum along in their thousands without thinking about it at all. No one is in charge. Everyone is a little responsible.

## The Visitor, Heard From Inside

When you drift into a colonnade, the inhabitants do not see you. They hear you, and what they hear is *a gap*.

Every creature in the basin has a pitch, some faint note carried in the body from the hall it grew up in. You have none. You move through the chord like a small hole moving through cloth, a soft, wandering absence. The shoalers part around you without quite knowing why. The lurkers open their mouths a little wider and close them again. The Tender at the arch does not look up, but its fingers pause on the fiber for a moment longer than the work requires, and it seems to be listening to the place where you are.

Then you pass, and the chord closes behind you, and the fiber is pressed into place.
