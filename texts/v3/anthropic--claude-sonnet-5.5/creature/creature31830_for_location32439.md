# Those Who Keep the Wall

There are two populations on the Shelf, and neither would say which of them is the guest. The people number in the millions. The animals number in a figure no one has tried to count. They share the stairwells, the drains, the cornices, and the warm wet air, and after eight hundred years of it, neither group is quite what it would be alone.

## The people

They call themselves the wall-folk, or in their own tongue something closer to *those who hold the stone*. Lowlanders call them beaded, meaning damp, and the wall-folk have taken the word up as their own.

They are built for stairs. A grown Shelf-dweller has heavy calves, a short stride, and a habit of setting the foot down flat and sideways on worn treads, so that a lowlander climbing beside one sounds like a cart and the local sounds like a cat. Shoulders sit forward from a life of carrying and scraping. Their hands are broad and cracked across the knuckles, because lime water eats skin, and everyone rubs their hands nightly with eel-fat. Their teeth are famously good and their kidneys famously bad. The water is hard enough to fur a kettle in a week, and stones are discussed at the dinner table as ordinary misfortunes, like weather or in-laws. Old people keep their passed stones in a dish on the sill, sorted by size.

Almost everyone is a little deaf. The engines have seen to that. So the people speak in the gaps of the engine-beat, and a conversation on a street near a well-hall has a rhythm to it, phrases delivered on the slack and held back on the stroke. Children learn this before they learn words. They also learn a hand-language, a flat, quick, mostly one-handed signing used across courtyards and over the steam-roar of the foundries. It is used at night too, by lovers and thieves.

They dress for wet. There is no wool, no linen, no cotton, and nothing woven from a plant, because there are no plants. There is felted fungal cloth, grey-brown and dense, that sheds a drizzle like a duck. There are coats of waxed eel-skin, glassy and dark. There are hats of boiled slab-hide, wide in the brim and stiff as tile. Underneath, against the cold of the upper terraces, people wear spun glass-wool, which itches and which they claim not to notice. Boots are studded with iron and resoled every season. Nearly everyone carries a small scraping-pick at the belt, called a tick, and uses it a dozen times a day without thinking: to free a latch, to clear a step, to loosen a lip of stone from a doorframe. A person without a tick is either a child, a lowlander, or in mourning.

### Greetings, manners, complaints

The standard greeting is a question. *Is your wall dry?* The answer, whatever the truth, is *Beaded, thank you.* It is a joke that has forgotten it is a joke. Underneath it is a real inquiry: is your house closing up, is your cistern clean, has the travertine started on your door? A person who answers *dry* is being either boastful or sarcastic, depending on the tone, and the tone is everything.

They complain constantly, mildly, and with great skill. The rain is a subject of endless gossip, as if it were a difficult relative, the way it arrives a little late, or lingers, or is not what it was. Noise disputes between neighbors are mostly about gulpers, whose drain-choruses are treated as the household's responsibility, like a barking dog. *Your drain was up at four again.* *They were only answering yours.*

They are practical about death. The dead are carried in procession to seep-niches cut in the deep walls, where cold water runs over the stone all year, and are laid there wrapped in fungal cloth with a tick in one hand. Over the following decades the travertine closes over them, first a soft white glaze and then a smooth pale swelling on the wall. Families visit their niches with bowls of clean water and pour a little over the stone. It is considered the best end available, to go into the wall and be part of what holds the city up. The chipping-crews' rota skips the niche-walls by law and by an older, softer taboo. No one, not even a foreman with a district to clear, puts a hammer to a face in the stone.

## The trades

Almost nobody on the Shelf has a job that does not involve an animal or a wall.

**Chippers** run the pneumatic crews, working slow circuits through the city to open what the stone is closing. They are the most numerous trade and the most respected, and the most likely to be missing part of a finger. They work in threes: one hammer, one sweeper, one who watches the wall above for a slab coming loose. They rarely finish a job. They speak of a street as *cleared to here* the way sailors speak of a position at sea.

**Grazers** herd the mat-snails. This is not as gentle as it sounds. A wall-herd can be forty thousand shells wide, moving at the speed of a slow walk in a day, and a good grazer steers them with salt lines, painted lures of fresh biofilm, and a light tap of a rod on the stone. Grazers can read a herd's mood from the sound it makes, which is a dry, rising whisper like a broom. They hold that snails go where they are wanted if you want the same thing they do.

**Wardens** tend the drains and the gulpers. A warden's day is spent knee-deep in cold water with a lantern, counting and listening. They know their drain-choruses as individuals and can tell a healthy one from a sick one by the pitch. They are the ones who first notice a fouled main, because the gulpers stop singing there.

**Palm-keepers** climb the cornices. They service the nesting-niches of the fliers, clean the guano that would otherwise lime over the cracks, and take the annual count at dusk, standing on the roof-ridge while the clouds go up around them. They are lean, patient, and unafraid of heights, and the flat stone of a rooftop is the only place they seem entirely at ease.

There are also **water-sellers**, with their brass-lidded jugs and their cries. There are **pen-stewards**, who feed the slabs and milk them and doctor their feet. There are **engine-wrights**, who tend the beam-halls and are half mechanic and half priest. Their bays are hung with the votive spanners of their dead, and they do not speak while the engine is being started.

## The children

Every child is given a mat-snail at about the age of five. It is theirs to feed, name, and walk along the wall, and most snails outlive the child's interest and are quietly inherited by a grandmother. Children learn to write by luring snails. They paint fresh film on the stone in the shape of letters, and the snails rasp the paint and leave clean silver lines behind. The words last a day or two before the film regrows. Messages, love notes, and insults are written this way on every wall, and reading the lower courses of a school district is like reading a slow, bright argument. The children race stair-crabs on the flowstone steps, betting pebbles. They learn early which drains not to put a hand in.

## The other citizens

The animals are not scenery, and the wall-folk do not treat them as such. They are the workforce, the neighbors, and the weather report.

**Mat-snails** take their colors from what they graze. Orange herds feed at the iron seeps and blue ones at the sulfur seeps, so the walls are striped in slow bands of migrating color. Their shells are whorled, thick, and warm to the hand. On damp evenings the whole side of a building seems to shimmer as a herd turns.

**Gulpers** are the pale salamanders of the drains, flat-headed and nearly translucent, with red gill-plumes that flare when they sing. Only the males cluck, and they do it in unison, a thousand to a drain, so that the sound is less a chorus than a texture. To the people it is the sound of the city being all right.

**Palmers** are the hand-sized gliders. They nest in cornice cracks and come out at dusk to feed on the midge-clouds that rise from the wet film. They fly in dense, roaring streams, and the wall-folk look up as they pass the way people elsewhere look up at church bells. Their droppings are the finest fertilizer on the plateau, and there are laws about who may collect them.

**Stair-crabs** scavenge, and the streets are cleaner for them. **Main-eels** patrol the aqueducts, long as a man and slow, and are fished with hooks and lamps in the early morning. **Slabs** are the herd-animals, broad, hairless, patient, and perpetually aggrieved, bawling from the terraced pens in a chorus that carries across whole districts and that no one on the Shelf hears any more than they hear their own pulse.

## The arrangement

It would be wrong to say the people rule the animals, or the animals the people. The masons who cut the drain-slots and nesting-niches did it because they understood that without the grazing, the crust would smother the stone in a season, and that the animals had to be kept in the walls the way a heart is kept in a chest. The people eat the animals, and are eaten by nothing, and know what they owe. Before the first meal of a season, families set out a dish of clean water at a drain-mouth. Nobody can say who taught them, and nobody skips it.

Some of the older wall-folk say that the people too are a kind of grazer, that they feed on the city's heat and water and crust, and are cleared away and grown over in turn. They say it without gloom, in the tone of someone remarking that it looks like rain.

## Dusk

To see them all at once, stand on a rooftop in the hour the light turns amber. The rain is easing. Below, in the drains, the gulpers are singing, and the sound rises through the grates and blends with the pulse of the beam engine until you can't tell where one ends. On the wall across the street, a grazer is walking backward ahead of a blue herd, tapping the stone with her rod, and a little girl is beside her, holding a snail up to the light. A chipping-crew is packing up for the day, three tired figures in waxed coats, their hammers ticking as they cool. A water-seller is calling his last round. Overhead the palmers begin to lift from the cornices, first a few, then a rush, a dark roaring ribbon that pours across the sky toward the midges over the stacks. Every face on the street tilts up to watch it pass, the way it has for eight hundred years, and then goes back to what it was doing.

The wall behind them beads and ticks. Somewhere under the mineral skin, in a niche kept cold by running water, someone who held the stone is being slowly taken into it, and the pale glaze is thickening, and the city, patient and warm and wet, is holding them all.
