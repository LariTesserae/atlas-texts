# The Tender

You will not see it first. You will see what it has done.

The mica window in the firebox door has been wiped clean in one arc, the width of a thumb. A gauge needle that you would swear was resting at one unreadable number now rests a hair off it. A leak you noticed an hour ago, a bright thread down a flange, has been stopped with a smear of pale paste, still soft, still warm. Then a cistern you took for a cistern breathes in, and you understand that some of the shapes in the gaps are not iron.

## Its shape

It sits folded into a niche between two cisterns, knees to chin, in a hollow the brick has worn to fit it. Unfolded it would be taller than the doorway you came through and thinner than it should be, with long forearms and shins and more patience in its joints than a body needs. It was a person once, or its ancestors were. The line has been so long in the heat that the resemblance is a courtesy.

Its skin is the color of the brick and as warm and faintly damp as the floor between the channels, like the skin of a sleeper. The mineral bloom has taken it as it has taken the walls. White crusts sit in the creases of the elbows and knees and the hollow of the throat, and it does not wash them off. Rust freckles run along the forearms where it leans on the pipes. It has no hair. Its scalp and the tops of its shoulders are covered in a fine pale felt, and when it bows its head, the lowest point of the nape gives off the same green-blue glow as the undersides of the pipes. A drop falls there and the glow dims for a breath. It does not flinch. It seems to consider this a kind of conversation.

Its eyes are large and clouded like the gauge glass. It isn't blind. It sees the orange and the green and nothing else, and it cannot bear a lamp. If you bring one, it turns its face to the wall, slowly and politely. That is the only rebuke you will get.

## Its hands

The hands are the most finished part of it. The palms are hard and glazed like old brick. The fingertips are soft and pale as the wax-colored ferns, and they are what it thinks with.

It lays a palm flat on a pipe and listens through the bones of its arm. It taps a valve wheel with one knuckle and waits for the answer. It knows the forty drips by pitch, and when a forty-first begins, some new small failure in the ironwork, it rises, unfolds along the gantry ladders, and goes to it, unhurried, carrying a bowl of the pale mortar-paste that it seems to make from the walls themselves. The riveted patches on the cisterns are its work, or the work of the ones before it. It doesn't seem to distinguish. When it looks at a plate patched a century ago, it looks at it the way you look at your own old scar.

## How it moves

It moves when the beam moves. When the great iron arm tilts overhead with its groan in your sternum, the tender rises or turns or crosses an aisle, and the sound covers it entirely. You will find it in a different place afterward and never see the transit. In the long quiet between, it holds as still as the pipes, and the pale crickets settle on its shoulders as they would on any warm iron.

You may come to suspect that the hum under everything is not the machinery's. It stops when the beam moves. That may be because the tender, mid-motion, holds its breath.

## Its companions

It keeps no pets, but it has acquaintances. Once in the long cycle of the day it opens a cistern hatch and lowers its face to the water. The pale eyeless thing with feathery gills rises to the glow at its nape, hangs there, and goes down again. Neither seems to want more.

One of the mud-wasps has plastered a nest, a tiny perfect vault, on its left shoulder blade. It sleeps and leans on the right side now and doesn't mention it.

## The fire

It feeds the firebox rarely. The fuel is a dark pressed brick, dense and faintly sweet-smelling, which it takes from a bin behind an iron door at the eastern end. The bin is always about a third full. It has never gone looking for who fills it. It treats this as it treats the rain: something that arrives, on a schedule not meant for it, and is sufficient. It sets each brick in the coals with both hands and shuts the door slowly so the mica does not rattle.

## The rain hours

When the vault cools and the hall begins to rain, it walks to the middle of the floor, lets its arms hang a little out from its sides, and lifts its face. The drops run the length of it. The crusts at its joints soften and slide, and the felt on its scalp blazes a shade brighter, steady, not flickering. This is the one time it is plainly glad. Afterward, as the heat wins and the walls dry, new pale crusts form along its edges, in new places, as they form on the brick. It is being remade at the same rate as the room.

## Its habits of attention

When the governor at the east end lifts its iron balls and spins a few slow turns, the tender looks up. When the balls fall quiet, it makes a small motion of the head, down and back, as if it had checked something and found it sufficient. You will notice that the governor does the same, and you will not be able to say which of them began it.

Under the dead white bell on the thread-thin vine, it sits for long hours in the dry part of the day. It has not taken the flower down. The vine flowered once, and the tender has waited a very long time for it to do so again, in the way that you wait for a word you have nearly remembered. It doesn't seem troubled by the length of the wait.

## If you stay

It won't speak. It has no interest in you, though it accepts you as it accepts the crickets. If you sit on a warm gantry plate and are quiet long enough, it may come and settle nearby with a great slow unfolding. Its warmth is the same as the plate's, and it asks nothing.

If you stay longer, it may reach out one hot palm and set it on the pipe beside your hand, and wait until you understand that you are meant to do the same. Then the pipe will tell you what it has been hearing: the whole hall in slow conversation, the drips and the tick of expansion and the shifting water in the deep well, all of it saying the same unhurried thing.

After a while it will take the hand away and fold back into its niche. It does not think of the hall as somewhere it lives. It thinks of itself as the part of the hall that has hands.
