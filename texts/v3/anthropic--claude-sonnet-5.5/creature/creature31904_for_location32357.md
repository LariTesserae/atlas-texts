# Those Who Live in the Felt

You will not find them by looking. You will find the place where you have been looking at them for some time.

They are about the height of the willow, knee to waist, and built low and broad, like cushion plants that have learned to breathe. When they are still, which is most of the time, they are a rounded olive-and-rust hummock with a grey-violet lichen bloom, identical to a hundred others on the slope. It is only when the mist beads on one and the beads do not settle into the same pattern as its neighbors that you notice the warmth. They give off a faint steam, thinner than the vents', and it rises in slow pulses, about half as often.

## What they are made of

They are furred, but the fur is a pelt in the way the hill is a pelt. A fine dense underwool holds a living coat of moss and liverwort, and they tend it the way other creatures groom. Each carries a lichen pattern on the shoulders and back. It is grown deliberately and reseeded when it fades, and it tells which vent a person was born beside, which lobe of the mat their family rides, and which of the old iron things they are kin to. The patterns are slow to read and slower to change, and a person with a wrong lichen is a person in trouble.

The faces are small and set deep in the coat. The eyes are large and dark and lidded twice, the inner lid a clear film that sweeps sideways against the mist. There are no ears. They listen with their hands and chests. The hands are long, flat-fingered and cool, and the fingertips are faintly furred with a white filament that looks like mold and is not. It is a living mycelium, worked into the skin over generations, that lets them plug into the threads under the mat. When one kneels and lays both palms into the moss, it is not resting. It is reading the ground: seep, tick, weight, and the low potential of the basin. A person can lie for an hour listening to a hillside think.

They are slow, and then they are not. A body that has been a mound for the whole morning can cross forty yards of pelt in the time it takes you to turn your head. It uses all four limbs and a flattened, rolling gait that barely disturbs the moss. Afterward there is no trail.

## The houses that ride

They build nothing. What they do is coax.

A household begins at a vent. The chimney of packed black moss, chest-high and sour-breathing, is the hearth, the well, the clock, and the parent of the house, and each has a name that is really a count: the number of pulses it gives between one long sigh and the next. Around it they bend the willow, knee-high and tangled, over years, tying it with fibers from the tree-fern's crosiers and grafting living stem to living stem until the thicket becomes a low dome with a doorway on the leeward side. The roof is turf, laid live. The floor is the warm ring itself. The whole house is alive and eating and slightly moving.

This matters because the ground moves. A house is not planted on a place. It is planted on a lobe, and the lobe travels. Over a generation a household slides downslope with its vent, folded along with the creeping tongue, until the toe begins to roll under. Then they leave. They do not flee. There is a long, methodical decamping, with cuttings taken and grafts wrapped, and the old dome left to go under. A child born in a house may live to see it enter the black fold. Decades later, the family will say, it will come back up steaming, roofed over in new green, and someone will have to be told whose it was.

Their dead go the same way. They are carried to the toe and laid on the moss at the lip, not buried, and the slow fold takes them under. What rises in the wet black behind, the haze of liverwort and then the bright green, is spoken to. It is not spoken of as *them*. It is spoken to as a neighbor.

## The ticking clocks

They keep the pressure domes. The swollen, ribbed cushions that hiss and reseal are their livestock, their calendar, and their cisterns. A person who wants water goes to a particular dome at a particular hour and presses two fingers into the valve slit, and a cold sweet mouthful comes out and the slit closes. Each dome has a known interval, and they have kept rows of them so that a slope at noon is an instrument. Someone walking through can tell the hour and the weather by the pattern. A stretch of the chorus running fast means a front is coming. A stretch that goes quiet all at once means something else, and the people in that stretch have already gone up the slope by then.

They know the signs of a slide, of a raft about to go. They lay their palms into the mat and feel a drag in the current, a change in the seep-tick, a thinning of the hum. When it goes, some of them do not run. They step onto the raft. Riding a failure is the fast road: a quarter-mile in minutes on a tilting, buckling plane of living turf, with the wake of compressed ridges rising on either side. They ride it standing, low, all four hands in the moss, and step off at the end into whatever is there. Some do not step off. This is understood. The papery sigh and the thud in the shins are, to them, a kind of weather, and they mark those who did not come back by leaving a cutting where the raft began.

The bog-bursts they fear. Nothing rides a burst. The domes of gas-swollen peat give a warning that is only a slight lift of the ground and a smell, and there is a word, or a knuckle-knock, for the house that has stayed too near one.

## Light in the dusk

At dusk, in the wet hollows, the hum in the teeth grows a little. The people come out of the domes and kneel in a loose ring with their fingers in the mat. Then the pale flicker crawls over the damp pelt, a faint greenish-white thread that runs a few seconds, and dies, and a second one answers from across the hollow. This is speech, or something that would be speech if it were not also a kind of touching. They are pushing the small current through the fungal threads between them, and the light is only what leaks out. It carries news, names, requests, a count of pulses, and things they will not say aloud, because they say almost nothing aloud. A visitor sitting at the edge of the ring will feel the prickle and see the light and understand nothing, and be conscious the whole time of being included in it.

## The bird and the hare and the beetles

The small round grey bird in the willow is theirs, in the sense that a bell is a town's. It speaks once an hour, a single thin note that carries a very long way, and the people answer it. Not with a sound. Wherever they are, they stop for a breath and press a palm to the ground. The note is how the country knows that time has gone by. Whether the bird knows this, nobody has asked it.

The long-legged, wet-furred hare-thing is a go-between. It crosses the open pelt at dawn from hollow to hollow, and the people leave small bundles of seed and filament in its runs, which it carries, without meaning to, to the next household. They do not own it. They do not want to. They watch it go the way one watches a letter.

The glossy beetles that work the warm rings are farmed, if that is the word. The people rake the pale fat-leaved growths nearest the steam into beds and let the beetles eat, and in return take the beetles' dark, oily castings, which they mix into the turf of a roof to keep it living through the deep frost. Nothing here is eaten as meat. What they eat is harder to say: a paste from the rust-colored mid-ring, a sweetness drawn from willow catkin in spring, and, they say, the hum.

## The iron

They do not know what the old machines were for, and they have never wanted to. But they know them.

The cast-iron boiler on its shelf of the hill is a nursery. They think of it as a plant, an ancient one of a kind that no longer sets seed, whose body has hardened and whose only remaining work is to hold soil. They sow it every spring. Cushions grow up its sides. Children are brought to it at their first frost, and their hands are laid against the cold rust and then against the warm moss, and told to feel the difference. Nobody says more.

The line of rust-orange humps across the slope is a road. It is the only road. It rises and tilts with the turning, so it is never where it was, but a person who follows the humps will always end somewhere that was once somewhere. They walk it in silence and call it the Spine.

The toothed wheel in the bog pool has willow in its spokes. It is the nearest thing they have to a shrine. They approach it on hands and knees and do not touch it. They say, if pressed, that it is turning, only very slowly, and that when it has gone all the way round the country will change. Whether it is turning, no one who has looked at it for an hour would say.

## What you notice, if you stay

You will not see them move. You will simply find that the number of hummocks on the slope has changed. The mound you were sitting near, which you had taken for a stone, has a lichen pattern you have been looking at for some time.

They are curious about you in a way that does not require your consent. A palm will have been laid in the moss a foot from your knee, listening to your weight. At some point a very small offering, a cutting of red moss tucked into a crease of your pack, will turn up, and you will not know when it was put there. It is a greeting, or a question, or a marker that says *this one has been through*.

Stay an hour. The tongue across the valley advances by a hand's breadth. A vent sighs. The far raft tips and starts to slide. And all across the slope, unhurried, in a ring that had been a scatter of stones a minute ago, a hundred low warm shapes set their hands into the ground and turn their faces, together, to feel it go.
