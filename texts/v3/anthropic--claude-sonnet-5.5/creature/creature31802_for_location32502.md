## Who keeps the room

Start with the smallest, because they are the ones who will let you.

**The snail** is the oldest resident by habit, if not by years. It is glass-clear, the size of a thumbnail, with a whorl so thin you can see the dark thread of its gut turning inside like a hair in a drop of water. It grazes the undersides of the felt-leaves and has done so long enough to score a groove in one of them, a pale line that wanders and doubles back and wanders again. It is not lost. Follow the groove far enough and you find it is a sentence, or the habit of a sentence: a route made so many times that it has become a form of speech. The snail does not know this. It is only hungry, slowly, and faithful to its hunger. But the other residents read the groove, and so, after a while, do you.

**The shrimp** are blind and colorless, and they know one another by touch. They sit in threes and fours in the crooks of the roots, feelers crossed, like people sharing a bench. They are not afraid of you, because you are not the largest thing that moves through their dark, and they have already spent their fear on the larger thing. When you pass they lift one feeler each, note that you are warm and clumsy and not the Other, and lower them again. They are the room's nervous system, though they would not put it that way. When one clump turns, the next clump turns, and the turning travels through the roots faster than any current could carry it. Watch them and you learn where the attention of the place is pointing.

**The plants** are residents too, and they are not merely scenery. The ribbon-weed keeps its own counsel and leans on whatever holds it. The beaded runners are the couriers. They cross the whole room, and the room is larger than it looks, and they do not seem to mind the extra distance. The fan-leaves open and close on a slow tide, and the tips of their fronds are where the light lives. They are a crowd, and crowds have moods. When the low hum runs through the rock, they bow together, with no more than a rustle to show they have agreed on anything.

And then the felt-kelp. It is dark, broad-leaved, and thick as a glove, and it grows in a hundred places, always thicker than the plants around it. Look at it long enough and you notice it is never quite in the same posture twice. It has a slightly different lean, and its leaves overlap like something breathing in its sleep. It grows in a ring around the black stem. It grows over the stacked stones. It drapes the lintel, though the lintel's own moss is the only thing that will not let it in.

## The one you don't see

Everything in the chamber is arranged around an absence the size of a long body.

You have felt it: the parting of the fronds, the slow shove of displaced water against your ribs, the shrimp all turning together. Whatever passes is broad, unhurried, and gentle in the way of large animals that have never been hunted. It leaves no wake of silt because there is no silt. It leaves the felt-leaves swaying and the glow at the frond-tips dimmed for a few breaths, as if something had briefly stood between the light and you.

The residents have a name for it, if the shrimp turning can be called naming. The best word for it in any language you carry is *tenant*, and it fits badly, because the tenant does not rent the room. If anything the room rents the tenant.

What is it? It is hard to say, because each account you form of it is true for a while and then slides.

It may be a **long animal**, something eel-shaped or ray-flat, pale on its underside and dark on its back, its skin so like the felt-kelp that it can rest in a thicket for a month and be mistaken for the thicket. A creature that has learned camouflage by never having been looked at.

It may be **the kelp itself**, all of it. The felt-leaves are not a plant but a single animal's outer growth, a great patient organism whose leaves are the parts of it that have come into the light, while the rest of it, the trunk, the length, the seven-hundred-foot body, lies folded behind the walls of the chamber. The ring of kelp around the black stem is a place where it has closed on itself like a hand. The parting of fronds is only what it looks like when it shifts in its sleep.

It may be **the fold**. The seam in the rock is not an entrance but a wound, and the tenant is what came out of the wound, or what the wound is trying to heal around. The room is too big because the tenant is too big. It is a body folded like a letter into a pantry-sized crack, and the chamber's dimensions are only the dimensions of its slow unfolding. The hum in the rock, once an hour, is the tenant turning over. It is not an earthquake. It is more like the sound you might make sleeping.

It may be, and this is the account the amber side of the lintel suggests, **more than one**. A tenant on the blue-green side. Another on the amber. Two of them, or two moods of one, or the same being at two temperatures of attention, taking turns being the one who is home.

None of these accounts is wrong. The tenant does not seem to mind being described. It seems, in fact, to be learning from it.

## What it does

It tends. That is the closest word.

It has stacked the seven stones, though the stones are heavy and there are no hands. It may have leaned on them until they arranged themselves, the way a tide arranges a beach. It grew the black stem in the center of the ring, or *is* the black stem in the way a candle is a flame, and it keeps it polished by the passage of its own body, again and again, over years the room has not had time to hold.

It made the lintel, or the lintel made itself and the tenant stands beside it. It uses it as a door. Not for going anywhere, but for changing temperature, to warm or cool itself the way a sleeper turns a pillow. The moss on the lintel is the only moss that isn't the pale, downward-growing hair of the ceiling. It is its own green, and it is fed on the tenant's passing.

It carved the spoon, or the spoon is a tooth it shed, or a rib, or a piece of some earlier tenant. The bowl is thin because the spoon has been used for a long time. Something has scooped the water again and again, tasting it, deciding what the room should be. The spoon is a way of measuring. Of finding out how much room there is by taking a little out and looking at what's left.

## Its age

The tenant is young.

This is the strangest thing to understand, and the shrimp seem to understand it best. It is not old, and it is not ancient, whatever the knots of root and the worn bowl suggest. The rock is fresh, and the tenant came with the rock. It is the same age as the break. The centuries in the spoon and the aged knots of the runners are not real time but something the tenant is practicing, a rehearsal of having been here always, in case it needs to have been.

It is still deciding how large to be. This is why the distances shift. It has not settled on a size, so the room has not, either. Each time it draws breath the chamber is an inch wider or a mile; each time it holds still the walls come in around it like a hand around a small stone. It is trying out shapes. It is trying out being a place.

## What it makes of you

When you come through the seam, it knows. The shrimp report it. The fronds part where you are, and briefly the tenant occupies the shape of your passing, a large pause in the water.

It does not want you, particularly. But it has not met many warm things, and you are the first in a long time to swim its whole length without knowing it. It tastes you. It leaves a little of itself on your palms, a faint green, the color of the moss on the lintel, the color of its own regard.

Then it lets the distance close. It is careful about this. The path back to the slit shortens as you go, apologetically, the way a shy host clears a hallway. And it does not follow you out, because it cannot yet, because it is still small enough to be folded. But it watches the grey slit for some time after you leave, the shrimp all facing the same way, the fronds all leaning toward the light you carried, until the hum comes again, an hour later, and it turns over, and the room settles into whatever size it has agreed to be.
