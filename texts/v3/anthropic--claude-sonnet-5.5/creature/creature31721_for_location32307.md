# The Tenants of Harrow Vent

Begin with the people, since you will look for them first. There are none. The foremen's houses are furnished and open, and the tin cups sit on their shelves under a skin of white glaze. But look at the chairs. The chairs that face the steam-slots are glazed like everything else, except the seats. The seats are bare, and worn in shallow hollows, and warm to the hand even between tides. Nothing you can see uses them, and something does.

Whoever lives at Harrow Vent lives here in layers, and the layers do not much resemble one another.

## The Mat-Folk

The oldest and largest tenants are the bacterial mats, and they are the only ones who could be called the population. They hold the terraces in bands: bleached yellow-white at the vents, orange, rust-green, and liver-brown where the water cools. Each basin is a small country with a hot border and a cold one, and its citizens sort themselves along the gradient with perfect discipline. When a sluice is opened and the temperature shifts by a few degrees, the borders move. The orange creeps uphill over a season, the brown follows, and the bleached center recedes like a hairline. This is the closest thing the place has to politics.

The mats do not know the city is there, and yet the city is arranged around them. The gutters are cut to the width that keeps their margins wet. The settling pans are placed where the runoff carries the right minerals. Whoever laid the sluices was building a farm, or a garden, or a set of pastures for something that does not graze.

## The Gray Threads

Under the overhangs and along the undersides of bridges hang the tufts of gray filament, slick and beaded, the only soft things in the place. They do more than hang. Follow one back into a crack and you find it is not a tuft but a tip. It runs through the sinter as a network, thickening at the junctions of pipes and thinning along the belts, and it is warm at the joints where the machinery is working hardest. It seems to be listening to the machinery, or feeding on the heat and vibration that leak from it. Where a bearing runs dry, the threads pile up thickly nearby, as if they had noticed first.

The same threads, finer and paler, live in the glass bulbs at the intersections. There they drift in cloudy water and glow, brighter when the tide is coming. They are the same organism, or the bulbs are its eyes. Nobody has found the wire between the tide and the light because there is no wire. The threads taste the change in the steam before it arrives. Ten thousand green bulbs brightening across forty kilometers is a single animal drawing breath.

## The Small Commoners

The rest are small, and they carry on as if the larger matters did not concern them.

The translucent flies hold their stations over the warm basins, each in its own column of condensation, and tick their wings when the tide nears. They live about a day. The mats feed them, they feed the salamanders, and the pale salamanders, blind and finger-long, slide through drains at temperatures that would scald a hand. They are so smooth that they seem to have been poured. They are the plumbers of the place. They eat the flies' eggs and the mats' overgrowth, and by doing so they keep the drains open.

The snails cover the inner walls of the cooling towers in sheets, thousands to a square meter, their shells like ground glass. They graze the mats that grow in the warm dark. Their trails are what keep the condensers from clogging. In the quiet between turbine cycles you can hear them, a faint continuous crisping, like frost being walked on very far away.

## The Waders

Then there are the tall gray-legged things that cross the far terraces once in a long while, stepping from dam to dam.

Watch one for an hour. It never breaks a rimstone edge, and it never steps in a basin it could step over. It stops at certain places, always the same ones: a clogged runnel, a cracked dam, a pan with a skin of foreign scum. It lowers its narrow body, does something you can't see, and moves on. When it has gone, the runnel flows. Its legs are the gray of the threads, and slick in the same way, and they bead in the same way. It is not an animal in any sense the flies and salamanders are. It is not a device, since nothing about it is cast or machined. It is what the threads send out when the city needs hands, a limb the network grows and lets walk.

They are the caretakers, and they are the rarest and most patient tenants. They do not answer to anything you would call an owner. It is likely that no one owns Harrow Vent.

## The Sitters

Which returns you to the chairs.

At the turning of the tide, when the steam wave rolls out white and blinding and the bell-metal plates begin their long chord, the houses are not empty in the way they were a moment before. This is not something you should test. The steam is hot enough to burn. But those who have watched from a distance at the edge of the terraces say the windows change as the wave passes. The vapor does not stream evenly through the slots. It parts, briefly, around shapes at the height of a seated body, and closes again behind them.

Perhaps it is the mat-folk, exhaled as a warm mist and settling into the shape of the furniture. Perhaps it is the gray threads, rising to occupy the seats their makers left. Perhaps it is only the steam, eddying around the chair backs in a way that looks like company. The hollows in the seats are real, though, and they are very old. Someone, or something taking their place, has come to sit through the tide, facing the window, for longer than the glaze has been forming on everything else.

## What Is Not Here

The builders are gone, or changed, or were never separate from what they built. The turbines turn and send their power into the rock, and the rock holds it. Everything that lives at Harrow Vent lives inside a large, slow, warm, self-tending arrangement whose purpose is not given to the tenants. The mats do not ask. The flies live their day. The salamanders slide. The waders mend. The threads glow when the tide comes.

At the last of the crags, looking back at the copper coin of the sun through the steam, you can see the green lights come up across the shelf, patient and even. That is the population at its census. It is not counting itself, and it is not in a hurry.
