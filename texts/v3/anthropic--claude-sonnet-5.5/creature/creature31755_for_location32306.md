# Who Lives in the Warm Sink

Start with the ones who are easy to count, because counting them is how you learn to see.

## The ones who are everywhere

The **slate frogs** are the sink's commonest citizens and its best clerks. Each is about the size of a plum, the color of a wet roof, with a throat-sac that goes translucent when it fills and shows the pale bar of light inside. They do not call. They *report*. A frog on a fig trunk flashes once, long, and a frog three trunks away flashes twice, short, and something has been agreed on that no one else was told about. They sit in the elbows of leaves with their eyes half shut, patient as bookkeepers. In the afternoon rain they turn their faces up and drink it off their own lips.

The **swiftlets** live in the wall-scallops in rows, hundreds of mud-and-spit cups fixed to the stone like a second, smaller town. They are brown, plain, and quick. They fall into the sink at dusk with their wings folded, open at the last moment, and skim the pool without a sound, taking gnats off the duckweed. Their chicks make a dry clicking in the nests, like someone shuffling pebbles in a pocket. The click is how the parents find them in the dark. Every parent knows its own clicking, and a chick that has never left the cup can be picked out from four hundred others by ear.

The **eye-winged moths** hang in brown clusters on the undersides of the broad leaves. Each wing carries a round dark mark ringed in cream. When one opens, the whole cluster seems to blink awake at you. They are not fierce. The eyes are a bluff, and the moths are soft, dusty, and edible, so the bluff is all they have. But at dusk, when the lantern moss glows and the frogs answer, the eyespots catch the glow and hold it a moment longer than they should, and the moths drift off the leaves and flutter in slow spirals through the blue. They seem to be dancing, though what they are doing is being lured upward by light they mistake for a moon.

The **millipedes** travel in processions along the wet logs, forearm-long, glossy as boot leather, each with a hundred small legs rippling in waves. They eat the fallen and the dead and turn them to black soil. Ask the sink what it is made of and the millipedes are half the answer.

## The ones you glimpse

The **gliders** are pale-bellied, big-eyed, and unhurried. In the last gold of the evening they climb to the high forks, and one at a time they simply lean out. The loose skin between their limbs flares like a small kite, and they cross forty feet of open air and land on the next trunk, quiet as a dropped glove. They hold on with soft, clever hands. They carry leaves in their mouths for nests. When they meet on a branch they touch noses, lightly, like people checking each other for fever.

The **spotted cat** lives in the ledges of the south wall and is seen perhaps once a season, and then only as a change in the shadow. She is lean and low, with a coat that copies the dapple of light through fern. She has been in the sink all her life and never once seen the sky except as a lid. She hunts gliders, and she hunts them well, because she has learned the hour they climb and the fork they favor. But she also sits for long stretches on a ledge above the pool, tail hanging, doing nothing at all, watching the frogs' lights come on. She does not seem to be hunting then. If a cat can be said to attend a ceremony, she attends.

The **heron** is broad, pale, and enormous in the dimness. It stands in the shallows so long that the duckweed closes around its legs. Its patience is not a technique. It seems to have simply agreed, at some point, to be slower than everything else. When it strikes, the sound is a small wet *tock*, and then the heron is standing exactly as before, with something silver going down its neck.

## Under the pool

Nobody has measured the pool's depth, and there is a reason it stays unmeasured. Whatever is down there does not like being found.

What is known is this: the rings that spread across the surface come from something that rises slowly, breathes, and sinks again. It is not a fish. Fish do not breathe with that patience. The rings are wide and soft-edged, made by a body that pushes up gently, lets the air in, and goes back without a splash. Some think it is a very old salamander, pale and gilled and the length of a man, raised in the warm seep with no need to ever grow up. Some think it is several of them. The heron has never been seen to look at the spot where the rings begin, which may mean something.

## The ones who left the handprints, or their descendants

There are people in the sink.

You will not see them at first, and they would not mind if you never did. There are perhaps thirty, in two or three families, and they live in the dry places: the overhang with the ochre hands, the lower flights of the north stair where the roots have made rooms, the hollows behind the fig buttresses. They are small and quiet and moss-dusted, with skin gone pale from generations of green light. They walk with their shoulders turned and their heads ducked, the way you learn to move in the understory, and they leave no paths. Where they have passed, the ferns have simply been asked to move, and they move back.

They do not have much. They have fired clay, and the clay is thumb-marked, because the thumb is how the maker signs it. They have cups and shallow bowls. They have baskets of split vine. They have ochre, which they grind on a flat stone, and once a year, in the dry weeks when the mist lifts a little, they press a hand into it and then onto the wall beside the older hands. They do not say why. When asked, they say only that the wall likes to be answered.

They keep the **lantern moss**. This is their real work, more than gathering or hunting, though they do both lightly. They carry small scrapings of it in clay cups from the deepest crevices to places where it is thin, and they tuck it into cracks the way one might mend a hem. They are not sure it needs them. But the moss has been thinner in the years they neglected it, and so they tend.

They talk very little. Their speech is low and close to the breath, and much of what passes between them passes through the hands: a palm turned up, two fingers laid on a wrist. When they need to be heard across distance, they do not call. They learned long ago from the frogs. A cup of lantern moss, uncovered and covered, uncovered and covered, makes a pale wink in the dusk, and someone on the far side of the pool winks back.

## What starts the wave

At the last hour of dusk, the flicker begins on the east wall and crosses to the west. This is the thing everyone in the sink knows and no one can explain.

The frogs say it is the frogs, and the moths say it is the moths, insofar as either says anything. The people say it is the moss. The cat, on her ledge, says nothing, but she turns her head east a moment before it begins, every night, as if she felt the first breath of it in her whiskers.

What's true is that there is a place on the east wall, in a crevice too narrow for a hand, where the lantern moss is thickest and oldest. The first frog to flash each evening is always in that crevice. It is always the same frog, or always a frog of the same line, which for a frog comes to the same thing. It is small, older than it should be, with one eye clouded. It does not choose to begin. It simply cannot help it, the way a bell cannot help the wind. Then the next frog answers, and the next, and the wave rolls out across four hundred paces of dark, and the swiftlets stir in their cups, the moths lift, the gliders stop mid-branch, the cat's ears go flat with pleasure, and the people put down their bowls and stand still with their faces lifted.

Nobody thanks the frog. Nobody has ever seen it. But in the crevice beneath it, pressed into the stone so faint you must tilt your head to find it, there is one small red handprint, a child's, and it is the newest on the wall.

## And the sink itself

Perhaps the last thing that lives here is the thing that holds all the others: the slow, warm, sweating, scalloped body of the place. It breathes in through the vines and out through the seams. It is fed by the drip and the rot and the falling. It does not think, or if it does, it thinks at the pace of limestone, one idea in ten thousand years, and the idea is *this*, only *this*, the warmth, the green, the answering light.

When the wave reaches the west wall and dies, and the dark comes down and the drops keep falling, everything in the sink is briefly listening, and what it listens for is the next answer.
