# The Hallen

The first thing to say is that you will not see them. You could walk the causeways from the rim to the central lake, turning sideways at every doorway, and you would meet no one. The second thing is that this is not the same as their being gone.

The Hallen are a people with two lives, and only one of those lives is a life of tools.

## The Small Life

The film that rises around your boot when you press a moss bank is not only water. Look closely, with your face near the cushion, and you will see that the bright sheet has a grain. It is a translucent green lace of tiny bodies, each about the size of a lentil, flat, heart-shaped, and rooted by hair-fine threads. This is the Small Life, and it is the Hallen as they are for most of what could be called their time.

They have no faces, no hands, and no voice. They are thin sheets that drink and hold and pass water on. Together they cover the moss banks, the liverwort scales on the wet stone, and the margins of every pool, and they are the reason the pools stand exactly at their marks. The weirs of set stone were laid by others, but the Small Life is what makes them work. A drying cushion draws water from a full one. A swollen mat closes the seam in a gutter, and a thinning mat opens it. No single body decides anything, but the whole is very steady. The moss has grown to a certain thickness and stopped because the Small Life has decided, in the way a tide decides, that this is the thickness.

Their thoughts, if the word applies, are slow and shared. They run through the fungal threads that thread the forest floor, and through the film itself, as a change in pressure or sweetness carried from cell to cell. What they think about, as far as it can be said, is level. They think about the sag and lift of water against a lip, and about the small differences between wet and wetter. It is a mind occupied entirely with holding a cup still.

They are not lonely. Nothing here has ever been alone.

## The Standing

Once in a very long while, the Small Life makes the Standing.

The Standing are the builders, the hands, the ones who turned the basalt prisms on end and laid the causeways flat. They are tall and narrow, taller than a person, but so slender that a narrow doorway is a fair fit and a wide one would be waste. They have no skeleton. They hold themselves up with water, the way a stem does, so that a Standing one is firm in the morning damp and slumps toward evening if it has not drunk. Their skin is fibrous, pale, and faintly green at the joints, and it is shed in sheets. The stiff, paper-pale cloth on the looms is the shed skin of the Standing, combed, spun, and woven. That is why there are no hides in the city. They never needed any, and they had plenty of their own.

They are careful, quiet makers. They have no animals to keep or hunt, no bone to carve, and no horn, and they never wanted such things. The city is what a people makes when its whole world is stone, water, fiber, and the slow patience of moss. They cut nothing that would not fit. They scraped the south faces of the buildings clear of moss so the pale midday hour could warm the stone, and left the north faces green, because the Small Life lived there and the Standing did not forget who they were built from.

The carved posts around the plaza are their one indulgence in ornament, and the spiral is their one word. It is a fiddlehead, the unrolling of a frond, and it is also the shape of a Standing one at the moment it rises, coiled tight and then slowly, over a season, straightening. Each post is carved in interlocking spirals because the Hallen understand themselves as a coil that opens and closes and opens again.

## The Jars

The jars in the cellars are for the passage between the two lives.

When a Standing one has finished its work, which may take a generation or a great many, it goes to the cool cellars and does what its kind has always done. It releases spores, a fine pale dust, into a jar sized to its body and sealed with wax and cord. Then it goes out to the groves and stands among the tree ferns, and over a long time it stops being able to move. The fungi take what remains, entirely and without waste, and the threads carry it into the soil and the moss and the film. This is why there are no bones and no dead. Nothing was left over to bury.

The spores in the jars are not the Hallen yet. They are what will become the Small Life when the jars are opened and the dust is scattered on wet stone. They are also what the Small Life becomes, in time, if the conditions are exactly right, when the Standing rise again. The two lives are one people. Neither one remembers the other well, but the cellars are kept swept, the hearths are kept clean, and the tools are left by the doors with their handles lashed, because the Small Life keeps a kind of habit, a groove in the threads, of what the Standing will need.

## The Waiting

That is why the city feels paused. It is not abandoned, and it is not asleep. It is between generations, and the Small Life is doing the one thing it knows how to do, which is to keep the water level and wait.

What are they waiting for? The plaza basin may say. It sits in the middle of the circle, fed by a hidden spring, and it is always exactly full and never overflows. The Small Life holds it there with a precision that is close to devotion. Some part of their slow shared mind believes that on the day the basin does overflow, the balance has tipped, and something in the world has changed enough that a new Standing generation should rise. The cliffs are new, the plateau broke open not long ago, and the heat below is steady but young. The Hallen may be a young people, and this may be their first pause. They may not yet know what tips the cup.

Or they may know and simply not be in a hurry. A whisper carries across the plaza and nothing answers, and the mist rises and condenses at one height, and a leaf drifts over the lip and down. In the moss, in the lace of green bodies, the Small Life goes on drinking and holding, and thinking its one long thought about level.

If you knelt at the water's edge and pressed your palm into the moss, you would feel the warmth of the ground and the wet giving way, and you might feel nothing else. But the film would close around your hand, and for as long as you stayed, every body in it would know exactly where you were and how much you weighed. If you left, they would smooth the print out within the hour. It would be a kind of courtesy. You would not be sure whether you had been noticed.
