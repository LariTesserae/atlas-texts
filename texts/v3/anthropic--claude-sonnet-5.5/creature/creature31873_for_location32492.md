# The Tenants of Harrow Weir

Nothing here lives alone, and the longer you stay, the less sure you are where one tenant ends and the next begins.

## The ones you meet

The **lampsnails** are the first to notice you. Stand at the mouth of a boiler shell and the glow along the walls goes still, then brightens all at once, a wave of green-gold running ahead of your shadow. They aren't afraid. Each one is telling its neighbor that something heavy has arrived. The message travels faster than you can walk, so by the time you reach the far end of the shell the news is already old, and the wall is dimming back to its shifting constellation. They live slowly. A snail may not move more than an arm's length in a season, and it keeps to the seam it was born on, grazing a pale road through the rust-film that closes behind it within a week. In the heat of a working day, if there were one, they would seal their shells with a thread of mucus. Here there is no heat, only the fog, so they never seal. They stay open and faintly lit, like people who have forgotten what it is to close a door.

The **mud-skippers** live in the moment. They are quick, foolish, communal creatures the size of a hand, with pop eyes set high and fins that end in small jointed fingers. On the flats they stand in flocks of forty or fifty, all facing one way, and sway. When the beam groans they bolt as one, slapping the mud, and then drift back and take up the same posture, though now perhaps facing another direction. Nobody has learned what determines the direction. It may be the pull of the water, or the memory of an old current, or the position of the one animal in the flock that happens to be looking at something. They mate in the sheds during the flood, in inches of black water, and the sound of it is a rapid wet applause.

The **iron-eels** you rarely see except as a ripple or the small kiss of a mouth breaking the surface. They are blind and have no need of eyes. Their skin reads the tremble of the ground, and they can tell a skipper's step from a crab's from a boot's. They are patient and solitary, and each keeps to its own stretch of flue or pipe like a lodger who owns one room. When the flood comes they spill out across the flats to hunt, and for an hour they are almost social, crossing one another's paths in the shallows without a quarrel. Then the water drains, and each goes home by a route it has plainly never forgotten.

The **grey bats** hang in the engine house, still and beaded with water. At dusk they let go all together, drop, and open their wings a hand's breadth above the floor, and the fine columns of insects rise to meet them. In the morning they return in ones and twos to the same trusses and hang in the same order. They are the only tenants that leave the basin. Where they go, and whether they carry anything back, is unrecorded.

## The keepers

The **weir-crabs** are the nearest thing the Sump has to a profession. Pale, broad-backed, slow, each has one claw grown into a smooth plate like a spade or a shield. A crab walks the pipes until it finds a seep, presses the plate into the crack, and settles. Over the following days, minerals precipitate around the plate, and the crab feeds on the film that forms behind it. When the leak is sealed, the crab moves on and leaves a small pale scar in the rust, like a healed wound. When a crab dies in place, the plate stays. The basin is full of these small white plugs, the memorials of a hundred generations of repair.

The largest of them lives in the engine house, in the well beneath the great beam. It is the size of a cartwheel, and its plate is the size of a door. It has held the same crack for so long that its shell is fused to the stone with a collar of orange mineral, and it cannot leave. It doesn't seem to want to. When the water heaves and the plunger-rod lifts past it, it does not flinch. It waits for the rod to pass, then settles its plate again. If it dies, the crack will open, and the water may no longer rise and fall in the way that moves the beam.

## The floor is a body

The stranger tenant is the **mat**. Under the crowded life on the iron and the pipes lies a continuous skin of rust-bacteria, layer on layer, laid down in the bog iron across the whole basin. It is not a colony in any tidy sense. Cut a sample from the north seep and one from the flooded flue by the chimney, and they turn out to be bound by fine threads that thread through the hardpan, drawing up water and passing signals in slow pulses. The pulses can be felt if you press your palm to a wet slab and wait: a faint, patient push, once every few minutes.

Some who study the Sump hold that the mat is the tenant, and everything else is its fauna and furniture. In that reading, the basin is one body. The hardpan is its skin, the black pools are open eyes that never close, and the rise and fall of the water, the great tide that lifts the beam, is its breath. The eels are its errands. The crabs are its sutures. The lampsnails are a nervous system grown outside the skin, and the skippers are its restless attention, turning together toward something it cannot say.

Others say this is a comfortable story people tell about a wet hole in the ground, and that the water simply rises and falls because of a tide in the rock below. Both may be true. The beam tips either way.

## What answers at dusk

Once a day the fog thickens and the lampsnails brighten as one. They do not flash or pulse. They rise together, slowly, until the boiler shells are lit like lanterns and the pale plugs on the pipes glow faintly by reflection. The skippers stand still. The bats hold their breath in the trusses. Under your hand, if you have your palm on the iron, the slow push of the mat comes a little stronger, a little closer to the surface, as if something below were leaning up toward the light.

Then it passes. The snails dim. The skippers resume their swaying, the eels turn in their flues, and the beam, which has been hanging in the air for hours, begins its long groan downward.

If there is a tenant beneath, it has not shown itself, and it has asked for nothing. It keeps the machine going, or the machine keeps it, or the two have long since become one habit. The crabs plug the leaks and the snails hold the lamp. Everything in the basin is a small part of some larger patience, and it is not clear that any of them, or all of them together, know what they are waiting for.
