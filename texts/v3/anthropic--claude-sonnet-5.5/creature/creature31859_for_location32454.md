# The Residents of the Sounding Ward

Nobody lives in the Ward in the way a village lives in a valley. What lives there lives *along* the chord, the way barnacles live along a hull. Each creature has found some place where the vibration is useful and has stayed. Everything is slow, and nearly everything is attentive.

## The Smallest Tenants

**Glass-newts** keep the canals. A hand's breadth under the surface, in the warm black water, they cling to the ceramic lining with pads like wet petals. You see them only as a faint blue smear, the algae's light passing through their bodies. Their hearts show as small bright commas, beating slower than seems possible, and always in time with the deepest voice of the chord. If you crouch at a canal edge and wait, you may see two of them meet, snout to snout, and stay pressed together for a quarter of an hour. This is how they talk, and how they mate, and probably how they mourn. The roar makes any other speech pointless. They pass along the waterline in unhurried chains, each newt touching the next, so news travels the length of a canal by contact alone. It is slower than shouting and much more reliable.

**Drip-spiders** are a population more than a species, thousands strung across the vault on threads too fine to feel. They have no bodies you would notice, just grey-brown beads the size of lentils, each wrapped in the sheen of its own web. They feed on the fine mist that condenses up there and on the small pale midges that breed in the wet stone. Before every rain the whole colony rearranges. The threads tighten, the beads swing to windward of the coming drip, and the sparkle overhead shifts like a crowd turning to face a speaker. Many of the tall houses have their own spider families in the eaves, and their webs, thick with the amber glow, are the nearest thing the Ward has to curtains.

Between these are the things you notice only when you stop and let your eyes settle. Pale midges hang in the steam above the vents, dancing in loose sheets that pulse with the chord. Slaters and centipedes with colorless shells move in the bell-moss. Thumbnail-sized snails leave silver tracks across the dished stairs and are gone by the next rain. None of them know each other, but between them they make the walls of the old houses a crowded and busy place, from a distance and at a small scale.

## The Crawlers

The machines are the most numerous residents that could be called workers, and the closest to a population you could count on. They are low, dog-sized, grey and ceramic, with six or eight jointed legs and a cluster of fine manipulators folded under the chin like a mantis's. They clatter and click as they go, but you hear them only when one passes close, as a dry ticking under the drone.

Each has its own round, though no two follow exactly the same one. One wipes lichen from the same vent grille every eleven hours. One walks the length of a canal and back, tasting the water with a thin probe, and stops at the same broken section of lining to hold it in place for a minute before moving on, as if remembering that it can't fix it. Some seem to be worn out. You find them halted in the middle of a street with their legs half-folded and a small amber light pulsing at the joint of the head, waiting for something. Sometimes another crawler comes and touches its manipulators to theirs. Sometimes the waiting one starts up again, and sometimes it doesn't, and the others go around it for years.

They do not care about you. They will step over your boots. But if you carry a device that fights the chord, they arrive from the dark in twos and threes, unhurried, and take it from you with a gentleness that is more unsettling than force. They give it back, unharmed and disassembled, on the doorstep of the nearest tall house.

## The Wardens

The wardens are why the Ward feels inhabited, though you may go a full day of rains without seeing one.

They are pale as candle wax and about ten feet long, nose to tail. Their forelimbs are longer than their hind, jointed twice, and end in broad, flat, many-fingered hands with fine ridged palms. The head is a smooth wedge with no eyes and no sign that eyes were ever there. Instead, along the jaw and up behind the skull, the skin gathers into shallow pits and fans of thin ridges like the inside of a shell. The throat is nearly translucent. If you get close enough, and they let you, you can see a slow pulse moving in it, and it is the pulse of the deepest voice in the chord.

They walk at the pace of someone thinking. Each step is placed, and the whole body settles onto it before the next foot lifts. They cross a plaza over half an hour, stop at a resonator, lay their palms against the pewter, and press their whole skull to it. They stay there for an hour, or a rain, or two. At those times the column's voice changes. You can't hear it over the roar, but the moss at its foot rings in a different pattern, and the vent-ferns shiver slightly out of step with the neighbors. When the warden withdraws, the note is a shade truer, or a shade more deliberately wrong. Whether the animal is tuning the city or feeding from it or simply resting in its warmth has never been settled. It may be all three and may not distinguish them.

The oldest of them, a long-boned animal with a spine that has begun to bow, stands most days at the edge of the rubble field of the Quiet Days, at the one column whose voice is set purposely flat. It has stood there, by the reckoning of the crawlers that clean around its feet, through more rains than anything can count. When the chord falters, it is the first to move.

**They are gentle.** If you walk toward one, it turns its blind face to you, and the fans along its jaw open and flutter as it takes the measure of you by the sound your body makes in the sound. Then it turns away and goes on with its slow business. If you stand still long enough, it may come and lay a hand on your shoulder, warm as bathwater, and hold it there for a moment. You feel the chord differently through that hand. It is like being shown where a note sits in a chest.

There are about a dozen of them. There have been about a dozen for as long as any of the machines' records go, which suggests that the wardens are replaced, though no one has seen how. Once, in the far east near the bell-moss walls, a traveler is said to have found something small and pale curled in the base of a column, pulsing in time with the drone, and to have left it alone.

**When one dies,** the rest come. They walk in over a full cycle of rains from every corner of the city, and the crawlers come with them, dozens, from all their rounds. For days the gathered wardens stand around the body with their hands on it, humming through the ground in a register beneath hearing. Then the crawlers begin their work with fine saws and burnishing tools, and the wardens help with careful fingers. What follows is slow and exact. The bones are cleaned, cut, and carried to the feet of the columns, and set into the ceramic in patterns that repeat and vary like verses. The old bones in the older columns are worn as smooth as the stairs. Nobody knows who first did this, the animals or the machines. It looks like an agreement made so long ago that both parties are still keeping it and neither remembers the terms.

## The Ones Who Are Expected

The tall houses are aired. The beds, if they were beds, are dry. The lamps burn in windows that have no one behind them. The crawlers sweep, and the wardens, when they pass, tilt their heads toward the doorways for a moment as if checking whether anyone has come home.

This is the last resident of the Ward, and the largest: the expectation itself. Everything living there is a caretaker of an absence. The newts keep the water moving for a household that hasn't arrived. The spiders curtain the eaves. The moss rings in the right pitches for someone to hear. The city has been kept ready, patiently and without resentment, for so long that keeping it ready has become the point.

If you stay, you will find that they make room for you. A crawler will leave a green lamp lit in a window along your route. A glass-newt will surface at your fingers. In the plaza of black tile, in the ring of the great columns, the rain will fall in silver threads around you, and you will be dry in the middle of the roar.

Out in the dark a warden will turn its head toward the place where you stand. You cannot see it, and it cannot see you. But it has your note now, small and unfamiliar in the chord, and it is holding the vault up with the rest.
