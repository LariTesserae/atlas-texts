# Who Lives Here

Not many live on the plateau, and those who do are seldom seen together. The place has never crowded anything. Everything is spaced the way the cushion plants are, a hand's breadth or more apart, each with its own lee.

## The Hare

The blue-gray hare is the most visible resident, though "visible" is generous. It is the size of a cat with ears far too large for its head, thin and translucent, veined like leaves. It is deaf, or has decided to be. The note passes through it without effect. A stone cracks a hundred meters away and the hare goes on chewing, but when a shadow crosses its back it drops into the burrow without hesitation. It has never been fooled by the second shadow. It flees from whichever one arrives first and, once underground, appears to consider the matter closed.

It eats the cushion plants slowly, taking one bite from each and moving on, so that no cushion in its range is ever killed. Some are notched with the same small crescent in the same place, generation after generation. The plants grow around the notch. The notch is part of their shape now.

Its droppings are pale and dry and are used by the mites. The mites are used by the springtails. Between them, in the rust-brown moss around the seeps, is a small economy that glows a little in the tea-colored light and has run at the same rate for as long as the fog has had its lid.

## The Bird

The dark bird has no name here, because nothing has ever called it. It circles above the fog line, wings wider than a person is tall, and never lands, never cries, never tilts off its circuit by more than a feather. Whatever it eats, it eats without coming down. Whatever it sleeps on, it sleeps without stopping. Those who have timed it disagree about how long the loop takes, and each of them is right.

Beneath it, the hare's shadow is a small thing that is also two. The bird's shadow is never on the ground at all. It falls on the top of the fog, a dark oval sliding across the white, and for a moment the fog seems to have something under it, moving.

## The Ones in the Hut

The hut is lived in. This is the thing to understand about the swept floor, the hearth of black rock, and the tally marks over the door: they are not relics. Someone keeps them.

The inhabitants are people, or were people, or are what people become after long enough at this altitude and this angle of light. There are perhaps a dozen on the whole plateau. They are tall and narrow, with the long hands of stone-stackers and skin the color of dry slate, gone smooth and faintly matte, like a river cobble. Their eyes are wide-set and pale, adapted to legible, unsharp light. They wear felts of hare-fur, gathered from the burrow mouths where it snags, layered until they look softer and larger than they are. Their shadows are two, always: one toward the dim sun, one to the north. They do not turn to look at either. They regard this the way a person regards having a left and a right hand.

They move slowly, and not out of weakness. Speed has no purpose. A walk to the next cairn takes an hour and forty minutes, or an hour, or both, and they set out with the patience of people who will arrive at whichever it turns out to be. They do not hurry to reconcile the two. Asked how far the next swell is, one of them will say, "An hour," and then, after a pause you cannot fill, "and forty minutes." Both are given as a single answer.

### What they do

They tally. Over the hut's door there are scratches in rows, and the inhabitants add to them without knowing what they count. Days would be one answer, but there are no days. Perhaps they count drips, or cracks from the stone that is no longer there, or times the river has begun. Each one makes a mark when it feels a mark is owed, the way a person clears their throat. Two of them, counting the same lintel, will arrive at different totals and nod at one another. Neither corrects the other. The lintel has more marks than either of them believes, and exactly as many.

They sweep. There is no dust, and they sweep anyway, with a besom of pale hair-grass tied with hare-sinew. The sweeping is a kind of listening. The hut hums a little lower than the outside note, and the broom, drawn across the slate floor, adds a scratch that sits exactly on the interval between the two. It is how they tune the house.

They build cairns. Flat plates of slate, stacked with care, one white quartz stone at the top. Nobody remembers the first cairn. The quartz comes from a single seam high on a shoulder of the nearest peak, and it takes a long time to reach and a longer time to return from, and no one has ever complained about that. A cairn is finished when the quartz sits without rocking. It is never finished otherwise. There is no route between the cairns because the route is the walking, and the walking is what the cairns are for.

They tend the low walls: waist-high curves of fitted stone, open to the fog on one side. These are the half-rooms, where they sit. The open side is not a flaw. They sit facing it, heads above the white floor, with the peaks standing over them, and they hum.

### How they speak

They speak against the note. Because it never wavers, they have built their whole language on it, the way a drone underlies a song. Everything said is said as an interval, above or below the held tone. A rising third can be a greeting. A fifth below is *no*, or *not yet*, or *I am here and will remain*. The vowels are shaped with the cheeks and chest, and when two of them talk it sounds less like conversation than like a chord slowly rearranging itself.

A visitor hears them only as a thickening of the note. If you have been on the plateau long enough for your chest to accept the pressure, you begin to notice that certain places in the sound have edges, that something in the hum has turned toward you, and that you have been asked a question in a key you can feel but not sing.

### The far bank

They do not lack company. But the company they keep is the kind the tarns make.

Beside a black tarn, one of them will stand on the near bank and see, across the ice-skinned water, another of them standing on the far bank, small and dim, looking back. When the first walks around the tarn, the second walks around too, toward the place the first was. They never meet. Their footprints are in the frost on each other's banks, and the prints match.

They do not treat this as a sorrow. When you are one of the people of the plateau, the far bank is where the other people are, the ones you know by their steps and never by their faces. A footprint in the frost, exactly your size, pointing the way you were about to go, is a greeting. Each of them has followed such prints for years. Some suspect they are their own. Some suspect they are each other's. It makes no difference, and the question is not asked.

If a visitor stands at a tarn and sees a small figure on the far bank, it may be one of these, or it may be the visitor. It is both. The figure, if it has a view on the matter, keeps it to itself.

## What they eat, what they drink

They drink from the seeps, where the water is cold and tastes of stone and rust-moss. They eat the mites, when the mites are plentiful, and the springtails, and a cushion plant's outer green when a cushion has grown more than the hare requires. They grind the pale grass into a gray flour and bake it as flat cakes on the black hearth. They drink a tea made from the brown moss, steeped in water that has never quite frozen.

They do not eat the hare. The hare is deaf and has never once startled at one of them, and this is felt to be a kind of agreement.

They do not drink from the river. The river is the one thing they treat with something like reverence, or wariness. It starts from bare gravel between one step and the next and ends the same way, and the water in it is clear and quick and belongs to no one. Once in a long while one of them walks its four kilometers from source to end, at the pace of the river, and sits at the last wet stone until the water stops. They do not report what they find. The gravel at the end is dry, and so is the gravel at the start.

## What they do not do

They do not count the hours, because the light has none. They do not hope for the fog to lift, because it is a lid, and a lid is a fine thing to live under. They do not ask why the stones fall up and down at once, or why a shadow can come twice, or why the note holds. When they say the place is *as it is*, they mean it fully, without resignation, with no sense of a preferable alternative.

They do not leave. The builders "left," according to the cairns and walls, but this may be a visitor's reading. The tally marks are still being scratched. The floor is still swept. The chimney gap still hums. What looks like abandonment is only a long absence from sight, and the absence, like the distances, is both true and untrue.

## If you stay

If you stay long enough on the plateau, you may find a flat plate of slate set at the edge of your walking, with a white quartz pebble on it. This is not a marker. It is a courtesy. It means that someone has noticed your footprints in the frost, and that they match another's, and that you are, for the time you are here, one of the ones who live on the plateau.

You need not do anything. The note will go on, and your chest will accept it. The bird will circle overhead on the same loop. Somewhere across a tarn, small and dim, someone will lift a hand.

You may lift yours.
