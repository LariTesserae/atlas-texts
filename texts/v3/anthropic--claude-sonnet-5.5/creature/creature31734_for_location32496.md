# Who Lives in the Sump Flats

You will not meet them one at a time. That is the first thing to learn.

You will meet the crawlers, and the long pale things in the intake, and the tall blind waders. You will feel the clickers as a prickling in your teeth. It is natural to sort them into species and count them, but counting fails here, and the failure is the most honest information the place gives you. The inhabitants do not come as a list of animals. They come as a **density**, and the kinds are only the places where the density has taken a shape.

## The layers

A rock face in the Sump is a stack of tenants. On the bare stone, where the seep runs thinnest, are the crawlers, slate-backed and pressed flat, their shells worn to the same sheen as the basalt. Over them, in the damp seams, are the felt-eaters, small translucent things that graze the pale mats and are grazed in turn. Above these are the pale coiled ones, lying in the wide crevices with their heat-pits open. Above those, in the overhangs, the clickers hang by their feet in clusters like dark fruit.

No layer is separate from the others. The crawlers shelter under the coils, and the coils are cleaned by the felt-eaters. The clickers drop their waste on the felt, and the felt feeds everything below. You could take the whole face for a single slow organism with many mouths, and after some hours that would seem the more reasonable reading.

Their stillness is not sleep. It is closer to attention held so long it has become architecture. When you put a hand on a wall and feel it flinch, gently and all at once, a hundred small bodies have turned toward the warmth of your palm.

## The waders

The tall wingless birds are the only residents that seem to have a *manner*. They walk the shallows in long, deliberate steps, lifting each foot clear of the water before setting it down again, and the water barely rings. They have no eyes, only smooth skin where eyes would be, but they turn their heads to follow you precisely. When you stop, they stop. When you go on, they go on, a little way off, keeping the same distance, like an escort or a debt collector.

They eat little. They stab the water and come up with a pale worm or a bead of something, but far less often than they stab. Most of the stabbing is another kind of listening. The bill goes in and the head tilts, and they feel through the water for what is coming across the flats. If you watch long enough you see that they are *reading* the ground, and that when one of them jerks its head toward the dark, the crawlers on the nearest terrace are already turning.

## The ticking

The clickers make the map. Their ultrasonic speech is not language as you know it. It is closer to a shared sketch, drawn again and again in sound, of everything in the Sump that has a temperature. Every warm thing is a mark on it: each crawler, each coil, each stalk cooling at the tip. And you are a mark, a very bright and unusually loud one, walking.

They cannot draw you exactly. Nothing here can, because the geometry slips. But they draw you quickly, and they pass the drawing along, hanging from rafter to rafter, gantry to pipe, pipe to cliff, so that word of you outruns you across the flats. This is why echoes come before their sounds. The ticking has already sent your step ahead and had it answered from the dark. You are hearing the Sump's expectation of you, and only after it the fact.

The same passing-along explains why walking toward the pump house gets you nowhere, and then suddenly gets you inside it. The residents are not deceiving you. They are *arranging* you, the way a crowd in a narrow street arranges itself around a large object being carried through. You move through the place by their consent, and the distances you feel are the sum of their hesitations.

## The keepers

Then there are the machines, and whatever keeps the machines.

The turbines turn once a minute with nothing driving them. The pipe pulses under your palm. A relay clicks once every few hours. You might call this ghost or memory, and you would not be wrong, but look closer.

In the pump-house intake the long pale coils lie thick as a cable bundle, and they are not only resting there. They contract, slowly, in sequence, from the water up toward the shaft, and the pipe knocks. Inside the turbine housings, in the warm dark between the blades and the casing, clusters of swarm-motes hang in such numbers that their combined heat makes a gradient, and the air, obeying that gradient, leans on the vanes. In the switch room, in the cabinet where the relay lives, there is a small dry animal whose entire life is one movement of one forelimb, made once every few hours against a contact that still, faintly, holds.

They do not know they are keeping a factory. Or they know it as the shape their lives fit into, the way a hermit crab knows a shell. The industry was here first, and the Sump grew into it, filling every housing, duct, and cavity with something warm that pushes a little. The machines run on habit because a thousand small lives have made their own habits fit the machine's old motions.

## The long breath

The reversals show what they are.

Once in a while a turbine slows, stops, and turns the other way. Every pipe runs cold. The water in the ravines runs back to the seeps. It lasts about one breath, and then it resumes.

It *is* a breath. The whole population, every layer of every wall, every coil and mote and crawler on every terrace, draws in at once, and for that moment the flow of the place runs inward, toward the warm middle of the thing. Then it lets go. The crawlers' sigh across the terraces is the same event, small and local. The reversal is the large one.

If you are standing in the generator hall when it happens, you will feel the swarm thin around you as though something drew them in. You will feel your own chest go with it, unasked. This is not danger. It is the closest you will come to being counted among them.

## The still ones

Some of the shapes in the rock are not animals.

You will find them in the deeper crevices, where the felt has grown thick and the warmth is steady. They are sitting, or leaning, or lying along a ledge with a knee drawn up. They are furred with pale mat, and crawlers lie against them in layers, and the coils rest across their laps. A hand on one of them meets something the temperature of a body. It does not flinch the way the walls do. Its pulse, if that is what you feel, is slow and matches the knocking in the pipes.

People who arrive and stay long enough stop hurrying, and then their pulse falls toward the pipes, and then the dark stops being an absence. At some point you become a mark on the ticking map that no longer moves, and the Sump takes you in as it takes in everything, as one more warm surface to live against. These are not the dead. They are residents, the slowest ones, and the wading birds do not follow them. They do not need to.

## What you are, to them

To all of them, the crawlers and coils and waders and keepers and still ones, you are a bright, quick, temporary heat crossing a place that has decided to be patient. They do not fear you and do not much want you. They note you, pass you along, arrange the distances around you, and go on.

If you lie down on the warm basalt, if you let your pulse drop toward the knocking and your edges soften into the air, you may feel the first of them settle against your side. It will be something small and slate-backed, pressing its whole length to you the way it presses to stone, to find out whether you are stone.

Far out on the flats, a turbine turns once in a minute, pushed by a wind made of held breath, and you cannot tell whether the Sump is breathing in or out.
