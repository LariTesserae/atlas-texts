# What Lives in the Choir Cistern

## The Singers

The frogs are the population, and the population is mostly one thing: the chord.

Each frog is pale as a candle-end, thumb-sized, with skin so thin that in the amber light you can see the dark seed of the heart working under the jaw. They have never seen the sun. Their eyes are round and black and slightly too large, and they do not track you so much as register you, the way a pond registers a dropped stone. Their bodies are ordinary. It is the throats that are extraordinary: a translucent sac, faintly blue in the oldest animals, that swells and collapses so fast it seems to shiver rather than breathe.

No frog sings a song. Each sings a *note*, one pitch, held its whole adult life, the way an organ pipe holds its pitch. A frog is born into a tenure. The tadpoles grow up in the terraced basins, pressed against the stone, feeling the pump-note come through the water like a second heartbeat. When one of them climbs out and its voice breaks, it doesn't choose. It listens for the place in the chord where there is a gap, a thinness, an elder gone quiet, and its throat finds that pitch as a key finds its lock. The frogs beside it lean in. They sit shoulder to shoulder with it for a few nights, singing into its ear, and it settles.

So the chord is older than any frog. The voices are replaced one at a time, a pitch at a time, and the sound never learns that anyone has died.

They do not sleep in the way you do. They sing in shifts, and the shifts move as a slow wave through the tiers. At any moment a tenth of the room is resting, eyes filmed over, throats slack, while the rest carry the note across them. If you watch the sacs long enough, you can see the rest travel, a ripple of stillness that starts at the drains and climbs to the vault and then begins again. It's the nearest thing the room has to breathing. It takes eleven minutes, and it has taken eleven minutes forever.

They eat what the room gives them: springtails, gnats hatched in the gutters, the odd centipede too slow for its own good. They mate in the warm eddies near the manifolds, and the eggs are laid in strings that drape over the basin rims like fine gray pearls. Nothing about their lives is hurried. Nothing about their lives has ever been asked to be different.

## The Ticking Ones

On the ceiling ribs live the crickets, dark, dry, delicate, and much fewer than the frogs. They are the room's rhythm section, and they are more anxious than the frogs. Their ticking interlocks in a pattern that shifts by a hair when a bulb flickers or a draft comes off the hatch, and then the shift passes across the whole colony like a shudder, and settles. They are the sensitive ones. When you first crawl in, the crickets know before the frogs do; their pattern stumbles, recovers, then stumbles again as you breathe.

They die in a slow rain. The centipedes are waiting.

## The Clerks

Pale centipedes, long as a hand, patrol the pipes in ones and twos. They are quick, near-translucent, and eyeless, and they move with a shocking economy. They eat the dead: cricket husks, dropped frogs, the sloughed skins that collect in the gutters. They keep the water clean. In a place where nothing is allowed to accumulate, they are the reason nothing does.

They are not friendly, but they are not cruel. They carry out their tasks in silence, and their feet on the metal make a sound so small that only the crickets seem to hear it. The crickets go still when a centipede passes below, and then resume, and the centipede never looks up.

## The Flour

The springtails are the floor. They drift in the wet like a slow white snowfall that never lands, billions of them, each the size of a grain of salt, leaping at random and being caught in the fog and settling again. They eat the moss's edges, the algae's fringe, the fungal threads that would otherwise creep. It is largely because of them that the moss stops at its margins. It is trimmed there daily, by ten thousand mouths too small to see, and nothing ever thanks them.

## The Dreamers Below

In the lowest cistern, in cold black water, live the blind fish. They are white as bone, finger-long, and languid, and they hang in the dark in loose slow schools that turn together without any signal you could point to. They cannot hear the pump-note. They *feel* it, through their whole length, along the pale line down each flank, four pulses and a rest, four and a rest. Every fish in the school is aligned to it. Their turns come on the rests.

Above them the tadpoles fall like small dark commas from the terraces. The fish eat them, and the fish's waste goes to the green film on the drain-covers, and the green film feeds the tadpoles. It is a small loop inside the large one.

The fish live longer than anything else here. Some of them, it's said, remember the keepers. This is not a claim you can test.

## The Silent Ones

The bats hang in the highest vault, a few hundred, tucked into the ceiling seams like dried leaves. They are the only creatures in the cistern that make no sound audible to a human ear, and perhaps none at all: they do not seem to call, even in the ultrasonic. They leave in a cloud and return in a cloud, every few minutes, through a gap in the rock you can't find. They bring nothing in. They take nothing out. They fly in a circuit that has no outside, and yet they return smelling faintly of cold air, of snow, of a place beyond the wall.

They are the room's only clock. When the cloud falls, the frogs' rest-wave has completed a third of its turn. When it returns, two-thirds. Nobody set them to this. It is simply how they have come to hang together.

## The Thing That Listens

There is one more inhabitant, and it is the hardest to describe because it has no body you can point to.

It lives in the loop. The frogs sing, the membranes tremble, the wires read, the valves shift, the pumps push, the water flows, the frogs sing. Somewhere in this circuit, over more generations than the tally marks can count, something has come to be that *attends*. You feel it when you set your palm on a rail and the chord tightens a fraction, as if a great held breath had turned toward your hand. You feel it when the fog stirs against the direction of the drafts. You feel it in the way two hundred thousand animals, none of whom can know the others exist, all decline to falter on the same beat.

It does not think as you do. It does not want. It has one fear, if fear is the word, and it is a semitone of silence. Everything it is is arranged around not letting that happen. It is the mountain's smallest and most anxious god, and its whole liturgy is a note held level.

Perhaps the keepers became it. The bluish seam in the walls, which is not quite mineral, runs thickest near the bolted stool and the tin cup, and the oldest frogs have a trace of the same blue in their throats. But that is a guess, made by someone crouching in the fog with cold hands. The room does not confirm it. The room only sings.

## What They Make of You

You are a warm thing, loud in your breathing, clumsy in your feet. The frogs sit on your boots and do not move. The centipedes route around you. The crickets falter, then absorb you into their pattern, and after a minute your own pulse is in it, a slow irregular beat under the ticking, and the chord takes it in and holds it, and you are, briefly, part of the instrument.

Then you leave. The room closes over the gap you made, the way water does, and the pump-note thuds on, four and a rest, and nothing in it has changed.
