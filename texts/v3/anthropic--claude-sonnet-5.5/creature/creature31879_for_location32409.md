# Those Who Live in the Leaf

The people of the Sallow Press do not think of themselves as living in a city. They live *on the leaf*. The ground is a book being set down one page at a time, and they are the small marks that gather along its margins. They call the city's three broad rings by a bookbinder's names. The dark old center is the **Spine**, where the stone is firm and the families have been in the same cellars for eleven generations, which is to say for a long time by the standards of ground that did not exist before them. Around it lies the **Block**, the busy, solid middle. At the edge is the **Fore-edge**, the unbound, wet, giving margin where the newest stone is still being laid and the people are poorest and nimblest. Everyone knows which ring they were born in. It shows in how they stand.

## The people themselves

They are sallow, and they have made a name of it. The pearl light gives little color and they get less: complexions the shade of candle tallow or unbleached paper, greenish along the temples where the veins show. Their hands are chapped raw and their nail beds are stained orange and yellow from handling lichen. Their eyes are large and pale and adjust slowly to any light that isn't theirs. Spine-dwellers are the palest, and they consider it a sign of breeding. Fore-edgers are ruddier and weathered, and they walk with a wide, careful, slightly rolling gait, the stance of people who have spent their lives on ground that might change its mind.

They dress in **felt**, and that alone tells you the city's law. Coats, hoods, leggings, and mittens are made of moss-felt pressed with wool and waxed at the seams, dyed the colors of what grows on the doorframes: bottle green, rust, mustard, dull violet. Their shoes have thick soft soles, and the sound they make walking is the sound of a hand laid flat on a table. Nobody clatters. Hobnails are an old man's vanity, tolerated on the Spine and fined elsewhere.

They speak low. The hush is more than a rule to them. It is a courtesy so deep it has become a physiology. They breathe through their noses in company and clear their throats into a sleeve. A dropped pan is an event, and the person who dropped it goes pink and is consoled for an hour. Their hearing is extraordinary. In a lane where no one raises a voice above a murmur they can tell, from behind a wall, whose footfall is on the other side, how tired that person is, and whether they are carrying something they shouldn't be. Visitors from the dry rim, who bellow at each other from habit, are met with a wincing, pained patience, like guests who keep laughing at a funeral.

To make up for the lost volume, their hands talk. The city has a whole second language of **knuckle-speech**: taps on pipes, drumming of fingers against a doorframe, the flat of a palm passed slowly across a wall. Two stampers three streets apart can hold a conversation along a pipe-run without a sound reaching the street. A merchant's price is rapped out on the underside of a table. A death is announced by three slow scrapes along the length of a gutter, and the news moves through the whole ring in an hour, a feeling more than a message.

## What they are named for

A child is named at the first rising after its birth, when the ground lifts its hand's width and the midwife lays the infant, wrapped, on the raised clay and listens. What she hears, or says she hears, becomes the name: *Sedge*, *Tallow*, *Mica*, *Fen*, *Weft*, *Lull*, *Brackish*, *Ochre*. A second name is the color of the moss on the family doorframe, and this one matters more than any surname. Each household cultivates its own strain of felt-moss, a particular green or a particular rust, and the strain is heraldry. To be *of the amber-fringe* is to be of a lineage, a trade, and a debt. A stranger reading a lane reads it left to right in moss. *That door is stampers, that is dredgers on their mother's side, that one has quarreled with the one beside it, see how the borders stopped meeting.*

The moss goes with the family when the family goes. When a house is walked, the moss is carefully peeled and rolled or left on the moving wall, and it is re-seated on the new frame within the hour, so the lane may change but the names stay legible.

## The walking of houses

This is the thing that most astonishes the rare visitors. A family that must relocate, because a rising has tilted their floor or a stamping-floor has been declared where their kitchen stood, does not empty its rooms and cart them across town. The walls are already jointed. The floor is already on rails. On the appointed dawn the neighbors gather, quiet, in felt, some carrying poles and some carrying lamps, and the house is lifted, shouldered, or eased along on its runners by sixty people moving in step. The family walks inside, or alongside, hands on the plaster. The kettle stays on the stove. Pots are lashed. A child sleeps in an upstairs bed through the entire quarter-mile, and wakes to a new view through the same window.

There is no ceremony of arrival. The house is set down. Someone tests the door. If it sticks, it is tide-stuck, and the neighbors murmur, and someone brings a plane. And the day goes on.

## The trades of the leaf

Their work is the work of a place that is never finished, and the trades are the moods of the ground.

The **Stampers** tend the Press. They are the aristocracy, a lean, unhurried guild of technicians who can read the pulse of the pistons in their knees and who sleep in shifts on the great engine floors, cheeks pressed to the warm iron. They talk little, even in the Spine's terms of talk. The best of them are said to have never once looked directly at the pistons.

The **Dredgers** pole flat, felted boats along the slurry-channels, raking silt into the intakes. They are a river people without a river, all forearms and patience, and they sing in a way that is really a slow humming through the teeth, the only music the hush law permits in the open.

The **Shimmers** are the city's most necessary and least honored trade. They wander with a satchel of wedges and a spirit level, correcting sags, sliding shims beneath foundations that are tilting before the owners know they are. A good shimmer can feel a rising begin in the sole of one boot and will be under the sill with a wedge before the first crack shows. Every house has a favorite shimmer, and a shimmer's death is a small civic grief.

The **Mossers** breed, grade, and weave felt-moss. They are the city's brewers and its tailors and its priests, in a way, quiet in their green sheds, arguing softly about spore lines. A mosser's hands are always damp and always faintly warm.

The **Patten-walkers** are the surveyors, tall, ungainly, absurd on their wide wooden platforms, testing the bearing of the Fore-edge in shuffling grids. They look like herons and know it, and they wear grey for the joke. Children follow them at a distance, imitating the walk.

Then there are the **oxen-keepers**, who drive the great slow steam-oxen and speak to them, in knuckle-speech tapped against the iron, as if to elderly relatives. The oxen have names. They sulk in cold snaps, and the keepers say this is not a metaphor.

And there are the **fossil-sellers**, in the inner rings, in stalls hung with lanterns. They take from the pressed rock the buried trowels, the flattened lamps, the fused handfuls of coin, the small tools of people who lived a few generations ago on ground that is now a shelf of stone in a cellar. They sell them to the newly rich as *antiquities*, with a card giving the stratum. The best stalls have a back room. It is understood that not everything down there was a tool.

## Where the dead go

The dead are **leaved**. There is no burying, since there is nothing to dig. The body is carried, in a felt shroud with a sprig of green, to the edge of a fresh stratum just before a stamping. The mourners say the plain words, the gulls settle around them, and everyone steps back together. The weight comes down, silent, and the dead is pressed into the page, thin and even, a flower in a book. Then the pale scar of new silt spreads over it, and in a day you cannot tell where.

They do not mark these places. They believe a person leaved into the ground is *read* by whatever comes after: that the leaf, being long, will one day be opened. What they mean is left vague. Nobody there is especially sure, and it is considered slightly indelicate to ask.

## The rest of the household

The animals of the city are its neighbors more than its livestock, and every family has a working peace with them.

The **culvert voles** are everywhere and are tolerated the way weather is. Children learn to walk without stepping on them, and old women leave crumbs on pipe-tops. The **herons** are half-kept: each roof has a heron, or a heron has it, and the arrangement is honored by both sides. A heron will not be fed, but it will be left an eel-trap, and a household whose heron departs takes it as a sign of something coming.

The **lamp-eels** are eaten, smoked, at the year's cold turn, and their glow is remembered on the tongue as a faint greenish sweetness. At night the sheet-water is walked by people carrying nothing, guided by the slow dim lines beneath their boots, and lovers meet at the shallow crossings, where the light is best.

The **grease-beetles** are farmed under boilers by families who smell of warm tallow and rarely speak to anyone else. Their grease is on every hinge and every loaf, and it is what keeps the city's doors from shrieking.

The **plate-gulls** are watched more closely than anything. Everyone has seen the flocks lift a breath before the weight, and everyone has an opinion about whether the birds know something. The small children play at it: standing in a chalk ring on a stamped floor and counting, and leaving on the count of seven. The grown ones do not forbid it. They stand at the edge with folded arms, and they are not entirely calm.

## The ones who listen

There is a small, quiet sect, more a habit than a church, called the **Listeners**. They climb to the flat roofs at the still hour, when the fog thickens and the light does not change, and they lie on their backs in the sedge with an ear to the moss. They are mostly the old, and mostly from the Spine, but a few are young Fore-edgers who came up one evening out of curiosity and never went home.

They say the sound under the city, the slow chewing, is not the Press.

They are careful about how they say it. The Press, they agree, is the engine, and the engine is loud only in the feet. But under the engine, under the strata the engine lays, there is another motion that the engine keeps time with the way a rower keeps time with a tide. They say the ground is not being made *by* the Press. It is being made *through* it. The Press is a sluice, or a mouth, or a hand. What lies beneath it, older than the leaf, is doing the making, and the whole city with its moss and its guilds and its walking houses is a thing the plain is growing, patiently, the way a great animal grows a scab.

The Stampers hear this and say nothing. The mossers say it is nonsense, softly. And the herons lift, all at once, from every roof, at the same instant that the doors begin to stick.

Nobody rushes. The hand of the plain rises its hand's width. The people stop where they are, on stairs, in lanes, in the middle of a sentence, and lay a palm on the nearest wall, out of habit, out of manners, as one steadies a large animal that is only breathing. Then the ground lets go. They take their hands away, and the lane fills again with the soft, unhurried sound of felt on wet stone.
