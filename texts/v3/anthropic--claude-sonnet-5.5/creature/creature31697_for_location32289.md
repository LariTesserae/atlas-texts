# Who Lives on the Shoulder

Three kinds of things live here, and the boundaries between them are not what they should be.

## The ward-folk

They have no name for themselves as a whole. Each ward has its own word, and the words are not translations of one another. The people of Ninefold Spur call themselves *the Held*. In Lower Kettle they say *Kettlers*, and in the small, ill-lit ward at Sable Saddle they say only *us*, with a flat emphasis, as if someone had asked. When a stranger wants a single word, they are told "shoulder people," which is a joke about the map and also, slowly, becomes true.

They are not a tall people or a hardy one, whatever the thin air might suggest. Several generations have made some difference. Chests run deep and barrel-like, and in the older families the lips and fingernails have a faint bluish cast that the physicians call *stone-tint* and everyone else calls *good color*. Miners who work the lantern-stone seams carry the dust in their skin. In the unlit hours, a hand held close to the face gives off a glow like something remembered. Children like to compare theirs under the blankets.

They dress for wet. Waxed wool, oilcloth capes with hoods cut long over the brow, boots with a double row of hobnails, and everywhere copper, in buttons, in buckles, in thread worked into cuffs. The copper is partly ornament and partly a superstition, since it is thought to "stay true." Nearly everyone carries a moss-hook on a lanyard, a short iron crook for pulling growth out of gutters, door-tracks, and the joints of one's own machinery. You can tell a person's ward by the hook. Lower Kettle hooks are plain, Ninefold hooks are engraved, and the hooks of the outermost wards are worn to a bright sliver from use.

**They listen.** More than sight, hearing is the sense of the place. Someone raised here can tell you from a single whistle which ward sent it, which shift it marks, whether it is early, and whether the echo has come back true or wrong. The echoes are the first thing children are taught to read. A whistle that returns three times means ordinary weather. A whistle that comes back before it is blown is a forecast, and the child is taught to stop where they stand, count to ten, and look at the ground. Many adults are deaf in one ear from the mills. They turn the good one toward you and are thought polite for it.

Between wards there is a language of whistled phrases and lamp-flashes, a kind of drawn-out shorthand. It is used across the cloud gaps in the hour before dark, and it is mostly about small things: a child's fever, a late shipment, a recipe. The great whistles at dusk are, in the words of one old signalwoman, "the wards saying *are you still there* and *yes.*" There are fifty of them. It takes about a quarter of an hour, and no one speaks over it.

### Trades

The city's work is divided among people whose jobs would seem invented anywhere else.

The **plumb-wardens** walk the foundry floors three times a shift with a weighted line and a bag of iron filings. They are the most respected people in any ward and the least talkative. They are chosen for steadiness rather than skill: a warden who fidgets sees things that aren't there, and one who is too calm misses things that are. They tend toward a dry humor and a habit of touching walls as they pass, lightly, the way you touch the neck of a nervous horse. When the plumb hangs crooked they do not shout. They set the line down, ask for the fix in a level voice, and the whistle changes its pitch.

The **fixers** work in crews of six along the pipe-lines and seams, hauling hoses and painting the pale grey-white lines across the ground with long brushes. They move slowly and sing while they work, quiet work-songs with words about arithmetic: *Two and two and two make six, six make eight if the fix is thick.* Their hands are stained the color of old candles. They have the strongest opinions about the loose ground and share almost none of them.

The **surveyors** are cheerful and doomed. Every season they re-run the measures and every season the numbers change. They keep their instruments in felted cases and sell their maps with an eraser tied to the corner by a red cord. In the surveyors' hall hangs a chart of the range with a hundred years of corrections layered on it, gray over gray, so that the whole thing has gone soft and smudged. It is considered their finest work.

The **mine-masters** re-cut portals. Every few years the entrance to each gallery must be lifted, because the mountain has carried it up out of reach, and the old lintels are kept as memorials in a long row along the ledge, each stamped with the year, like a ladder of growth marks on a doorframe.

The **moss-scrapers** are the largest trade and the least noticed. They walk the roofs and gutters at dawn with hooks and long-handled blades, clearing what grew overnight. It is dull, damp, unending work and there is something faintly dignified about it. They have a saying, *we hold the line at the eaves.*

And there are the **drift-naturalists**, who keep the ledgers. A naturalist is usually a retired clerk, a schoolteacher, or a mine-master's widow. They go out on the terraces with brass field-glasses and record, in tidy columns, which grazer had the extra joint and which bird's beak was two shapes. They are gentle, tireless, and somewhat proud of the ledgers' length. Some of the ledgers now run to sixty volumes.

### Manners

The habits of the place are mostly careful, and cheerfulness holds them together.

Every door has two locks. Nobody in the safer wards will explain this, and nobody in the outer wards needs to. The second key is traditionally given to a neighbor, "in case," and the neighbor never asks in case of what.

A common farewell is *if it holds.* It is said lightly, without a pause, the way other people say *God willing*, and it is answered with *it will.* Children who say it wrong, with a rising note, are corrected, gently but at once.

Births in the outer wards are registered twice, in two different books, by two different clerks who do not compare notes. It is an old precaution. The families do not remark on it. When a person dies, both entries are closed on the same day, and on several occasions this has been a matter of some interest to the clerks.

Tea is served whenever something odd happens, and something odd happens often enough that the kettles are never quite cold. If you see your own coat hanging in a signal-box you have never entered, you sit down, you are handed a cup, and someone says, "It'll be the ground. Drink that." You drink.

**They are not afraid**, exactly. It would be truer to say they have arranged their fear into routines: the second lock, the plumb-line, the eraser on the map, the echo counted to ten. What is left over is a kind of wry courtesy toward the mountain, the manner of people living beside an enormous, slow, and not unkind relative who is still working out what they want to be.

## The unfinished animals

Everything living in the green is described in the ledgers, and yet the ledgers are always behind.

The **grazers** move in files along the terraces with their inward-curling horns, and to a casual eye they are simply herd animals, dense and shaggy and mild. The herd-watchers, the shepherds of the upper meadows, say otherwise. Each herd has a *count* that shifts by one or two through the day and, they insist, through the seasons. A herd of forty might be forty-one at noon. Nobody sees the extra one arrive. It is simply there, eating quietly, with a slightly different cast to its coat, and by evening the herd is forty again and no one can say which is missing. The shepherds do not count aloud any more. They count with their lips shut, and if the number surprises them they say so only to the dog.

The **pale moths** rise at dusk from the meadows, not as an ordinary hatch but as a slow smoke, in which every third or fourth moth has an extra pair of wings, or a body like a folded leaf. The children catch them in jars and are told to let them go. The **cliff swifts** pour along the rock in their loud dark rivers, and now and then one turns in the current and is, for the length of a breath, a different bird. The **pink-bellied salamanders** in the wet places are said to number two kinds, though no one has found the second kind without also finding the first.

Above all this turn the **great scavenger birds**, slow and wide as tram-cars. They have no drift, or none that has been found, and this is the strangest thing about them. They are the most settled creatures in the range. They arrive on the thermals each morning and they wait. Sometimes they circle a piece of empty ground for days, steady and patient and silent, looking down at something. When they leave, the ground is unchanged. The naturalists log the place and the dates, and there are drawers in the ledger-room that contain nothing else.

## The tenants of the soft country

And there are those who live where the ground has not yet decided.

They are few. Some are people who chose to go, and some are people who did not, and it is difficult to sort the two. A handful of shepherds keep huts in the loose ground, following the grazers as they file in and out, and they have learned its manners the way sailors learn a coast. They keep no clocks. They mark days by moss and by the ticking of the rock, and they will tell you, seriously and without pride, that they are not sure how long they have been there. Their huts are sometimes found in two places. A woman known as *the Widow at Half-Saddle* is said to have two chimneys, one on each side of a ridge, and to be seen at both windows in the same hour, though she claims, with perfect calm, that she has only ever been in one.

Then there are the others, the ones nobody names. The drift-ledgers have a heading, added long ago in a careful hand, that reads *drift, human*, and under it are recorded the people who arrive. They walk out of the loose ground into an outer ward in the middle of an ordinary afternoon, calm, dry despite the mist, and carrying nothing. They remember a street that does not exist yet, or a family that lives in a ward that has not been built. They are given tea. They are given a bed and a name, sometimes their own, sometimes a new one, and a line in both books. Most of them settle. Some grow quietly less definite over the seasons, more like the memory of a person than a person, until the two clerks, comparing the books one spring, find that the entries no longer agree. That is all the ledger says. The neighbors keep the second key a little longer than they need to.

And past them, farther in, in ground so soft that no measure will hold, the naturalists believe there is something else. They have no better word than *tenants.* Perhaps they are early versions of things: the first drafts of animals, of people, of whatever the world will eventually settle on. Perhaps they are only the scavengers' interest, projected onto empty air. When the herds walk in, they walk in trusting, and when they walk out they carry, in their shifted counts and doubled beaks, something of whatever they met. The old shepherds say the mountain is not making the tenants. The tenants are making the mountain, testing shapes, discarding them, keeping one, and the rest of the world is simply what has been kept so far.

Nobody has an opinion they would swear to. But at dusk, when the fifty whistles go up and fall over one another across the moss, and the furnaces come on like embers, and the moths rise with their wrong wings, the people on the outermost ledges go quiet for the length of the last echo. They turn their good ears toward the dark plateau. Once in a while, from a long way in, something whistles back. It is never quite in tune, and it is never quite late, and they answer it anyway, the way you answer a neighbor who has not yet learned the words.

*If it holds*, they say, to the dark.

*It will*, says something, and no one asks who.
