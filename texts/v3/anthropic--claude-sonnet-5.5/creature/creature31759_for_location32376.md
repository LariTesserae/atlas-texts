# The Residents of the Settled Basin

The basin has no ruler and no single people. What lives here is a set of tenancies nested inside each other, and each layer knows the one beneath it only as weather.

## The Multitude

Most of the residents are the ones you see first and never count. The rice-sized shrimp move in clouds that behave like one animal, thickening toward warmth and thinning toward the herons' shadows. They have no leaders. Each one copies the flicker of its neighbors a fraction late, and the copying is the whole of their thought. The lantern-veils above the water are the same kind of creature in another form, tiny winged things that pulse in relay. Their glow is not a signal to any one recipient. It is the basin's pulse, and everything else keeps time to it. Frogs breed to it. Eels rise to it. The mat-flowers close against it.

The frogs, thumbnail-sized and beaded across the reeds, are the closest thing to a population with a culture. They sleep in heaps against the warm side of the spire, and each heap has a slow inner weather of shifting, swapping places, and rewarming. If you watch one heap for an hour you will see that the animals at its center are always the oldest, carried inward by the others. Nobody decides this. They are simply kind in a way that has no name and requires no memory.

The eels are the basin's couriers. They travel the clay in long translucent lengths, trailing phosphor, and where they cross each other's trails the glow flares briefly and changes color. This is gossip, or accounting, or something the eels themselves would not recognize as either. They carry news between the reed-stands and the dome, and they carry the dead down into the clay, where something receives them.

## The Standing Ones

The herons are not what they seem. They are tall, big-eyed, and grey as the light before steam burns off. They stand at intervals so even that you begin to suspect a survey. They do hunt, and their necks unfold, and the shoals lessen. But hunting is what they do with their hands while their attention is elsewhere.

They are listening. Their throats flutter constantly, and the calls pitched beyond hearing pass from heron to heron across the basin, faster than sound seems able to carry. What they hold between them is a shared picture of the whole place: the level of the water, the temperature of the clay, the count of the shrimp, the small sicknesses of the reeds. They are the basin's attention, spaced out like the neurons of a body that has spread itself very thin. When the afternoon cloud gathers, they lower their heads together, and the hush that follows is not fear. It is deference to the hour.

They do not think of themselves as many. If you approach one closely, the others turn their heads at the same instant, all the way to the rim, and you understand that you have been seen by something a kilometer wide.

## The Tenders

Along the lip of the dome, in files, work the small pale ones. They are the size of a thumb-joint and the color of the clay, six-limbed, smooth, and faceless except for two shallow pits where the senses gather. They are not insects, and not quite animals in the way the frogs are. They are closer to a craft that grew legs. They clean the coral-ordered growth, carry grains from the shallows to the rim, and replace what has worn. When a piece of the lip cracks, a file of them arrives within minutes, each carrying a single crystal of the same pearled material, and the crack closes as the last one settles.

The Tenders speak through the ground, in taps and slow tremors, and when many of them speak at once the black stones hum with it. This is the murmur under your palm. They keep no records. Their work is remembered in the shape of the thing they tend, the way a path remembers its walkers. Each Tender lives a season and is buried in the clay at the rim, and the growth takes up what is left of it. The lip of the dome is therefore partly made of ancestors, which the Tenders would find unremarkable.

They have never been seen to enter the dome's round mouth. They line it, feed it, and guard its edge from the mat-plants. They do not look in. If a Tender is lifted and set down facing the mouth, it turns itself around and resumes work.

## The Makers, or What Remains of Them

No one has ever seen the ones who raised the spire, the arches, and the dome. The Tenders do not speak of them, and the herons hold no memory of them. Yet the works are too exact to be accidents and too patient to be abandoned.

There are three possibilities, and the basin seems to hold all three at once.

The first is that the makers left, and everything else grew into the space they vacated, the way lichen fills a fallen fence.

The second is that the makers are the Tenders. Long ago they were something larger and more particular, and they gave up their size, their faces, and their names in exchange for staying. They shrank into their own upkeep. That would explain why they never look into the mouth of the dome. It would be like looking into a grave.

The third is that the makers are the basin. The clay is so fine and so thoroughly sorted, the water so slow, the flatness so total, that it feels less like a landscape than a deliberate medium. Something may live *in* it as a film, a mind spread thin across the whole kilometer, breathing through the lantern-veils and thinking through the herons. The arches, the spire, and the dome would then be its organs of measurement, not its home. The bright surface would be a skin, and you would be standing on it.

## The Hour of Rain

Whatever the truth, the residents agree about the afternoon. When the cloud gathers over the dome and the pull begins in the ears, everything stops. The lantern-veils go dark in one ripple. The frogs lower into the reeds. The Tenders freeze in their files with grains held aloft. The herons bow, and the spire's hum climbs a full step.

For that hour the basin is not many things but one. The water runs inward, toward the mouth of the dome, carrying petals, shed scales, and the small dead, and the dome takes them without filling. It is the closest the place comes to speech. Whatever is being said is said in rain, to something that does not need to answer, and the answer, if there is one, is that the light returns and the pulses start again, one, then a thousand, and the whole living dish breathes gold as though nothing had been asked.

## If You Stay

You will not be threatened here, and you will not be welcomed. But the frogs will eventually sleep against your leg. A file of Tenders may cross your foot, carrying grains toward some repair you cannot see. A heron will turn its head, and the others will turn with it, and for a moment you will feel counted, not as a stranger but as one more small warm thing that has come to rest in the shallows. Then the attention passes, kindly and without curiosity, and the basin goes on being the most crowded and quietest place you have ever stood.
