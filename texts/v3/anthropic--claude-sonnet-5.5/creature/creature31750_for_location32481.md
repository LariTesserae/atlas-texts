# Who Lives on the Shelf

## The first sight of them

From a distance you see the herds before you see the people, and you see the people only as a disturbance in the herds. A slow bulge in the river of grazers, a gap that holds its shape, a dark upright mark walking beside the animals and not driving them. Then, closer, you understand that the marks are people, wrapped to the eyes in thick undyed cloth the color of peat and wet slate. They walk at the herd's pace and stop when it stops. They carry long staffs, iron-shod at one end and worn glassy at the other by hands.

They are the Shelf-folk, though they seldom name themselves as a people at all. Asked, they say where they are from, meaning which city or which stretch of scarp, and then the season. A person is placed by ground and time. *Ledge-of-the-Long-Seep, in the thaw before the dry spring.* The land moves, so an address is only true for a while.

## Bodies made for the height

They are built for thin air and steady cold. Broad through the ribs, with deep chests and a slow, wide stride that spends no breath. Their skin is weathered to a dark ruddiness across the cheekbones and the bridge of the nose, where the thin sun and the wind have worked at it for generations. The eyes are narrow-lidded against glare and mostly dark, with a pale ring at the edge of the iris that catches light in the evening and makes them look briefly lit from behind. Their hands are large and dry-skinned, with thick, capable fingers. Many have lost a fingertip or two to frostbite and speak of it without complaint, like a tax paid.

Their voices are low and unhurried. They speak softly because the cold makes shouting expensive, and because the plateau is so open and so quiet that a normal voice carries a long way. Conversation is sparse. A sentence may be followed by a long pause in which the other person looks at the sky, or the ground, or the herd, before answering. Newcomers take this for coldness. It is only that no one here has ever been in a hurry to be wrong.

## The two mornings

The day has two beginnings, and they keep both. First Morning is the white one, when the fog fills the hollows and the world shrinks to a few meters of gravel and breath. It belongs to indoor things: mending, cooking, the reading of ledgers, the quiet talk of people who cannot see far anyway. Second Morning comes when the sun lifts the fog and the plateau reappears, wet and bright and entire. People step out onto the terraces at that moment and stop for a breath, all together, city-wide, without signal. They say something to the light. Not a prayer exactly. Most say a plain word like "again," or "there you are."

Work happens between Second Morning and the first sleet. The afternoons are for shelter, for tea kept hot in clay flasks, for repairing, teaching, arguing mildly. Evenings are amber and quiet and belong to the elders and the very young, who sit at the terrace rails and watch the herds settle.

## The people of the cities

City life is layered like the walls. Families live in the same courses their grandparents lived in, and moving upward is a thing done at marriage or at a death. Older people tend to live low, near the cisterns and the cold, and the young live higher, in the glass-warmed rooms. The houses are plain inside, warm, dim, and smelling of wool, smoke, and a resinous herb burned in small clay lamps. The floors are the same pale ceramic as the walls, faintly warm underfoot even in the deep of night, and people go barefoot indoors and set their boots in rows by the door.

They do not, as far as a visitor can tell, understand the walls the way an engineer would. They understand them the way a person understands a well-tempered horse. They know its moods and its needs, and what is a good sign and what is a bad one. A wall that sweats too little at dawn is reported. A wall that hums is listened to. Children are taught early to lay a palm flat on a building and wait, and to say what they feel. Many can tell you correctly, from the texture of the warmth, whether rain will come before dusk. Asked how the walls drink the fog, they shrug, the way you'd shrug if asked how your lungs know to breathe.

Some among them do know more. They are the **easers**, the keepers of the strain-pylons, and they hold a place somewhere between mechanics, physicians, and priests. Easers walk the fault lines in pairs, on foot, in all weather. They lay hands on the pale pylons and listen, and they carry ledgers of the ground's tension written in a dense private notation. They speak of the crust as a living thing with a temper. They say "easing" and never "controlling." An easer who spoke of mastering the ground would be avoided, and possibly asked to leave. Their gravest fear is not the big earthquake. It is the slow year when the ground goes *quiet*, because a quiet fault is storing what it will one day spend all at once. On such a year the easers walk their lines more often, pressing their palms harder, and the whole city gets a little more careful with its jokes.

## Sleepers on a moving floor

All of them have lived their whole lives on ground that turns over constantly, and it has shaped them in small ways. They sleep lightly and well at once, the way sailors do. Stillness unnerves them. Visitors from the old settled lowlands are teased for how they startle at a quiet room. A baby is soothed by being set down on the floor beside a bowl of water, so that it can watch the surface tremble. Mothers hum to it in the rhythm of the shiver. The oldest lullaby on the Shelf has no words, only a slow rocking phrase that rises and falls at the same pace as the ground, and adults hum it absently when they are anxious.

They read the small tremors like weather. A strong one is discussed briefly at the rail, with a nod toward the herds. A very faint one is only noted, the way you note someone breathing.

## Herders and walkers

Outside the cities live the herd-followers. They have no fixed houses, only a few known camps in the lee of certain scarps, and each camp is recorded in the ledgers and shifts a little every year as the ground shifts. They move with the grazers, not to own them or cull them at will, but in a long agreement whose beginnings no one remembers. They take milk, a little wool from the shed coats, and the occasional old or fallen animal. In return they keep the coursers back from the calving grounds and turn the scavengers from the weak. The herd does not seem to mind them. The grazers accept the dark upright figures the way they accept weather.

A herder learns the land by the pace of the seasons and the smallest signs: the change in a whistle from the burrowing rodents, a pool that goes suddenly clear, a fresh line in the gravel like a hair drawn across the ground. Herders are the ones who first notice a spring dying or a new one opening, and they carry that news to the cities. Their reports are copied into the ledgers, with the herder's name and the date, and those ledgers are the closest thing the Shelf has to history or law.

The herders read the animals as instruments. When the grazers bunch and turn before a tremor, the herders stop where they are, put down their staffs, and wait with their palms on their knees. They do not run. Running is what strangers do.

## Customs of water and ice

Nothing is held more firmly than the custom of water. A cistern belongs to no one and can be drawn by anyone: the stranger, the enemy, the child who wandered from a caravan. To foul or bar a cistern is the one act for which a person can be sent out of a city without discussion. No one is put to death for it. They are simply given a flask, a blanket, and a direction, and the gate is not reopened. That is enough. Hardly anyone has ever needed to be told twice.

Before crossing frozen water, a person taps the ice three times with a staff: once for the ice, once for the water under it, once for whoever comes after. Children learn it before they learn to read. Even the impatient do it. Even the coursers, some people say, though that is likely a story.

When someone dies, the body is taken up onto a high scarp-top and left in the open for the raptors, and the family walks home by a different path from the one they took up. Nobody is quite sure why the different path. It is simply done. A small cairn of scree is left, and after a few seasons the rockfall or the quake takes it, and no one minds. Nothing here is meant to stay in place.

## Travelers and the road

The people who ride the silent long vehicles between the cities look tired and unhurried, and it is the tiredness of habit, not of hardship. Riders sit in rows, wrapped, drinking hot tea from flasks. They read, or mend, or sleep against the window, watching the gravel flats slide by a hand's breadth beneath them. Conversation drifts in low fragments. The vehicles themselves are neither admired nor feared. They arrive, they depart, and the ledger is updated.

The great bridges are treated the same way. They are spoken of like the sky, as something that has always been there. A child pointing at one and asking who made it would get a mild, mystified look from an adult, and an honest answer: "They were here when the terraces began." Whatever hands laid the first courses of the cities and drew the pencil-thin spans across the gorges, they are not remembered as gods or as engineers. They are remembered as *earlier*. The Shelf-folk see themselves as tenants who have taken good care of an inheritance. They add courses to the walls, walk the fault lines, update the ledgers, and do not ask too loudly what they don't know.

There is a sadness in this that they don't hide, and a steadiness too. They know the ground is not finished, and they suspect the cities are not either. They are one more layer in the scarp.

## The others who live there

The Shelf-folk do not think of themselves as the only ones who live here, and they would object to an account that left out the rest.

The **grazers** are the largest presence, thousands strong, long-legged and shaggy, with heavy coats the color of old rope. Their breath hangs above them in slow clouds. They are unhurried, mild-eyed, and enormously aware. Their attention moves through the herd like wind through grass, and the whole body of them will turn as if it were one animal. People say the herd has a second sense that comes up through its feet.

The **coursers** follow, pale and lean, hunting in loose patient packs. They are not enemies. They are one of the ways the herd is kept honest, and the people make room for them at the edges of camps and leave them the bones. A courser will sometimes sit at a distance and watch a fire without approaching, and it is considered rude to stare back.

The **scavengers**, heavy and low-slung, worry the stragglers and are not loved, but they are not driven off either. Somebody has to clean the ground.

Above, the geese and cranes pass in their skeins twice a year, and the whole Shelf goes quiet to hear them. The broad-winged raptors ride the warm scarps and take the dead from the high places. The burrowing rodents keep the drier gravel honeycombed and whistling. In the tarns, the pale slow fish live on against all reasonable expectation, and children are taught not to catch them in the first week of thaw, when they are thin. The bogs hum with insects at noon. The rock itself seems to weep, and the people do not think of that as separate from the rest of the living.

## An evening, and a person in it

At the end of the day, on a terrace of one of the pale cities, an old woman sits at the rail with a flask of tea cooling between her palms. She is wrapped in dark cloth. One of her fingers is missing at the first joint. She has walked the fault lines as an easer for forty years, and her knees have finally told her to stop. Below her the herd is settling in the lee of a scarp, breathing in clouds. A young man beside her is reading out the evening's changes from the ledger, in a low, careful voice: a spring gone dry near the eastern braids, a new crack on the third ledge past the road, a seep opened in the barley field of someone's cousin.

She nods at each. She doesn't write anything down. She knows what she needs.

Then the tarn near the road shivers once, the faintest ripple, and she pauses with the flask halfway to her mouth. The young man stops reading. They both wait, the way you wait for a sleeping person to finish turning over. When the surface goes still, she lets out her breath, sips her tea, and says, "Good. Go on."

Across the dark flat the cranes are lifting, and the pylons hum along their line, and the city's first windows come up amber one by one.
