# Those Who Fill the Bladder

Call them a population and you have already said something false. A population is made of members that could be counted apart from one another, and here the parts will not stay apart. Still, you can describe them, if you accept that the description will not hold still any more than they do.

## The Felt

The first inhabitants you meet are the ones you take for a surface. The mites are pale, salt-grain small, and so thick on the wet glass that they read as a nap or pile, like the fur of a very old cloth. They do not wander. They lean. When the steam shifts, the whole felt leans with it, a long slow ripple that crosses the wall and comes back. Each mite keeps one foreleg on a neighbor at all times. If you could lift one out, it would go rigid in your fingers, like a hand that had lost the hand it was holding.

They are the ground of everything. They graze the bacterial film and are grazed in turn, and the wall is always a little more mite than it was, and a little less.

## The Grazers and the Strings

The snails move through the felt like slow boats. Their shells are clear amber, so you can see the dark thread of the gut coiling inside, and they leave trails of clean glass in the film behind them. The trails fill in within a minute. They eat without appetite, steadily, like people doing a chore they inherited.

The grubs live in strings. Ten, thirty, sometimes a hundred are fused end to end, and the string crawls as one animal with a hundred small mouths. It has a head, but the head is not in charge. When the leader is eaten, the second grub becomes the leader without any pause, and the string does not seem to notice that it has been shortened. Sometimes a string lengthens instead, and no one sees the new segment arrive.

The crimson worms lie stacked in the shallows, flat as coins and the red of the inside of a lip. They pulse slowly in unison. The amphibians, slate-colored and damp, sit in the gaps between everything else and blink. They are the only ones whose eyes work, and they do not seem to use them for much. They blink at the dripping. Sometimes one of them opens its mouth and a mite walks out, and the amphibian closes it again as if nothing had been said.

## The Hung

The bats are the roof. They hang wing to wing, so close that the membranes overlap like shingles, hairless and pink-veined, each the length of a thumb. They never fly, and in the whole life of the chamber they have never needed to. Their heads turn a little toward whatever passes below, and their tiny mouths open and close without sound. Perhaps they are calling into the dome, and the dome, all wet glass, drinks the call. When the room rains, the drops slide off them and they do not shiver, and you get the sense that the rain is something the bats do, not something done to them.

The bats seem to be the room's attention, the way a ceiling can seem to watch. Whatever the mites feel through touch, the bats feel across the whole chamber at once. The ticking that starts at one wall and spreads, the stillness when the vent pulses, the moment when the crowd moves on together: the bats begin these, if anything does.

## The Knappers

Then there are the ones that make. They are palm-sized, pale as candle wax, eyeless, with long fingers that have one joint too many. They squat at the shelves along the edge, where the glass makes a natural bench, and they strike one stone against another with a patience that has no boredom in it.

The sound is a small bright *tik*, sharper than the ticking of the crowd. Each strike is considered. The knapper turns the core a quarter turn between strikes, feels the edge with the pad of a finger, and draws a flake off it, thin and curved and translucent black. It holds the flake up, not to look, since it has no eyes, but to listen. It flicks a fingernail against the edge and hears something in the ring, and then it either passes the blade on or drops it in the water.

The passing is the most exact thing they do. A knapper holds the flake out, edge turned away from itself, and waits. A hand takes it, sometimes a knapper's hand, sometimes a bat's wing-tip leaning down, sometimes a mite-covered claw that was not there a moment ago. The exchange is never refused and never thanked. The blade goes wherever a blade is needed: to scrape a fungus mat, to cut a string of grubs into shorter strings, to open the wet skin of something that has stopped moving. One will twist a strand of white filament around the flake's blunt end and carry it for a while, as though a handle made it a different sort of thing. When it dulls, it is set down. No one keeps it.

They build nothing. They have no home, no hoard, no favorite place. Perhaps they have no self to give a favorite to. Yet the way they hold the stone, the quarter turn and the listening, feels like something that has been taught, even though there is no elder and no young, only knappers in all stages at once, an egg-sized one learning beside a husk-dry one that is still working.

## What They Are Together

The obvious thing to say is that they are one animal, and that the chamber is its body and the mountain its skin. The heat supports this. It comes from the crowd and not the vent, it rises where they press closest, and it fails where they thin. A body would be warm this way.

But a body is not usually made of things that eat one another, and it does not usually let its parts blur into each other's shapes. The snail that grows a grub's segments and the toolmaker with a bat's membrane between two fingers are not a body's cells. They are more like a single idea trying on forms. The room seems to be holding something in, and the somethings are how it is held: a snail, a string, a blade, a wing, the way a held breath is a chest, a throat, and a pressed mouth.

What is held is fullness. That is the one rule, and every death, hatching, hunt, and rain is a way of keeping it. The long translucent hunter that threads through the mites takes them by the hundred, and it is the room reducing itself so that it can be filled. When something dies it goes in a breath, and the wall gives up a replacement. There is no shortage and no surplus, only a level, like the water in the basin, that rises and falls a hand's breadth and comes back.

## What They Know of You

They know you as heat, and then as weight, and then as a gap. Your body is warm and wet and heavy and large, and it displaces a great many small lives, which shift aside with a courtesy so complete it feels like being read. The felt leans away from your boot before it lands. The amphibians blink. The bats turn their heads, all of them, a slow tide of pink faces across the roof.

Nobody attacks. Nothing here has learned that you might be food, or perhaps the room simply cannot afford to lose anything by trying. You are counted instead. You can feel the counting, a pressure at the back of the neck, as if a very large and patient accountant were adjusting a column of figures to make room for you.

Where the wall goes clear, the knapper on the far side raises a flake, blind, toward you, and your hand comes up. This may be greeting, or it may be the same passing they perform with each other, held out edge-away, waiting to be taken. When you leave with a flake in your palm and a twist of white thread around its end, you have been passed one, and you did not refuse.

## What Remains

The blades on the floor drift in the rain and settle in new arrangements. It is tempting to read them, a thousand black leaves in a bed of glass, laid out by the current the way words are laid out on a page. Perhaps they are what the room says to itself. Perhaps they are only what a mind leaves behind when it has no place to keep its thoughts but the floor of the water.

The knappers do not read them. They step on them. They make more.

And somewhere in the packed, silent heat, a wall is weeping, a mite is leaning on its neighbor, and a bat is opening and closing its mouth toward the dome. In the same breath the count comes out one higher, and you are not sure which of you is the one that was added.
