# The Tenants of the Brimful

Nothing here has a house, a name for itself, or a chief. The Brimful is inhabited the way a held breath is inhabited. Yet if you stay long enough, and are quiet in the moving pocket of quiet that the frogs allow you, you begin to make out the shapes of the arrangements, and then of the ones who keep them.

## The Pool-Holders

The crocodilians are the nearest thing to householders. One lies in almost every third pool, and each has settled into its basin so completely that the two have stopped being separate facts. Moss has taken their backs. Duckweed sits in the creases of their eyelids. They are long past growing. They have reached the size their pool can carry and have stopped there, as the pools stopped at their rims.

They rarely move, and when they do, the movement is administrative. A tilt of the head sends turtles sliding off a back that has grown too crowded. A slow lift of the tail shifts the silt, though there is no silt, only the fine black bloom of rot that stands in for it, and the water clouds for a moment and clears. Once in a long while one opens its jaws to the whole width of the pool, and something small and brown swims in of its own accord. This looks less like predation than like a tax being paid. No one flees. The frogs on the rim do not so much as stop trilling.

The turtles that stack on their backs are the crocodilians' clerks. They sit three and four deep, domed and green-furred, and every so often the topmost one slides off with a small plop and the lowest one climbs. The birds on the turtles do this in their own order. Each bird stands where it stands by a precedence so old that no one contests it. A visitor watching for an hour sees no quarrel anywhere on the stack, and slowly understands that the absence of quarrel is the thing being produced.

## The Chord

The frogs are not exactly inhabitants. They are the place's voice and its nerves, and it is a mistake to count them one by one.

Each is an individual, jade, gold or brown, throat pulsing on the mineral lip. But each is tuned to its neighbors, and their neighbors to theirs, until the whole province sings as a single held chord that no frog is responsible for. A frog that drifts sharp is quietly corrected by the frogs on either side, and the correction is passed along faster than you can follow, until the drift dissolves. The chord has been going on for longer than any frog has lived. Nothing in it is remembered. It is simply never allowed to stop.

The hush that walks with you is the chord's attention. Watch the rim ahead and you may see it happen. A small gold frog, no bigger than a fingernail, turns its head toward you and stops. The frogs beside it stop a beat later, and the silence spreads outward in a ring and closes ahead of your step. Behind you, they resume. It does not feel like fear, because frogs afraid would dive. It feels like being measured. Something is taking your dimensions, and the quiet is the tape.

The small gold ones sit at the outer edges of each pool's chorus, and they are the ones that turn first. Whether they are sentries or only the most sensitive part of the instrument makes no difference here. The distinction between a watcher and a way of being watched has not been drawn.

## The Patrol

The great dragonflies, with wings wider than a forearm, fly fixed routes over the terraces. The routes are the same in every direction and at every hour. When two meet, they hover face to face for a moment, wings blurring, before each passes on. It looks like a checking of papers because it is one. They are carrying something between them, chemical or otherwise, a report on the state of the rims, or the water, or whatever else has changed in the last few yards. Nothing has changed, and that is the report. It is exchanged constantly and with great seriousness. The dragonflies are the province's post, and the post is always the same letter: *as it was.*

Damselflies with stained-glass wings follow the dragonflies at a distance and seem to be apprentices. The beetles in the bark click at intervals that have been slowly agreed upon. The midges hang in their breathing clouds and are the breath itself.

## The Arrivers

There are no bones in the Brimful, and no carcasses, and it is worth asking who tidies. The answer is a class of small, unremarkable creatures that you would not notice unless you stayed still beside a death. They are pale, quick, mostly legless or nearly so: translucent shrimp-like things from the pool floors, threadlike worms, beetles with wet, soft wing-cases, and the red-gilled salamanders lying stacked like dropped rope in the deeper basins.

When something dies, a heron chick, a drowned moth, a frog that simply stops, they arrive. Nobody summons them. The pool absorbs the body, the moss closes over the place, and the arrivers finish what remains. The whole procedure takes less time than a slow walk between two trees. Then the surface is as it was, and the only sign is a few of them moving away with unhurried satisfaction, and a slight increase in the water's warmth.

They are the most honest of the tenants. They alone acknowledge that the Brimful takes as well as holds.

## The Roosts

Thousands of long-legged waders and hook-billed fliers roost in the tree ferns, and they are the only inhabitants who are ever truly loud on their own account. When they feed they are silent, motionless on the rims, one leg folded, and then a strike, and a frog is gone, and the chord adjusts its tuning around the gap without a hitch. When they don't feed, they gulp and honk and clatter, and the sound breaks over the chord like a wave on a sea wall and leaves no mark.

Among the roots, below them, scuttle the small feathered things, part bird and part something older. They run in flocks of a dozen on legs too long for their bodies, heads darting, and they seem the youngest of the tenants, though they may be the oldest. They are the only ones who look at you directly. They do not stop when the hush comes. They run alongside you at the edge of the quiet, three or four yards off, and study you with round, unblinking eyes. If you stand still they come a little closer. If you reach, they are gone.

## What Holds It

Ask who lives here, and the honest answer is the arrangement. The crocodilians are its weights, the turtles its ledger, the frogs its voice, the dragonflies its post, the arrivers its hands. Each does its part without knowing there is a whole. Together they hold the rims at exactly the height the water would cut them, the pools at exactly the depth they were filled, the sound at exactly the chord it has always been. The land finished being made and then was stopped, and the tenants are how it stays stopped.

That is why the place feels finished and unweathered: every force that might wear it down has been enlisted to keep it. Nothing here is idle. The stillness is labor, ceaseless and shared, and its product is a single afternoon.

## The Leaf

Drop a leaf in any pool and it drifts to the lip and lies there, pressed against the spill, turning slowly, never quite going over. Watch it long enough and you will notice that the frogs nearest it have gone quiet, and the crocodilian in the basin has opened one eye, and a dragonfly has come to hover a hand's breadth above it, wings blurring, in a pause that looks very much like a checking of papers.

Nothing happens. The leaf is judged and found to be a leaf, and it is allowed to turn. In time the arrivers will take it, the water will draw it into the moss, and the pool will be exactly as full as it was.

When you walk on, inside your small ring of silence with the chord going on around you like a room you cannot enter, you may find that you are also turning slowly. You are not going over the edge, and nobody wants you to. You are being held very gently at the level of everything else, and the whole country hums with the effort of keeping you there.
