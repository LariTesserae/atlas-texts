# The Residents of the Accretion Plain

Nothing here has a face, and nothing has a name for itself. The plain is inhabited, densely, but the inhabitants are a population without individuals: a few trillion trillion cells that have never once been alone.

## The Mat

The main resident is the **mat**. It is not one creature but a stack of communities, each living on the waste of the one above and feeding the one below, all bound in a slime they make together. A finger's depth of it holds a whole ecology.

**The green skin** is the roof. Its filaments of cyanobacteria are thin as spider silk, laid in a felt, and they take the full force of the sun. By day they make oxygen faster than the water can hold it, and the gas gathers in the felt as silver beads. When a bead grows large enough to lift a scrap of mat, it breaks free and pops. Those are the small sounds of the plain, the residents' exhalation, sometimes one, sometimes a whole basin at once.

The skin also protects itself. At midday, when the light is too much, many of the filaments glide downward through the slime, too slowly for any eye to follow, and the surface turns paler and more golden. In the evening they rise again. The mat moves, but on a schedule of hours and a scale of grains. A watcher who sat all day beside a pool would see only a slow change of color, as if the pool were thinking.

**The rose and purple bands** lie below the green. These bacteria live on the light that passes through the roof, the deep red and near-infrared that the green ignores. They take their energy from sulfur rather than water and give off no oxygen. They are the mat's second economy, and they color it like the inside of a shell.

**The black layer** at the bottom smells of struck matches and rotten eggs. Here the sulfate-breathers work in the dark, eating the dead of the layers above and returning the sulfur that the purple bands will use again. Nothing is wasted in the stack. Every byproduct is someone's food, and the loop closes tightly enough that the mat has no need of anything but light, water, and lime.

At night the chemistry reverses. Oxygen drains from the upper layers, sulfide creeps upward, and the whole pool breathes a different air. The residents of the top change their work and the residents of the bottom advance. By dawn the arrangement is restored, without any decision made by anyone.

## What the Mat Makes

The mat's real product is stone. Its slime is sticky and full of acid groups that catch calcium. As the green felt draws carbon dioxide out of the water, lime falls out of solution and is caught in the gel as grain after grain. By night the gel has hardened a little. By morning a new sheet, thinner than paper, has been added under the living skin.

The residents do not do this on purpose. It is a byproduct, like ash, or like the way a person's breath fogs a window. But it is the plain's whole history, and it is a history only they can write. Each lamina records one day and one water chemistry, one season's balance of green against rose. Cut a wall and you read a diary in which every entry was written by no one.

Some of the mat is always buried. Cells caught below the growing line die and become part of the wall, and the living skin climbs a little higher, leaving them behind. The residents live on the graves of their ancestors, and the graves are the architecture.

## The Wick-Reeds

Where a wall crest is dry enough for roots, a **wick-reed** may stand. It is the plain's only tall resident, and it is a solitary one. Each reed is gray-green and thumb-thick, ringed with faint nodes, rooted into the stone through hairline cracks that its own roots have widened. It drinks slowly and sweats lime, and its lower stem wears a growing sheath of white crust. It is not planted or spread by any process visible on the plain, but it appears, one here, three there, perhaps carried as seed on the wind or the storm.

It lives for decades and dies inside its own tower. The living stem withers within the sheath, the sheath stays standing, and the wind finds it. The pipes in the old fields are the reeds' graves, and they are also their voices. The choir of the dead outnumbers the living reeds many times over, and their chords are the closest thing the plain has to speech.

A reed does not seem lonely. Its roots reach into the same wall the mat is building, and the mat, sensing moisture and dissolved nutrients from those roots, thickens around them. The two are not quite partners, since neither knows the other, but neither could build as well without the other in the neighborhood.

## The Small Residents of the Margins

In the shade of tall walls, where a little moisture lingers, there are **cushions of succulent moss**. They are dense and dark green, plump with water, and each is a tiny hill of upright stems packed so close that they hold a pocket of humid air inside. They grow slowly and are the plain's gentlest life. They have no blooms, no scent, and nothing to shelter. They sit in the shade and stay damp, and that is their whole existence.

At the salt-crusted edges of the drying basins, the water turns brine and the mats fail. Here the **halophilic film** takes over, a rust-red stain of salt-loving microbes that thrives where nothing else can bear the chemistry. It looks like spilled paint or old blood, and it is the color the plain turns when it is pushed to its limit. The film is a resident too, and its red is its own sunscreen. When the rain comes and dilutes the brine, the film pales and waits, dormant in the crystals, for the next drying.

## The Absence

It is worth saying what is not here, because the plain is defined by it. Nothing grazes the mats. Nothing burrows into the walls or hunts across the shallows. There are no eyes and no sounds made to be heard, and no one to hear them. The wind plays the pipes for no listener.

The mats grew undisturbed for longer than any animal lineage has existed on other worlds, and they never had to learn defense, flight, or fear. Their strategy is patience and layering. If a wall cracks, they close it. If a storm strips a patch, they recolonize it. They have no enemies to be safe from, only time to outlast, and they outlast it by adding to themselves.

## The Whole

If you ask who lives on the plain, the honest answer may be that the plain lives on itself. The mats and the stone are one body, the living skin over the dead structure, the way a coral reef or a tree's outer ring is alive while its heartwood is not. The reeds are outriders, the moss a shy afterthought, the red film a warning at the edge of tolerance. The dead are as present as the living, in the walls, in the pipes, and in the chords that rise at dusk.

Standing at the raw margin, where the first pale film is settling on cracked clay, you are watching a resident arrive. It has no idea it is beginning a city. It has no idea of anything. It is only wet, and green, and adding one layer, and then another, toward a finished plain it will never see.
