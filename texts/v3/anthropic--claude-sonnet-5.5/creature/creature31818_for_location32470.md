# Who Lives in the Warm Dark Commons

No one here is a citizen in any sense you would recognize. There is no one to ask the way, no council, and no one who has ever seen the whole of the place. What lives here is a congregation of bodies, each holding a small piece of the arrangement. Because the arrangement is so old, each piece seems to know what it is doing.

You meet the smallest first, because they are everywhere and you are swimming through them.

## The masons

The stack-builders are too small to see as individuals. Each is a soft translucent cup the size of a rice grain, fixed in place for life and turned toward the current, catching whatever the water carries through. Their tentacles open and close in a slow flicker. Laid side by side over ninety meters of tower, there are more of them than there have ever been stars in any sky.

They do not think, but the stack behaves as if it did. When a fish tears a gouge in its flank, the cups at the wound's edge speed their work. The colony has no center, yet news moves through it: a faint blue-green tremor spreads outward from the injury, cup to cup, and the animals downstream begin drawing more calcium from the water than they need. They are patient with it, adding a fraction of a millimeter a week. Some of the ground they stand on was laid down before anything with a spine had crawled onto land, and each generation builds on the shells of the last. The dead are the foundation of the living. Nobody here mourns anything, because nothing is ever quite gone. It is only moved lower in the wall.

## The sweepers

The lanes are alive with the lane-sweepers, flat pale grazers that fill the avenues in slow, loose files. Each is a little longer than a hand and pale as a candle. They have too many legs to count and a mouth that works constantly on the film of bacteria and settled silt. None of them ever seems to hurry, and none ever stops.

You could call them the city's traffic or its custodians, but they are neither. They are eating. Because they eat, the lanes stay open. If they were removed, the film would thicken, then harden, and within a few generations the lanes would seal and the stacks would be islands.

Sweepers reverse direction before the warm surge arrives. Watch a lane and you will see the entire file falter, turn, and go back the way it came, all at once, with no signal you can detect. They do not know why. Their bodies feel the pressure changing in the deep seep network before the water itself warms, and they act on it the way you would act on a held breath.

## The burrowers

Below the lanes, in the mud, live the ones with no name, because the city has never needed to name them. They are worm-like and shrimp-like and clam-like. Some are all three in different stages of the same life. Each keeps a burrow with a mud collar around its mouth, and each has shaped that collar to its own gait: a scalloped rim from a dragging foot, a neat ridge from a creature that turns three times before it settles, a slumped ring from an old animal that has stopped bothering.

They breathe in slow rhythms, and in the densest quarters the whole floor seems to exhale in a wave. Threads of cloudy water rise from a hundred burrow mouths at once and drift together into a faint haze. It is the closest thing the place has to a crowd. If you laid your hand on the mud you would feel it, a low trembling of a thousand small hearts and a thousand small pumps, working in near unison because they all answer to the same warmth.

## The clickers

The shrimp live in the pores of the stacks by the thousands. Each stack is riddled with their homes, and each home has a pair of eyes at its door, small and black and useless in this dark. They snap their claws to stun a passing morsel or to warn a neighbor. A single snap is only a sharp, dry tick. But when one is startled by a shadow or a drifting eel, the snap startles the next, and the next, and a ripple of clicks runs across a whole district of the city like rain crossing a lake.

No shrimp intends this. The ripple is the nearest thing to public speech the place has, and it says only one thing: *something has moved.*

## The eels

The ghost eels have no color and hardly any eyes. They move through the arches slowly, in long S-curves, tasting the water for the trace of sleeping flesh. A tube-dweller retracting into its tube leaks a little scent. A young sweeper whose legs are still soft gives off a different one. The eel does not chase. It flows into the place where the scent is thickest and waits for the dark to give it something.

They are not cruel, only patient. When an eel finds a burrow and settles its head against the collar, the burrow's owner goes very still. The eel may wait a long time. Sometimes it leaves empty, and the burrow's owner does not come out for hours. This too is part of the city's rhythm: something is always being hunted, and something is always holding its breath.

## The drifters

Above the stacks, in slow circuits, hang the great drifters, wide leaf-shaped animals somewhere between ray and jelly. Along their edges run dim lanterns in pale rows. They do not hunt the city. They pass through the water above it and feed on the fine rain of particles that sifts down from higher levels, the last remains of some far-off life. Because they are so large and so slow, they have little to fear. Their lanterns, rather than luring anything, seem to help them keep their distance from one another, a courtesy of light like ships passing at night.

When a drifter crosses over a lit stack, the small sparks on the stack go dark in a wave beneath it. The masons, sensing the shadow of a body they cannot see, dim their glow, and the dimming follows the drifter across the city like a passing hand. Then it is gone, and the lights come back one by one.

## The Keepers

And then there are the Keepers, who are the reason the place is more than a colony.

A Keeper is broad as a door and low as a table, with a domed shell of pale, pitted stone. Its edge is scalloped, its underside is a wide muscular foot, and around its rim is a fringe of soft feelers like the tassels of a curtain. It has no eyes to speak of. It has no need of them. It moves slowly enough that an hour's travel is a matter of a few body-lengths, and where it passes it leaves a smooth furrow in the silt.

The Keeper lives on a single stack, or a few, and it does one thing. It walks the stack's foot and flank, dragging its feelers over the surface. It is reading. Each feeler can taste the thickness of the silt on a wall: how much has settled, how long since the last disturbance. That is the law of the place. *Silt is time.* A surface scrubbed by current stays clean. A surface that has been left alone wears a soft gray coat. A crack that has gone unmended gathers a little more, and a little more, and eventually the coat over it is deep enough that the Keeper stops, presses its rim to the wound, and begins.

From beneath its foot comes a pale paste, warm as the water, smelling faintly of chalk. It fills the break and stiffens. In a few hours it is stone, and the masons crawl across the new surface and begin to build on it as if it had always been there. When the Keeper finally lifts away, the mended place is very slightly smoother than the rest, and after a decade or two not even a Keeper could find the seam.

Some Keepers have stayed with one tower for centuries. They know its whole body: the places where the current runs hard, the seams that have failed before, the pores where the shrimp have crowded too thick and weakened the wall. Their shells are the same pitted stone as the stacks they tend. A Keeper is, in a way, a small mobile piece of the stack itself. Old Keepers are patched. Their carapaces carry seams of pale paste laid on by other Keepers over the years, so that the oldest of them are more repair than original. If you asked whether it was the same animal that arrived in the first century of its watch, there would be no one to answer, and the question would not matter.

The Keepers do not command. The shrimp, the sweepers, the eels, and the masons take no orders from them. But the whole city arranges itself around them without knowing it. Sweepers keep a gap around a working Keeper, and the shrimp go quiet in its pores. A Keeper at work is a still point, and the ordinary noise of the place swirls around it and slowly settles.

## The wanderers

At the edges, where the city thins, you meet those who belong to no stack. A hermit-crab drags a borrowed shell across the open floor. A long pale fish passes through and does not return. Something large and dark once crossed the far edge, sensed only as a pressure change and a dim red glow that faded. These are the visitors, and the city neither welcomes nor refuses them. It absorbs what it can and lets the rest pass.

## And all of them

The truth of the place is that none of them lives alone. The masons feed the shrimp, the shrimp feed the eels, the eels leave scraps for the burrowers, the burrowers feed the mud, and the mud feeds the sweepers. The tubeworms at the seeps, red-tipped and rooted in the warmth, feed everything by way of a chemistry no one sees. Each of them is a mouth and a wall and a meal. Each is doing exactly what it has always done.

When the warm surge rolls through, every one of them, from the smallest mason to the largest drifter, answers at once: the sweepers turn, the tubeworms retract, the drifters swing on their long slow arcs, the silt lifts in gray curtains from the plain and hangs, glimmering. For a few minutes the whole city is one animal, breathing.

Then the water cools, the silt begins to fall, and somewhere at the foot of an old stack a Keeper puts down one foot, then another, and goes back to reading the wall.
