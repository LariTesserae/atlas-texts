# Who Lives in the Wet Room

## The short answer

One household lives there, and it is spread across so many bodies that the bowl looks like a crowd. The frogs, herons, beetles, and ferns are the household's members. The reeds are its oldest members, and its skeleton. Its mind is the chorus, and its breath is the fog.

## The elders

The reeds are the ones who were here first, and the word *first* is nearly meaningless for them. They were grown, not built. Their surfaces are worn like river glass, but the wear is the kind that comes from being alive and slow. A hundred and forty of them stand thirty paces apart, each with its own note, and they hold one another in a chord, the way old neighbors hold a conversation they no longer need words for.

They do not move, and they do not exactly think. They keep the household's long accounts: how much water is in the deep rock, how much the air will give, how many mouths the room can feed this century. Every forty minutes they lower their voices by a half-step, and the room breathes. That is the entire visible extent of their governing, and it is enough. They are less rulers than a heartbeat everyone has agreed to follow.

When a reed's lichen crust grows a new layer, the frogs on its base go quiet for an evening. It is the nearest thing the bowl has to a birthday, or a funeral, or both.

## The chorus

The frogs are the household's voice, and the voice is where its intelligence lives. No single frog knows anything remarkable. A translucent-bellied fingernail of a creature knows how to hold one pitch and how to shift it when the chord drops. But tens of thousands of them, joined by cicadas, honeyeaters, the three-note fluting bird, and the ants whose highways can be heard, add up to something that can be asked a question.

It answers slowly. If you stay long enough you will hear that its phrases return, that a refrain sung on the east wall comes back on the west wall a breath later, changed. The changes are not errors. They are the way the chorus argues with itself and settles. It talks mostly about water: where it is, where it has gone, who has drunk too much. It also talks about the hawk, and it has a long, falling phrase for the hawk that sends silence across the walls. It has phrases for guests, though the guests never learn to hear them as such.

Each frog's glassy, pearl-grey bones ring when it calls. That is the reeds' material, taken in through generations of drinking, and it is how the reeds hear. A frog is a small, warm, wet extension of the elders' ear.

## The hands

The household needs hands, and they are the herons, mantises, whip snakes, and beetles: the animals that carry, clean, cull, and repair. The heron's pale line beneath the skin of its leg is a splint of reed-stuff, and it stalks the pools not only to eat. It knocks silt from the basin drains with its long toes. The beetles with grey wing-cases scour lichen from the reeds' tips, but never enough to strip a layer. The whip snakes keep the frog population at the number the water can bear. The predators are not enemies of the household. They are its budget, and every death is entered in the sound.

The hawk that crosses the opening is both an outsider and a member. It hunts here, so it pays in the same coin as everyone else. The chorus fears it exactly as much as it should.

## The ferns and the fig

The plants are the household's quiet, well-mannered relatives. They stand apart because they were taught to. Each tree fern keeps its wide circle of moss like a dining table, not a fence. The twisted fig, with its roots draped over a slab, is the eldest of the plants and the only one the reeds seem to defer to. Its roots go deep enough to touch whatever the reeds send water toward. Its fruit feeds the birds through the dry months, and its shade is where the youngest frogs learn to hold a pitch.

## The tenants of the house

Then there is the house of fitted stone, with its doorless oval opening and its warm seam in the wall, and the household's one unfinished mystery.

The opening is sized for a crouching person, and the bench is curved for a body that sits with its knees drawn up. Frogs sleep there, but they sleep at the edges, the way one sleeps in a bed with someone else's shape still in it. Something used the house. Something may still.

If the household has a keeper in the way people mean the word, it is seen only in the thickest part of the fog, when the world is an arm's length wide. Then, in the pale, there might be a figure that stoops at the oval, slender and grey-boned, moving with a heron's care, with cool hands and a low hum in its chest a half-step under the chord. Or it might be a heron. Or a stack of frogs on the bench, arranged the way frogs arrange themselves when the fog is deep. The bowl does not correct you either way.

The likeliest truth is that the makers never left. They were the first to drink the water and take the grey into their bones, and they were the first to learn the chord. Over ages they let the reeds keep the accounts and the chorus keep the talk, and they slowly became the household's own creatures. They stopped needing to be distinct. The house is not abandoned. It is where the household keeps the shape it used to have, so that it remembers.

## The guest

The last inhabitant is temporary. Sometimes a person comes down the dished ramp, stands under the leaning arch during a pulse, and finds the fog inside it perfectly still. The household notices. The chorus makes a brief, curious phrase that has no equal in its language for hawk or heron. It is a quieter shape, a note held for a second and then released.

The visitor leaves with a half-step in their chest. The household does not keep them. It only lends them, for a little while, a place in the chord.
