# Those Who Live in the Vault of Black Leaf

Nothing here walks, and nothing watches you descend. The stair rings under your boots, and no head lifts at the sound. The inhabitants of the vault are the ones who have no heads: the black-leaved, the heat-turned, a slow congregation that has been arranging itself around one flame for longer than the flame has been anyone's business.

## The Fountains

The great ones are the tree-ferns, and they are the nearest thing the vault has to elders. Each stands on a trunk gone ropy with aerial roots. The roots hang, braid, and thicken until the trunk looks less like a stem than like a rope someone has been paying out from the crown for a century. The fronds arch out and over in a dark spray and droop at their tips, so that every fern is a fountain caught in the instant before it falls.

They do not crowd. Between one stand and the next lies a margin of bare, glassy rock, and this spacing is the first thing that makes you suspect they are in negotiation. A fern's shadow of warmth, the field of heat it has claimed from the fissure, ends where the next one's begins. Where two crowns overlap, the lower leaf is pale and thin and starved, and the upper leaf is glossy, fat, and faintly feverish. There is no violence in it, only a slow arithmetic of who stands where the warmth falls. The pale leaves are not mourned. They are dropped, and they add to the litter.

All of them lean together toward the far wall, a few degrees, in agreement. It is the posture of a congregation, or of a field of listeners turned toward a speaker who has never said a word.

## The Rosettes

Low to the ground, wide as cart wheels, the rosettes live like people who have decided to keep their heads down. Each is a cupped whorl of ribbed leaves, layered inward like the petals of a flower that never opens. The centers hold water. A drop from the ceiling, arriving after its long fall, strikes a rosette with a tick, and the whole plant gives a faint shudder along its ribs. This is as close to an event as the vault provides.

The rosettes are the great collectors. Their leaves are finely pitted like sponge, and the pits hold beads of condensation. A rosette is always slightly wet and slightly warmer than the air. Lay your palm on one and it feels like touching a sleeper's forehead.

## The Hanging Vines

The vines never touch the ground. That is their whole character. They descend from the broken teeth of the ceiling, thick as an arm, and sag in long slow catenaries, and at the low point of each curve they simply stop, a hand's breadth or an arm's length above the floor. Some sway on the convection currents, so slightly that you see it only against the flare.

They are the vault's oldest gamblers. Every so often, at the sagging point, a pale root-tip forms, lengthens over seasons, and hangs, reaching for a floor it has not touched. If one does touch, the ground may take it, and a new stand will begin. Nobody knows how many stands began that way. It is easy to imagine the whole grove as the slow, accumulated result of vines that finally, patiently, arrived.

## The Dust-Bearers

There are no flowers, but the oldest fronds carry grey bladders at their tips. The clusters swell over weeks, tighten to a drum-skin sheen, and split with a small papery pop. What they release is a pale dust, fine as flour, that hangs in the air as if unsure.

Then it rises. The convection takes it, and it drifts up through the red-brown dusk, past the lamps, past the hanging vines, toward the largest break in the ceiling and the dark beyond. In the vault there is no wing to carry it and no foot to trample it into new ground. The plants have sent their children upward for ages, into the underside of another dark. Whether any of it lands somewhere warm, in some other hollow of the rock, is not something the grove can know. It releases the dust anyway. It has the quality of prayer sent up a chimney.

## The Pale Moss

Along the copper cable, in bright thin filaments, lives something smaller and stranger. The moss follows the braid the way a river's green follows its banks, in a single continuous line across the whole bowl from dynamo to last post. It is pale, nearly white in the lamplight, and it grows on the cable and on nothing else. If you follow it from lamp to lamp, you follow the moss.

The plants nearest the cable are the largest in the vault. Whether the moss shares something with them, or feeds on stray current, or merely marks where the copper is warm, no one recorded. It has the character of a courier or a lineman: thin, dutiful, everywhere along the line. It does not mind the lamps. In the failed fifth lamp, with its red thread of a filament that never quite goes out, the moss has reached the socket and gone no further, as though respecting the last small ember.

## The Red Slime and the Crust

At the pool's margin and in the wet seams of the wall live the ones that are barely plants at all. The orange and white crust that stains the edge of the pool is alive in places: thin mats of something that eats minerals and leaves color behind. It is brittle as frost and grows by the width of a hair each year. Where the rock sweats a soft bead of iron-red slime, the slime lives too, in its way, a smear of patient chemistry that turns stone into rust and rust into a little more of itself.

These are the vault's foundation. They were here before the black leaves, and they made the first crumb of soil, the dark wet handful under the ferns that no boot has ever been able to make from basalt. They ask for nothing, and they keep the pool's margin bright.

## The Engine

Some will say that the engine is not a resident, that it is only a relic of the people who left. But watch it. The flywheel turns once every few minutes, with a long pale gasp of steam and a tick of the valve gear, and there is no hand on it. The boiler blisters and bleeds rust and holds its pressure. The dynamo, furred green, makes its current. The flare feeds the boiler, the boiler feeds the engine, the engine feeds the lamps, and the lamps feed the moss and warm the largest ferns, which lean and drink and drop their litter and grow toward the flame. This is a circle, and no one is tending it.

If anything here has a heartbeat, it is the engine: slow, mechanical, rust-eaten, and faithful. It turns because the gas still comes. It will stop when the gas stops or the rust wins, and the plants will not notice the day it happens. They will only, over some seasons, lean a little differently.

## The Whole of Them

Perhaps the truth is that the vault holds one inhabitant. The ferns lean to the same wall. The leaves rotate together on the swell of the flare, a motion you catch only by looking away and back. The dust goes up in a single slow breath. The moss carries the current's news along the cable, the pool's crust keeps the ground, and the litter lies in its perfect, undisturbed drifts, because there is nothing here to disturb it.

Whether that adds up to a mind, or only to the shape a mind would take if it were made of leaves and heat and waiting, you cannot say. But when the flare shrinks to a blue bead, and the whole grove seems to hold itself still, and the drip falls somewhere in the dark and ticks against a leaf, you feel it plainly, the way you feel a room full of people who have stopped talking because you walked in. Then the pressure builds behind the rock, the flame swells and roars softly, and the black leaves turn, a little, toward the light.
