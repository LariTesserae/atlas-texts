# The Residents of Ouel Cirque

The first answer is that plants live here, and the plants are the town. There are no tenants in the buildings, because the buildings are the tenants. A wall is a colony of stems, and a street is the space between two lives that have not yet quite touched. But the more slowly you look, the less that answer holds, and the town turns out to be crowded with kinds of living that overlap, depend on one another, and do not always agree.

## The Masons

The cushion plants are the oldest citizens and the most numerous. Each dome is a single individual, though it looks like a crowd. Ten thousand growing tips share one root and one slow decision. A dome grows perhaps the width of a thumb in a decade, and the tall ones by the moraine gap may have been seedlings when the ice was still in the lower bowl. They live by accumulation. They keep their dead, sheathing every stem in the leaves of every past year, and the sheath holds heat, water and shape. Their lives run inward. The living green is a skin, a finger deep, over a body that is mostly memory.

A mason does not build the street. It leans toward light and warmth and away from the neighbor's shade, and it dies at the middle when the middle can no longer be fed. The hollow it leaves is what everyone else calls a court. A mason's death is the town's civic event: a room opens, a pool forms, and the neighbors begin, at their own speed, to lean in.

## The Stovekeepers

The great rosettes are different in temperament. Where the masons are patient and dense, the rosettes are extravagant. Each one spends sugar through the night to keep a small warmth in its crown, and that warmth is a kind of wealth it cannot help giving away. Small ferns and gentians gather at the base. A brown bird nests in the felt. Springtails wake early in the damp breath.

They live alone, one to a court, and they seem solitary, but they are the most social organisms in the bowl. Every living thing near one orients to it. If a rosette dies, its ring of gardens goes with it, and the court goes cold and gray. The masons take a decade to close the hollow, and until then a dead rosette stands there as a pale hollow post, shaggy and unlit.

Some stoves are old and some are young. The young ones are small silver fists at the edge of the ice-scraped stone, barely warm, with only mosses around them. Their gardens are a rumor.

## The Roofers

The tree-heathers and dwarf willows are the town's weathered elders, though they are not the oldest. They look old because they are bent. Every winter the snow presses them flat and every summer they get up again a little crookeder. Their trunks have learned to lean and knit, and their arms are grafted in places where the bark has worn through and grown back as one. The roof over a court is often a single tangled body with several root systems.

They do not like the masons, exactly. They drop leaf litter onto the domes and shade them. The masons, in turn, build peat and hold water at the willows' feet. It is an old dispute, negotiated in centimeters. The moss curtains that hang from the roofs belong to neither party. They are tenants of the air, and they take water directly from the fog without asking anyone.

## The Stair-Gardeners

Under the cliff seeps, tiers of liverwort and saxifrage hang in the sheet water. They are the town's first citizens in a real sense: they were here when the walls were bare, and the peat below them is largely their leavings. Each tier is thinner than a page. They are so soft and so shallowly rooted that a rockfall can strip a stair in a moment. A stair regrows in a few years, from spores and fragments, in the same place, in almost the same shape. They are the town's memory of where the water comes out.

## Below

Under the peat, a felt of fungal thread runs among the roots of everything. It has no crown, no dome and no face, and it is the one resident nobody can point to. It carries sugar from the stoves outward and phosphorus back inward. It carries water uphill by small margins. It is why the courts are so evenly spaced, or at least it is one reason. A rosette's warmth reaches only so far, and the threads pass its gifts along to whatever can use them. The nearly regular angles of the streets may be nothing more than the geometry of what can be fed from where.

Whether the felt is one individual or ten thousand cannot be said from above. It might be that the town is a single thing, with masons, stoves and roofs as its organs. It might be that the town is a treaty between strangers. The felt does not resolve the question. It lives by being the answer to neither.

## The Small Tenants

Then there are the animals, who alone treat the place as a place.

The **voles** know it best. They have worn their runs into the streets, tunnels within tunnels, and they know which domes will bear weight and which are hollow. A vole is born knowing the town's bends the way a person knows a stairwell. They rarely cross a court in the open. They sleep in the dead centers of old domes, in chambers lined with moss, where the peat holds a little of yesterday's warmth. They are the town's gossips, though they say nothing. Their droppings, in tiny neat heaps on the dome-tops, are how the masons get fed.

The **springtails and pale beetles** are the crowd, the ones you notice only when the thaw brings them out by the thousands. They are grazers and tidiers. They eat the first rot of dead moss and make soil of it. In the pattering hour of morning they cross the wet surfaces like a grit that has decided to move, and then they vanish again into the curtains.

The **brown bird** is the loneliest. One pair to a stove, perhaps, or none. It lays its eggs in the warm felt of the crown, and the eggs do not chill. It says almost nothing, one thin upward note from the roofs, like a question that will not be finished. Nobody knows what it eats in winter, or whether it leaves. Some say it sits out the snow inside the chimneys, in the blue light. Most of the year you do not see it, and you learn its presence only by the single note.

The **fairy shrimp** are the sparks. They hatch in the courts each thaw from eggs that have waited, frozen and dry, perhaps for years. For a few weeks each pool holds a handful of them, red as embers in the black water, and then they lay their eggs and are gone. They are the town's briefest citizens, and they carry the most faith: each egg trusts the same pool to be there when it wakes.

## The Others

At the edges, where the ice has just left bare stone, the pioneers work. Crust lichens and thread-mosses take the rock a grain at a time. They are the first course of a future district, and they will not see it. They live, if that is the word, at the speed of stone, and they are indifferent to the town behind them.

And there are visitors, occasionally, who come in from the valley and walk the streets with their shoulders turned sideways. They find the place beautiful and slightly wrong. They hear their own voices die at arm's length. They feel the breath of a stove on their faces and are unsettled by how much like a hearth it is. They usually say the town seems planned. Then they leave, and the town does not notice them. Neither the voles nor the masons have ever registered that a person was there.

## What Lives Here, Then

The honest answer is a process wearing many bodies. A town is here, moving uphill by inches, following the cold. Its oldest districts fall in behind it, and the dead domes, the cold stoves and the collapsed roofs go back to peat, which feeds the next street. Nothing here is the town, and yet everything is. The masons are the walls only because the stoves are warm, and the stoves are warm only because the felt feeds them. The felt lives only because the peat is wet, and the peat is wet because the stair-gardens catch the seeps. The voles, the shrimp and the shy brown bird are guests of an arrangement that no one made.

If you stay long enough, it seems the town is what water and warmth do when they are given four hundred paces and a few centuries. What lives there is the doing itself, made visible, with a thin upward note coming from somewhere in the roofs.
