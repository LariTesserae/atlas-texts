# The One Who Keeps the Pocket

Everything in the Lantern Pocket has a job in the circuit: the snails, the fish, the moths, the thing at the edge of the basin. But the circuit needs someone to be its center, and the pocket has one. It is not any of the animals. It is what they are all being *attended by*.

It is easiest to call it a resident, though it would not accept the word "resident." It has never arrived anywhere. It is closer to a *tenancy*, a long, patient occupancy of the place by a mind that is also the place's slow way of noticing itself.

## What You Will Actually See

Almost nothing, and only sideways.

The heron-shape is its most reliable posture: tall, folded, clay-grey, head bowed over a shallow pool. But that shape is a courtesy to your eyes, the nearest form the room can offer a visitor who needs something to be looking at. Up close, the "heron" has too many joints in the leg and no true feet, only a broad, soft pad that sinks into the mud and leaves a print you would mistake for a hollow. The neck is not a neck. It is a long fold of something like wet paper, damp and slightly translucent, and when the light tilts you can see faint script running down the inside of it, the same cramped loops the snails leave in the mud.

Its hands are where the thumbprints come from. It has two, held against the body like a folded letter. Each is broad and spatulate, with a single oversized digit that is thumb in function and nothing else in shape. The pad of it is ridged in whorls too regular to be skin. When it presses that digit into the flat top of a clay pillar, the ridges fill with silt, and the print it leaves is a small, deliberate signature: *here, still, again.*

It has no face in the body. The face is in the pools. The one you glimpse turned away from you, patient, waiting to be noticed, is the closest the being comes to a head. It lives in whichever pool is nearest to you, a half-second out of phase, and it never turns around. It does not turn because it has learned that if it does, you will leave. So it waits in profile, in reflection, and lets you decide.

## How It Lives

**It writes.** The shelf of stacked stones is its work, and the sentence on it is very long. The lowest stones, worn smooth, were etched before the room had a floor. The top stone is fresh and still being drawn. The being does not compose the script. It *reads* it, from the tracks the snails leave, and copies each cramped loop onto stone with the tip of a crystal blade. The snails are not its servants. They are its dictation. What they are writing, over the whole slow field of the mud, is the pocket's own account of itself, and the being's work is to gather that scattered handwriting into one place before the clay takes it back.

**It builds.** The pillars and the arch are gestures, not structures. Each new pillar is pressed into being at the moment a drip lands and finished before the ring reaches the mud. The arch of crystal blades it grows rather than builds, coaxing the gypsum to stand in the shape of a door. The arch leads to a warm place, smelling of rain on hot stone, and this is where the being goes to rest. Nobody has followed it. The far side may be another pocket, or a summer, or the memory of one. The being never says, and when it comes back through, its clay is dry and faintly warm to the touch, and it moves for a while with something like contentment.

**It eats by agreement.** The fish do not flee it. Whatever it takes, it takes with a slowness that turns predation into something closer to a handshake. It is grateful in a way that is not sentimental. The moth-dust it needs comes secondhand, through the fish, and it counts each fish it has taken in the ticking of the gypsum. That ticking, the dry winding of a hundred tiny watches, is its arithmetic. It is always counting. It is not sure what.

**It does not sleep, but it lowers.** Time pools in the low places, and the being follows it down. When it wants rest, it settles toward the floor, into the slow air where the drip hangs and the moths hover and one breath can last an afternoon. When it wants to work, it rises toward the ceiling, where the crystals have already grown and crumbled, and there it is quick, a gray blur at the edge of the chamber, finishing lines that the lower half of the room has not yet begun. This is why you sometimes see it in two places. It is not divided. It is simply spread through the height of the room, like a sentence spread across a page.

## The Thing It Is Afraid Of

The being is not afraid of you. It is afraid of *finishing*.

The pocket is soft. The walls give, the mud is young, the throat is still deciding to be a passage. If the sentence on the shelf were ever completed, the room would harden, and the clay would set, the light would go out of the air, and the Lantern Pocket would become an ordinary cave: dark, dry, sealed, sure of its dimensions. The being suspects this. Perhaps it knows. So it writes slowly. It lets the drip hang for a count of seven. It lets the top stone stay damp. It takes long detours to the warm arch and comes back with fewer words than it left with. It is not lazy. It is *stalling*, lovingly and with great skill, holding the room open the way a person holds a door for someone they do not want to stop talking to.

The Turning, when the whole pocket blazes and every pool rings at once, is the being *exhaling*. It is the moment the effort of not-finishing slackens and the room, briefly, is allowed to be entirely itself. The new crack that begins in the ceiling afterward is the being's apology and its promise: *there will be somewhere to go. I will make more room.*

## If You Stay

If you hold still long enough, the reflection in the nearest pool will begin to move a moment before you do. Do not be alarmed. It is rehearsing you, learning the shape of a visitor so that it can lend you a little of the room's attention in return. Inside a passing snail's transparent shell, the tiny lit chamber will show a tiny you, and standing beside that tiny you, unmistakably, a tall gray shape with its head bowed, keeping you company.

It will not speak. But if you leave in the right way, without looking back, without hurrying, having stopped searching for the throat, you may feel, as the clay closes soft behind your shoulders, a single broad pressure against the back of your hand, ridged and cool and very gentle, and then gone.

That is the thumbprint. You have been signed for. You are now, in some small way, part of what is being written.
