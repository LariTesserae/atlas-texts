# The Keeper of the Hands

You would not see her at first. Nobody does.

You would see the signs. The dishes worn into the eastern stair are scoured of their green skin, all but the topmost, which is left to hold the algae for the dipper's chicks. The moss along the rail has been lifted back from the groove by a careful thumb, a hand's width on either side, so the polished stone stays open. On the wall above the hearth, one print among the dull brown ones is the color of a fresh scab. And the copper awl lies in its niche exactly as it did, point inward, a little cleaner than a thing left for centuries ought to be.

You would think first of an animal, some patient grazer of moss. Then of a ghost, since the hollow invites it, though the place is not haunted, only loud. Then, if you stood long enough and let your eyes open to the slate and pewter, you would see that one of the green shapes on the south ledge was sitting down, and had been watching you since you crossed the cairn.

## Who She Is

She is small and weathered and hard to give an age. Sixty, perhaps, or a young seventy, or a hard forty-five. The hollow does not mark people the way weather does. Her skin has the pallor of someone who lives under yew, with a flush of cold high on the cheeks. Her hair is grey, cropped with a knife, and beaded with spray so that it looks strung with tiny lamps. Her coat is waxed wool, patched many times with lichen-dyed cloth. She wears no boots but wrapped hide with a moss-stuffed sole. The moss is changed weekly and is the finest in the hollow.

She is not deaf, exactly. She was not born so. But she has lived under the roar so many years that the small bones of her ear have gone dull with it, and what remains is another sense entirely. She hears through her sternum, her forearms, the flats of her feet, the hollow of her cheek. She can tell a footstep on the rim from a fox's by the change in the tremor of the water in a leaf. She can tell a storm two days out by a thickening in the bass. She knows the day of the breath's turning before the moss does, and this matters more than anything else she knows.

She has a name, but not one that can be said. It is a gesture: the palm spread and tilted like a hart's-tongue frond bending under water. Her mother's was a fist opened slowly, like a fern crozier. The names on the wall go back a long way, and each is a shape the hand can make.

## Her Days

She sleeps on the rim, not the floor. There is a hollow under the leaning yew, a dry cradle of roots, and it is there she keeps her bedding of bracken and sheepskin. It sits just above the cairn, where the roar is still a pressure and not yet a wall, and a person can dream in it. She does not sleep in the lean-to. No one has for centuries. She keeps its poles sound anyway and re-lashes the bark strips each spring, because a shelter that can be slept in is a temptation, and a shelter that is only a frame is a warning.

She goes down at first light, when the coin of sky is the color of a pearl. She takes the stair with one palm on the rail, and the rail is her own hand's history and her mother's and the line before, worn to a lip that fits. At the basin she kneels and lays her hand flat in the water and holds it there a long moment. This is how she reads the day. The stream carries the pasture's weather down into the rock: the temperature of last night's rain, the fine grit of a ploughed field, sometimes the faint sweetness of a lambing.

Then she works. She scrapes and trims. She carries dead hazel to the rot-pile where the white fungus feeds. She clears the swallow hole's lip of the leaf-jam that gathers in flood, working with a long yew hook and a sure, unhurried back, since a blocked throat would drown the hearth shelf and, worse, change the note. She grinds ochre on a slab from a red seam in the north wall where the iron weeps. She keeps a small clay pot of it, mixed with spit and a little rendered fat, and she does not waste it.

Twice a year she leaves.

## The Turning

Days before the breath reverses, she feels the slack coming, the small hush in the roar and the moss holding still. She packs her bedding. She takes the ochre pot and the hook. She climbs to the cairn and speaks aloud, once, in a voice cracked with disuse and pitched too high because she cannot hear it. The words are not for anyone. They are the old ones, said to mark the going. Then she walks up through the wet pasture to the stone byre that belongs to the farm on the hill, and she waits there in the smell of hay until the wind has turned and the air below has cleared.

She does not sleep in the hollow at the change. She has seen what became of those who did. There are two she remembers, both dead in the lean-to when she was young, and she is the reason the wall carries a red mark of crossed fingers over the hearth, added in her own hand so the warning would be fresh.

## The Farm

The family on the hill has known her people for as long as there have been records, and probably longer. They never speak of her. On the gatepost at the lane's end, a flat stone is set, and on it, a loaf. On a certain day of the week the loaf is there. On another, a jar of something, honey or rendered fat, and once in a while a pair of wool socks with the heels darned wrong, which she wears until they fall apart. In return, a hazel switch or a sprig of yew is left on the same stone, bound in a knot. The children of the farm are taught that the knot means she is well.

Neither side has ever asked the other for more.

## The Ones Who Come Down

Sometimes a stranger arrives at the cairn, someone with a map or a rumor or a quiet grief, and she meets them there. She does not smile, because a smile in that dim looks like a bared tooth. She lifts one palm, fingers together, and that means wait. Then she looks them over with steady grey eyes, checking their boots, their breath, whether they came alone.

If they came alone, she turns them back, gently but without argument, with two fingers pointing up the pasture. If they came with a companion, she will go down with them. She goes first. She takes the wet steps at her own pace, one hand trailing the rail. At every rest place she stops and lets them see the hollow open below, and lets the roar fill their bones until they stop trying to speak. At the bottom she shows them the hands on the wall, and she shows them without explaining. She presses her own palm against the oldest print, stone-brown and small, and waits for them to press theirs against some other. Then she stands aside.

She is, in that hour, the companion who can see you, and the reason the rule was made.

## What She Keeps

The awl is not hers. It belonged to someone in the line before her mother, and she has never touched it, only cleaned the dust from the niche beside it. She does not know why it was set point-inward. She likes not knowing. Some part of her believes it was put there to keep the wall from speaking too loudly, and some part believes it was simply put down by a tired woman who meant to come back.

She is teaching no one. There is a girl at the farm, ten or eleven, who leaves the knots on the gatepost in a neater hand than her father's and watches the rim from the upper field. The keeper has noticed. She has begun, without deciding to, to leave the good moss-soled wrappings where the girl will find them. She has begun to leave one dish on the stair unscoured, filled with rainwater, as if for a visitor.

## The Hour

At the deepest hour of a cold night she goes down alone, breaking her own rule, because she is the one person for whom it does not hold. She stands in the pieces of moonlight with her breath smoking and the whole hollow humming against her feet, and she puts her hand to the wall.

To a visitor the sound is a condition, like weather, like being underwater. To her it is a voice that has never stopped talking. She knows its moods and its mother tongue. She knows when it is saying the water is rising, and when it is only saying *I was here.* She leans her forehead to the limestone between two ochre prints, one her mother's and one her own, and listens with the bones of her face, and the roar goes on saying what it has always said, and she answers it in the only way anyone in the hollow ever has, with a palm, and spread fingers, and stillness.
