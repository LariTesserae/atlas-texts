# Those Who Keep the Count

The animals are not the only ones who live in the Warm Cistern. About sixty people live there too, and they do not think of themselves as its keepers. They say they are *in the count*, the way a fish is in a school. The pumps set the count, the tide sets the count, and the water-hammer marks the hours. Whatever else a person is, they are one more thing that keeps time in that room.

## The People

They are lean, long-limbed, and unhurried. Their skin has taken a faint green cast, from the algae that live in it and from the long light through the windows. Children are born pale and go green in their first year, and the green deepens along the spine and the backs of the hands. Nobody knows whether this was bred into them or just happened. Their hair is shaved or cropped close, because kelp tangles, and a tangled swimmer can drown in warm water. Their eyes are large, with a second lid that slides across in the iron-tasting water. Their ears are small and set flat, and beneath the skin of the jaw they are very sensitive. They hear the pumps through the bone.

They are not amphibious the way a seal is. They hold their breath a long time, and they have learned to pace themselves by the pump-stroke: in on the upstroke, hold through the downstroke, out slowly with the bubbles. A woman can work at the bottom of the dome for the length of forty pulses without appearing to strain. Visitors, if there were ever visitors, would see them move through the water as if they were being carried by it, and would notice how they tighten and loosen with the pulse-fish.

They live on the upper terraces, in the shelves and gantry-rooms that the tide exposes. Their dwellings are hollows in the slumped walls, lined with dried kelp-sheet and hung with woven curtains against the drip. They have no fire, and the vault is always warm. What they have is a great many small lamps, jars of glowing fronds kept alive with a little nutrient. The jars hang in rows along the terraces, and at night the windows go black and the whole upper vault glows a soft pale green.

## The Two Speeches

They have two languages and cannot use both at once.

**Tide-speech** is spoken aloud, in air, at the low water, when the upper terraces stand dry and the roar of the sluices has faded. It is a low, slow, humming language, mostly vowels, made for a room that is mostly echo. It is used for stories, quarrels, naming, and instruction. It is poor at precise things, and people talk around a subject for a long time before they arrive at it.

**Pipe-speech** is used under the water. It is done by rapping on the iron: knuckles, a bone mallet, the flat of a shell, against the cable runs, the pylons, the ribs of the walls. The water carries it across the whole vault. It has few words and a great deal of rhythm. *Food here. Grazers on the east curtain. Child lost. Ray low.* A good rapper can make the sound of a pulse-fish school turning, and the fish will turn.

Everyone knows the third speech too, the water-hammer, but it is not theirs and they do not answer it. When the knocks climb the walls every voice in the room stops, human and animal alike, and they listen the way you would listen to someone older than you speaking in the next room. Then the hammer finishes and life goes on.

## What They Do

They do not farm. They say the garden is not theirs to plant. They gather from it, kelp and fan-leaf and the tender pale shoots of the low forest, and they cut only what the current has already loosened. They eat the pulse-fish sparingly and never during the hour after the hammer, when the schools are nervous. They will not touch a lantern-ray. Children who have been told a ray is *someone's food* grow up understanding the idea very literally.

They scrape. This is their one deliberate labor. On the mineral curtains, where the scale-grazers have missed a stretch, they work with flat bone blades to keep the steel breathing. Most of them cannot say why. The old ones say, *the walls want to be seen*. Others say that where the grazers strip a wall back and the rust begins again, there is a moment between the two when the metal shines, and a good day is one when you catch it. They apprentice to the scraping the way others might apprentice to prayer, and they are quietly proud of a wall left clean.

They grease. There are valves within reach of the terraces, wheels the size of a person's chest, and no one knows what any of them do. A family is assigned a wheel and keeps it free with kelp-oil and patience, turning it a little each tide. Sometimes a great boom sounds behind the walls, and the family says, without looking up, *that was ours*. It probably wasn't. They are content to be wrong.

## The Wheel and the Grate

There is a rule about the great valve wheel in the corner of the dome, the one that turns a quarter and back by itself. **No one touches it and no one swims near it at the turning.** This rule is older than tide-speech. A child once put her hands on it in the moment it moved, and it is said that she went with it, a quarter turn, and did not come back the same. She lived. She is old now, and is called the Quarter. She is very gentle and answers questions a beat late, as if the words had to come around a corner. People do not speak of it. They bring her the best fan-leaf.

The grate at the bottom is another matter. There is a small order of them, six or seven, who call themselves **listeners** and spend their days on the deep floor, lying along the warm stone with an ear against the iron. They go down on the downstroke and come up on the upstroke, hour after hour, tirelessly. What they hear is the hum, the faint tune carried in the sweet air that rises in slow bubbles. Each listener hears it differently. One hears a lullaby. One hears the same three notes forever. One will not say. They have never agreed on what it is, or whether it is one thing. Some say it is the voice of the builders, still kindly, still down there. Some say it is the voice of whoever comes after. The oldest listener says only that it sounds like someone who has been waiting a long time and has learned to enjoy it.

They have never dug. Everyone knows how it could be done, and no one has done it. There is a saying: *you do not open a door on a song.*

## The Under

Beneath the three pumps, in the scale-crusted dark, something moves that the people call **the Under**. They do not call it a monster. They speak of it with the flat, accepting tone people use for weather or for a very old relative.

Those who have seen it, and it is only a few in each generation, describe a length like a hull, slate-colored and barnacled with scale, sliding under the engines in no hurry. A dark eye, wide as a porthole, opens and closes with the slowness of a shutter. Where it passes, the low fronds flare, blue-white, in a long wave. A lantern-ray drifts out of the gardens over it and does not return.

The people have noticed something else, which they mention less often. If you are very still in the deepest water, beyond the three thudding pulses of the pumps, you can feel a fourth. It is slower and lower, arriving once in every eleven or twelve strokes, and it is not in the count. Some think it is a fault in the machinery. Some think it is the Under's heart. A few think the three engines were built to keep time for it, that the whole warm room is one long slow lullaby for a sleeper, and that the people, the fish, the kelp, and the wheel are all just the sounds it makes while it dreams.

They do not say this in tide-speech, only in pipe-speech, in the low soft knocking they use for the dead. And then only once, at the turn of the tide, when the sluices are open and the roar covers everything.

## A Cloud Crosses the Sun

When the vault goes gold-green and dim, they all lift their faces at once. Nobody has told them to. The scrapers stop mid-stroke, the listeners rise from the grate, and the children on the terraces go still, with the fish scattering out of the light and the kelp leaning one way. For as long as the shadow lasts they hold their breath together, the whole village, sixty green faces turned toward the clouded windows, and beneath them the pumps go on with their long slow beat.

Then the sun returns, and the coins of light slide back across the floor. They exhale. Someone laughs, and someone raps a quick joke on the nearest pipe, and the count goes on.
