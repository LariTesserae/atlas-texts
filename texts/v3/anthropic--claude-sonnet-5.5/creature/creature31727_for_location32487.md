# Who Lives on the Sweating Plateau

Everything that lives here is organized around a single event: the hour when the stone begins to sweat. The plateau holds many kinds of creatures, but they share one clock, and it is set by the cooling of rock at dusk. The grazers, the birds, the foxes, and the one human tender all shape their days around the same wet walls.

## The Pale Grazers

The most visible residents are the long-necked, thick-woolled animals that the tender calls *lickers*. They stand about as high as a person's shoulder at the withers, with necks a third again as long as a horse's, and their fleece is the color of old bone, matted at the belly with ochre from the ground. Their hooves splay wide and are padded like a camel's, and they walk the brittle sinter with an unhurried, testing gait, setting each foot down as if the terrace might change its mind.

They live by the tongue. At dusk the herds file toward the weeping walls in a fixed order. Mothers with calves go first and take the low, freshly beaded faces, and the yearlings crowd behind them. The old males come last, and they are the ones who reach highest. Their necks let them work the upper courses of the wall, where the water beads longest in the cooling air. They lick with slow, broad strokes, and the sound carries a long way in the thin air, like a great many people turning the pages of stiff paper. The stone has minerals in it, so the water tastes of salt, chalk, and iron, and the animals need all three. Their tongues go gray with it, and their teeth wear down flat and smooth. An old licker's death is often a matter of teeth: when the grinding surface is gone, it can no longer strip the lichen from the rocks between walls, and it drifts to the edge of the herd and stays there.

They sleep out on the open tuff, folded down in rows on the warm ground, with their long necks curled back along their flanks. The plateau's heat is their blanket. In the pre-dawn, when the fog lifts from every pool, a sleeping herd looks like a field of pale stones with steam coming off them.

## The Waders

In the warm shallows stand the pink-legged birds, packed so tightly that a person watching from the rim sees a single pink-and-white surface with a thousand small heads bobbing in it. They sweep their bills sideways through the water, each stroke a small arc, sifting out brine shrimp and the red mites. The pink of their legs comes from what they eat. The tender says that in lean years the legs fade toward the color of a peeled twig, and that you can read the season's abundance from the flock's ankles.

They are noisy in a thin, bright way, with a constant conversational murmur that erupts into alarm-calls when a fox shows itself. The whole flock lifts at once, wheels twice, and settles back into the water, a little rearranged, as if nothing had happened. At night they stay standing in the warm pools, because the water keeps their feet from the cold that comes off the snow-line air, and they sleep on one leg with their heads turned back under a wing.

## The Sinter Foxes

The hunters are lean, ash-colored, long-eared animals with oddly narrow paws, blackened at the pads from a life on hot crust. Every fox has learned the plateau's floor the hard way. Some crust holds a body's weight, and some is a skin over scalding mud, and the older foxes carry the scars of that lesson as pale patches where the fur never came back. A mature fox has a map in its feet that no one else can read.

They hunt by patience. A fox will lie in the shadow of a sinter rim for an hour, its chin flat on the stone, while the herd shifts and the birds resettle, and it seems to watch nothing at all. What it waits for is the calf that drifts a stride too far from its mother, or the wader with a bad wing, or the incautious lizard. They also steal. They will loiter at the rookery edge and take eggs, and they will run off the small scavenging birds from a kill. They have no packs, only loose acquaintanceships around the good seeps, and in the dusk their soft, dry barks pass across the tuff from one rim to the next.

## The Small Ones

The lizards are the plateau's thermometers. They are flat-bodied and mottled to match the sinter, and at dawn they press themselves against the vent-lips to warm through. As the sun climbs and the stone heats past what is comfortable, they shuffle around the rim, always keeping to the band where the temperature is right. A colony on a single vent looks like a slow clock hand, each lizard a minute mark. At noon they seek the shade of the pipe supports and go still.

The beetles are thumbnail-sized, black with a green sheen, and they plow through the ash with their heads down, leaving trails like a loose handwriting across the flats. They eat what falls: dead shrimp, dropped feathers, the wool that the herds shed in tufts. After a morning's traffic, a slope of ash is covered in their script.

The burrowing rodents are round, brown, and short-tailed. They live in the warm slopes where the ground is heated from below, and the ground heat is their winter. They do not hibernate, since they never need to. They dig into the warm ground when the snow-line air is at its coldest, and in the evening they emerge at the same time as everything else and cross the open ground to the walls in short, tense bursts, always watching the sky, where the scavengers hang.

## The Scavengers

The broad-winged birds are bare-headed and dark, with breast feathers stained yellow by the sulfur they fly through. They rarely flap. They find a thermal over a vent and rise on it in slow spirals, then slide off across the plateau in a long glide, and rise again from the next vent. From below, they look like a kind of slow weather. They nest on the gantries of the geothermal station's stack, where the updraft is steady and the metal holds warmth through the night. Nothing can reach the nests there, and the birds have come to regard the station as theirs.

They clean what the plateau leaves: the dead lickers, the fox-killed calves, the flamingo-like waders that drown in the hotter pools. They wait their turn behind the foxes, and the two species have a wary courtesy, a matter of distances kept.

## The Quiet Majority

Most of the life on the plateau cannot be seen from a standing height. It lives in the pools as felted mats, in bands of orange, green, and brick-red laid down like layers of cloth. The colors sort themselves by temperature, so that a hot spring is a color chart, each shade marking the degree at which some particular kind of life is comfortable. Even in the blue-black water near the boiling point, where nothing seems possible, a thin film of something clings to the rim. These mats are older than the herds, the foxes, and the birds, and everything else ultimately eats what they make. The shrimp graze them, the mites swarm over the shrimp, the waders sift the mites, and the foxes take the waders. The whole plateau stands on this microbial cloth.

## The Tender

There is one person on the plateau, or at least one who stays. She is no longer young, and she has been at the geothermal station longer than she likes to count. The company that built the station has forgotten most of its obligations, but the turbine still turns, and someone is needed to grease the valves, read the gauges, and keep the manifolds from freezing on the coldest nights. She is that someone. Her quarters are in the lee of the turbine hall, where the wall is warm and the hum comes through the floor. She has lived in it so long that she notices it only when it changes.

She breathes slowly, from long habit, and she moves at the pace of the herds. At dusk she walks the pipelines with a wrench and an oil can, and the animals let her pass. To the lickers she is one more feature of the landscape, a tall, slow thing that smells of grease and steam. She knows individual foxes by their limps. She has names for some of the old male lickers, and she knows which weeping walls are best in which months, and which ones have stopped giving.

She keeps a secret. On the eastern line there is a flange that has been leaking for years, a thin, steady drip of condensate that pools in a hollow under the pipe. The herds found it long ago and have come to depend on it. She could fix it in an afternoon. She has not, and she does not intend to. She writes "minor seepage, monitored" in the log every month, and that is true.

## The Ruins and Their Tenants

The abandoned works belong to the animals now. Foxes den in the iron ore-carts and sleep with their noses on the rusted rims. Small birds nest in the tramway buckets, which sway a little when the wind rises, and the young grow up rocked. The lizards have taken the winch house, and the sulfur, blooming yellow over every surface, has made it all one color. No one lives there in the human sense, and yet the ruins are among the most densely inhabited places on the plateau.

## Night

When the dark comes fully, the tuff is still warm underfoot. The walls are wet from top to bottom and glitter under a sky crowded with stars. The lickers are folded down in rows, the waders stand asleep in their pools, and the foxes make their slow rounds. The tender sits on the step of the turbine hall with her back against the warm wall, and the hum goes through her. Every living thing on the plateau is breathing out pale steam into the still air, and from a distance you could not say which plumes come from the ground and which from the animals. It is all one exhalation, and it goes up together into a sky with no other clouds.
