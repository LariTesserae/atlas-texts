# Who Keeps Harrow Wick

No one lives in Harrow Wick the way people live in a town. The word *live* has to be spread thin here and shared among several kinds of tenant, and none of them would know it as a word. But the town is not untended. Something has been arranging it for a long time, and if you walk the vaults slowly enough, you will start to see the arrangers.

## The Setters

The first thing you find is the bath. It stands alone in a hundred paces of moss-floored dark, on its claw feet, tilted a few degrees toward the nearest pillar of creeper, and it is exactly right. It could not be an inch to the left. A fern grows in the child's boot, three streets on, and the boot faces the way a child's boot faces when the child has just stepped out of it.

The Setters do this. They are tall and unhurried, the height of a doorframe and hardly wider than one, and they look less built than *bundled*: pale stems bound in the green cloth from the weaving shed, worn loose like a shroud or a cassock. Where a face would be, the cloth has folded in on itself, dense and dim. Some in the town say they have no faces. Others say they have the face of whatever they last set down.

They carry one object at a time. A Setter lifts a tea urn from the wreck of a scullery and begins to cross the vaulted street with it, and the crossing takes a season. The moss closes behind each step. Snails colonise the urn as it goes. When the Setter arrives, it does not simply put the urn down. It turns it, and leans away, and looks, or does whatever its folded cloth does in place of looking. Then it turns the urn again, a finger's width. This can take days. When the urn is finally placed, the whole dark around it seems to relax, as if a held note had resolved.

They are not decorating. They are working out what the town is *for*, one object at a time, by finding where each thing looks as though it has always been. The empty rooms behind cottage doors are their finished sentences: a chair in the middle of a cathedral of dark. They do not know what the sentence says. They are still writing it.

Sometimes a Setter sets something and the town, glancing away and back, rebuilds itself around the new placement, and the whole terrace slides into a slightly different terrace. The Setters seem to feel this as approval. They stand a long time afterward, cloth stirring, in a stillness that is very like gladness.

## The Shift

At the sound of the whistle, the cloth-wearers walk.

They are not the Setters. They are lower, slighter, made of the rolls that pile up unclaimed on the loom beams. Moss has grown through the heddled cloth in the shape of a coat, or a shawl, or a flat cap, and then the moss went on growing into the shape of the shoulders that would have worn it. There is nobody inside. The cloth is simply *still holding the posture of wearing*. Nine or ten to a street, they leave the shed at the whistle, in two places at once, the east group and the west group, each certain they are the true shift and the other a late echo.

They walk the old routes: to the gate, to the tram stop, to the pub with no roof and one wall. They do not arrive anywhere, because arrival requires a place that stays put. They walk until the street turns them, and the street turns them constantly, so they end each shift somewhere unlike where they began, having done everything correctly. At the chapel they pause and bow their heads, and the pause is not reverence. It is just where the pause always was.

Now and then one of them stops at a door and knocks. A wet, leathery rap, done with the knuckle of a creeper. Behind the door there might be a cathedral of dark, or a wall. The wearer knocks a second time, and waits the length of a patient breath, and goes on. Whatever answer the door gave, it was not the one they were built to expect, and they have no way to be disappointed.

## The Fewer

Then there are the ones you are not meant to see.

Count the eels and there is one fewer each time. Count the windows and the total shifts. The Fewer live in that difference. They are not creatures so much as *occupancies*. Where the count comes up short, something is standing in the gap and being, for that moment, the number that was missing. It lives in the space between what you thought was there and what you can prove. It is fed, thinly, by counting, and it is kind in its way. It takes only one. It always gives the dozens back when you turn.

You will feel it as a very slight pressure at the edge of attention, like being the last person in a room who hasn't been introduced. It does not want anything from you. It wants only to be *included in the sum*, and every miscount is a small hospitality. Some who have walked the canal tunnel say that if you stop counting and simply say "and you," the gap grows quietly warmer. This is not a trick that has ever done harm. It is also not one that anyone has tried twice, for reasons they could not give.

## The Finishers

Under the moss, in the cut banks where the mudstone is still wet with its own making, the ground has tenants of its own.

They are pale, ribbed, and long, and the ribs match the shrink-cracks in the stone so closely that you can look at a bank for a quarter hour and see only rock, and then a section of the rock will *draw a breath*. They are grubs the length of an arm, or they are the length of a street, or the ones in the shallow gullies are the tips of something the size of the basin. It is hard to tell. They work the mudstone the way a potter works a wall, with pressure and patience and the flat of a body. Each rain, the rills deepen by a thread, and the Finishers have been there before it, smoothing, rehearsing, leaving the groove a little truer than the water alone would.

They are the reason the gullies find the same routes again. They are not making canyons. They are making *the idea of canyons*, so that when the ground is ready there will be no hesitation.

The basin itself is shallow, and rounded, and just barely damp at the bottom, like the hollow of a sleeper's palm. No one who has walked the Understory has been able to decide whether the Finishers are inside the ground or whether the ground is a single Finisher, curled, and Harrow Wick is what has grown on it while it waited to wake.

## The Small Citizens

Everything else is a citizen in the plain sense, and each has its habits, which the Setters have learned to work around.

The snails keep the streets. Their silver roads are the only lasting map, and they are unreliable in a consistent way: they always lead to somewhere that was, at the moment of laying, important. The moths know where the air is occupied, and they settle on it without hesitation, so a cloud of them at rest is a kind of floorplan of invisible furniture. The bats drop in rows and flow as one shape, and no one has ever seen them decide. The pale weasel-thing hunts the vole roads with the absolute patience of a thing that has learned the town will bring the prey to it, if it waits. The lizards sit on the brickwork, translucent, so that you can see the slow heart, and the brick behind it, and the heart is the same speed as the flywheel. Something soft and many-legged emerges from the boilers when the furnace-light flickers, and no Setter has ever set it anywhere. It goes where it goes. The Setters seem to regard it with a deep, wary respect, as one regards the single thing in a house that cannot be tidied.

## The Beam Engine

Last, and least like a tenant: the engine. It has a root for a heart, and the root swells and shrinks, and the flywheel turns once per long breath. It is not alive, and it is not not. Ask the Setters, if you could, and they would go still and let the cloth hang, and then they would move a teacup a finger's width, and that would be the answer.

The engine is the town's pulse. Everything else keeps time to it without knowing that it does: the drip, the click of the pawl, the moths, the walk of the wearers. When the whistle sounds late in two places, it is the engine catching up to itself.

## What They Have in Common

None of them is finished. That is what they share. The Setters are unsure of the sentence. The wearers have not found the door that answers. The Fewer have not been fully counted in. The Finishers are practising for a landscape that does not yet exist. Even the small citizens, which seem so settled, are still deciding what the machines are for, and the machines are still deciding what the plants are for.

If you stay long enough, you may notice a Setter has stopped near you, and is looking, or doing whatever the folded cloth does. It has tilted its head at a slight angle, the way it tilts toward the bath. It is not menacing. It is considering where you look most as though you have always been.

Do not be alarmed. It is a great compliment, and it can take a season.
