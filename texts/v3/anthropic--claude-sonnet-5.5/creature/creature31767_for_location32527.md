# Those Who Keep the Shift

Nobody in the basin is idle, and nobody is hurried. That is the first thing you notice about the residents. Every creature seems to be in the middle of a task it has done many times, at exactly the pace the task requires. They look like a crew, and the longer you watch, the more sure you become that they are one.

## The Hands

The grey salamanders do the work that looks like work.

They are the length of a forearm, with damp, slate-colored skin and a paler band across the shoulders, and each band is a slightly different width. When they believe themselves alone they rise onto their hind legs and walk with a sober, flat-footed gait, arms held a little out from the body like people carrying something heavy through a doorway. If you look straight at one, it does not startle. It lowers itself to all fours partway through a stride, and the step finishes with a small hitch, like a machine catching a cog.

Their tasks are precise and produce nothing you can name. One wipes the rust-colored pores of a bellows-bog with the flat of its palm after each exhalation, and the pores are already clean. Another carries a pebble from one worn socket in the streambed to the next socket downstream. Later another carries a pebble back, and it may be the same pebble. A third simply sits with one hand flat on a turning cobble, feeling it, as a doctor holds a wrist.

When a salamander tires, another comes and touches its brow to the tired one's brow. They hold that for a full nine-second breath. Then one climbs into the moss to sleep and the other takes up the task at the exact point where it was left. Nothing is dropped and nothing is said. If you count the salamanders from the floor you get forty. If you count them from the rim you get forty. The faces are not the same forty.

Their skin has a faint sheen, and behind each one's left ear is a small smear of amber. They have all been to the bowl.

## The Foremen

The herons are not in charge, though that is the word the basin gives you for them.

They stand in the shallows at regular intervals, tall and colorless, with long yellow eyes whose pupils lie sideways like slots in a coin box. They do not watch the workers. They listen. Each bog's breath passes through the ground and up their legs, and they mark it by lifting one foot and setting it down. If you stand among them you feel it in your own knees, a very slight pull toward doing the same.

Very rarely, at the moment the whistle blows, a heron turns its head slowly toward the eastern lip, the one you came over. It holds the look for the length of one breath and then turns back. The herons are not waiting for a person. They are waiting for a relief, the next shift, and they have been waiting so long that it has become a kind of posture, like the lifted foot.

When you first crest the rim, they all notice you. The salamanders drop to four legs. The nearest heron lifts its foot and, for a moment, does not set it down. It stands on one leg through the whole drop of the bog, considering you. Then it sets the foot down, and the basin resumes. You were not the relief. No one is unkind about it.

## The Clerks

The small deer keep to the tree ferns, one crowd to each fern, each crowd a little apart from the others. They are the size of terriers, fawn going to charcoal at the legs, with lashes so long they seem drawn on. They graze the crosiers, which are always halfway open, and the frond they eat is halfway open again by the time the deer has swallowed.

They are grave, methodical animals. Each stands at its own fern with its nose lifted to the same spot, chewing a certain number of times, moving a certain number of steps, and returning. If one deer leaves the crowd, the ring stays exactly as full, and you cannot say who filled it. They have no young. Nothing in the basin does. There are no fawns, no elders, and no bones in the moss. Everyone is at the age of doing the work.

They are the ones who go to the amber bowl. They come in ones and twos, dip their muzzles, and go back smelling of mint and machine oil. Nothing in them changes when they drink. It does not fortify them, and it does not seem to be meant to. It is a courtesy the basin extends to the crew, like a cup of tea passed down a line.

## The Looms and the Gauges

The copper beetles are the weavers, thousands of them in the birch bark, and none of them knows more than its own line of the pattern. Each drums one rhythm, unchanging, and listens for the beats that come before and after it. The shuttle-sound you hear is not made by any beetle. It exists only between them.

Carry one out over the rim in your cupped hands and it will keep drumming, and the sound is a sentence with the middle missing. It taps into the gap where its neighbors should be and waits, and after a while it stops and sits very still. Put it back in the bark and it resumes at once, seamlessly, without any hesitation at all.

The wrens sit along the edges of the bogs, and their ticking song rises as the moss swells and falls as it sinks. It is a working gauge, the only readout the place has. A second before the whistle blows, every wren falls silent together, and when it is over they resume mid-note. No one has ever seen who blows the whistle. Some say it is a wren, some say a frog, and some say the last of the amber drip catching in the stone bowl. None of them has looked closely enough to know. Their nests hold eggs that are warm and speckled and never hatch. Like the fronds, they are at exactly the right stage.

## The Slow Residents

Some inhabitants take a long time to notice.

The bellows-bogs themselves are the largest of the residents, wide sheets of moss that breathe and are, in whatever way moss is, awake. Their rust-colored pores are attentive as eyes. Salamanders lean against them as they would against a sleeping ox.

The cobbles in the streambed are older still and slower, and they turn in their sockets with the deliberate patience of things that have always been in the middle of a rotation. Some turn with the current and some against it, and they never touch. That is a courtesy too, or a rule. Each stone is polishing its cradle and being polished by it, and each is exactly as worn as the other.

## The One at the Seam

Along the line of ferns where shadows split, people report a figure standing with its back to them. It is tall, in something like oilskin, with its shadow reaching both ways. When you walk toward it you find a heron, or a fern, or a leaning column of mist. The figure is never there when you arrive, and it is always there when you look back.

The residents pay it no mind. They do not avoid the seam or gather at it. But the salamanders will walk across it upright even if you are watching, and the herons that stand nearest never lift their feet late. Whether the figure is the foreman of the foremen, or the relief who is always about to arrive, or just the shape a shift takes when it has gone on long enough, no one who has stood there has been able to say.

## What They Are, Together

All of them are workers. They are also the work. The beetles are the loom, the wrens are the gauge, the herons are the clock, and the salamanders are the hands that tend it. The deer feed on what the ferns give, and the ferns give what the deer take. A crew and its machine are the same thing here, and there is no owner. The shift has been in progress for as long as the granite has been fresh, and each of them keeps their place in it without complaint and without hope, and without any sense that something is missing.

They do glance at the rim, though. The heron's long look, the deer's lifted head, the salamander who pauses mid-stride with a pebble in its hand: at the whistle, all of them turn their faces a few degrees toward the sharp eastern lip and hold there a moment. Then the bog breathes, the foot comes down, the pebble moves on to its socket, and the roar closes over the place where the pause was.
