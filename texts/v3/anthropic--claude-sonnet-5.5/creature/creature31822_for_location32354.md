# Who Lives on the Plateau

Start with the frogs, because they are the only ones who look at you.

They sit in the leaf-cups of the terraces, one to a cup, each no bigger than the last joint of your thumb. They are the pale green of the inside of a stem, and the light passes through them so completely that you can see the dark seed of the heart and the thin wet ribbon of the gut. The throat is a clear bladder. It swells and collapses, swells and collapses, and no sound comes out.

Watch one for a minute and you'll notice the swelling is not the quick flutter of a frog at rest. It is slow, and it matches the beat between two towers, the long swaying pulse where two near-identical notes rub against each other. The frogs are not singing. They are *counting*. Every terrace has its own, and each is tuned to whichever pair of towers is loudest at that spot. If you walk the causeway between two rings, you can see the throats change their tempo as you pass from one interference to the next, like a field of small lungs handing a breath along.

If the chord ever moved, they would be the first to show it. It doesn't, so they only sit, and swell, and wait. The frogs do not seem to be waiting for anything to happen.

## The ivory ones

The beetles are longer than your finger and narrow as a grain of rice is narrow, with a body like a segment of old piano key. They live in the hollows of the causeways, and you know them by the scrabbling under your feet, a dry, unhurried sound that has a tempo of its own, always a little behind your steps.

They are the reason the plateau doesn't overgrow. When a stem browns at its tip, one of them is already there. Where they come from at that moment, you cannot tell. There is no hunting and no searching. The beetle takes the dead tip in its jaws, works it down to the pale living wood, and backs away, and the new shoot rises into the space it has cleared, filling the shape of the old stem with a fit so exact that the seam closes over it. The beetle eats the dead part and leaves the rest, and the rate at which it eats is the rate at which the tower grows.

Their number is fixed, as everything is. When one dies, and one must, since even ivory wears, you never find the body. A larval bead is already swelling in a chamber of the cane, and a new one is already grazing. No one is born early, and none late.

They do not fight or feed on each other. Nothing feeds on them. They are simply what the plateau's forgetting looks like: the small teeth of the place, keeping its edges clean.

## The tall one

The heron-like thing stands in the shallows between two towers, and here you have to be careful about the word *stand*.

It is taller than you are. It has a long neck folded into an S, and a bill like a closed blade, and its body is the gray-green of wet slate with a paler wash along the breast. It looks like a heron the way a shadow looks like a person. Nothing about it is wrong exactly, only too settled. It has no restless feather, no shifting weight from one leg to the other, no flick of the eye. Water lies against its legs without a ripple, and the meniscus where the surface meets the skin is unbroken, a tiny mirrored collar, as though the water had accepted it a long time ago and stopped noticing.

You can watch for an hour and not see it move. You can return after a day, if a day means anything here, and find it in the same place, facing the same way. Which is toward the low ground, the distant place the gutters all run downhill to, where the haze thickens and the eye gives up.

I think it is a listener. Not to the chord, which has no news in it, but to the place the chord drains into. It is the one creature on the plateau not given a task, unless attending is the task. Or unless its stillness is itself the measure: everything else on the plateau is held steady by small motions, the frogs' throats, the beetles' jaws, the seepage in the walls. The heron holds steady by not moving at all. It is the still point that the others correct against.

Do not walk up to it. This is not because it would harm you. There is no reason to think it can. But the half-hour to the next tower is long, the sound closes over you as you go, and the thing will be exactly as still when you arrive as it was when you set out, and you will have added nothing to it.

## What is not seen

No one has ever seen what waits at the low place, so this part is inference. Everything drains there. The gutters bend toward it, the terraces spill toward it, and the heron faces it like a plant faces light. The chord, if you follow it with your body, thickens in that direction, as if the sound were being gathered.

There may be nothing there but a still pool. But the plateau has never wasted a notch or a channel, and a design this complete does not run all its water to a place with no purpose. Whatever is at the low ground is where the plateau ends and is either received or released. It may be the largest inhabitant of all, or it may be only the last drop of a long sentence. I have not gone to see, and the haze does not encourage it.

## The one in the pillar

Inside any tower, in the round green room, the pillar hums, and the water around it holds a pattern of concentric rings that never drift. There is no animal in that water. But if you stand at the rail long enough, you begin to have the sense of being looked at by something that has no eyes and needs none, the way a bell is aware of a hammer.

This is the true resident, if there is one: the pattern itself. The frogs are its gauges, the beetles its teeth, the heron its reference, the towers its throats, the causeways its nerves. None of them lives *on* the plateau so much as *as* it. Take one away and the others would not die. They would only stop being parts. Nothing here is a tenant. It is one thing, in many sizes.

## What it does with you

You arrive with a rhythm of your own, your steps drumming on the causeway a little out of time. For the first quarter hour, the place lets you keep it. Then, without your choosing, you find you have settled onto the beat, one footfall to each slow swell of the frogs' throats. The beetles beneath fall in behind. Your breathing lengthens. You are being *included*, gently, the way a new note is included in a chord that already has room for it.

And when you leave, the beat you brought is released. The frogs' throats swell and fall as they did. The beetles graze. The heron does not turn its head. The rings around the pillar do not so much as tremble in memory of you. Nothing was disturbed, so nothing needs mending.

That may be the most unsettling thing about the inhabitants. They receive you completely, and they keep nothing of you.
