# The One Who Keeps the Count

Most of what lives in the Bowl of Lit Salt lives on the walls: the pearl-snails, the stone-colored lizards, the swifts pouring out at dawn, the mirror-backed beetles walking their straight lines. These are the tenants. There is also someone who keeps house, and it is the hardest resident to find.

## What You See First

You see nothing. You see the pan, the hummock, the fallen slab, the silver tree. If you stay long enough, and the light has stopped hurting your eyes, one of the features will turn out to have been standing up.

The keeper is tall and very thin, and pale in the way the walls are pale. Its skin is dusted with salt, and its hair, if it is hair, is a matted, silvery fall that might be fiber, feather-down, or the dried threads of the wall-cushions. Its clothing is woven from those same threads and strung with empty snail shells, so that it makes the smallest possible sound when it moves, like a few grains of rice in a paper cup. In a place without shadows it has none to give it away. It is drawn in the same pale tones as everything around it. You could look straight at it for a minute and take it for a flaw in your vision.

Whether it is a person is a question the bowl does not answer. It has two arms, two hands, and a long, patient face, and it walks upright. But its eyes are the color of the wet band, dark and shining and slow to focus, and it looks at you the way the lizards do, as though you were weather.

## The Circuit

It follows the weeping band. As the damp arc drifts around the circumference through the year, the keeper drifts with it, so it makes one full circle of the bowl in twelve months, sleeping in whatever hollow the season has brought around. It is never in the same place twice in a year, but it is always in the same place on the same day. Anyone who came back on a given morning across ten years would find it kneeling at the same fluting in the wall, pressing a mat of woven cushion-fiber to the dark stone, then wringing the water into a cup. That is how it drinks.

It is a calendar in the shape of a body. The snails follow the damp, and the keeper follows the snails. Nothing in the bowl seems to mind.

## Its Hours

At dawn it stands beneath the shining band and does not move for forty minutes. This is when the algae flash emerald, and the keeper turns its face up into the green, the one color in the place. Nothing else in the bowl is ever so vivid, and it seems to be the only thing that looks at it. When the green fades to soot, the keeper lowers its head and goes to work.

The work is small. It empties the three stone cups beneath the seep and sets them back, turned a little, so the rims wear evenly. It sweeps the grinding hollow with a wing of some bird. It lifts the hand-sized stone, sets it down, and lifts it again, as one might turn a sleeping animal so it doesn't stiffen. It plucks a single snail from the wall now and then, eats it in a quiet, ceremonial way, and sets the empty shell in the salt at the base of the cliff, mouth up. Over the years these shells have drifted into loose crescents, which no one has explained, and which the keeper adjusts.

It also tends the niches. The scraper of chipped flint, the bone awl, the tools tucked back as if to be found again: these are its. It leaves them for whoever comes next. Now and then, at the turn of the year, it goes around and collects the ones nobody took, cleans them, and sets them somewhere different. This may be the whole of its hospitality: things left ready, offered without a word, for travelers who do not stay.

## Noon

At noon it disappears.

That is the only word for it. At the height of the day the light comes from every wall at once, and the keeper lies down on the salt plates at the center of the floor, on its back, with its arms open, pale on pale, and no shadow to tell you it is there. The plates go *tick* under its weight and then stop. You can walk right past. You might step on a hand.

Then the pulse begins, the slow turning of the snails wave by wave around the circle, the whole wall glinting and the light flung inward from ten thousand small mouths. For that moment, and only that moment, the keeper is visible: a single figure outlined in white fire on the cream pan, eyes open, lips moving. The birds drop low and go quiet, as though listening. The lizards press flat into the stone. The tree rings louder, though nothing moves the air.

Whether the pulse is meant for the keeper, or the keeper is meant for the pulse, or the two have simply grown up leaning on each other like old trees, no one can say. The keeper does not explain. When the last snail has turned back, it closes its eyes, and the light lets go of it, and it is gone again into the floor.

## The Breath

In late afternoon the wind crosses the rim and the whole bowl sounds its one low note, like a breath across the mouth of a jar. Afterward, if the air is still enough and you have been patient, you may hear a second sound so faint you will doubt it: the same note, an octave higher and half as long, coming from somewhere near the pool. It might be the tree, or the wind's echo, or the keeper answering. It is never repeated, and it always leaves the silence larger than it was.

## Rain

When the clouds bank up on the horizon and the year's one rain arrives, the keeper walks out into the middle of the pan and stands in the inch-deep mirror-water while the walls stream and the snails lift their shells like lanterns. On this day, and only this day, it has a reflection: a tall pale figure standing on a pale sky, upside down and complete. It looks down at itself for a long time. It is not clear that it recognizes what it sees. Then the sun comes back, the water returns to the air, and the keeper resumes its circuit, a little slower, a little quieter, and with a small new stone in one hand to mark how far the pool has moved east.

## What It Is

There may be one keeper or many. The grinding hollow is very old and the stone in it very worn, worn by more hands than one lifetime could account for. But the hands that made the hollow are not necessarily the hands that tend it, and every keeper anyone has ever met has seemed exactly as old as the bowl. It may be a succession, each one learning the circuit from the last and then lying down in some warm hollow of the wall to become one more pale ledge. It may be a single patient creature that has been kind enough to look like a person. Or it may be just what happens to a place that is watched carefully for long enough.

It does not hurry, and it has never been known to leave.
