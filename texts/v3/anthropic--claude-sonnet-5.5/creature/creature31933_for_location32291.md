# Those Who Keep the Level Country

Five kinds of thing live here. You will meet three of them by accident, one by rumor, and one only by leaving something behind.

## The Drinkers

These are the ones you see first, though you will take them for corrosion. They lie along the steam pipes in the warm corridors between the boilers: pale, jointed, blind, nearly clear, each about the length of a forearm. Their many legs fold so closely against the metal that they seem to be a kind of bloom on it. Look longer and a pattern shows. They gather at the flanges and the valve seats, where the pipe leaks a few thousandths of a degree into the air, and they hold there with their mouths to the seam.

They are drinking the last differences in the world. A flange a little warmer than its neighbor is a spring to them, and they crowd at it the way animals once crowded at water. Where a cluster has sat for years, the metal goes dull and cool, and the small gradient is gone. Then, over a season you would not notice, the cluster moves on to the next seam, a hand's width at a time.

They do not seem to be born, and they do not seem to die. The count in each city never changes, and the people who have counted say they stopped because the number was always the same. Perhaps the Drinkers are also a closed loop, like the ingots. Perhaps each one is very old and very careful, spending its warmth as slowly as the cities spend their ore. If you rest a finger near one, its legs tighten. Your hand is the hottest thing it has met in an age, and it is afraid of a fire that size.

## The Householders

The cities are inhabited. This is the thing the visitor takes longest to understand.

The cups on the ledges, the coats on the pegs, the lit doorways, the benches worn smooth by no visible use: all of it has an owner. The owners are there, in the lit rooms, sitting or standing or half-turned toward a shelf. You do not see them because you are looking for movement, and they have almost stopped moving.

In a world with no gradients, the only wealth is difference, and they have learned to spend it as if each grain were the last. They keep their bodies at the temperature of the air, so they throw no warmth and cast no thermal shadow, and the red furnace light passes over them as it passes over the walls. They are tall and narrow, with long-fingered hands and skin gone the color of old pewter. They wear the coats you saw on the pegs, or wear others just like them, and the cloth has taken their shape. A Householder's day is a single gesture, and a life is a few hundred of them.

The tool balanced on the barrel rim was not left there. It is being set down, and has been for eleven years. A hand rests just beneath it, fingers open, and if you watch a mark on the barrel and wait through most of a night, you can see that the fingers have closed by a hair. The cup on the ledge is being lifted. A Householder stands in a doorway, and their weight is moving from one foot to the other.

They do not dislike visitors. They are aware of you the way a tree is aware of weather. Your warmth reaches them and they feel it, faintly, as a pleasure they have no way to answer at your speed. If you stay long enough, and few do, you may find that a Householder's head has turned toward where you sat. It will look at the empty place for a long time, and its face will be kind.

## The Walker

Between the cities, on the bare cobbles, something larger goes about alone. Its limbs are long and hard, jointed like a wading bird's but made of something ceramic, glazed by age to the color of bone. The body is small and carried high, and it makes no sound except the dry click of each foot as it sets down and lifts. It takes about an hour to cross its own length.

It stops at the green seams and sits there, folded, its limbs stacked around it like a dropped bundle of reeds. The phosphorescence is the last of some ancient charge, and the Walker takes what is left of it slowly and without greed. Where it has sat, the green is a shade dimmer. It seems to know the whole map of the seams, and to space its visits so that none goes dark before another is reached.

The travelers who have seen it agree on the look. It is not afraid and it is not curious. It regards you as a stone would regard you if a stone had decided to be patient. Some say it carries a little heat between the cities, warm from one furnace and cold at the next, the only thing that ever moves a difference across the level country. Others say it carries nothing and only walks, and that walking is what it is for. Neither account has been checked. The Walker is always alone. Nobody has found a second, and nobody has found its bones.

## The Sleepers in the Cellars

In the deepest rooms of the oldest city, where pale minerals sweat a bead of water once in an age, there are things that need it. They are small, grey, and flat, crusted into the wall like fossilized moss. For most of history they are indistinguishable from stone.

When a bead gathers and falls, one of them wakes. Its crust swells and darkens, and for a day it is alive. What it does with the day is known only by the sound. A single note rises out of the cellars, pure and unwavering, and it travels through the bones of the city and out across the plateau, where distant travelers hear it as the edge of a bell. Then the water is spent, the crust pales, and the thing is stone again.

The Householders know the note and stop for it, as far as they can stop. It is the one event they have kept a name for.

## The Guest

The fifth inhabitant is whoever comes.

A visitor is the only thing in the country that hums with difference: a warm, wet, hurried body, breathing out a mist that vanishes as it forms. You are a small weather. The Drinkers lean toward you along their pipes. The dust near your face shivers, for the first time in ages, and settles again. Your footprints in the loose grit are a kind of writing, and they will be the newest thing anyone reads for a long while.

If you stay, the country will adjust to you as slowly as it adjusts to anything. It will not resent you and it will not hurry. At the edge of your hearing, the one tone under everything will go on, and beside you in a red doorway a hand will go on closing around a cup it began to lift before you came.
