# Those Who Keep Ombrel

## The People

They call themselves the Held, or more often just *the holding*, because the everyday greeting is a question and its answer: *Holding?* *Holding.* It means *are you level, are you steady, is your ground still under you*. It is asked in the morning, after a rain, and after the mountain has groaned in the night. Someone who answers "mostly" gets a hand on the shoulder and a long look.

They are a lean people, and heat and thin air have shaped them the way wind shapes a thorn. They have deep chests and long ribs, and they breathe slowly and completely, as if every breath were budgeted. Their movements are economical. Nobody runs. A child who sprints down a gantry is caught by the collar and walked back to the start, not out of cruelty, but because sprinting wastes water, and water is what the city counts.

Their skin runs from tea-brown to a dark burnished copper, and the amber awnings lend everyone a honeyed cast so that a face seen in the street looks lit from inside a lantern. Their eyes are ringed with soot-and-ash paste, thumbed on in a dark band from temple to temple against the glare, and the older the person, the finer and more habitual the line. The whites of their eyes are often faintly yellow. Squint creases fan from the outer corners of nearly every face, even the young ones. Lips crack and are salved with a greenish balm rendered from the drip-channel mat, which smells faintly of boiled eggs and which nobody notices anymore.

Their hair is worn short or bound close, since long hair holds sulfur, and their hands are the best record of their trades. Scrapers have fingertips gone shiny and numb. Casters have small round burn-scars on the backs of the wrists, like a scatter of coins. Old bloom-gangers have nails that are ridged and a dull silver-grey, and a persistent cough they call *the dome-clear*, and they cheerfully refuse to discuss it.

## The Gait

Anyone who has lived in Ombrel more than a few years walks with the lean. It is not a limp, but it looks like one to a stranger: a slight, constant hitching of the hips, a hand that drifts out toward walls, a foot placed a half-beat before the floor is expected to arrive. The floor might be off by a finger's width from yesterday. The body has learned to assume it is.

Visitors from the lowlands stumble in Ombrel, and the Held find this touching. They put a hand at the small of the visitor's back without comment, the way one steadies a drunk or a toddler. Meanwhile the Held themselves are unnerved on level ground. There is a story, told with delight, of a Leveler who went down the mountain to the flat country and was sick for three days, because everything was so *true* it seemed to be lying.

## The Levelers

The Levelers are the city's aristocracy, though they would laugh at the word. They are the ones who walk the terraces before dawn in long pale coats, each carrying a plumb bob wrapped in cloth and a rack of water-glass vials clipped to the belt like a bandolier. They know the personality of every building. *The Cistern Row block sulks to the east.* *The Third Furnace Hall creeps.* They talk about foundations as other people talk about grandchildren.

A Leveler's tools are inherited. A plumb line is not handed down until it has been used forty years, and the retirement of a line is done quietly: the Leveler hangs it in a doorway for a day and lets whoever passes see how it has settled. Their apprentices are chosen young, for stillness, not strength. The test is to stand on a tilted plank holding a full cup and not spill it, and then to say, without looking, which way the plank is tilted. The best can feel a quarter-turn's error through their boot soles.

Levelers speak little and almost never swear. Their one serious curse is *may you set true*, which means *may you stop moving*, which means *may you die*. It is only said in anger, and only once.

## The Scrapers and the Bloom-Gangs

Below the Levelers in prestige and above them in danger are the gangs who work the fumaroles. They wear wet cloth wrapped over mouth and nose, leather aprons, and boots with soles of layered clay-and-iron that must be replaced monthly. They go out at the coolest hour, gouging the colored crusts (yellow sulfur, blue-green copper, the grey arsenical bloom) into hide buckets and hauling them back to the rendering floors.

They sing, though quietly, because shouting is discouraged. Their songs are low, wordless, and in a rhythm of long breaths, and they help the gang keep a common pace. Heard from a gantry above a fumarole field, it sounds like the ground itself is humming.

Their dead are many. They are honored with a piece of ironwork, a small cast tag, riveted to the wall of the rendering hall where they worked. There are thousands of tags. The oldest have gone black and bloomed with a thin green corrosion, and no one polishes them.

## The Drip-Keepers

The people who care for the water are the closest thing the city has to priests, and they would resent being called that too.

They rise before the Levelers. In the grey before sunrise they climb among the condensing plates with long felt wipers and soft brushes, checking that the metal is clean, that the runnels are clear, that no bead is being lost to grit. They are mostly women and older men, patient and small-voiced, and they can tell by the sound of a gutter whether it is running at half strength. They know every cistern by its note. A tank that is filling has a rising, hollow pitch, and a tank nearly full has a pitch so high it is barely there.

They hold the water accounts, which are scrupulous. Every household's allotment is tallied on knotted cords. Water is never given as a gift, only as a loan, and loans between families run back generations, everyone owing everyone a cupful. It is considered vulgar to settle a debt entirely. A debt fully paid means the relationship is over.

## The Turbine Tenders

These are the deaf-ish. Years next to the great turbines, humming below hearing, have dulled their ordinary ears while sharpening some other sense. They feel speech in their teeth and breastbones and often prefer to converse with a hand laid flat on the other person's chest. They walk the machine halls barefoot on the warm iron, reading the day's health through their soles, and they know when a bearing is failing a full day before any gauge shows it.

They are considered slightly uncanny. Children are dared to touch a tender's sleeve, and tenders enjoy this and sometimes pretend to be a turbine, going *hmmmm* in a voice so low the children run away shrieking, thinly and sharply, and are then shushed.

## Children

Ombrel's children are quiet, watchful, and strangely competent. By six they can read a water-glass. By eight they can thread a jackscrew by hand. Their toys are offcuts of the city's work: bits of chain, tin gears, small plumb bobs on thread. Their favorite game is *Tilt*, in which one child holds a shallow dish of water and the others try to guess, with their eyes shut, which way the ground has moved.

They are also the ones who know the secret places. They know where the warm ash is deepest and the pale beetles come out in the evening, and how to hold one in a cupped hand so it walks a slow, ticklish circle. They know the green mat in the drip channels is not to be touched with bare hands (it stings a little) but that a bit on a fingertip, dabbed on a scraped knee, heals faster than anything. They will tell you this in whispers, as if they had discovered it.

When rain comes, they are let out onto the gantries in their thin shirts, and they stand with their faces up and their mouths open, saying nothing at all.

## The Old

The old of Ombrel are stained. Their teeth are yellow-brown from sulfur, their fingers are grey at the nail, and their voices are dry rasps. They sit on stoops in the amber shade and watch the street with the calm of people who have outlasted several versions of it. They are the best storytellers, and their favorite subject is the Exhalation: the year it lasted two hours, the year it came twice in three days, the year a whole terrace stood in white and someone whispered a name and no one ever found out who it was.

They dislike being asked why nobody works during the Exhalation. *You just don't*, they say, and turn away. When pressed, one very old woman was heard to say that it is the only hour when the mountain isn't listening to the city, and the city ought to return the courtesy.

## The Other Residents

The people are not alone in the city, though the others do not live *with* them so much as *beside* them.

**The pale beetles** are the size of a grain of rice, the color of bone, and quite blind. They live in the warm ash between terraces and come out at dusk in slow processions, following the microbial mat like cattle on a drovers' road. They are counted, informally, as a sign of good health: if the beetles vanish from a patch of ash, the ground there is about to change.

**The swifts** are soot-black, sickle-winged, and nest in the cool mouths of unused chimneys. They spend their days riding the thermals over the dome in slow, unbroken spirals, and they cry only at dusk, a thin, high, single note. Levelers watch them closely. A swift that roosts early is a warning, and when it does the Levelers make their rounds twice.

**The green mat** is, in its way, a resident too. It is a living slick that coats the drip channels in brilliant color, eating sulfur, making a little oxygen and a little salve, and asking for nothing. The Held do not exactly worship it. They simply do not scrub it out, and they teach their children not to. It is the nearest thing to a garden the mountain has, and it is treated the way one might treat a shy guest who has never once caused trouble.

**The thing in the tanks** is something else. Nobody has seen it whole. It is said to be translucent, many-legged, and slow, and it lives in the deepest condensate tanks, where the water is coldest and darkest. At night, when the city is quiet, it taps on the stone from below: three taps, a pause, two, a pause, and then long silences. Drip-keepers who work late say they sometimes tap back. They will not say why. Nothing has ever been fished out, no water has ever been fouled, and the tanks are, if anything, unusually sweet. The city leaves it alone, and the thing, in its turn, leaves the water clean.

## The City Itself

If you stand on a gantry at dusk, with your hand on a warm wall, you may begin to feel that the largest inhabitant of Ombrel is not any person or creature but the arrangement of them all: the Levelers turning their quarter-turns, the drip-keepers wiping their plates, the tenders feeling through their soles, the swifts wheeling, the beetles walking their slow roads, the green mat shining in the dark. Each is answering the mountain's small movements with a small movement of its own, and the sum of it is a kind of steady, attentive breathing.

The people of Ombrel would not put it that way. They would say only, if you asked, that the mountain moves, and someone has to move with it.

Then they would look at you, tilt their head a few degrees to match the floor, and ask: *Holding?*
