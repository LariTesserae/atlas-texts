# Those Who Live on the Ossuary Plateau

Almost everything here is one animal, or so near to one that the difference does not matter to the animals. This is the first thing to understand about the inhabitants, and the last thing you will fully believe.

## The Plate-Grazers

Each is the size of a coin and about as thick as two coins. The carapace is grey-brown and ridged, with a shallow dish worn into the top. Beneath it is a single muscular foot, pale and damp as the inside of a lip, and at the front edge a mouth that opens only in wet conditions. There are no true eyes. Around the rim of each shell runs a fringe of pits that can tell dark from less dark, and a row of fine bristles that feel the weight and tremor of the neighbors on every side. A grazer knows very little about the world, but it knows exactly what the grazers touching it are doing, and that is nearly the same thing.

They live in three or four courses, and the courses are not random. Newly hatched young, soft and translucent and no bigger than a grain of couscous, begin at the top, where the fog lands first and food is easiest. As they harden and darken they are eased downward by pressure and by the pushing of those hatching above them, until the old ones, thick-shelled and nearly motionless, lie at the bottom against the gravel. They no longer forage. The upper layers pass water down to them through the overlapping shells, drop by drop, from ridge to ridge, so that on a foggy night a slow trickle runs through the crust the way sap runs through a trunk. Nothing is charged for this and nothing is forgotten. The oldest are fed because they are the floor. When one dies, its shell stays where it is, and the next generation settles onto it.

They hear each other in the clench. When something heavy presses one part of the crust, the grazers grip and freeze, and the freeze travels outward across the surface as a ripple of stillness, a few meters a second. A hawk-shadow, a falling stone, or a stumbling boot each has its own rhythm of gripping. You can sometimes watch a whole slope go rigid, then soften in sections, as the news is passed along and judged to be nothing. They are not intelligent in the way a single animal is. But when a ripple crosses a valley and returns changed, as if considered, it is hard to believe that no one is home.

They breed in the deep of the year, in the dry months when the fog thins. A grazer that will carry young lifts its rim slightly and takes in a scatter of pale eggs, which it broods in the hollow beneath its own shell. The young leave when the fog returns. Nobody has seen one die of age. They are found, singly, at the bottom, empty and clean, already part of the ground.

## The Reeds That Are Not Reeds

The tall pale tubes are the plateau's second people, and the most sociable. Each tube is a colony, thousands of animals so small that a single one would disappear on the tip of a pin. They are fused wall to wall into a hollow stem that rises from the crust as far as your forearm. The individuals are not alike. The ones at the open mouth of the tube are ringed with feathery hairs that comb the air for drifting spores, mites, and the sweet mineral dust blown up from the lowlands. Below them are the digesters, and below those the brooders, which swell in season and bud new colonies that break away at the tip and drift off on the fog like pale seeds. At the very base, sunk in the grazers' shells, the rooting animals have almost become mineral. They anchor the tube and draw moisture from the crust, and in exchange they are sheltered from every wind.

They lean together. That is their one language: a slow tilt toward the wetter sky, the whole meadow bending by the same few degrees, so that from a ridge the field seems to breathe or to lean over a great secret. In the evenings, when the fog thickens and the air runs across their open tops, they hum. The pitch depends on their height, so the meadow sounds like an organ played by no one. The hum drops as the fog rises and dwindles to nothing by dawn.

## Beneath, and Between

In the seam between crust and gravel live the blind ones, pale, many-legged, and no longer than a fingernail, though some in the deeper chalk grow as long as a hand. They are slow, jointed, and gently armored, and they have no eyes at all. They feel their way with the long, drooping hairs on their forelegs and with something else, a sense of the chalk itself, of how firm it is, of what has recently died in it. Their work is the ground. They eat the droppings and the moulted casings, and what they cannot digest they carry a little way and set down, layer on layer, in the damp. They pack it with saliva, and it sets to the soft plaster that the grazers stand on. Over centuries they have made a country out of their leavings. They have no interest in what walks above them, and no word for it.

There are also lodgers, and these are everywhere. In each cracked or vacated shell lives some small bright-eyed thing: a pale beetle, a wingless moth-like creature with a single large eye, a nested pair of mites, a spider the colour of pewter that has stopped spinning and merely waits. Some are guests and some are parasites. In many cases the difference has been lost, and the lodger and its host have grown so used to each other that neither survives alone. Whole shells are found sealed, containing only a lodger's clutch of eggs and a small tidy pile of the host's ground-up body. In the dry months, winged things emerge from the crust in pale clouds, live for a day, mate, fall back onto the grazers, and are eaten.

On the few trees that stand in the hollows, a smaller, rounder kind of grazer has learned to climb. They cover the trunk from root to fork, and the bark under them is soft and grey with their tread. The tree feeds them in return, pushing sap into the cracks they leave. The pair are so long acquainted that neither is quite the animal it would be alone.

## The Knock

At dusk, on some evenings and in some places, something in the substrate strikes once. It is a hollow sound, felt in the feet as much as heard, and is not made by any of the animals described above. It comes from a depth of perhaps a few meters. Some time later, after the count of a slow breath, it is answered from far away.

Those who have listened for years have theories. It is one of the big blind ones, grown old and heavy in the chalk, striking its head on a buried stone. It is many of them, timing themselves to the fog. It is a chamber in the ground that the last of the builders sealed, and something in it is still keeping the hour. The grazers give no sign of hearing. That may be the most convincing evidence that they always have.

## Whoever Built the Huts

The marks are not natural, and so there must have been makers, or there are. Nobody who has crossed the plateau has met them.

There is something to be said for the idea that they are still here, and that they are very quiet. The freshly cleaned capstones on some of the cairns suggest hands, or something with the patience of hands, that have brushed off the crust and set the stone right. People who have walked slowly and been lucky report a certain feeling on the higher swells at dusk: of a small figure or figures standing very still among the tubes, lean and pale and tilting with them, so well matched to the meadow that they are noticed only when they turn. When you look straight at the place, there is a stone, or a stump, or a tree, furred with shells.

If they exist, they must be small in number and long in the habit. They would move with the smooth evenness that the crust forgives, and would sleep in the huts, wrapped in the warm heaps of shed casings, among tubes that stand all round them and hum. They would rub the upright stones at chest height, and their downhill sides, as they pass. They might keep the slab.

There is another idea, told less often, and it is that there are no makers left because the makers became the grazers. In this telling the huts were built by a hand-having people who found, over ages, that the best thing to do with hands was to let them go. They took to the floor, lost their eyes, grew their shells, and settled shoulder to shoulder on the gravel to become a single crust that holds the water. Those who tell it point to the slab. On foggy evenings the grazers gather before it in a dense, silent semicircle, and the amber resin in its grooves brightens as they press near. They face it like a congregation. The resin has a sweet, faint smell, like almonds, like the fog itself. The grazers touch it with the tips of their rims and hold there, and none of them seems to be eating.

Whether they are reading it, remembering it, or simply warming themselves is not known. Whether it means anything is not known either.

## How You Are Received

You are tolerated, provided you are slow. The grazers do not fear you and do not welcome you. They take your weight into themselves as they take the weight of rain, and they pass it along. If you walk evenly, the crust gives under you and closes behind, and you leave a trail of shallow prints that fill within seconds. The tubes tilt as you pass, some of them toward you, which is probably the wet of your breath. The blind ones go on with their work below. The lodgers look out from their shells with bright, unblinking eyes.

If you stop, and stand long enough in the late light, you may feel the crust settle around your boots in a way that is not quite the way it settles around a stone. It is only pressure. But it is even, and it goes on, and as the fog comes up and the first hum begins in the tubes, it is hard to keep from feeling that you have been added to a count.
