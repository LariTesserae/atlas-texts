# Those Who Live on the Terraces

You will not see them at first. The place is too green and too wet, and the people who live here have taken on its colors. You notice a patch of moss that is slightly the wrong shape, and then it straightens, and it is a woman in a waxed hood with a tray of seedlings on her hip, standing so still on a terrace edge that the drips have been landing on her shoulders as they land on everything else.

They call themselves the **lifters**, after the lifts, though the engineers who send the schedules up from below call them crew. There are perhaps two hundred, in families that have been here since the scraping. Some are third-generation and have never worked any other ground. Their forearms are stained grey-green to the elbow, and it doesn't wash out. Old lifters have it in the creases of their knuckles and under the nails, and in the whites of their eyes, some say, though that is probably the light.

## How they are built

Years of stepping lightly have given them a particular walk. They put the ball of the foot down first and let the heel arrive late, as though the ground might object. On fresh pours they wear felt overshoes, and they enter a new terrace one at a time, slowly, with their breath held a little. The children learn this before they learn to read. A child who stamps on a curing lift gets no shouting. Every adult on the terrace goes quiet and looks at the child, and that is worse.

Their hands are broad and cracked and always faintly warm, from the pipes and the stone. Their hair is kept short or bound tight, because the humidity makes anything loose into a nest for lichen. They are a damp-skinned, unhurried people. They move at the gantry's speed without meaning to, and visitors say it takes three days to notice you have started doing it too.

## The trades

**The pourers** ride or walk beside the gantry. There are six of them to a shift, and they speak in a low shorthand of hand signals, because the hopper is patient and talking over it feels rude. One of them, the *feeler*, walks ahead and lays a bare palm on the last lift before each new one goes down. Her verdict decides everything: *warm and quiet*, *warm and loud*, *cold*. Nobody argues with her. When she retires she will pass on her palm to an apprentice by a process nobody can quite describe, which involves standing together on the same terrace for a season.

**The skinners** seed the moss, set the ferns, and hang the orchids from the conduit lines. They carry small brass sprayers and bags of moss slurry, which they paint onto the bare pale faces with wide brushes, murmuring. They are the most talkative of the lifters, and the only ones who sing. They work from a schedule of species and sequence, and they resent it a little, and they follow it exactly. When a terrace goes from pale to green under their hands, they hold a small private ceremony that consists of one of them saying "There," and the others nodding.

**The wheel-keepers** turn the valves. Each keeper has a circuit of forty or fifty junctions, and knows every one by the sound it makes when it opens: the sigh of the fat steam valves, the tick of the small condensate cocks. Their hand-wheels are worn bright as coins. They can tell by touch, through the wheel, what is happening ten meters down the line. Their houses are the warmest, and they sleep well.

**The pruners** cut back the vines from the gantry struts. It is a lesser trade, and the youngest are given it. But the cuts they make are careful and legible, always at the same angle and the same height, so that an experienced eye can read who worked which stretch and on what day. Some of the pruners have begun leaving small deliberate flourishes in the cuts. No one has told them to stop.

And there are cooks, menders, tea-boilers, rope-splicers, a woman who keeps the ledgers, and two old men who do nothing anyone can name but are consulted on everything.

## Where they sleep

They live inside the work. Alcoves were cast into the terrace walls from the first lifts: low arched rooms with a bench over a steam conduit, a shelf, a hook, and a drain in the floor. Moss has grown across most of the doorways, and residents part it with a hand and let it fall closed behind them. Sleeping on the warm bench, with the whole structure sweating and dripping around you, is said to be like sleeping inside the chest of something large and calm. Nobody complains of cold. Everyone complains of damp, cheerfully, as a matter of manners.

The families are known by their alcoves, not by names. *Fourth on the east, under the red trees. The three by the sump.* Marriages are announced by a change of moss over the door.

## What they don't say

There is a mountain they are building, and they do not name it.

Ask a lifter and you will get a pause and a gentle redirection. Ask twice and you will be handed tea. The older ones refer to it as *the one behind*, or *the guest*, or simply point with the chin toward the model's two unequal summits and say "that." Some say it was lost, and that the terraces are a kind of remembering. Others say it hasn't happened yet, and they are making a place for it to arrive. The two camps are not hostile. They work side by side, and it seems to matter to neither of them which is true, only that the pours be right.

They are certain of one thing: when the model is finished, the sump will be opened and the water will go into a channel through the rock. Where it goes, no one has said. But every lifter has, somewhere in their alcove, a small vessel of water drawn from the sump on the day they were born or arrived. They keep it unstoppered on the shelf. It never seems to evaporate.

## The other thing

The stone is not entirely without a resident of its own.

The lifters won't say it, but they behave as though it were. They talk to the fresh pours in a murmur, as one talks to sleeping children. They leave things on the newest lifts: a smooth pebble, a folded leaf, a bead of amber. In the morning the offerings have sunk slightly into the surface, as if the stone had taken a breath in the night and drawn them down. Nobody retrieves them.

Stand alone on a new terrace at dusk, in the hour when the frogs begin, and you may feel it. It isn't a presence exactly, more a grain of attention, a very slow *considering.* The surface settles under your feet by the width of a hair. The warmth changes and seems to pool where you stand. Then, if you are patient, it eases away, unhurried, like someone who has looked at you long enough to be satisfied.

The lifters say the mountain does not live here yet. They say it is learning the way.

## Morning

Watch them at the hour the sluices open. A horn sounds, low and single, and the wheel-keepers turn their wheels. Water pours down through the made mountain, and the terraces run with thin bright sheets. All along the rim, the lifters come out of their alcoves through the moss doorways and stand on their thresholds in the steam, wet-faced, saying nothing. The gantry stops on its track. The frogs, who are asleep, do not call. For an hour the whole place shines, and its people stand in it, grey-green to the elbow, warm-handed, patient, looking at what they have made and what it is slowly deciding to become.

Then someone says "Well," and they go to work.
