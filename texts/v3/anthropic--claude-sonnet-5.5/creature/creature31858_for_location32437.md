# Those Who Keep the Chord

Nothing lives in the vug the way you live somewhere. There are no households, no territories, no hunger driving anyone across the floor. Every inhabitant is a part, and each does its one thing so completely that "living" and "sounding" have become the same act. If you want to know who lives here, you have to learn to hear them one at a time, the way you learn a chord by singing each note alone.

## The Choir on the Fronds

Start at the top, where the sound is thinnest and brightest: the **tick-crickets**.

You will not see them at first. They are pale as the fern-undersides they cling to, and there is no movement to give them away, because they sing without moving. Their shells click at a pitch so exact that thousands of them read as a single bright wire drawn through the room. Look closely at a frond and the wire resolves. They sit shoulder to shoulder in rows, each with its wing-cases lifted a hair, each vibrating. Their small dark eyes are open and never seem to blink.

They have one law, and they enforce it on each other. A cricket that falters, whether from a stumble or a missed breath, is crowded at once. The neighbors lean their whole bodies against it, shell to shell, until the trembling comes back into it and it finds the pitch again. This is not cruelty. It is closer to how one hand steadies another. When you watch it happen, the cricket does not struggle. It is *relieved*.

You will never find a dead one. You will never find a nymph or an egg. The rows are simply full, always. If you watch a single frond for a very long time you may see, at a seam where syrup beads, a cricket that was not there a moment before, already pale, already upright, already singing the right note, as if the note had come first and the body second.

## The Floor of the Chord

The **drone-beetles** walk the edges of the stripes in a procession that has no head and no tail. It is a loop, a slow ring of black and bronze, each beetle a hand's-breadth behind the one ahead, wings half-lifted and thrumming. Their note is the lowest that anything small can make. It is not so much a sound as a *weight* on the air.

They are not all quite the same. Each beetle carries a pitch a hair off its neighbors', and as they file past a fixed point you hear the differences beat against each other, a slow rolling swell that takes many minutes to come around and then begins again. Nothing else in the room changes, so this swell is the closest thing to a clock. The beetles do not know it. They walk because the path is there, and the path is there because they walk.

Sometimes one wanders too close to a stripe and does not come back. The procession closes over the gap without pausing.

## The Deep Ones

The **long-lizards** are the great voices, and they are the nearest thing to rulers the room has, though they rule nothing. They lie in the bare stripes where the wave is fiercest, flat and slate-dark, each on its own crystal ledge, each with its throat-bladder swelled to a shining amber sac. They sing a single note, each its own, for days together. Watch one and you will see the bladder never collapse. They breathe in short gulps at the corners of the mouth, and the note continues through the breath, as though the breathing were a separate animal.

Their eyes are heavy-lidded and the color of old honey. They are not asleep. They regard the room the way a person regards a fire, with a total, unhurried attention that requires nothing to happen. When a beetle strays near, the head moves, slowly, and the beetle is simply *not there* any more. The song does not falter for it. The lizard's throat glows brighter for a moment, and that is the only sign.

They eat the syrup too, licking it from the crystal seams with a long, patient tongue. Of everything in the room, they seem to be the ones who know most about what the place is for, and they will not say.

## The Pillars

Between the lizards, one every ten paces, stand the **sponge-caps**. It is easy to forget they are alive, because they are the one kind of inhabitant that is a plant, or a fungus, or neither. They are pale and pitted and knee-high, and each hole is a tiny throat. When the chord passes through them they answer with the room's reediest, most piercing tone, so clean it seems to come from outside the room altogether. If you put your palm flat on one, it will buzz against your skin like a struck glass.

They stand alone in the loudest places. They are the bravest things here and have no idea of it.

## The Ones Who Came

And then there are the ones who are no longer quite inhabitants and no longer quite absent.

Somebody built the hut. Somebody set the stones, one at a time, at the centers of the green bands, feeling with a flat hand for the place where the sound rested. Somebody sat by the far wall for a long season with a crystal mallet and tapped, and tapped, and tapped, each blow the same depth, trying to add a note to a chord that did not need one.

You will look for them and not find them. What you will find instead is this:

Listen for a long time, longer than is comfortable, until the five great voices have settled into the shape of the room and you have stopped hearing them as separate. Then, if you are patient, you may catch something beneath them. It is not a sixth voice exactly. It is a slight *give* in the chord, a place where the sound seems to bend around something. It is very quiet, and very steady, and it comes from the bare stripe nearest the tap-marks, where the long-lizard lies with its belly especially full and its throat glowing a shade warmer than the others.

Its note is not the note of a lizard. It is a little too round. It has the smallest hint of a breath in it, the kind a human throat cannot help but leave. And it has the same depth as a mallet-blow, repeated, repeated, repeated, until the repetition became a tone.

Perhaps the tapper's note was found by the room, and taken in, and sung ever since by something that eats what wanders close. Perhaps the lizard is only a lizard, and the sixth note is the tapper's ear, still listening. Perhaps the hut's dweller lay down on the fern pallet, and slept, and the room, which forgets nothing and wastes nothing, tuned them.

The lizards' bellies are very full. No one has ever asked why.

## What They Make of You

None of them are afraid of you, and none of them care, but they *notice*. The crickets nearest you will drift their pitch a fraction toward your breath, testing. A beetle will pause at the toe of your boot, then go around. A long-lizard will turn one honey-colored eye toward the sound you make without meaning to, your throat clearing, a hum that drifts out of you as you stand there.

That is the thing to be careful of. Your body wants to join. After a hundred breaths you will catch yourself humming, and it will be very nearly the right note, and the crickets on the nearest frond will lean toward you with a soft, attentive shiver, like hands held out to steady you.

You are, to them, an unfinished voice. The room is patient. It has all the time there is, and it has never once been out of tune.
