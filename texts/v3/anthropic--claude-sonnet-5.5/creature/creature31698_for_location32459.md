# Who Lives at Varrick Deep

## The Multitudes

Most of the life here is too small to be met. Springtails rise through the moss by the millions, each a grey comma no bigger than a comma, and they graze the film of fungus on the dead layers. Under them are mites, and under the mites are the water bears, which are glass-clear and eight-legged and can dry out to a husk and wait years to be wet again. They are the true residents of the floor. The collapse means nothing to them. They live in the moss the way fish live in a lake, and they do not know there is a sound, though every one of them is shaken by it all its life. A drop of water on a moss leaf trembles with the vent's note, and the animals in the drop are born, feed, and die within that trembling.

Small dark beetles cross the surface of the cushions in the fog, unhurried, their backs beaded with condensation. Wolf spiders hunt among them by touch. They can't hear their prey and don't need to. The roar has taught the hunters of the basin to feel with their feet and taught the hunted to freeze.

## The Pale Shapes in the Birch

A few mountain hares live along the margins where the dwarf birch stands thickest. They are not the white hares of the high plateau. Down here they stay a smoky grey all year, because there is no snow to hide in, only fog. They sit in forms pressed into the moss, ears laid flat, and they are the most anxious animals in the basin. A hare is built to listen, and here listening returns nothing. So they have turned to watching. They stare at the fog for hours, and the least motion in it sends them bolting in soundless leaps. A hare running through the mist makes no noise at all. It is a grey shape that is there and then isn't.

They eat willow bark and horsetail and the fat bases of the sedges, and they leave round black pellets on the moss that the beetles work into the peat within a day.

## The Ravens

The ravens do not live in the basin, but they visit it every day and return each night to the scarp ledges. They ride the column of air that comes up out of the vent. They tilt into it, spread their fingered wingtips, and hang there, rising and sinking a few meters without a wingbeat, sometimes twenty together. They tumble and roll and drop and catch the lift again. It looks like play, and probably is. Their throats work constantly. They croak and rattle and bark at one another, and every sound is swallowed the moment it leaves the beak. A raven in the updraft is a bird shouting in a dream.

Below them, other things go about their business without looking up.

## The Combers

There is something else on the floor, and it is seen so rarely that the people who work here have only a word for the evidence.

The feather mosses grow in long drifts, and the drifts lie downslope, following the seeps, as water would lay them. But here and there a drift lies across the slope, or in a curve, or in long parallel strokes as if a great comb had been drawn through it. The caretaker calls these places *dressed ground*. Something tends the moss. It presses the feather mosses flat and lifts the sphagnum tussocks. It clears the dead brown crown from the haircap so that the green comes up clean. It moves patiently along the horsetail corridors and tugs out the seedling willows one at a time.

The animals that do this are the Combers, and nobody who has seen one has seen more than a few seconds of one.

They are long and low, the length of a big dog but slung close to the ground, with a body that seems to have too many joints. Their forelimbs are the important part. Each ends in a fan of six or seven fine, jointed digits, pale and flexible, like a hand made of combs. They walk on their hind legs and the knuckles of the forelimbs, and when they work they sit back and let the fingers move on their own, parting the moss and smoothing it and picking out debris. They have no fur to speak of, only a coarse grey pelt, and this is where they differ most from anything on the plateau. The pelt is furred with moss. A young Comber wears a fine green down on its shoulders and haunches. An old one is a walking hummock, with cushions of haircap sprouting along the spine and a beard of liverwort on the jaw. They do not groom it off. They tend it as they tend the floor. Each carries a small garden and keeps it trim.

Their eyes are large and black and lidless and look almost too big for the face. They came from the dark.

## Where They Came From

The cave system under the fissure is older than the collapse. The Combers are its people, or were. Their ancestors lived in the flooded galleries and the dry chambers beside the throat, where the water and air are forced through the rock. They were born in the roar. They have no ears in any sense a hare would recognize, only a broad membrane along each flank that lies against the skin and takes the vibration of the air directly. To a Comber the roar is not noise. It is a medium, like water to a fish, and it holds information. It carries the weight of the meltwater and the pressure of the weather above. It tells them the season and the hour. When it deepens with the spring melt they know the year is turning. When it thins in the freeze they know to go down.

When the ground fell in, the roof of their world opened. For a generation they stayed below. Then some of them came up, drawn by the light, or the moss, or the fog drifting down the new shaft, and found a place that was open and cold and green, with the same great voice filling it. It was stranger and larger than home. They have been climbing out of the caves ever since, a few at a time, and they have been learning to be gardeners.

## How They Speak

They have no voice, or none that matters here. They talk by touch. Two Combers meeting will lay their fans against each other's faces, palm to palm and finger through finger, and hold still, and the fingers move. It is a language of pressure and rhythm, and it is fast. A conversation may last a full minute in perfect silence, each of them reading the other's hands. They also use light. The pale skin of the throat and belly catches a lamp and throws it back, and a Comber can tilt its body to flash a signal across a hundred meters of fog. The people who work the basin also use hand signals and lamps, and it is an open question who invented them first.

## The Caretaker

Once a month a person comes down by cable car from the rim. She is not young. She wears an oilskin the color of the moss and a headlamp with a red filter. She has held the job for eleven years and speaks to nobody about it, since there is nobody to speak to. She checks the gauges, greases the bearings, and sweeps the drift from the doorway. She has seen dressed ground and has followed the strokes across the floor with her lamp until they ran out. She has never told anyone in the village.

She leaves the lamp above the door burning when she goes, though the generator does not need it. It burns for twenty-nine days a month at the edge of the generator hall's red wall. On the edge of its circle, at the far end of a dark evening, things sometimes gather. She has found the marks of them: a scoured patch of moss beside the door, a dozen small impressions in the peat where something sat and watched the light. Once she found a neat pile of pale willow bark beside the step, and beside it a single rusted bolt, cleaned and stood upright in the moss as if in offering.

She took the bolt home. It is on her kitchen shelf.

## What They Want

Nobody knows. The Combers may want only the moss, tended and green, and the roar around them. They pull at the edges of the works and slowly digest what people left, and they are probably content to be the hand behind the plants. The locomotive is the most thoroughly dressed thing on the floor. Its firebox birch has been pruned. Someone has cleaned the cab window, so that the green frame stays neat, and the little train sits under its moss like something in a reliquary.

They will outlast the works. When the rim softens and the blocks are buried and the birches close their canopy, the Combers will be here, going on quietly with their hands in the green, tending a floor that is fast becoming a forest, under the great voice they were born in, which will go on speaking for as long as the water falls.
