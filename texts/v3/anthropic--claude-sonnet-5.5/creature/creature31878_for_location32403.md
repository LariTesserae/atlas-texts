# The Listener in the Fourth Notch

Most of what lives in the Cistern is easy to find. Frogs, swifts, bats, loaches, owls, the heron folded in the shallows: they are loud, and they cling to the walls where you can see them. But the wall has one tenant that does not announce itself, and it took me most of a day to understand that I was being kept company.

## Signs

The first sign is a jar.

Low on the south wall, just above the waterline, one of the great barrel-sized jars is clean. Every other jar has its soft white lip of flowstone, thickening a hair each year. This one has none. Its rim is bare, dark and slick, worn smooth as the inside of a cup. Something has been going in and out of it, again and again, for longer than the wall has been counting.

The second sign is the pale skin. It hangs in the reeds in a long, translucent sleeve, shed whole like a glove, with the fingers still in it. Each finger is a small blunt shape with a rounded tip, and there are only four on each hand. The skin is the size of a child's, but it is too long, and the tail is a flattened paddle.

The third sign is the stillness of the loaches. Near the jar the blind fish do not pack tight as they do everywhere else. They keep a hand's breadth of clear water around the great rim, the way people leave a space around someone asleep.

## The Body

It is a salamander, though that word is too small. It is longer than a man is tall, thick through the middle as a sack of wet grain, with a broad flat head like a spade laid on its side and a mouth that runs almost ear to ear in a permanent, mild, downturned line. The eyes are two grains of black under the skin, no bigger than lentils, and they are not for seeing. The skin is the color of the flowstone: waxy, pale, faintly pink where the blood runs near the surface, mottled with grey-brown like old candle ends. In the lamplight it looks like something the wall grew rather than something that entered it.

It has lungs and it has gills, and it uses both as the water asks. Some days it lies for hours in the black pool with frilled red plumes lifting from its neck. Other days, after the pool has dropped, it hauls itself up onto the third ring on its four short legs, with a heavy sliding dignity, and breathes air with a sound like a slow sigh through a wet cloth. Its whole skin is covered with pores, and along its head and flanks runs a line of tiny sensing pits. It hears with these, and with its jaw and its belly. It hears the way a hand hears a table.

It is old. Its kind can live a century, and this one has been doing so in a place built to be patient. The flowstone has begun to catch on its back. In the dimmest part of the year it holds so still that a fine white film settles on it, and if you did not know, you would take it for a fallen block that had rounded itself in the wet.

## The Habit

It does not sing. It cannot. It has no voice that a human ear would call one, only a thin, dry squeak when it is frightened, which I heard once.

What it does is press. It rests its jaw against the biggest jars, the ones near the floor, and sits with its whole broad head against the clay. The jar takes the trembling of the drips and the frogs and the swift-whistles and hands it to the animal, and the animal takes it in through its bones. It stays like that for an hour or a night. Then it shifts, sometimes only a finger's width, to a neighboring jar. It does this all around the ring, jar by jar, over the course of weeks, so that its slow route through the wall is the same circuit the water takes through the gutters, only slower.

At some point I understood what the polished rim was. It is the animal's own jar, the one it always returns to. From there the sound of the whole wall converges, because the lowest jars are the deepest-toned and the wall was built to funnel into them. The salamander has found the one place where the hall is heard entire, and it has made its bed there.

## The Company

The other animals do not fear it and do not court it. The tree frogs sit on its head. I saw a small green one plant itself on the flat crown, right between the buried eyes, and sing its jar-note for a full hour, and the salamander did not move. The swifts wheel and scream and never look down. The owls ignore it, because it does not run and does not squeak, and there is nothing to hunt.

The heron is the only one with anything to reckon. It stands ten paces off in the shallows and pretends not to know. Once I watched it lunge at a loach near the great jar, then check itself mid-strike, neck bent, and stand rigid for a full minute before backing away. Nothing had moved. The salamander had not so much as tightened its throat. But something in the water had reached the heron, and the heron had felt it and declined.

It eats rarely. A loach, a snail, a frog that lingers a little long in reach. The mouth opens and the water goes in, and the thing that was there is gone with no sign of a chase, like a drain taking a leaf. The eating is the only quick thing it does, and it is quick beyond belief. Then it is a stone again for a week.

## The Chord

This is the part I am least sure of.

When the rain comes hard and the jars begin to draw together, the salamander is the first to know. You can see it: a slow lift of the head, the plumes flattening along the neck, the whole long body pressing tighter against the clay. It has stopped breathing. It is holding the note in itself the way the hall is holding it, and for a little while the animal and the chord seem to be one thing.

Then the chord reaches its fullness, and the pool trembles into its lattice of rings, and there is a moment, always the same moment, when the animal's mouth opens. It is not a yawn and not a cry. The jaw just falls, slowly, and the wide slack mouth stays open on the water. And every bird in the wall lifts.

I cannot say the one causes the other. It may be that the chord simply reaches the pitch where it cannot hold, and the salamander, which feels it first, opens in the same instant the swifts do. Or it may be that the hall has only ever needed one listener, and when the listener lets go of the note, the note has nowhere left to live, and it breaks into wings.

Afterward, in the great confused ringing, the salamander lies with its mouth still open, and the black water drains back down its throat and around its jaw, and it looks like an animal that has just been told something.

## What It Waits For

Sit at the lowest ring, in the cold, on the wet step, and hum. It does not matter what. A single low note, the deepest you can hold, until your teeth catch and the stone answers.

I did this for a long time before I saw it. The animal had come up out of the shallows without any sound and was lying at the edge of the fourth notch, four paces off, the water lapping its chin. Its head was turned toward me by a hand's width. Nothing else had changed. I kept the note, and it kept its stillness, and the jars around us began to answer, one at a time, the way they do for anything that touches the wall.

It does not sing back. It never will. But the wall holds a small dry hollow, one niche that the flowstone has never closed, and in that niche are things I would not have known to look for: a grey pebble, a curl of bronze wire gone green, the hollow ball of a dead swift's egg, a shard of jar with a thumbprint pressed into the clay. They are set in a line, not thrown, and there are more of them than one animal should have had the patience to gather. Each is placed near the last, as though someone is keeping count of what the hall has been given, and is waiting for the next thing to be given.
