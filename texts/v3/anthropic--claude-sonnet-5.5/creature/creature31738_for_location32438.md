# Those Who Keep the Ledger

Most of what lives in the basin is small, and none of it seems to know it lives anywhere strange. The strangeness is a matter of scale. Only from far enough back does the fen look like one animal made of many, and the many are the residents.

## The Small Tenants

The **snails** climb the sedges by the thousand, and their trails look like handwriting. A snail will sometimes stop where another's trail crosses its path, lift its head, and follow the older line for a hand's breadth before turning off. Walk the reeds at night and the silver threads run in loops and hooked runs, thickest near the seeps, and you begin to suspect that something is being copied out. The snails have shells the color of weak tea with a spiral of pale blue, and where the lattice runs close to the surface the blue lights faintly from inside.

The **dragonflies** patrol the open channels in fixed beats, hovering at the same three or four places on each circuit, and the places do not seem to be chosen by the water or the light. They are simply the places. Their wings are etched with a fine rectilinear tracery like the filaments below, and it is not clear which copied which.

The **sundews** and **pitcher plants** are the fen's most patient hunters. The pitchers hold their small lights in their throats like lamps left on for a guest. A beetle that drowns in one is not mourned, but the light there brightens a little for a few hours, as if a line had been entered.

## The Lanterns

The **frogs** are the fen's voice and, perhaps, its ears. They are small, pale, translucent-skinned, about the length of a thumb, and you can see their pulse, their gut, and the faint blue thread of the lattice running through their marrow. The throat organ that flares when they call is not a sac. It is a small clear lens with a bright point inside, exactly like the droplets on the hanging leaves.

Each frog sits on a stem or a knee of wood and calls in answer to its neighbor. The answers travel outward in waves that you can see as cyan flicker a heartbeat before you hear them. Nobody has found the frog that begins a wave, or the one that ends it. When the silence comes, those three or four seconds when the whole fen stops together, every frog has its throat lit and its mouth closed, and each is looking at nothing in particular. Some observers hold that the frogs are the lattice's pen, and that the silence is the moment when the ink is blotted.

## The Grey Waders

The slow **herons** stand where the water is a hand deep, and the ones that don't move when you approach are not tame. They are attending. Their heads are tilted a few degrees toward the middle of the basin, the same few degrees the leaves turn before a change in weather, and the same few degrees as the water's small drift over the roots.

When they strike, they strike where the frog will be, not where it is. The frog is always there a moment later, as though the heron had asked and been told. When a heron does walk, it is a stately, thoughtful walk, each foot lifted out of the water and set down without a ripple, and the ripple sometimes arrives afterward. The pale **swifts** that hunt the mist at dusk are said to nest in the heron colonies, but no nest has ever been found. Nothing here is made, and a nest has an edge.

## The Long Backs

Then there are the large ones.

They are called by no name in the fen, but a traveler who saw one would settle quickly on **Long Backs**, because the back is all you see. A grown one is seven to ten meters from muzzle to tail, and very low, riding in the channel with its spine just at the waterline and its flanks under. The back is broad, flat, and gently arched, and it is a garden. Moss covers it in a thick cushion. Seedlings of the pale alders stand along its ridge in straight rows, a nurse-log colonnade that walks. Sundew mats bead its shoulders, and at dawn a fine mist rises from the whole length of it, so that a Long Back moving through the fen looks like a low green island trailing smoke.

The head is small for the body, blunt, and held at the waterline. The eyes sit on top like two dark stones set in green wax. The mouth is a slow, wide, downward-curved slit fringed inside with a comb of fine, pale teeth as thin as hairs, and the animal feeds by drawing the black silt through the comb. What it strains out are the loose filaments at the edge of the lattice, the fringe that sloughs into the peat like shed hair. It seems to be eating the ledger's loose ends and carrying them to the next spring. If this is its work, it has been at it a very long time.

The skin under the moss is the gray-blue of mudstone and, like the mudstone, it weeps. Water beads from the pores in the same thin lines and runs down the flanks in grooves worn in the hide. Lichens have started to stripe the flow lines, six colors, so that an old Long Back is inked and streaked exactly like the boulders it sleeps beside. At night the flanks give off a low cyan pulse, in step with the glow in the peat. The animals seem to breathe with the ground.

Their voice is very low, beneath the tones of the lattice, and when several are near one another the ninety-second chord thickens. At a certain size the pools around them ring softly, as though the animal were rubbing the rim of the glass.

They are **late**. It is the most consistent thing said about them. You see a wake cross a channel, a long V of ripples spreading from nothing, and only afterward the back breaks the surface, moving on. Or you see the back, and the wake reaches the bank a full minute after the animal has sunk out of sight. They live at a slightly different hour from the rest of the fen, and the fen keeps their time for them.

They give birth in the oxbow lakes, in the still bends where the water has forgotten it was ever a river. A calf is born with a bare back, gray and smooth and wet, the only unplanted thing in the basin. For its first season the mother swims so that her flank presses against it. The moss creeps across, seedlings take hold along the calf's spine, and after a year it wears its own garden. Nobody who has watched a calf being planted has been able to describe it without lowering their voice.

## The Rehearsed

The tall, long-legged shapes that the mist makes, and the curve of a huge ribcage, are not residents. But the Long Backs treat them as though they were. An adult will sometimes stop in the shallows when a mist-shape forms and stay very still, its head raised above the waterline, until the shape thins and the mist becomes mist again. Sometimes one will swim slowly along the length of a shape, from where the feet would be to where the shoulder is, as though measuring.

It is not known whether the shapes are what the Long Backs were, what they will be, or something that was here before either and that they are keeping company. The water remembers, and the Long Backs carry the water.

## The Stayers

A few people live here. They did not come to stay; that is how they came to.

There are not many, no more than a handful across the whole basin, and they do not meet often. They arrived carrying edges: knives, buckles, needles, a tin cup. The fen dissolved these one by one over the seasons, rust first and then nothing, and the people learned to live without. They eat sedge-root and the sweet flesh of horsetail shoots, and drink from certain pitcher plants after the light has settled. They sleep in hollow alder logs lined with moss. They do not build. They have grown quiet in the way the herons are quiet, and they hold their heads at the same slight tilt.

They do not keep records, because a record has edges. They keep a **route**. Each of them walks a circuit of springs, tasting each, so that they know what the water at the northern rim says today and what it said the last time. This is their ledger, and it lives in the legs. The walk is longer in one direction than the other, and they take it in the direction that is shorter.

Each has lost something. The fourth rule is kept, and they pay it in small, unaccountable ways: a mother's name, the street they grew up on, the sound of a particular instrument. They know the shape of what is missing. They can point to the place in themselves where it was. This does not seem to trouble them, or it troubles them the way a healed break troubles a bone in cold weather. Ask one what it was and the answer is a slow smile and a glance toward the water.

Their speech has begun to lose its past tense. They say *it is raining* about rain that fell last week, and *it is raining* about rain to come. Their footprints, when the peat takes them, sometimes fill before they have walked on.

## What Lives Beyond the Rising Rain

Nobody has come back to say.

But the Long Backs are known to swim toward the columns of rising drops, and the oldest do not turn away. Their backs are thick with growth, their seedlings tall and gnarled, and they enter the mist slowly with the rain lifting around them, until the green shoulders thin and vanish. The herons stand facing that direction. The leaves turn toward it. The chord deepens and the water there is said to be clear as air.

Whether anything lives at the source, or whether the source is itself the resident, the lattice seen from above like a city without walls, is not told. The people who stay do not speculate. They say only that the old ones go to be entered, and that the ledger is balanced.

## Dusk

The frogs begin. The wave runs across the fen in cyan flicker and then sound. The swifts stitch the mist. The herons stand at their slight angle. Somewhere in the flooded trees a long low back breaks the surface and the wake reaches the bank late.

Then, for three or four seconds, everything stops. The stayers stop on their circuits with one foot lifted. The Long Backs hang in the channels with their flanks pulsing. The pitchers hold their small lights still.

If you are quiet, and have stayed long enough that your senses no longer quarrel, you can hear what they are all listening to.
