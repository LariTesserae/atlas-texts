# Who Lives in the Loud

## The People, Seen from Outside

You would know them first by their chests. The people of Ouren Tal are built broad through the ribs, deep-barreled, with a long collarbone and a thick throat, because they hum from birth and the body has given way to it. Their bones are dense. Their skin is the color of the meadow, brass in the lighter families and plum in the darker, and it carries a faint sheen from the oil they rub into it against the dry, thin air. Their eyes are large and dark and ringed with amber at the rim, an inheritance from ten thousand generations of dim honey-light.

Look at their foreheads. Every adult has a small flat callus at the center of the brow, pale and smooth as a thumbprint in wax. They call it *the speaking-place*. On loud days, and every day is loud, a person leans in, presses brow to brow, and speaks. The words travel through the skull. Nothing is overheard. A conversation in the open meadow is a private thing, two heads bent together like people sharing a flame, and the whole vast roar goes on around them without touching what is said.

They are not a quiet-voiced people even when they lean in. They speak in low, full tones that seem to come from the floor of the chest, and they finish sentences on a falling pitch, the way a struck string settles. Strangers say the people sound like they are always about to sing. This is because they always are.

## Names Are Notes

A child is not named at birth. A child is *heard*.

In the first hours, a Keeper comes to the bedside with a tuning-rod and waits for the infant to cry. The cry is struck against the rod, and the pitch it lands nearest becomes the child's ground-note. The name is built on it: a syllable, a family chord, and the note. A woman may be *Sesh-of-the-Third-Terrace, low D*. To her family she is only the D. They call her by humming it, two beats, a little downward slide. Across a roaring plaza, a mother can call her child home with a pitch and no shouting, and the child, three terraces down and deep in play, will lift its head.

So the people learn early to hear one another the way other peoples learn faces. A stranger's arrival is *tasted* in the chord of a room. A marriage is said to be sound when the two notes make an interval that neither one alone would find. An old couple's voices, decades in, settle into one another until you cannot hear where one ends. The people find this beautiful and slightly frightening.

## The Rule of Silence

Silence, to them, is not peace. It is the thing that comes before falling.

A silent person is therefore someone who requires attention. A man who stops humming at work is asked, gently, what is wrong. A child who goes still in a doorway is picked up at once. The very old, when they begin to fade, are placed in rooms with the best resonance in the house and sung around the clock by their descendants in shifts, so that they never lose the floor beneath them. Death is not a going quiet. The family keeps the dead one's ground-note alive for nine days, humming it in relay, and on the tenth day the note is sounded once, all together, as loud as the household can manage, and then released.

Some say it is folded into the beams. Some say it goes down into the mat and is sung there. The Keepers, asked, say only that they have never once heard a flat note in a house where the nine days were kept properly.

The worst thing you can be called is *dead-air*. It does not mean stupid or cruel. It means someone who takes and does not sound: who watches, holds back, brings the hush of a held breath into a room. Spies are dead-air. Liars are dead-air. The people are not naive about this. They know that the loud can lie too. But a liar's voice, they say, always carries a crack, a place where the note fails to hold, and a trained ear finds it.

## The Keepers

Every spire has one, every terrace has several, and Ouren itself has a college of them, three hundred strong, who walk the hanging city day and night with long rods of pale reed-wood held to their ears.

A Keeper is not a builder. This is the thing they say first. They do not know how the fingernail-machines were made, and they do not know how to make more. What they know is how to *listen*: how to walk an arch and hear that the blue-line beneath it is a hair sharp, how to put a palm on a wall and feel the chord thinning like breath on a cold morning. When they find it, they do what small things they can. They retune a cluster by hand with a rod. They coax a fading machine with a second, borrowed tone. They sing into the wood.

They are grave people, thin-lipped, with the flat tired eyes of those who listen too closely for too long. Many go partly deaf in middle life, and then they learn to hear through the hands and feet, and the best of them end their careers reading a city through the soles.

Among themselves they whisper a worry they do not say to the terraces. The great note is *drifting*. Not much: a hundredth of a step across three hundred years, by the oldest records, and the records are unreliable. But it is drifting in one direction. And a few of the old ones have begun to say, quietly, that the machines are not the source of the tone at all. Perhaps they are answering. Perhaps something under the mat, older than the towers, is holding a note, and the small singing things in the beams are only tuned to it, like reeds beside a struck bell. If so, the Keepers do not tend a machine. They tend an agreement. And no one alive knows who signed it.

## Water

The people are the only creatures on Ouren Tal who can drink it, and they treat this as a kind of guilt.

Everyone carries a flask, oil-wax sealed, worn under the arm or against the belly where the body's warmth keeps the seal from cracking. Water is rationed in small measures, drawn from deep cisterns in the cities, ferried out under guard to the lone spires. It is never spilled. A spilled cup is a small emergency: it is wiped up at once, and the cloth is burned. To let water fall on the meadow is to hurt it, and the people say they can *hear* the hurt, a thin whine in the drum-moss, spreading.

On the sour days, when the frost comes down and the whole plateau flinches, the people seal their windows with oil-cloth and sit close together in the inner rooms. They tell stories. They eat dried grazer-meat and hard seed-cake. Children are taught that the frost is a visitor who must not be looked at, and children, being children, peek. Every adult remembers a first glimpse of the white rime falling in the black noon, the meadow shivering below it, and the terrible beauty of it, and the thought that arrives with it: *we are made of the very thing that poisons the world we live in.*

Weddings therefore end with a mouthful of water. The couple share it from a single cup, in silence, the only sanctioned silence in their lives. It is a promise: *I will carry with you the thing that could hurt us both.* Then the whole hall roars.

Tears are a peculiar problem. In the open meadow a weeper covers their face with the sleeve, or turns from the ground. The people say a person may grieve anywhere, but only a fool weeps on the moss.

## The Drovers and the Stagger

Between the spires the plateau is wide and mostly empty, and those who cross it are a separate kind of people. They are called *drovers*, though many of them herd nothing but their own errands. They walk in loose caravans of six or twelve, on foot, in long dark coats stitched from grazer-hide.

They walk the *stagger*. This is the first thing a drover-child learns: never step on the beat. The drum-moss booms with each footfall, and the hollow-hunters beneath feel the boom and rise, but they follow rhythm, and a walker who breaks rhythm on purpose, skipping, dragging, doubling, confuses them. So the caravans move across the open in a strange lurching syncopation, each walker's steps deliberately at odds with the next, until the whole line sounds from below like a rain of random blows. Drover-children play a game with it, hopping between spires of drum-moss to a chant of broken counts. Adults watch, approving. It is not a game, and everyone knows it.

Drovers speak less than city folk. They have learned that in the open the noise is not a thing to add to but to listen inside. Some of them say they can tell the season, the weather, the health of a swell, and the mood of a herd from the sound of the mat underfoot. They are the only people who talk about the meadow as though it were someone.

## The Gray Scars

When a swell breaks open a pocket of old stone, the people go. They go in numbers, in long silent processions, silent in the sense that they cannot make themselves less loud but try, walking barefoot, humming low. The scar lies on the meadow like a lifeless eye, gray rock, dry, motionless. It has no sound. To a person born to the plateau, standing on it is like stepping into a held breath.

They lay their palms on the stone. They stay until it hurts, some minutes, some an hour. Nobody speaks. And then, with visible relief, they leave, and the roar comes up around them like warm water.

Some do not come back the same. There are stories: the woman who stood on a scar for a whole night and afterward could hear nothing else, not a word, not a reed-organ, only a faint rising tone from beneath her feet that would not fade; the boy who walked out onto the gray and returned mute and cheerful, and hummed all the rest of his life a note no one could identify, which the Keepers wrote down and could not find on any rod. The Keepers watch these people closely. They do not say why.

## Others

The people do not think they are alone. It is not only the grazers and the hunters they mean.

Every Elder is treated with courtesy. Nobody climbs one, cuts one, or camps in the shade of one after dark. A traveler passing beneath a crown will pause and hum a greeting, low, and wait. Sometimes the Choir-Birds fall silent for a moment before they answer. Sometimes the whole crown seems to lean.

The people say the Elders are not gods. They say it firmly. They say they are only very old neighbors, and that it is good manners to be polite to neighbors who were here before your grandmother's grandmother learned to speak. They say the Elders keep time.

Ask what time, and the answer is a shrug, a hum, a hand laid flat on the ground.

## The Whole of It

This, then, is who lives on the plateau of Ouren Tal: a people who have made noise their faith and are only half certain who is listening. They are broad-chested, careful, and sound-minded. They sing to their children and to their dying, hold their water like a knife, walk crooked to fool the dark, and keep the great unwritten note alive in the beams over their heads.

They are afraid of exactly one thing, and it is not the frost, or the hunters, or the vast black sky. It is the moment, which they think will come, when the long humming under all the world finds it has no reason to continue, and every terrace, every bridge of tone, every drifting amber tide of light hesitates, and in that hesitation someone, somewhere, for the first time in ten thousand million years, hears the quiet.

Until then they go on. They lean brow to brow and speak into the bone. They hum the day's last note at the doors of their houses, and the meadow, far below in the honey-colored dark, hums back.
