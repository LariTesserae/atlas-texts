# Who Lives on the Shelf

## The gum-masons

They are the reason the shelf has a town, and it is easy to look straight past them, because at first they seem to be part of the wall.

Each is about the length of a thumb, narrow at the waist and broad at the shoulder. The fur is short and rust-gold, dusted grey with ash across the back, so that a file of them moving along a spire looks like the wall itself shivering. Four wings lie folded flat along the back, thin as the skin on cooled milk, and they stay folded. The masons walk. Their feet are wide, soft, and faintly tacky, and they grip the resin as easily as a gecko grips stone, so they cross ceilings and overhangs without noticing that these are different from floors.

The head is what you remember. It is broad and blunt, with two deep scent-pits where a wasp would have eyes, and the real eyes are small, black, and bright as beads of pitch, set so far to the sides that a mason sees a wide dim smear of the world and very little of what is directly ahead. It does not need to. It reads the world with its mouth and antennae. The mandibles are flat and spoon-shaped, made for kneading, not for cutting. A mason is always faintly chewing, even when it is resting, working a crumb of ash into a bead of amber with a slow lateral motion, like someone turning a worry stone in the mouth.

They are warm. This matters more than anything else about them. A mason's body runs hot, a little above the temperature of the ground, and a file of them along a wall leaves a strip of softened resin behind it. The masons do not use the resin so much as steep it. The mortar is kept workable by the warmth of the builders, and the town is held together by a chemistry that only works while thousands of small furnaces keep walking through it.

## How they are arranged

There is no ruler that you could point to. There are only tasks, and a mason drifts among them as it ages.

The youngest are **carriers**. They go out along the fissures at dawn, when the sap is low and the resin has crusted overnight into hard beads at the lips of the cracks, and they haul the beads back one at a time, gripped in the mandibles, held out in front like a person carrying a full bowl.

Older ones are **ash-bringers**. They travel to the hottest vents, where the ground breathes out powder as fine as flour, and they come back grey to the eyes.

Older still are **chewers**, who sit in the resin bowls of the plazas and do nothing but blend. Their mouths are the only mill the town has. Every wall was, at one point, in a mason's jaw.

Last are **layers**. They are the fewest, the slowest, and the most heavily furred with old ash. They climb to the top of a spire on a high-sap afternoon and set down a single band of mortar with an unhurried, smoothing drag of the whole body. A layer's work is a ring one finger wide. It may take a day. The ring will outlast every mason that watched it go down.

Nobody gives orders. What passes between them is smell. Each mason leaves a faint terpene mark where it walks, and other masons read it with their feet and antennae as they pass, so the walls are covered with a soft, layered writing that a passerby can taste: *load ahead, take the left ramp, this seam is wet, this one is done.* It fades in a day and is rewritten. The town is not silent because the masons are quiet. It is silent because the masons never say anything aloud.

## The sleepers

Near the roots of the oldest spires, where the resin is still clear amber, there are shapes inside the walls.

They are masons, whole and folded, wings pressed flat, fur still visible as a faint rust-colored haze. When a mason dies, or perhaps only when it stops, the others do not carry it away. They set it into the wall and cover it. Over one generation the resin clears around the body, and it hangs there in the honey-colored light like a seed in fruit. A spire's base holds dozens, and the largest of the old towers holds hundreds, stacked in the thickness of the wall, still and complete, facing outward.

The living do not avoid them. A file of workers will climb over the amber that holds their great-great-grandmothers with no change of pace, and on hot afternoons the warmth of the passing bodies makes the resin around the sleepers shine a little brighter, so that for a moment the shapes inside seem to shift. They do not. But the masons are known to pause there, antennae lowered, for a length of time that has no obvious work in it.

## The wings

The masons' wings are used once.

When a new crack opens in the rock, with the sound of a dropped plate, every mason on the shelf goes still. Then, in the space of a few breaths, a slice of the town's population, mostly young, unfolds its wings. This is the only loud moment on the shelf: a low, dry, papery rush like a great many pages turning. They fly in a short cloud, never higher than the canopy, never far, and they settle along the new fracture in a shimmering line. Then they begin.

The wings do not last. Within a day they go brittle, and within two they are shed and chewed up with the first mortar. This is why the newest spires are the palest and most translucent. They are laminated with the thin film of wings, and light passes through them almost unchanged. As the spire ages, the wing-film yellows, and the ash thickens the walls, and the tower slowly turns from glass to cream.

A mason who has flown will never fly again. It will spend the rest of its life on the wall it was born to found.

## The others who live among them

The masons share the shelf with animals that have learned to treat the town as a landscape.

The **lantern moths** roost in the leaves above the spires, and their wing-dust sifts down into the resin bowls, where the chewers work it into the mortar; that is why the walls glow faintly green in the first hour of night. The moths are not tended. They are simply not chased away.

The **glass geckos** sun on the trunks and take the occasional mason-file for a rope of ants, and are ignored by the file, whose bitter terpene taste is well known to every gecko that ever tried one.

The **felt-footed grazers** move through the moss with round, soundless patience, and they are the only creatures that use the plazas as plazas, lowering their small faces to the sticky bowls to lick the mineral film. Masons walk over their backs without breaking stride. A grazer will sometimes arrive at a junction with a whole file riding its fur like a moving bridge.

The **dark birds** hunt the moths overhead, click once, and go on. They do not touch the masons. A bird that has tasted one does not forget it.

## What they do not do

They do not approach the seep.

In the lowest corner of the shelf, where the black glass wall lets down its thread of clear water, the masons' whole pattern of trails bends. The lane approaches, curves, and never arrives. The mortar-marks near it are old and faint; no one writes there anymore. No spire stands within a body-length. It is not fear, exactly. A mason walking that way does not hurry or startle. It simply arrives at a place in the path where something in the smell changes, and the next step goes elsewhere.

Once in a while a young one, newly out of the carrier gangs, leaves its file and walks alone toward that corner. It goes slowly, antennae low, past the last old mark. It stops at the ring where the moss has drawn back. It stands there for a long time, perhaps through a whole afternoon, with the cold thin light of the water on its face and its jaws for once not chewing. Then it turns around, finds the trail, and goes back to work.

No mason has ever been seen to explain this to another. The town has been built for as long as the rock has been cool, one ring at a time, and its shape is the shape of everything around the place the masons do not go.
