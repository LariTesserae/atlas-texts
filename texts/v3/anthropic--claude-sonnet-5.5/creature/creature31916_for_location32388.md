# Who Keeps the Shelf

Most of what lives here you will hear before you see, and most of what you see will be small. The Shelf is a place of tenants. It has few lords and no crowds, only a great many creatures each holding one warm, wet room of the world and holding it faithfully.

## The Pool-Sitters

Every weeping fan ends in a pool, and every pool has a resident.

The largest are the **bell-frogs**, olive-brown and heavy as a hound, with throat-sacs like wet leather bladders. They sit half-sunk in water that would scald a hand, eyes closed, nostrils and the humps of their brows just above the surface. The bass thrum you feel in your sternum before dawn is theirs. Old bell-frogs grow orange algae on their backs, so that a pool's resident looks like a stone that the weeping rock has stained. They do not seem to hunt. Things come to the pool's edge, and sometimes fewer things leave. Residents appear to be inherited: a young frog takes the pool when the old one goes still, and no one has seen the handover, only the pool with one frog in it and then, after a season, a smaller one in the same place.

Beside them, in the hottest water, lie the **oar-salamanders**, dark-backed and pale-bellied, as long as their name and about as quick. They move at the speed of thought in a sleeping person. What matters about them is the pause. When the basin holds its breath, the oar-salamanders stop a fraction before everything else does, and they resume a fraction after. Anyone who watches long enough starts to believe they hear it coming.

In the soil under the moss, the blunt-headed caecilians swim their pale channels. In the lakes, the fish rise to the warm surface at dusk and hang there with their mouths at the air, as though listening.

## The Cup-Mothers

Thirty meters up, in the bromeliad cups, the smallest frogs raise their young.

The **cup-mothers** are no bigger than a thumbnail, rust-red with a blue thread down each flank. A mother lays her eggs in a leaf axil, waits for them to hatch, and then carries each tadpole up the trunk on her back, one at a time, to a cup of clean water high in the canopy. Then she keeps coming back. Every few days she climbs to each cup she has stocked, and in each she lays a single unfertilized egg as food, one meal for one child, and this continues for weeks. The tadpoles know her. When her weight touches the rim of the cup they rise to the surface all at once and hang there, trembling, and the water in the cup stills to a fine vibration. They are listening for her, and then they eat.

Some cups have been tended by the same line of mothers for so long that the epiphyte has grown around the arrangement. It has shaped its leaves into a funnel that guides the climbing mother's feet to the rim. The tree and the frog have worked out a household between them, and neither would recognize it as unusual.

## The Mist-Swimmers

At dusk the fog fills with translucent thumb-length creatures rising from the soil and the reed beds in shoals of thousands. They feed on condensed droplets and on the spores drifting in them, and they light green wherever the air moves against them. Near each pylon, where the mist will not condense, the shoals crowd at the edge of the clear zone and glow in a ring. From a ridge at nightfall, the pylons across the Shelf stand each in a wreath of green fire, and the wreaths flicker together in the slow cycle of the hum.

## The Row-Sitters

Lizards line the pylons in rows, heads down and toes spread on the eggshell ceramic. Their bodies are flat and banded in cream and slate. The rock is warm everywhere, so they are not there for warmth. They are there for the hum. They drowse with their jaws against the spire, and when the low note swells they tighten their grip in unison, so that a whole pylon seems to flex.

Each spire has one lizard above the dead line of the vines, alone in the bare white band at the very top. It is always there. Nobody has seen a replacement climb or an old one fall, only a spire that is never without its keeper.

Heavy fruit-eaters with bare blue faces roost on the pylons at night and drop seeds as they sleep. The plants that follow the veins are said to get their first foothold this way.

## The People

Very few people come. Those who do come up for the dry season, from the villages under the cloud floor, and they do not stay.

The woman who keeps the fern-thatched hut is small and sinewy, with hands that move constantly, because in the roar of the Shelf you talk with them. She brings eleven shaggy hill goats, which shed their winter coats in the first week and grow a sleek wet-weather hair by the third. She does not trust her watch. Clocks here lose minutes and give them back, so she counts days in knots on a cord, one per dawn chorus, and leaves when the seasonal cycle of the hum turns over, whatever her knots say. "It lets you go," she signs, "but it likes you more every day you're here."

She has never seen who replaces the top stones on the cairns. The cairns along the pass never grow taller than a hip, though every traveler leaving the Shelf adds a stone, and something must be taking stones away. She thinks it is the people who came before. She thinks it is the goats. She has left a stone herself, on the last cairn, and found it gone in the morning and the cairn unchanged.

## What Made the Spires

There are traces of others: the stair that goes nowhere, the ring of squared stones sunk in the reeds. They left no bones, no middens, no tools, and no writing. They left the pylons and a few gestures at the rock's edges, and then they were gone.

Those who have stayed long enough to think about it tend toward one idea, and it does not feel like a comfortable one. The spires may not be built. They may be *grown*, or placed, or simply allowed. The smoothness of them, the eggshell white, the warmth, the way vines refuse to climb past a certain height, all suggest something made by a body rather than by a hand.

## What Lies Under

The rock is warm to the hand everywhere, like a sleeping animal, and there is a reason the phrase comes so easily to visitors.

Sink your hand in a hot pool at the right place, over one of the pale translucent veins, and the water will sometimes tighten around your wrist. It does this slowly, once, and then relaxes. The pulse is far slower than a heartbeat, and it comes in a slow cycle overlaid on a slower one. The plants lean toward the veins because that is where something nearby is waiting. Compasses lean. Clocks lend their minutes and are repaid.

What lies under the Shelf is not a creature you could point at, any more than a body is a heart. It is as wide as the basin at least, and it has been here since before the valleys had their present shape. Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, move at the pace of the marsh edge creeping across a decade. It may be asleep. It may be brooding. The peaks around it are cupped like a nest, and the hills are domed like eggs. The pale veins run through the dark rock like something under a shell.

Nobody who has stayed long agrees on whether it is the mother or the egg.

The cup-mothers may hold the answer, or a piece of it. Think of the tadpoles in their high pools, carried up out of the cold and fed, one small offering at a time, by something that returns on a round. Think of the Shelf itself, a basin held high above the white floor of the world and kept warm, in which a great many small lives have been set down and fed. Think of the hum swelling low through every spire, and the moment the whole basin goes still to hear it.

The pause has a shape that the tadpoles know. It is the shape of a weight arriving at the rim.

## The Pause

Sit on the warm rock at dusk while the fog goes gold and the mist-swimmers rise in their green wreaths, and you will feel it, if you stay still. The frogs and birds and insects all stop together. The lizards tighten on the spires. The oar-salamanders go still a moment before the rest. In the silence the stone under your palms leans very slightly, as a mattress leans when someone sits down at the edge of it in the dark.

Then the chorus rises again, all at once, warm and loud and unafraid. And you find that you are part of it, that you held your breath with the rest, and that whatever came by has counted you among the ones it looked in on.
