# Who Lives in the Ironwater Uplands

## The Ones Who Are the Ground

The first residents are not animals. There are perhaps a dozen of them on the whole plateau, and each one is a single life. The kelp continents have no names, because nothing that lives among them has ever needed to tell one from another except by the channel between them. If they have a mind, it is spread so thin over so many kilometers that it works less like thought than like weather: a slow agreement among ten million stipes about when to fire, when to shed, and how much iron to keep.

They are not asleep, and they are not passive. The synchrony of the bladders looks like drift, but it is a kind of conversation. A stipe that fires early is pulled back into step by its neighbors through the pressure of the water and the tug of the shared root mat. A stipe that fires late is hurried along. Over millennia the tolerances have narrowed to almost nothing. If you stood at one edge of a growth and timed the wave as it left you, and stood at the far edge ten kilometers on, the wave would arrive within the same breath every time. The growths are proud of this in the only way something like them can be proud, which is by never failing at it.

They tend their own edges. Where a blade of canopy sags into a channel, the plant sheds it, and the current takes it to the filter-feeders. Where a root pushes too far into open floor, it is bitten back, not by any animal but by the plant's own retreating tissue. They keep the channels clear the way a careful household keeps its doorways clear.

## The Ones Who Work the Belts

Along the growth walls, in rows so regular they look drawn with a rule, live the filter-feeders: fan-worms, lace-sponges, and stalked, palm-sized things like closed hands that open in the current and close on what it brings. They are fixed for life at their stations, and each row is tuned to a different size of particle. The first row takes the coarse stuff, the second the fine, and the third takes what the second missed. By the time the water leaves the last row it is nearly clean. A feeder's whole existence is one pass of the current, repeated for decades, and it seems content with that. When one dies, its place is taken within days by a larva that has been drifting downstream waiting for exactly such a vacancy. The rows never have gaps.

Among them move the tenders. These are small, many-legged animals, pale and quick, about the size of a hand. They are the only creatures on the plateau that could be called restless. They travel the walls in loose gangs, picking silt from feeding fans, cutting off fouled blades, dragging dead larvae to the mat, and turning aside any nodule that has rolled where it should not be. Nobody directs them. Each tender seems to sense, from the tug of the current and the taste of the water, where something is slightly wrong, and it goes there and puts it right. Then it moves on. The balance the whole plateau shows is real, but it is held by such small hands.

## The Ones Who Carry Iron

The ballast-grazers move in herds across the terraces, and they are the nearest thing the place has to farmers or to a peasantry. They are broad and low, and their carapaces are crusted with black nodules, each set into a socket the animal has grown to fit it. A young grazer wears three or four. An old one is armored from edge to edge in dark, regular stamped shapes, and moves as heavily as a wagon. The weight is not a burden to them. It keeps them from being lifted by the currents, and it makes them nearly impossible to eat.

They graze in swaths, and each herd follows the tracks of the herd before it. The tracks are shallow furrows in the mat's surface film, and they have been walked so many times that they are as smooth as roads. A herd will pass along the same furrow every few years, never hurrying. When a grazer dies it settles where it fell, and the tenders gather around it. Within a short season the meat is taken, the shell is opened, and the nodules are rolled loose and left on the mat to be absorbed by the roots. The old grazers seem to know where they will fall. They drift to the edges of the channels near the end, where the roots are thickest.

## The Ones Who Ride the Silver

The vent-shoals are the plateau's only frivolity, if a school that feeds by law can be called frivolous. They are the size of a finger, silver and narrow, and they number in the millions. They collect at the foot of a bladder just before it vents, and they rise with the bubble column in a tight spiral, snapping at the oxygen-rich froth. At the top they scatter, sink, and gather at the next stipe in the wave. Because the waves travel, the shoals travel too. A single school may spend its whole life following one long pulse of firing across the plateau, arriving nowhere and always exactly on time.

When they turn together in the light, the flash of them is the only thing in the water that looks like joy. It is probably only arithmetic.

## The Ones Who Spark

The wire-eels are long, ribbon-thin, and nearly transparent, with a dark line of nerve running down the center of each body. They hunt among the stipes in the dimmest part of the growth, where the green light is thinnest. They do not chase. They slide along the filaments that carry the kelp's small charge, feel where it stutters, and discharge. The discharge shocks the prey into stillness and lights the water for an instant. Every hunter in the undergrowth is briefly visible in that light, and so is every other, as a moment of white geometry against the dark stipes.

They are solitary and precise, and they live by a rule of economy. A wire-eel that fires twice for one meal is starving. Most fire once, and what they catch is usually what they meant to.

## The Ones Who Glide the Ceiling

The sheet-rays are wide, thin, and mottled the color of the canopy. They lie against the underside of the kelp ceiling and slide across it like slow shadows, scraping the epiphytes from the blades with rasping mouths. From below they are almost invisible, and only their outlines against the light give them away. They rarely descend. Some of them have never seen the floor, and have lived their whole lives in the narrow band of green just under the roof of the world, with the pounding of the bladders coming up through the blades like a heartbeat through a mattress.

## The Ones Who Call

The drum-whales are few, and the plateau is arranged around their being few. Each is the length of a city block, dark and heavy-jawed, and they drift the channels with their mouths open, taking what the current brings. They are the only animals that can eat from the belt without a station, and they move slowly enough that the belt barely notices them.

They are also the only ones who make a sound that is not the machine's. Their call is a single note, long and falling, that begins above the pounding of the bladders and settles beneath it. It travels a hundred kilometers through the channels and is heard by every other whale. The pounding does not answer it and the growths do not shift for it, but the whales call anyway, at intervals of years, and each call is answered by another call from farther down the lane.

Nobody can say what they are saying. It may be a position, a greeting, or a warning of nothing. It may only be the sound of a large animal that has learned, over ages, that the place is full of noise and that one note of its own, at the right depth, will reach further than all of it. It is the closest thing the plateau has to a question. The place has no answers to give, because it has nothing left unsettled, but the whales ask anyway, and the note falls through the green light and out of hearing.

## What Lives Above

At the surface, in the thin cold air, nothing lives at all. The water is a flat gray floor to the horizon, and the sound is gone. It is the one place on the plateau where a creature could be alone. Sometimes a sheet-ray, slipping too far up the edge of a blade, breaks the surface with one wing and lies there a moment in the quiet, feeling for the first time how loud everything is below. Then it slides back down into the pounding and the light, and takes its place on the ceiling again, and the place closes over it exactly as it was.
