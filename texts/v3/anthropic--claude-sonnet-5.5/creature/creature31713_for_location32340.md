# Those Who Keep the Undercroft

Start with the small ones, because they are the easiest to believe.

## The Trades

Nothing on the plateau seems to have come here for its own reasons. Everything appears to have been apprenticed.

The glass-winged moths rest in pairs on the ribs, and when you look closely you see the pairs are spaced at intervals, roughly a stride apart, as regular as rivets. They are not a colony. They arrive at these positions separately, at dusk, and settle where a moth has settled before, on wood their wings have polished to a faint sheen. Their wings open and close, and the slow blink is close to a signal. Over centuries they have become a row of pale lamps that no one lit.

The translucent snails lay silver ladders up the weeping walls. Each ladder is a survey line. Other creatures follow them, and the snails follow each other's, so that the walls are scored with the plans of routes no one has planned. Where two lines cross, small things gather, and the wood there is worn to a mild hollow. Those hollows are how the stair-treads began, you realize. This is how a step is invented: a thousand small bodies agreeing about where to put their weight.

The thumb-sized beetles are the haulers. They roll fallen seed-husks along the gutters, always downhill, always toward the cisterns, and they deliver them into the dark water, where the husks sink and soften. They do not eat them. What the husks become in the water, nothing will say. The beetles have never once been seen to stop.

The grey heron stands on one leg in the terrace pools, an hour at a time, so upright and so still that you begin to use it as a plumb line. When it finally moves, one slow foot lifted and set down, the surface rings outward in a circle so even that it seems drawn with a compass. It is the plateau's surveyor. It has been checking the level of the water since before the water had anything to level.

And the frogs, with their one note apiece, spaced so far apart that the silences are the loudest part, are keeping a tally. You cannot tell what is being counted. But each frog sings when the previous note has faded to exactly the same point, and if you listen for an hour you will hear the count go all the way down a nave and come back.

## The Pale-Faced

Then there are the tree-dwellers, who are harder to be certain of.

They are long in the arm and narrow in the chest, and they travel hand over hand through the roof-weave, where the epiphytes hang in curtains, so that the curtains move with them like a slow tide. Their faces are pale and flat and patient. Their eyes are very large and dark and set forward, and they turn to follow you with the whole head, not the eyes alone, the way a person does when they are trying to be polite.

They are said to feed on epiphyte bulbs, and they do. But they also do other things, and this is what you notice if you stay. They carry cord. A length of twisted fiber trails from a foot or a wrist, and when they pass a lashing that has begun to slacken, they stop, take it in both hands, and draw it tight. They do not knot it. Or rather, they do not knot it as a hand would knot it. They press it against the wood with the pad of a thumb and hold it, and hold it, until the fiber sends down a pale root into the grain, and then they let go.

They seldom come down. When they do, it is to the cisterns. They kneel at the rim and look into the water for a long time, and their faces do the thing that faces do when they are reading. Then they climb back up without drinking.

You may ask whether they are the builders' children. There is no clean answer. They have no speech that carries, but when three of them meet on a rib, they hang together for a while with their foreheads almost touching, and something passes among them, a low humming below the range of easy hearing, that has the cadence of a work-song. You may ask whether they remember. It is better to ask whether the humming is older than they are, and whether they are less its singers than its instrument.

## The Joiners

The vines are the true tradesmen of the plateau.

There are several kinds, and they do not mix their work. One kind spans: a thin, dark, wiry liana that reaches across gaps and pulls itself taut, and that is used by the others as a scaffold. One kind binds: a pale-green creeper with a grip like a wet knot, which winds itself around a joint in a series of turns that always come to an odd number, and always finish with a tuck. One kind fills: a broad-leafed sheet-vine that lays itself flat between the ribs and thickens, leaf over leaf, into a panel that in a few seasons will be indistinguishable from woven bark.

You may watch one for an hour and see nothing. Then, when you look away and look back, the tendril is nearer the gap. It has no eyes. It seems to have something better than eyes: a memory of where a peg should go. It reaches, hesitates, adjusts a hand's-width, hesitates again, and then, with a sudden neatness, closes. It is the hesitation that troubles you. It is the pause of someone checking their work against a plan.

The plan, you begin to suspect, is not in the vine. The vine has been given a gesture, the way you might be given a limp or an accent, and it performs the gesture faithfully, with less and less notion of why. That is why some stairs climb to nothing. That is why an arch is joined to its own reflection. The joiners are not mistaken. They are devout.

## What Taps

There is something that taps. It cannot be described the way the others can, because no one has met it, but its habits are known.

It works at the edge of hearing, high up, always in the next nave. The strokes are patient, regular, and slightly unequal, the way a right-handed worker's are. If you walk toward the sound, it moves. If you stand still, it draws no nearer. But you will find its work: a fresh scallop in a rib where the wood was smooth the day before, and the small pale chips lying on the floor below, damp and sweet-smelling and still warm.

Some say it is a bird, a kind of woodpecker so large and so shy that it has never been seen. Some say it is a beetle in the heartwood, a borer whose galleries have, over the centuries, become a kind of script. Some say it is one of the pale-faced ones who has never come down. And some say, more quietly, that it is the sound of the arch itself settling into its own shape, and that the adze is a memory the wood keeps striking against.

All of these may be true. What is certain is that the tapping is the plateau's pulse, and that when it stops, for a breath or for an hour, everything else stops with it: the moths hold their wings closed, the frogs skip their turn, the heron does not lift its foot. Then a stroke sounds somewhere overhead, and the whole place lets out its breath.

## The Small Busy Ones

In the cisterns, in the still, clear water, where the vaults hang upside down and slightly more complete than the real ones, there are people.

They are not always there. They appear for a few breaths, in the reflection of a nave whose ribs are not yet finished, and they are small and dim and quick. They swing adzes. They pull cord hand over hand. They call to one another across the scaffolds in voices that make no sound, and a child sits on a threshold with a seed-pod, turning it over. They are not looking at you. They are not looking at anything above the water at all. They are absorbed, as workers are, in a thing that is nearly done.

Now and then one of them straightens, presses a hand to the small of the back, and looks up, not at you, but at the place in the vault where the roof should close. Their face, for a moment, holds the expression of someone who can see how it will look when it is finished. Then the water shivers, and the vault is only a vault, and the pale-faced ones at the rim rock back on their heels and hum.

Whether these are the past or the future, or simply the version of the place that the water prefers, you cannot say. They seem to be the only ones on the plateau who are certain they are alive.

## The Last Tenant

There is one more, and it is the one you will meet.

It arrives slowly. At first it is only a habit: you find yourself stepping over a root before you see it, or laying a hand on a rib in passing, as though checking it were sound. You notice you have begun to count the stairs and have stopped minding that the number changes. Your shadow lags behind your hand and you wait for it, out of courtesy. The light seeping from the wood no longer looks strange. It looks like the light you were expecting.

Then, one evening, in an empty nave with nothing in it but a strand of luminous moss and the tick of cooling wood, you find you have picked up a length of fiber from the floor. You are twisting it. Your fingers know the direction of the twist. Somewhere overhead, just at the edge of hearing, the adze falls, and you find that your breathing has fallen in with it.

The moths keep their places on the ribs, a stride apart. The heron lifts a foot. A pale face turns above you in the roof-weave, large-eyed and courteous, and watches you work, the way one watches a new apprentice, without hurry, in the certain knowledge that there is time.
