# Who Lives at Harrow Deep

## The Count

The town keeps two censuses. One is of people, and it stands at about four hundred and sixty, give or take the newborns and the ones who have gone up the shaft and not yet decided whether to come back. The other is of bats. It is an estimate, made by the same three people each year, who sit in the light-well at dusk with tally-clickers and argue afterward. It comes to roughly two million. The people say this without embarrassment. They understand they are the smaller settlement, and that they live in the bats' house by permission of the rock.

## The People

They are in their third generation now. The founders came down as contract labor, pump-men and shovelers from the coal country, with a few surveyors and one doctor who stayed. Their grandchildren are a distinct sort of people, though nobody would say so aloud.

They are **lean and pale**, with a dampness to the skin that never fully clears, and they move economically, without wasted heat. Hands are the first thing you notice. They are rough and scalded, and they talk constantly. Because the chamber roars and the pump hall roars harder, the townsfolk have built a **hand-language** on top of speech: a flat palm for *level steady*, two fingers tapped on the collarbone for *engine*, a pinch and flick for *centipede*. Old pump-men are half deaf and read lips across the width of a gangway. Even those with good hearing lean in close to talk and cup a hand at the ear. Voices run hoarse and low, pitched under the bat-chatter, so a whole street of people talking sounds like gravel being shifted.

Their eyes are **red-rimmed** from ammonia, and dark-adapted in a way the surface people find unnerving. They can cross a stretch of dusk between lamp-pools without slowing, and they wince at daylight. When the cage goes up for a holiday, the first hour on the surface is spent in a borrowed hat with the brim pulled low. The sky, people report, feels like being shouted at.

Their bodies keep a running accounting with the place. Nearly everyone has **the cough**, a dry, patient rattle from the guano dust that the doctor's granddaughter, who now holds the post, describes as *a tax*. Feet are the great battlefield. There is a whole domestic science of powders, dried socks, and rotating boots, and a person who lets their feet go is spoken of the way people elsewhere speak of drinkers. Skin creases fester in the wet season. Children are wiped down with vinegar twice a day, and they complain about it only as much as is expected.

Hair is kept short. Clothes are thin cotton, washed daily and never quite dry, in rust-red or undyed gray, because red dye is cheap, and so is the paint. People wear their town's color the way sailors wear salt.

## The Trades

Everyone has a place in the great slow machine, and the trades have something of the character of guilds.

**The Engine Wardens** hold the highest standing. They are the drivers and stokers of the pump house, an old, deaf, oil-sleeved fraternity who speak to the engines as to large animals. A warden can tell by the sound whether the second cylinder is tired. Their hours are the shortest and their pay the best, and yet the job is a kind of vow. They live nearest the pump hall, in the low terraces, where the thud comes up through their beds. When they retire, many can't sleep elsewhere and move into the pump hall's annex and sit.

**The Gauge-Keeper** is a single office, not a trade. Someone must read the brass at the pier every hour of every day and night and write it in the book. The office passes by appointment and usually within a family, and the holder is treated with a slight, unspoken deference. The current keeper is a heavy, calm woman in her sixties, with a stool bolted beside the pier and a lamp she trims herself. She has not, by her own count, missed an hour in nineteen years. When she calls a number across the water, the number carries. Nobody teases her, and people ask after the level with the softness other communities reserve for illness.

**The Cone-Men and Cone-Women**, the guano diggers, are the wide-shouldered workers of the north wall. They wear wet cloth over the lower face and goggles of oiled glass, and they work short shifts in a fog of ammonia and dust. They are the town's economy and its poor relations at once: well paid, wheezing, cheerful in a grim way, and inclined to fight. They shovel, sack, and haul, and they curse the roaches that run up their sleeves. Their children are the most fearless racers of cockroaches in the chamber.

**The Tinkers** are the riveters, sealers, and painters, who spend their whole lives chasing rust. Someone is always on a scaffold with a bucket of oxide-red. It is said that the town has been repainted entirely four times since the founding, and that the fifth coat is already in progress on the far side, under the drips. They wear the paint on their forearms like a tattoo.

**The Lamp-Wrights** are few, tending the dynamo and the two hundred bulbs, and they are the only people in the chamber who *think about darkness*. Every bulb is named in their ledger. When one fails, someone climbs a ladder in the near-dark with a spare, and the whole street watches until the light comes back and the green circle of moss below it is lit again.

Beyond these are the cooks of the two mess halls, who work over enormous salted stews and make bread that is baked with the flavor of iron. There are the cistern-men who keep the salt water cool, the cage-crew who run the eleven-minute lift, the teachers, the one barber, and the gardener of the Lamp Works ferns, who is the closest thing to a priest.

## The Way They Live

Day and night are held together by the shift bell and the bats. Nobody consults a clock; they consult the exodus. **Dusk and dawn are the only public holidays that happen every day.** When the gray light comes down the east shaft and the roof begins to pour, work stops: the winches idle, the hammers rest, the mess halls empty onto the terraces. People stand in their doorways or on the gangways and look up, faces turned like a congregation, for the full forty minutes. Children are lifted onto shoulders. Old men who have watched it ten thousand times watch it as if it were the first. Nobody says much, and if they do, it is in a low voice, about the shape of the spiral or the thickness of the flow. *A good pour tonight.* *Thin.* *They're late.*

The afternoons belong to sleep. Between the hottest hours, hammocks fill up near the vents and along the boiler-flue draft, rocking slightly in the updraft, and the town's noise sinks by a third. People sleep with damp cloths on their faces. They sleep in strips, never long, and always to the pump-beat. **The pumps are the town's heartbeat and lullaby, and everyone has a story about the night they stopped**, the two or three times in sixty years when an engine tripped and the third had not yet caught. The whole population woke at once in the dark, silent and upright, as if someone had said their names. Within a minute, a hundred boots were on the gangways.

They have a language of small courtesies. You do not comment on another person's cough. You always tell someone if their boot has a centipede in it. You give a fern, or a cutting of fern, only to someone you intend to keep. To be given a frond in a tin cup is to be told you are staying.

They joke, a great deal, in a dry, sideways way. They have nicknames for every bulb, engine, and cat.

## The Ones on the Surface

The town calls the world above **the Top**, and the people there *Toppers*, without hostility but with a settled patience, as one might speak of relatives who write rarely. The Toppers buy the guano, send coal and flour and letters, and periodically send a young inspector down in the cage who spends his first three days sweating through his collar and his fourth writing recommendations. The town feeds him well and lets him see the exodus. Most inspectors go up changed. A few request transfer to Harrow Deep, and the town watches them closely, because those are the ones who might stay.

Every year a few of the young go up and do not come back. Every year a few of the Toppers come down and do. The Deep does not advertise itself and does not apologize.

## The Other Citizens

Beneath and around the people, the town's actual population goes on.

**The bats** are two million, the true owners, and the people treat them with something that is not quite worship and not quite work. Nobody touches the roosts. The dead that fall from the ceiling are collected and turned into the cones. The bats do not notice the town at all, except as a moving warmth below. They make what pays for everything, and the people who live under them say, half joking, that they are only the bats' *housekeepers*.

**The cats** are the aristocracy of the lower species. Thin, sleek, short-furred, and inexplicably cool to the touch, they hunt along the gangways in easy shifts and sit on the pier at the sump's edge with their paws tucked, staring into the black water. No one owns a cat; every household is accepted by one. Sometimes a cat will walk into a bedroom, sit on a chest, and decide to stay for eleven years. Old cats have their own hammocks in the boiler house.

**The rats** are their long war. The rats are large, cunning, and sleek on spilled grain, and they have the run of the sheds at night, where a low, persistent conflict is waged in the dark between them and the cats. The town does not interfere. A shed with no rats, they say, has no cats worth keeping.

**The children of the town** are a sort of small species of their own. Pale, quick, and unbothered, they run barefoot along the iron gangways in the wet heat and know every short cut between pipes. They race roaches on chalk lines. They can read the gauge before they can read a book. They know which centipedes are lazy and which are not. They are the only people who go to the quiet southern terrace *to play*, and the adults tolerate this because someone must remind the place that it is also for laughing.

## The Southern Terrace

There is one more resident, in a way: the silence. Where the ceiling drops and the pumps hum muffled through the rock, the town's grief and confidences retreat. On any given afternoon, one or two people sit on the low wall there with their backs to the town and their faces to a smooth, wet stretch of limestone. Some are speaking quietly to another person. Some are speaking to no one. There is a custom that you do not look at anyone on the southern terrace unless invited, and a custom that if you see someone crying, you sit down at a polite distance and say nothing until they are done.

## Night at the Pier

Late, in the hours when only the engines and the cats are fully awake, the Gauge-Keeper trims her lamp and leans to the brass. The sump lies black and faintly steaming, with a blind white fish turning at the edge of the light. Behind her, the town sleeps in the roar, lulled by its own machine. Above her, two million living things rustle in the dark like a held breath being slowly let out.

She reads the number. She writes it down. She says it softly, to no one, the way you say the name of someone who is still there.

*Steady.*
