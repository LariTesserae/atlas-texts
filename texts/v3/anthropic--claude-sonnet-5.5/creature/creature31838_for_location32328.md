# Those Who Live on the Shelf

Three kinds of living things hold on here, if the word fits all of them. None of them makes anything, and none of them can be heard.

## The ones on the floor

Most of the shelf's life is in the warm shallows near the spring, where the water is barely cold. There the pool floors are pale and the crustaceans are paler. Each is the length of a fingernail, translucent as a drop of skim milk, with a gut that shows as a faint grey thread when it moves. There are thousands to a hand's width of floor, and you would not know it until one of them walks.

They walk on many small legs, slowly, dragging their mouthparts across the stone to scrape up the film of mineral and slime that coats every surface. That film is all they eat, and there is always more of it. They have no eyes worth the name, only two dark flecks that register the difference between bright and less bright, and here that difference hardly matters.

They are deaf, but "deaf" undersells it. The whole pool trembles with the chord, since the water is only a thin skin over stone that is itself part of the great instrument. The crustaceans feel it through every leg at once. They do not experience it as sound. It is more like the way a person feels the ground.

Because the chord never changes, the trembling is laid out across each pool in fixed lines of stillness and shaking, and the animals have sorted themselves along them. In the quiet bands they cluster thick and shoulder to shoulder, and in the shaking bands the floor is nearly bare. Seen from above through the clear water, each pool wears a faint pattern of pale arcs and blank ones, like ripples frozen in chalk. The pattern was the same last year and will be the same next year. Nothing decided on it. Each animal wanders until its feet feel comfortable and stays there.

They shed their shells, and each shell is a small pale crescent that the water cements to the rim within days. Over the ages the shelf has taken in countless generations of them. The rims that hold the pools are partly made of the animals that lived in them, and no one in the pools has ever known it.

## The ones in the air

The swifts nest on the cliffs above the shelf's upper edge, in crevices where the seep is thin enough to leave a dry ledge. They are grey, and the grey darkens to slate when their feathers are wet, which is always. Their wings are long and swept, and they come down out of the blue into the mist so fast that the eye loses them and finds them again a moment later, dark against the glare. Their eyes are large and black and shielded by a clear lid that closes against the light. They fly through the maze of columns by sight alone, threading gaps barely wider than their wingspan, because sight is all they have.

The roar is total for them as well. A swift cannot hear its mate, its chick, or its own alarm, so over ages the birds have built a language of the body. It is precise, and it is the only one on the shelf.

A wing-flash is the pale underwing turned to the sun for half a beat, and it means *here*. A tail spread and held means *come*, or *I am staying*, depending on how far the head tilts. A head tilted sharply and held, with the bill aimed like a finger, points out a place, a fly-hatch, a good crevice, or a danger. A bird flattened against a ledge with every feather pressed close means *something above*. A courting pair flies in mirrored loops, each matching the other wingbeat for wingbeat, and this is how one bird tells another it is worth trusting. Chicks learn all this by watching. Their first weeks are spent staring at their parents' bodies, and they seem to grow up reading rather than listening.

They have names for places, in their way. The rainbows never move, so a swift can point to the bow at the foot of the third great cascade and every other swift will know exactly where to fly. The rainbows are the birds' landmarks, and the fixed light is their map. A young bird who wanders looks for the nearest bow the way a lost child looks for a familiar door.

Where they gather at dusk on the high ledges, hundreds of them, the whole colony speaks at once. The air fills with flashing and pointing and tail-fans, and the only sound is water. It is the most eloquent thing on the shelf, and you could stand in it all day and take it for nothing but birds turning.

## The ones in the shade

In the blue-green shadows at the base of the tallest columns, the stone wears a thin brown-green skin. It is not moss or algae in any way that would satisfy a botanist, but a film of single cells in a gel, fed on dissolved mineral and the little light that gets through. It is alive in the way a coal is warm. It does not spread. It does not shrink. Each patch keeps its margins against the water that would scour it and the stone that would bury it, and neither side gains.

If the shelf has a patient resident, this is it. It has no need for the chord and no notion of the falls. It only holds, layer on layer, some of it turned to travertine and some still soft, so that a slice of the rock would show its whole history as a striped record of brown and cream. It is older than the birds and the crustaceans, and it will outlast them, and it has never once hurried.

## Passing through

A goat sometimes comes up from the lower slopes, having followed a ledge farther than sense allows, and stands at the shelf's edge with its legs braced and its eyes wide. It can hear nothing but the roar. The goat stays a day or two, drinks from the cold water, and goes back down. Occasionally it does not, and then it is a pale shape on a terrace, and later a smooth one, and later a rise in the rim that no one could name.

A beetle is blown up on the wind. It lands on a wet ledge, walks a few steps, and is already dusted with white.

Visitors also come, on their own feet, and stand a long time with their hands over their ears and then, slowly, with their hands down. They feel the chord arrive. They leave no path and no cairn, only a smoothness in the crust that the next rain will close over. They are the only residents who know they are visiting.

## What the shelf has

There is no ruler here and no builder, and nothing is remembered. What the shelf has instead is a settled arrangement of the small: crustaceans arranged by a note they cannot hear, swifts speaking with their bodies in a silence made of thunder, and a brown film holding its edge in the shadow. Each of them has learned to live inside something too big to be heard, by feeling it, by ignoring it, or by simply staying put.

The chord sounds beneath them all. None of them made it, and it makes room for them anyway.
