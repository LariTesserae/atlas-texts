# Those Who Keep the Sump

There are sixty of them, give or take a birth or a funeral, and they think of themselves less as a village than as a crew. Nobody says "I live in Harrow Lock." They say "I'm on the Lock," the way you would say you were on a ship, and they mean the same thing by it: the water is outside, the work is continuous, and we are all aboard.

## Their bodies

They are not tall. The upper lanes are low and the doorframes lower, and generations of ducking have given the older people a habitual forward tilt of the head, as if always about to enter somewhere. Their skin is damp more often than dry, and rarely cold. The women's and men's hands alike are broad and scarred across the backs from machete slips and pipe flanges, and the nails of the oldest are ridged and yellowed by decades in tea-colored water. Almost everyone has a pale crescent of scar on the left forearm, where the vines seem to reach in when you cut them. Children are proud of their first one.

They wear cotton that goes faintly green at the seams within a month, and they have stopped minding. Boots are rubber, resoled until nothing of the original is left, and hung by the door on pegs with the toes pointing down so they drain. Everyone carries something on the belt: a hooked knife, a rag, a length of cord. The crew who work inside the pump hall carry a small brass whistle as well, for reasons that will be clear.

Their hearing is bad, and they know it, and there is no shame in it. By fifty most people have lost the top of their range, and by seventy a good many hear the pumps only as pressure in the chest. What they have in exchange is a strange refined sense for the works themselves. A minder can stand in the doorway of the hall with her eyes shut and tell you that the number two bearing is running warm, or that a screen is starting to blind with weed, from the pitch of the whine alone. She could not explain how. She would say only, "It sounds thirsty."

## How they talk

Outside the hall they speak in short, loud sentences and look at each other's mouths, and the habit has leaked into every corner of life. Conversations in the lanes have a shouted, generous, slightly comic quality. A woman leans from her upper window and sends the day's whole news across the washing lines in six sentences, each one complete, each one delivered with a small nod at the end to show it is finished. Quiet talk is reserved for the sick and for the very small.

Inside the hall they don't shout at all, because it does no good. They use hand-talk, a working sign language that began as a dozen gestures for *stop*, *open*, *more*, *watch that* and has grown over a century into something with jokes in it, and gossip, and swearing. Children learn it before they learn words. Lovers use it across the canal. It has a sign for each of the three pumps, a sign for the river, and a sign, made by pressing the flat of the hand slowly downward, that means both *the water rising* and *be patient*. The two meanings were never separate.

## The crew

The cutters go out first, at gray light, in a line along the canal bank with machetes and long-handled pruning saws. They are mostly the young and the strong, and they are proud of it. Their day is spent clearing the flywheel guards, the intake grates, and the switchgear cabinets, and by the time they reach the far end the near end has begun to grow over. They know this. The oldest joke on the Lock is that the cutters have never once finished, and the second-oldest is that they never will, and that this is the job. A good cutter has a rhythm that carries into her sleep, and her arm twitches at night.

The skimmers are the weekly crew, and they work the duckweed with long rakes from the canal edges in a slow, wading, companionable kind of labor. They talk more than anyone, since the canal is quieter than the hall. What they rake up goes into the compost, and the compost goes into the boiler-plate boxes, and the boxes grow the chilies. Everyone can trace the loop and takes a quiet satisfaction in it.

The minders have the hall. There are nine of them, and they are a little apart from the others. They are precise and watchful and slightly stiff with the weight of what they hold. They oil, they grease, they listen, and they log everything in ledgers that go back to the steam days, in a dozen different hands. The head minder holds the post for life, and at present it is a broad, unhurried woman named Tamsin Oke, who is sixty-one, nearly deaf, and the best reader of the machines the Lock has had in living memory. She calls the pumps by nicknames, Old Meg, the Sister, and the Bishop. She does not think of them as alive. She only talks to them the way you talk to a large animal that you respect.

Then there are the wall crew, who go up onto the embankment and tend the great face of it, the clay and stone and turf that stands between the settlement and the river. They are few and serious. They walk the wall each dawn and each dusk, looking for seepage, for a soft spot, for the dark weeping stain that means trouble. They say very little about what they see, and the rest of the Lock does not ask. When one of them comes down early, the lanes go quiet in a way they do at no other time.

And there are the ones who do everything else: the cook at the canteen, whose kitchen window is never without its plume; the lathe-man who makes and remakes the parts no catalog carries; the teacher, who runs the schoolroom and wipes the mildew from the slate each morning with a cloth she has stopped noticing is damp; the switch-room keeper, who sits among the transformers with a book and a kettle and is the only person on the Lock who knows what silence is for.

## What they believe

They don't have a religion, exactly, but they have a temperament, and it is a very consistent one. They believe in maintenance. They believe that a thing looked after is a thing that lasts, and that a thing ignored is a thing already lost, and that most disasters are simply the small neglected thing arriving late. They are suspicious of grand gestures. They are not much impressed by cleverness. They admire steadiness.

They are patient in a way visitors find unsettling. Nobody hurries. The houses lean and no one panics. A door swells and is shouldered open. A vine takes a windowsill and is cut, and takes it again, and is cut. There is a shrug they do, a slow lift of one shoulder and a tilt of the head toward the river, that means roughly, *Well. It'll be there tomorrow, and so will we.*

And yet they know. All of them know, even the children, that the bowl sinks and the wall rises, and that a day will come, not in their time, but someone's, when the wall cannot be raised further or the pumps cannot be fed. They carry that knowledge with the same ease they carry the damp: as a condition of the place, not an argument against living in it. They will say, if asked, "It'll last longer than I will," and mean it kindly.

## The others who live here

The Lock isn't only theirs.

The strangler figs are residents in the truest sense, older than anyone alive, holding the warehouse corners with their braided roots. The crew talk about them the way one talks about elderly relatives who cannot be moved. The great fig on the boiler stack is the oldest, and its roots are known to have cracked the brickwork twice. Both times the crew went up and bound it with iron straps, and the tree simply grew around the straps. Nobody has proposed cutting it down. Its shade falls across the hall roof, and its leaves come down into the gutters, and that is understood to be part of the arrangement.

The heron is another. It arrives before the cutters most mornings and stands on the outfall wall. Some of the older people say it is the same heron, and others say there have been a dozen, and neither side is bothered by the contradiction. Children are told not to throw anything. Adults nod to it as they pass, without quite knowing they do.

The monitor lizard that crosses the lane is treated with courtesy, given right of way, and remarked upon: *"There goes the Magistrate."* The frogs are pitied a little for their silent singing. The snails are forgiven the chilies.

And the pumps, though nobody would say so aloud, are treated as something more than machines. They are fed, and rested, and listened to. Their birthdays, meaning the dates of their installation, are marked with an extra ration of sweet tea in the hall. When a bearing is replaced, the old one is not thrown away but hung on the wall of the hall, on a nail, with the date chalked beside it. There are hundreds now. The wall of old bearings, rusted and glistening, is the closest thing the Lock has to a shrine.

## Night

At night the lamps come on, yellow and haloed, and the lanes fill with the smell of frying and wet wood and chili. People sit on their steps and talk in the loud short sentences, and children are called in by name across the roofs, and the washing hangs unmoving in the warm air. The pumps thud on beneath everything, and by the time the last lamp is out the sound has gone through the floors and into the bed frames and into the chests of the sleepers, until no one can say where the pumps end and the people begin.

They sleep well. It is the one thing outsiders always remark on, that people who live in such noise sleep so deeply. The Lock's answer, when anyone asks, is a small smile and a tilt of the head toward the river, and after a moment, if you are still standing there, a sentence in the flat, unhurried voice of someone who has said it many times.

*"It's when it stops that you wake up."*
