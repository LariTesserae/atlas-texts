# Who Keeps the Shelf

The first thing to understand is that most of the inhabitants are not looking at you. Very little of the Shelf's life is turned toward the visitor. Frogs look at the insects a hand's width from their noses. The beetles at the vents attend to the fungus and the breath that feeds it. The vipers in the bromeliads are entirely occupied with waiting. The Shelf has a great many residents and none of them is a host.

But there is one kind of inhabitant that a patient person eventually comes to suspect, and then to know. Lowland people who have spent nights up here call them by a word that means something like *the sitters*. The word is almost a joke, and it is also accurate.

## The Boulders That Are Not

The plateau is scattered with rounded stones the size of houses, furred with moss, dark with rain. Some of them are stones. Perhaps one in nine is not.

The difference is hard to see at first. A sitter looks like a boulder that has been sitting in water a little too long: a low, domed back, green-black and upholstered like everything else, with ferns rooted in it, a scatter of small orchids, and a skin of algae the color of wet enamel. The dome is smooth but not quite symmetrical. It is warmer than the rock beside it, even by the standards of a warm mountain. If you lay your palm on it, you may after a minute or two feel a slow pulse through the moss, a swell and release about as long as the pause between the vents' huffs.

They are amphibians, or something in the long family of soft-skinned, water-tied things that resemble amphibians. A grown sitter is four to six meters long, though nobody has ever seen one stretched out. They are always folded, chin to the mud, limbs tucked under a body like a dropped sack of wet grain. The head is broad and low, the eyes small and set high, almost on top, with lids that are grown over with moss more often than not. The mouth is a long shallow curve, closed, patient, and faintly smiling in the way of large slow animals that have never had to hurry.

The skin is the remarkable part. It is not smooth. It is a thick, warm, mucous-slick hide, with a fine pelt of algae and filamentous green threads growing directly out of it. This is not neglect. The sitters cultivate it. In the mucus live colonies of microbes, and in the colonies live tiny mites and worms, and the whole thing is a garden on a living hill. Small orange frogs lay eggs in the folds behind the sitters' jaws. Water beetles ride in the wrinkles of the neck. Bromeliad seeds lodge in the moss on the back and thrive, so that the oldest sitters wear whole miniature forests, with pools in their leaf-cups and tadpoles in those pools. When a sitter finally moves, a rain of creatures falls off it, and most of them climb back.

## How They Live

They eat very little, and very slowly. Snails are the mainstay, drawn in from the warm streams with a tongue that unrolls and retracts almost too fast to see, the single quick thing about them. They also swallow the drowned contents of pitcher plants, fallen fruit that rolls into the pools, blind pale shrimp that gather at the springs, and the occasional unlucky viper. But a sitter may go many weeks between meals. Its metabolism is tuned to the heat from below the way a sundial is tuned to the sun. In the warm springs it is nearly self-sufficient, drawing energy from the gradient itself, from the heat and the mineral chemistry of the water, through skin and gill and gut, in ways that lowland naturalists who have looked at them still argue about.

They breathe through the skin and through a set of wide, slow gills tucked under the jaw, and now and then, at long intervals, through a pair of small nostrils at the tip of the snout. When a sitter surfaces to breathe, it is a dull, wet huff, a warm exhalation stirring the moss around its face.

This is the sound people mistake for the vents. In truth the two are entangled. The sitters settle over the cracks. They favor the fissures where the warm gas rises, and they lie across them like a cork, so that the ground's breath comes up through them. Some vents have huffed for as long as anyone has recorded, and have a sitter over them. Some have gone quiet since a sitter moved on. Whether the sitters found the vents or the vents were shaped by the sitters is an old question that nobody has a way to settle.

They live for a very long time. The largest are thought to be older than the stands of tree ferns that grow on their backs. Growth is measured, if at all, in the thickness of the moss and travertine that has built up around a sitter's flanks. Some have been sitting so long that they have sunk a hand's depth into the mat and been quietly ringed with white stone.

## The Shift Change

It is at the geyser cycle that the sitters become visible as inhabitants rather than terrain.

The line of small geysers along the base of the ridge fires north to south, one after another, over forty minutes. As the wave of hot mist rolls across the terraces, the sitters wake. Their eyelids crack the moss. The dome of the back lifts slightly, an inch or two, in a long inhalation. A deep sound comes out of them that is not quite a voice. It is closer to a pressure change, felt in the sternum more than heard, a low bell tone the length of a breath. On a still evening, when all of them do it in a rough sequence across the terraces, the sound rolls downhill in the same direction as the mist.

This is the tocking that visitors first take for frogs. It is not. The small frogs' tocking is a higher echo, a response that they pick up and pass along. The sitters' tone is the bell that the frogs are answering, and the ferns' unrolling and curling are keyed to it in turn.

Whether the sitters *cause* the geysers to fire in order, or the geysers are the clock that wakes the sitters, cannot be told from watching. There are hints in both directions. The geysers skip when the nearest sitter has died. A sitter shifts its weight, and the ground beneath it settles and the fissures under it narrow or widen, and a spring changes its temperature by several degrees within a week. It may be that the sitters tend the plumbing in the only way their bodies allow: by lying on it, shifting a little, and lying on it again. It would be the slowest possible maintenance, a repair done over generations by simply being there.

## The Others Who Come

Other things live in the sitters' orbit, and are their attendants in a way that is closer to weather than to service.

The swifts that arrive at each mist wave wheel in a cloud over the backs of the nearest sitters, and sometimes settle on them briefly to pick off the insects that the steam has driven up out of the moss. The sitters tolerate this the way a hillside tolerates birds.

The heavy, otter-like animal of the warm streams shares the snail-eating with them and seems to have made some accommodation. It slides past a sitter's snout without haste, and the sitter's eye-lid does not lift. They may compete, but a sitter's meals are so rare that an otter's daily consumption doesn't bother it. Sometimes the otters are seen sleeping curled against a sitter's flank, on the warm side, in the wet weather.

The dappled tree-cat, the once-a-season presence that silences the frogs, is said to avoid the terraces where the largest sitters lie. This may be nothing. A cat could well have no interest in a stone. But the lowlanders who come up rarely insist on it, and they insist too that the cats, when they do come down to the water, go around.

## The Lowland People

Those lowlanders are few, and they come for reasons they don't always give: medicinal moss, certain orchids, the mineral water, or a season of solitude. They have never stayed. They speak of the sitters carefully. Some say the sitters are the mountain's own body, that the Shelf is a single creature and the sitters are its knuckles. Some say they are the last of an older people, turned slow by the heat. Some say only that you should not sit on a boulder without first putting your hand on it.

They leave nothing for the sitters, no offerings, no marks. There is no cult here, only a habit of courtesy: a hand laid on the moss before resting, a quiet word at dusk, a way of stepping around certain shapes. The habit is old enough that no one can say where it began.

## The Pipe

Near the top of the third terrace, by the boggy pool, the tree has grown around its length of rusted iron. There is a sitter nearby. It is not a large one, rather a middle-aged sitter as they reckon it, three or four hundred years, its back dark under a mat of sedge. It lies with its snout just a few paces from the tree, and its posture is the same as that of all the others. But when the morning steam threads from the open end of the pipe, this sitter's eye is often, though not always, a little more open than it needs to be.

It would be too easy to say that the sitter watches the pipe, or waits for the one who made it. Nothing suggests it thinks in that way. But among all the made and unmade things of the Shelf, the pipe is the one object that does not belong to the slow accounting of moss and water, and the sitters, who tend the whole plateau by the simple act of lying on it, seem to have left this one alone. The small orange frog that lives in the bore is undisturbed. The wasps go in and out at the join. The tree has not been pushed over, and the iron has not been pulled out.

## What They Are, Perhaps

If you sit at dusk in the middle of a terrace, with the steam rising and the last drops falling from the fronds, you may find that you have been sitting beside one for an hour without knowing. A slow warm breath moves the moss beside your knee. The frog chorus rises as the bell tone rolls past under you like a low swell in the ground. And you understand that the machine you imagined, the well-maintained engine that forgot it was built, has operators after all. They are not clever, and they are not attending to you. They are only present, in the way a bearing is present in a wheel, warm, wet, patient, and very old, keeping time by lying still.
