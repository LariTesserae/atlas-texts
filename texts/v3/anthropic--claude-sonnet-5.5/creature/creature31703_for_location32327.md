# The Settler

You will not see it at first. The water is clear to the floor, and your eye slides down the terraces past the wheels and the pale weed, past the clean brick, and finds nothing. What you notice is the sound. Under the bronze *bonnng* of the hammer-rack, and the knocking wheels, and the hiss of the inlet, there is one soft note that belongs to none of them. Follow it down to the deepest terrace. The last plate hangs from its beam, and beside the beam something is standing in the current.

It is about the height of a child. It stands across the flow, where the weed and the silt and everything else in the room lean with it, and this gives it away. Nothing else here holds a position against the water.

## The body

At the base is a knot the size of a fist, dark as old iron and faintly rust-red where the light reaches it. It is not stone, though it sits on the floor like stone. It is fixed to the brick by a broad, thin disc of paler material that has spread outward a finger's width from the knot and stopped at the mortar line, as if it had read the joint and declined to cross it.

From the knot rise the filaments, several hundred of them, each finer than a hair and clear as the water. Each ends in a bead of hard white grit, and the beads are not all the same size. Toward the outside of the fan they are the size of grains of millet, and near the center they are as small as dust. The whole thing streams and shivers in the current like a harp strung in a doorway.

That is close to what it is. The beads are how it hears. When the plate hums, the beads answer, each in its own pitch, and the filaments carry the trembling down to the dark knot, where it becomes something like knowing. It has no eyes. It has no need of them. The lattice of light from the surface reaches it as a faint flickering warmth along the tips of the longest threads, and in that warmth it has learned the hour, the weather, and the passing of clouds.

## What it does

It feeds twice a day.

When the float and counterweight tip and the gates clatter open, the floor lifts into amber fog. The filaments spread wide, the whole fan opening across the current like a hand, and the beads comb the silt as it goes by. What clings is iron, sulfur, and fine mineral scum from the vent. The knot darkens as it takes them in and settles again as the water clears. The rest of the day it waits and listens, which for the Settler are the same act.

Almost nothing else could live on what it lives on. The water is clear because nothing else has come. It came up from the vent in a single gout of silver bubbles, in the first hour the room was filled, as a mote too small to see. It found the one place in the chamber where iron, warmth, current, and a struck note all met, and it stayed.

## What it knows

It knows the rhythm of the hammer-rack better than any mason knew it. The three cams against seven, then eleven, nearly repeat and never quite do, and it has spent every day since the filling trying to find the place where they come round. It has not found it. It does not seem to mind. Each time the pattern almost closes, the filaments lean together in a small clenching, and each time it slips they loosen. This may be the nearest thing it has to pleasure.

It does not know that the makers are gone. It has never known them. It thinks of the mechanism as weather, or as a large animal that breathes through the gates and speaks through the plates, and it lives inside that animal without any fear. The mallet on the fourth terrace, with its one side worn dark, is the only object it has ever laid a filament across that was not made to turn, ring, or hold back water. It has done this a few times, gently, and each time withdrew as if the wood were warm.

## What it is doing to the place

This is the thing worth knowing about the Settler. It is the first tenant, and it is also the first wear.

Its grit beads are shed and regrown, and in a year it lets go of thousands. Most wash out through the western sluice. A few lodge in the mortar, in the seams where the water runs slow. Look at the sluice-lip and you will see, if you look closely, one small fleck of white where every other joint is the color of wet bone. That is a bead that took hold. Mineral is gathering around it in a skin as thin as breath, and the skin will thicken, and the next bead will land on it.

In this way the Settler is the beginning of the bloom, the fur, the softening, the long slow loss of edges that in a hundred years will make the cistern look as though it had always been part of the hill. It does not intend this. It is only living. But something in its slow attention seems to know, because it keeps its fan close to the deepest terrace and almost never spreads beyond the beam. It takes what the surge brings and gives back as little as it can. It leaves the exact corners exact, for now.

## The hum

Watch the plate when the surge rises just right, and you will see the filaments and the bronze move together. Whether the plate wakes the Settler or the Settler draws the plate into its note, no one has been present to decide. The sound is soft enough that the hammer-rack swallows it, and it goes on through the racket, one low sustained tone under the bright, hard, clanging room.

It is the only sound in the cistern that no one made on purpose. It is the sound of the place beginning, quietly, to have someone in it.
