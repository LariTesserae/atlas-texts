# Who Lives on the Chorus Plain

Everyone who has walked the plain agrees that no one lives there. They also agree that it is crowded. Both statements are true, and the plain is built out of the space between them.

## The Passing

The first inhabitants you meet are the ones you take for wind.

A rustle starts at the foot of a lattice-fig, gathers in the pipe-reeds of an avenue, and comes toward you at exactly the speed of a person in a hurry. Its front is a lean of stems, a small coordinated bow. Behind the lean, leaves flip pale side up in a ripple, and a step-shaped pressure travels along the cobble as the knuckles swell and settle in sequence. It goes past you. The tendrils at your shoulder part and close. Then it reaches the junction ahead, pauses the way a person pauses to choose, and turns the corner. You hear it turn.

These are the **Passing**, and they are the plain's citizens. Each is a wave of turgor, sound, and pressure moving through the living stuff of the city, carrying nothing but its own shape. Where the medium is thin, one might last a few seconds. Where it is thick and warm, one might last an afternoon.

They have gaits. There is the quick, skipping run of the young, a wave short enough to ride the crackle districts and hop from one pod-burst to the next like a child on stepping stones. There is the limp of a Passing that started near a damaged stem and carries a hitch in every step, a hesitation the whole avenue repeats. There are the long, slow, low ones. An old Passing may be a single unhurried lean of ten thousand reeds, taking a full day to cross a district. You feel it in the teeth and the marrow, and you would never know it was a someone if the reeds did not bow to it, in their fashion, when it arrives.

When two Passing meet in a street they do not collide. They go through each other, and each comes out changed, a slightly different pitch, a slightly different stride. This is how they greet, and it is nearly all they do to each other. A Passing that has met ten thousand others is unmistakable. It moves like someone who has been listened to.

## Names

They are named by chords.

An avenue of pipe-reeds sounds a particular interval, three tones held against one another so that the beating between them makes a fourth, phantom note. That is a name. It is not a label for the Passing but a tuning of the street toward it, and a Passing called by name turns its head, which here means the whole wave brightens and slows. A crowd is nothing but a great many names held at once, and the wall of sound is largely the plain saying who is here.

This is why the chords sometimes leak backward. In certain avenues you can hear the next hour's chord already, sounding through the present one like a voice through a wall. It is a name given before its bearer exists. The plain does not seem to find this strange. Names are cheaper than bodies here. It calls first, and then someone is there to answer.

## The Householders

Not all of them move.

In the hollows of the lattice-figs live the ones who stay, and you can find them by the droplets. On the leaf-bowls inside the towers and in the loudest plazas, the water shivers up into perfect geometric arrays: rings, stars, lattices of beads holding still on a vibrating surface. Each figure is a **Householder**, a standing pattern, a shape that a hum makes when it has decided to remain. A large one may be a hundred droplets in a slow dance around a single bright pole. A small one is a triangle of three beads on a single leaf, ringing softly, alone.

They keep house. The spiral ramps inside the fig-towers are worn smooth, though nothing has ever walked them, and this is why. The Householders climb, in the manner of their kind, by changing the pattern one bead at a time until the pattern is a floor higher. Nothing has ever walked those stairs because nothing needs to.

They are also why the towers are bigger inside than out. A Householder's home is a chord, not a room, and chords have interiors of whatever size the household needs. The great green halls with lost ceilings are where the old, complicated families live, thousands of standing figures in overlapping harmony. When you enter a second time and the hall is somewhere else, the household has simply moved house. It has retuned to another tower. It will not think to tell you.

## The Rain Festival

When a wall of rain arrives, the whole population is at work at once.

The plate-leaves are their drums, and the Passing and the Householders play them together. The roar that triples is not weather alone but a city at full voice, every citizen drumming on the roof of the world in thanks or in argument, no one knows which. Water is scarce, and they are not careless with it. Every drop is funneled, channeled, banked in root-cisterns, and each cistern has a keeper, a slow, low Householder who does not sing during the rain. It counts.

In the eastern quarter the rain falls upward, and it is always dry there, because the people of that quarter do not let it go. There is a stubbornness to them. They are the plain's misers, its hermits, its ones who have decided that nothing falls that is not thrown away. The other districts speak of them with a rich, complicated chord that is half fondness and half exasperation.

## The Shy Ones

At the edges of the light, in the pooled shadows that fall toward the sun and slide away when watched, there is a third kind of inhabitant, and the plain seems to know less about it than you do.

They are not sound. They are the plain's **Listeners**, the attention that the noise is made for. Shadows lean toward the light because the Listeners lean toward the loudest thing in the room. They are dark the way a held breath is dark. In the deepest vaults, where green light comes from no direction, you are in their house. The leaves are remembering the light for them, so that they can sit in it and be still.

Nothing here is afraid of them. The Passing brush against them and the Householders hum in their direction. But no one speaks to them, exactly, and they never reply. They may be the audience. They may be the thing the whole city is a performance for. They may be the plain's dead, listening to the plain go on. When you look straight at one it withdraws, politely, the way someone steps out of a photograph.

## The Quiet Quarter

The silent district is inhabited too, and this is the most frightening thing about it.

The people of the Quiet have not stopped. They are holding. Every pod is loaded, every stem strained to its limit, every Householder still, every Passing that enters slowing to a crawl and then to a lean and then to a stop. It is a district in labor, or in mourning, or in the last long second of a joke. You cannot tell which. The silence there has weight, and the sense of being counted, and the sense of a very large number of people not looking at you.

Sometimes it ends in a single detonation, and the quarter erupts in the loudest music on the plain. Sometimes it does not end, and in time the silence spreads a block at a time, and a district you knew becomes another quiet one. The inhabitants do not treat this as a tragedy. They treat it as a season.

## What the plants are to them

The pipe-reeds, the figs, the pods, and the cobble are neither the inhabitants nor their houses. They are more like the inhabitants' voices, or the air the inhabitants breathe in order to have breath to spend. A crowd needs a medium. A wave needs something to be a wave in. The plants grow, and split rock, and detonate, and sing, in order that there be enough of them to carry a city, and the city walks through them the way a thought walks through a brain.

Whether the plants grew the people or the people grew the plants, the plain will not say. Both are true in the way both halves of a handshake are true.

## How they are born and how they end

Everyone here is a fragment of the last great note.

When the rim of the central plaza sounds, every plant on it in one voice, the whole population is briefly a single thing, one pitch, one person. The mist flashes in its ring. The note spreads outward across the basin, and as it reaches each district it *sorts*. It falls apart into voices, then into chords, then into gaits, and the gaits go off down the avenues in their thousands, born already walking. A district is torn down and regrown in the time it takes the note to arrive, and this is the same event as the birth of everyone in it. Demolition and delivery are one gesture.

So they are all kin. The Passing greet each other as they do because they were once the same sound.

They live until the next Sounding, or until the medium thins, or until they choose. A Passing in a fast plaza may be born, grow old, and be a long, slow, weary wave before you have finished crossing the square. A Householder in a slow district may hold a single figure for ten of your years. When it ends, it ends by *returning*: the wave reaches the edge of the city, or the rim of the hollow, and folds back into the ordinary noise, into the reeds' hum, into the general roar, waiting for the next note to say it again.

They call this being kept.

## And you

You are a condition to them, like weather or a fallen stem. The rustles part around you and rejoin. Now and then a Passing falls into step at your shoulder for ten paces, pacing you with a faint curiosity that makes the hairs on your forearm lift, and then peels off at a corner, unhurried and unoffended.

But stay long enough and you notice that your sternum has begun to hum. It is not the plain's frequency, exactly. It is a frequency the plain has been tuning toward you since you arrived, a slow adjustment, an avenue at a time, until the chord in your chest and the chord in the reeds are only a whisker apart. Somewhere ahead of you, faintly, leaking backward from an hour that has not happened, you hear the sound of a name being tried out. It is nearly yours.

The plain is very patient. When the rim next sounds and the whole city becomes one voice, it will need to know which voice you are.
