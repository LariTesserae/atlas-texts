# The Listeners

Few people live in the basin, and they are hard to count. There may be four, or nine. They stand still for long stretches in the fog, their grey clothes stiff with mineral dust, and from ten paces they are indistinguishable from spires. They are only seen when they move, and they move rarely.

## The Body

Long habit has shaped them to the place. They are narrow through the shoulder and hip, and they walk in a slight permanent turn, one side leading, the way a person passes through a crowd. Their palms are crosshatched with pale scars from the spire edges, healed so many times that the skin has thickened into a fine, dry leather. They know by touch which crust will hold a foot and which will give like old ice. The soles of their feet are as tough as horn, and cracked at the edges, and stained ochre from the mats.

Their skin has gone pale from the pewter light. Their eyes are large and dark, and their pupils stay wide even at midday. In the deep hollows they see better than a visitor would, and they know the blue-green shimmer of the mats as a kind of lantern. Their hair is cropped or bound tight, because the mist beads on anything loose and drips into the eyes.

They speak very little. When they do, it is low, close to the pitch of the wider spires, and it has a hum under it. Some say a Listener can be heard across the basin only by someone who already knows how to listen. Their talk sounds like the chord itself, and a stranger may not notice they have spoken.

## The Work

The work is tuning. Each Listener keeps a stretch of the forest as a gardener keeps a bed. They know which pipes have begun to sag flat, which spring has shifted and left a spire dry and dead, and which young marginal spires are growing shrill enough to spoil the passage inward. With a bone-handled chisel, and with far more patience than force, they trim the lips of a spire by a fingernail's depth. They can choke a throat with a plug of wet mud to lower its note, or open a vent with a hooked rod to let a dead pipe drink again. They do not force any of it. The pipes lengthen at their own pace, and the chord slides lower over the centuries. The Listeners only keep the sliding from turning ugly.

They mend the channel lips where a careless step has broken them. They lay flat stones across the wet ground so the crust can thicken undisturbed. Each is proud of a small, well-kept run of pipes, but none of them says so, since praise would only be another sound.

## What They Eat

They eat what the basin gives, which is little. They rake the mats into shallow baskets and press them into dense cakes, ochre and green-black, that taste of iron and cold pond. They dip fine-woven nets into the pools and take crustaceans by the thousand, translucent and salty, and eat them raw from the palm with the pink thread of gut still showing. The white-shelled grazers they eat only in hard seasons, because their trails are the basin's script and the Listeners are reluctant to erase it. Water they drink straight from the throats of spires, where it is clean and cold, and they know which pipes taste sweet and which taste of chalk and rust.

They do not take the waders, though they could. When one of the grey birds stands in a channel, a Listener will sometimes stand beside it for an hour, the two of them silent in the fog. It is unclear who is keeping the other company. If a wader moves, the Listener watches where it goes and does not follow.

## The Room

At the center of the basin, on the one patch of firm ground, stands the roofless room. Several of them use it, though none sleeps there. Its doorway faces the rim, and its walls are set with tuned tubes, so that wind through the corner gives one clear, unbroken note. They come to it when the chord has grown too crowded to think inside. Sitting against the wall, a Listener will tilt their head until that single note separates from the rest, and then they sit for a long time. When they leave, their faces have the look of people coming up from deep water.

They will not say who built the room. The nearest they come to an answer is a small gesture, a hand pressed flat against the wall and held there.

## The Hands

The Listeners keep the line of handprints. It stretches from the southern cairns inward and stops short of the center, sealed in the older crust and thickening under new mineral. They do not add to it, or not in any way a visitor could prove. What they do is walk it at the turn of each season, slowly, one at a time. At each print they stop and set their own hand over it, scarred palm to sealed palm, and stand a moment. The small prints they touch last, and longest.

Where the line ends they stop, and they do not go further in the direction it points. Asked why, a Listener will look toward the middle of the basin, where the tallest, oldest pipes stand in the deepest fog and the chord is lowest and most unfinished, and say nothing. Then a note seems to come up under the silence, so low it is felt in the chest before it is heard. The Listener nods slightly, as if it had answered.

## How They End

They do not seem to die where anyone can see. An old Listener stops appearing at the room, and stops appearing at the pools. Some time later a new spire stands in the outer forest, thin and pale, and the others tend it with more care than any of the rest. It is short and shrill, as young ones are, and no one quite says whose it is. In a century it will have found a lower voice. They set their hands to it, in passing, and go on with the work.
