# Those Who Keep the Reach

The first thing you see of them is a lamp moving.

It goes down a stair-lane at walking pace, swinging a little with the stride, and it lights a wet handrail, a fern, a stretch of dished tread, and a hand. The person behind the lamp is mostly an idea: an oilcloth coat the color of the walls, a hood, a breath of steam. Then the lane turns and the lamp goes into a foundry doorway and comes out somewhere else, or doesn't. You will see a thousand of these before you see a face, and you will come to understand that this is a courtesy. In the Reach you do not look up into a stranger's lamp. You look at their feet, at where they are stepping, because that is what matters, and because the light is theirs.

## What They Are Called

Outsiders say *Reachfolk*, and the people don't mind it, but it isn't how they think of themselves. They have no word for the whole population, just as a hand has no word for all its fingers. What they have is a way of naming that follows the work.

A person is called by the stretch they are currently tending. *Ninety-Stair Ines. Cistern-Four Odo. Ines-of-the-Leaning-Wall.* When a stair is rerouted or a wall comes down and is rebuilt elsewhere, the name drifts to follow the person's new charge. Someone's childhood name may survive only in the mouth of a mother, or a sibling three levels down. Nobody finds this strange. A name that stayed fixed while the valley moved would be a kind of lie.

They are not sentimental about it, but they are exact. If you ask for someone by an old name, the answer is always the same: a pause, a squint upward, and then *ah, she's on Sluice Nine now,* with a small gesture of the chin toward wherever Sluice Nine currently is.

## Bodies

They are built by stairs. The calves and thighs are heavy and the feet wide and quiet. They carry weight easily on slopes and set their heels first on wet stone, and they walk with a slightly rolling, unhurried gait that looks lazy until you try to keep up. Their hands are the most distinctive part: broad, cracked at the knuckles, the pads of the fingers pitted and polished by iron rails the way the rails are polished by them. Old workers have a bright, shiny patch at the base of each thumb, and they are quietly proud of it.

The skin is pale and always faintly damp, and the eyes are adapted to amber. They are slow to adjust to the flat gray of open sky, and on the rare clear-ish days when the ceiling lifts they squint and pull their hoods forward, and the children complain that the light is *thin.*

The coats are heavy oilcloth, re-waxed each season with a rendered fat and resin that gives every lane a smell of candle and pine. Moss grows on the shoulders of the older coats, and nobody brushes it off. A coat with a good green collar belongs to someone who has stood a long time in one place doing a careful job. It is a kind of rank, though no one would call it that.

## Speech, and Whistling

They speak softly. This isn't shyness. Moss and wet air take voices, and a shout in the lanes dies at arm's length. Ordinary talk happens close and low, with heads tilted together, so that a conversation between two workers on a stair looks like a secret whether or not it is.

For distance they whistle. The whistle-speech is thin and high and pitched to cut through fog, and it moves from terrace to terrace in short phrases, each about the length of a breath. It has grammar. There are phrases for *water rising,* *load coming down,* *I am on the plank, wait,* and *someone has fallen.* The last is the only one that is ever repeated three times.

Every whistle is tuned against the note of the penstocks, the low continuous tone under the whole valley. When a valve closes and the tone drops half a step, the whistling shifts to follow it, so quickly that the whole valley seems to adjust its pitch in one breath. Visitors never hear it happen. The Reachfolk cannot fail to.

## Houses

Homes are found more than built, then moved, then found again.

A family may live in a length of decommissioned pipe fitted with a floor and a stovepipe and hung with quilts against the condensation. Or in a room inside a retaining wall, behind a low door, with a window slit that looks out at the wet backs of other people's houses. Or in a gallery: a small cantilevered building with a kettle, four bunks, a cat, and a view of the fog, which is unbolted from its cables when the works require it and lowered by winch to a new level while the household goes on with its supper. Children lean out the windows on these descents and call the levels as they pass: *tea terraces, pipe-row, the bad rail yard, the good rail yard.* The kettle is not taken off the stove.

Everything has been lived in by someone else first. It is considered ill-mannered not to know who. A newcomer to a room is told its whole succession, all the way back to whoever cut the first window, and is expected to add a mark of their own, a peg, a repaired hinge, a patch of clean plaster, and to tell the next occupant what it was for.

## Food and Warmth

Nothing is offered cold. The custom of hospitality in the Reach is a cup of tea, thick and dark and drunk hot from a tin mug, held in both hands while standing in a doorway. Refusing it is possible but requires explanation. Water is everywhere, so water is never a gift. Heat is the gift.

They eat brassicas boiled to softness in broth, black bread, pickled mountain fruit whose bitterness is considered a virtue, and a fermented river-weed paste that visitors take a week to tolerate. Meals are eaten in shifts at long tables set into the walls of the lower works, where the furnace warmth comes through the stone. No one eats alone if they can help it. A place is always kept at the end for whoever comes off the stair late, and it is understood that this person is not to be asked how their day was until they have finished the bowl.

## Children

They learn the wet tread before they learn to read. By four they can cross a flooded lane on a plank without looking down. By six they carry planks themselves, one end each with a partner, and delivering a plank at the right moment to the right puddle is a game with rules and a serious literature of insults. They know which lamps mean which trades. They know the sound of each sluice. They know that a wall which has just been rebuilt should be touched, gently, with the flat of the hand, and greeted, because it is new and has not yet decided to stay.

They are taught early not to be afraid of the landslip. They are taught instead to *listen for the shift,* the tiny grinding sigh of made ground settling. Every child can do it. It is the first useful thing they know.

## Work as Ceremony

There is no idleness that anyone would call by that name, but there is a great deal of standing. Workers pause on landings with their hands on rails, watching water, and this counts as work. It is called *reading.* The good ones can stand for a quarter hour in a lane and then say, with no evidence you could see, that a downpipe three terraces up is about to clog, and be right.

Repair is the central act. When a wall falls, the crew that lays the new one uses the fallen blocks in their old bond, and before the last course is set the oldest person present presses a hand to the wet mortar and says a phrase, which is not a prayer but is not far from one: *Hold what you were given.*

Death is met the same way. The dead are carried up to the high terraces, to the manufactured soil between the rails, and laid into the beds among the tea, and it is said that they have been *put to the work.* Their unfinished jobs are completed by their neighbors exactly in their manner. If a mender always left a small diagonal seam in her patchwork, the seam is left. A single mark of the dead person's style is kept in the finished repair, somewhere hidden, so that the valley holds a little of every hand that ever worked it. The Reachfolk can walk you through a lane and name, by the mortar alone, eleven generations of people now composted under the fruit.

## The Others

They are not alone, and they do not think of themselves as alone.

The swifts are counted like neighbors. They nest in the pipe-galleries, and when a nest is found, the pipe is left, and the works are routed around it, sometimes at absurd cost. When the swifts stitch the fog at dusk, the lamplighters begin their rounds, and the two events are so linked that some children believe the birds light the lamps.

The frogs in the cisterns are listened to at night, and the shift on the cisterns will not begin until the first frog has sung. If they stop, all work in the lower works halts until someone finds out why.

The fox on the highest terrace is left alone and, by an unspoken arrangement, left a little bread. The rats are not loved but are tolerated, with a shrug, as a form of weather.

And the moss, and the ferns, and the small twisted trees that fall through roofs and are woven into the repair, are treated less like plants than like colleagues of uncertain reliability. One says of a vine on a gantry: *she's carrying that bracket for us this year.* And of a birch on a roof-ledge: *he's about to make trouble.*

## The Sluices

There is one matter on which the Reachfolk become quiet.

No one understands why the sluices open and close as they do. There are keepers, who work them by ledgers that were written by the keepers before them, who worked from ledgers written by keepers before them. The ledgers are contradictory. They give instructions that cancel each other, or that refer to conditions no one now remembers, or that say only *open at the third bell if the tone is low.* The keepers follow them anyway, each in their own reading, and the result is a flood that arrives in a lane at an hour no one predicted, ankle-deep, draining in minutes, in a pattern that is never quite random.

Some say the valley thinks by way of its water. Some say it is nothing but the sum of ten thousand small obedient mistakes. The keepers themselves say little. But on the housing of every sluice, at the base of the wheel, there is a small tin cup, and each morning someone fills it with hot tea. By evening it is empty, or cold, or gone over. It is never discussed. It is simply done, the way you would leave a cup for a neighbor whose habits you have never fully understood, and who has never yet let the whole valley drown.

## The Arrival

If you come in from above, through cloud, and you have no lamp and no plank and no name, someone will find you within an hour. They will look at your feet first. Then they will put a tin mug in your hands, and someone else will hand you a lamp that belonged to a person now in the beds, and someone will point up a stair that was not there last week.

*There's a wall on Seven that wants minding,* they will say. *You'll do.*

By the end of a month you will have a stretch of your own, a green collar beginning at the shoulder of a borrowed coat, and a name that has already started, quietly, to drift. You will find you can hear the tone drop half a step. And one night, coming home along a lane that reopened a meter to the left, you will notice that you have stopped looking up into other people's lamps, and that you are, yourself, a small warm light moving down a stair, and that someone on a terrace above is watching your feet.
